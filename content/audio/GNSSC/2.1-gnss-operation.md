This is GNSSC 2.1, GNSS operation. By the end you will know how a satellite receiver fixes your position, why it needs four satellites and what the fifth and sixth are for, what makes it wrong, and the habits, checks and rules that keep it working for you rather than leading you astray. There are worked examples and a few questions along the way, so pause whenever you want a moment to think.

## What the exam expects (GNSSC 2.1)

The exam wants you to explain how GNSS works, what limits its performance and what errors it suffers. That breaks into seven things. First, how it fixes position. Second, the operating habits that guard against navigational error and loss of situational awareness. Third, how to do typical navigation tasks on the receiver you fly with. Fourth, how the receiver shows that you have passed a waypoint. Fifth, the operational and serviceability checks. Sixth, the human factors limitations of using it. And seventh, the rules that apply to the pilot and the equipment.

## Why this matters

Almost every aeroplane you will fly from Jandakot has a GNSS receiver in the panel, or a tablet on your knee. On a cross-country to Albany or Geraldton, it will show you a moving map, your groundspeed and an ETA, more accurately than you could ever work them out.

That is exactly the danger. The receiver is only as good as the satellites it can see, the database it carries, and the waypoints you typed in. And it gives no warning when you have typed the wrong one. Pilots who let the screen do the navigating lose track of where they are. Then, when the unit fails, or quietly leads them towards a restricted area or a wall of cloud, they have no plan to fall back on.

The exam tests the principles: how many satellites, what RAIM does, and what DOP means. It tests the procedures: the checks, waypoint passage, and cross-checking. And it tests the human factors. It expects the VFR answer. GNSS supplements visual navigation. It does not replace it.

## How a receiver finds its position

GNSS, the global navigation satellite system, is the generic name. The American GPS is the system most aviation receivers use. Galileo, from Europe, GLONASS, from Russia, and BeiDou, from China, are other constellations. And many modern receivers use more than one.

GPS has at least 24 satellites, in six orbital planes, about 20,200 km up. They are arranged so that a receiver anywhere on Earth normally sees at least five or six of them.

First, measuring a range with a clock. Each satellite carries an extremely accurate atomic clock. And it broadcasts two things, continuously. Where it is, which is its orbit data, called the ephemeris. And what time it is. The receiver notes when each message arrives, and compares that with the time stamped on it. The difference is the signal's travel time. Radio waves travel at the speed of light, about 300,000 km per second. So the travel time, multiplied by that speed, gives the distance to the satellite.

Let's work one: how fine the timing has to be. A satellite is 20,200 km away, and the speed of light is about 300,000 km per second. How long does the signal take? And how big a range error does a timing error of one millionth of a second make? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
The travel time is 20,200 divided by 300,000. That is 0.067 seconds, about 67 thousandths of a second.

And the range error. 300,000 km per second, times one millionth of a second, is 0.3 km. That is 300 m.

So a clock that is out by one millionth of a second puts you 300 m out. The satellites' atomic clocks can manage that. The cheap quartz clock in your receiver cannot. And that one fact explains why a receiver needs a fourth satellite.

Next, trilateration: spheres that meet at a point. One range tells the receiver it is somewhere on a sphere of that radius, centred on the satellite. A second range, from another satellite, gives a second sphere. And the two spheres intersect in a circle. A third sphere cuts that circle at two points. One of them is far out in space, or deep inside the Earth, and it can be thrown away. So three ranges would fix a three-dimensional position: latitude, longitude and height. Finding a position from distances like this is called trilateration. It uses ranges. Triangulation would use angles.

Now the fourth satellite, and the receiver's clock. Three would be enough only if the receiver's clock were perfect. And it is not. The receiver's clock error is the same for every satellite it listens to, so every range is wrong by the same amount. Because each range contains this error, it is called a pseudorange.

With an unknown clock error, there are four unknowns. Latitude, longitude, height, and the receiver's clock error. And four unknowns need four measurements. The receiver adjusts its own clock until the four spheres meet at a single point. In doing so, it becomes as accurate as the atomic clocks in the sky.

[[diagram:gnss-trilateration]]
Picture it drawn flat, in two halves. On the left, each satellite's range is a circle centred on the satellite. With a perfect clock, the three circles meet at one point, the receiver's position. On the right, the receiver's clock is wrong. So every range is wrong by the same amount, and the circles no longer meet at a point. Instead they enclose a small triangle. Because the error is the same for every satellite, one extra measurement lets the receiver adjust its clock, shrinking every range by the same amount until they meet. In three dimensions, that extra measurement is the fourth satellite.

So four satellites give a 3D fix. Three for position, and one for the receiver clock. With only three in view, many receivers fall back to a 2D fix. They assume a height, and solve for latitude and longitude only. That is less reliable.

Now RAIM: a fifth satellite to check the others. Four satellites give a position, but no way of knowing whether one of them is lying. For example, a satellite with a clock fault could be broadcasting a wrong time. RAIM, receiver autonomous integrity monitoring, uses extra satellites to check.

With five in view, the receiver can work out several different four-satellite solutions, and compare them. If they disagree, one satellite is faulty, and the receiver warns you. That is fault detection. With six, it can work out which satellite is the culprit, and leave it out. Then it carries on with a good position. That is fault detection and exclusion, FDE.

When RAIM cannot do its job, because too few satellites are in view or their geometry is poor, the receiver gives a RAIM not available, or loss of integrity, message. The position may still be good. But nothing is checking it.

[[diagram:gnss-satellite-count]]
Picture a ladder, from three satellites at the bottom to six at the top. Three: a 2D fix only. The receiver assumes a height, and is less reliable. Four: a 3D fix, three for position and one for the clock. Five: RAIM fault detection. The receiver can tell that one satellite is faulty, and warns you. And six: fault detection and exclusion. It identifies the faulty satellite, leaves it out, and keeps navigating.

Question. Why does a GNSS receiver need four satellites for a 3D fix, when three spheres meet at a point?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because the receiver's clock is not accurate enough, so every range contains the same unknown clock error. That makes four unknowns: latitude, longitude, height and clock error. And solving for four unknowns needs four ranges.

Question. What is the difference between what five satellites and six satellites give a RAIM receiver?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: five let it detect that one satellite is faulty, because the solutions disagree. So it can warn you. Six let it identify the faulty satellite and exclude it, so it can keep navigating with a trustworthy position. That is fault detection and exclusion.

## Errors and limitations

GNSS is very accurate, but the accuracy is not automatic. Every error source enters the same way. It makes a measured range a little wrong, so the spheres no longer meet exactly where the receiver is. There are seven to know.

First, ionospheric and tropospheric delay. The signal slows as it passes through the atmosphere. This is the largest natural error. Second, satellite clock and ephemeris errors: small errors in the satellite's time, or in its reported position. Third, multipath: signals reflected off terrain, buildings or the airframe, which arrive late. Fourth, receiver noise, the electronic limits of the receiver. Fifth, dilution of precision, DOP. That is poor satellite geometry, with the satellites bunched together, and it magnifies all the other errors. Sixth, masking. Wings, terrain or the cockpit block satellites, especially in turns. And seventh, interference and jamming. GNSS signals are very weak, and jamming or interference can cause loss of navigation.

Take the atmospheric delay first. It is the biggest natural error, because the receiver assumes the signal travelled at the speed of light all the way. But it travels slightly slower in the charged upper layer of the atmosphere, the ionosphere, and in the moist lower layer, the troposphere. A slower signal takes longer. And longer looks like further away. Multipath works the same way. A reflected signal has taken a longer route.

Dilution of precision is the one that most needs a picture. Imagine fixing your position from two landmarks. If they are 90 degrees apart, a small error in either bearing moves your fix a little. If they are almost in line, the two position lines cross at a shallow angle. And the same small error slides the fix a long way along them.

Satellites behave the same way. Spread across the sky, their range spheres cross cleanly, and the errors stay small. Bunched together in one part of the sky, the spheres cross at shallow angles, and every range error is magnified. So a high DOP means poor geometry, and a less accurate fix, even when plenty of satellites are in view.

[[diagram:gnss-dop-geometry]]
Picture two panels, with the same range error on every satellite. On the left, the satellites are spread across the sky. Their range bands cross at nearly 90 degrees, and the area where the fix could be is small. That is a low DOP, and an accurate fix. On the right, the satellites are bunched in one part of the sky. The bands cross at a shallow angle, and the same error makes a long, thin area. That is a high DOP, and a less accurate fix.

Masking is GNSS's version of line of sight. The antenna on top of the cabin cannot see a satellite low on the side of the lowered wing in a steep turn. Nor can it see one behind a ridge, in a valley. The receiver may briefly drop below the number of satellites it needs. And RAIM may drop out first, because it needs more.

Interference and jamming follow from the signal's weakness. It has travelled about 20,200 km from a transmitter of modest power, and it arrives far below the background radio noise. A faulty electronic device in the cabin can drown it out. So can deliberate jamming, and NOTAMs warn of military GNSS testing. Either can stop navigation altogether.

Now augmentation. An SBAS, a satellite-based augmentation system, uses ground stations at known positions to measure the errors. Then it broadcasts corrections and integrity information from geostationary satellites. One example is the American WAAS. Australia and New Zealand are rolling out {SouthPAN|South Pan}. Check its current status, and whether its aviation service, the safety-of-life service, is approved for use, before relying on it. Without it, Australian aviation receivers rely on RAIM for integrity.

And one more limit. Height from GNSS is not altitude. The receiver solves for a height, but it is a geometric height above the ellipsoid, a mathematical model of the Earth's shape. It is not pressure altitude. Your altimeter, the airspace steps, and every other aeroplane's altitude are all pressure-based. So never use GNSS altitude in place of the altimeter, for vertical separation or for terrain clearance.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. It says that more satellites in view means a more accurate fix. That is only half true. Accuracy depends on geometry as well as number. Six satellites bunched in one quarter of the sky can give a worse fix, a high DOP, than five spread evenly across it.

Question. In a steep turn, the receiver briefly shows a RAIM warning. Then it clears when you roll level. What happened?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: masking. The wing and the cabin blocked one or more satellites on the low side. So for a moment, there were not enough in view, or the geometry was too poor, for RAIM to check the position. Rolling level restored the view.

## Using GNSS without losing the picture

The receiver will draw a magenta line, with a little aeroplane on it, with total confidence, whether the waypoints are right or wrong. The safeguards in this section all aim at one thing. You should always know where you are without the receiver, so that a wrong answer from it stands out at once. There are five habits.

The first habit: plan the flight on a chart first. Draw the tracks, measure the distances, choose the visual fixes, and work out the headings and times. GNSS confirms the plan. It does not replace it. A route you have planned yourself is one you can recognise when the receiver disagrees with it.

The second habit: check each waypoint against the plan. Check each waypoint's coordinates, and the track and distance between waypoints, against the flight plan. A typing error does not show on the screen as an error. It shows as a confident line to the wrong place.

Let's work one: what one wrong digit does. You intend to enter a waypoint at 31 degrees 40 minutes south. But you type 32 degrees 40 minutes south, with the longitude correct. One degree of latitude is 60 nm, and one minute is 1 nm. How far out is the waypoint? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 5]]
The latitude error: 32 degrees 40, minus 31 degrees 40, is 1 degree. And 1 degree times 60 is 60 nm, due south of where you meant.

So the leg on the screen now has the wrong track and the wrong distance. Compared with the 30 nm leg on your plan, it would stand out at once, if you checked. If you did not, the receiver will guide you accurately towards a point 60 nm from your destination. The answer once more: one wrong digit, 60 nm out.

Here is one for you to try. Your plan shows a leg of {245|two four five} degrees magnetic, and 34 nm. The receiver shows the same leg as {065|zero six five} degrees, and 34 nm. What is the most likely mistake?
[[pause 5]]
The two waypoints are in the reverse order. The distance is identical, and the track is the reciprocal: {245|two four five} minus 180 is {065|zero six five}. Re-order the flight plan, or check that you activated the right leg, before departure.

The third habit: cross-check against the ground and other aids. At every planned fix, compare what the receiver says with what you see. The town, the railway, the lake. A gross error almost always shows as a mismatch with the ground, long before it becomes dangerous. Where the aeroplane has them, the VOR and the NDB give an independent check.

The fourth habit: keep the chart open, and follow your progress on it. Mark the time at each fix. Then if the receiver fails or warns you, you are not starting from nothing. You know where you were ten minutes ago, and the heading you have flown since. That is all dead reckoning needs.

And the fifth habit: act on integrity messages. Monitor the RAIM and integrity messages. Act on any loss of integrity, or dead reckoning, alert by reverting to other navigation. A receiver in dead reckoning mode has lost its fix. It is extrapolating from the last known position, track and groundspeed. And its display may look almost normal.

Question. The receiver shows you on track, but the lake that should be 3 nm to your left is nowhere to be seen. What do you do?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: believe the ground and the chart until the conflict is resolved. Fix your position visually. Then check the active waypoint's coordinates, and the leg on the receiver. A confident display is not a correct one. A wrong waypoint, or a lost fix, gives an on-track picture of the wrong route.

## Typical navigation tasks on the receiver

Every receiver is different. The exam element says a specific type of aircraft equipment, because you are expected to learn the one you fly from its pilot guide, on the ground, before you fly with it. The functions, though, are common to almost all units. There are five to know.

First, Direct-To, the D-arrow key. Select a waypoint, and the receiver gives you a direct track to it from where you are now. That is useful for diversions. On many units, it replaces or suspends the active flight plan. So know what yours does.

Second, the flight plan. Enter the route waypoints in order. Check the track and distance of each leg against your plan. Then activate the leg you are flying.

Third, the OBS or hold mode. It suspends auto-sequencing. That is the receiver's habit of switching to the next leg when you reach a waypoint. With it suspended, you can fly a selected course to or from a waypoint, as you would with a VOR.

Fourth, Nearest. It lists the nearest aerodromes, with bearing and distance. It is the most valuable page in an emergency, or when the weather closes in.

And fifth, the data fields. Groundspeed, ETA, and cross-track error, marked XTK, which is the distance you are left or right of the desired track. Together they let you compare planned and actual progress.

The receiver measures where you have been, not where the nose is pointing. So the track and groundspeed it shows are over the ground. That is useful. The difference between your heading and the GNSS track is your drift. And the difference between your TAS and the GNSS groundspeed is your wind component. You read both directly.

[[diagram:heading-track-drift]]
Picture the aeroplane from above. The nose points along the heading. But a wind from the left side carries the aeroplane sideways, so it moves over the ground along the track. The angle between heading and track is the drift. With no wind, heading and track are the same. The receiver shows you the track.

## Knowing you have passed a waypoint

On a visual navigation leg, you know you have reached a fix because you see it beneath you. The receiver has its own set of signs, and you should recognise each one. There are four.

First, a waypoint alert message as you approach. Second, the TO and FROM indicator changing from TO to FROM. Third, the distance to the waypoint counting down to zero, and then starting to count up. And fourth, the receiver automatically sequencing to the next leg, so the desired track changes.

Some units use turn anticipation. They start the turn before the waypoint, so that you roll out on the next leg rather than overshooting it. So the sequencing happens slightly early. In OBS mode there is no automatic sequencing. So the TO and FROM flag and the distance are your indications. And the receiver will keep you on the old course until you change it.

[[diagram:gnss-waypoint-passage]]
Picture an aeroplane, seen from above, flying a leg to a waypoint. On approach, the receiver gives a waypoint alert. With turn anticipation, it starts the turn early, cutting the corner, and sequences to the next leg slightly early. In OBS mode, there is no sequencing. The receiver keeps you on the old course, past the waypoint. Underneath, the distance to the waypoint counts down to zero. In OBS mode, it then counts up, and the TO flag changes to FROM. With auto-sequencing, the display switches to the next leg, and counts down to the next waypoint.

## Checks before and during flight

Before flight, on the ground with the engine running and the avionics on, there are five checks. First, the self-test has passed. Second, the database is current. An expired database may contain wrong or missing waypoints and airspace. Third, the satellite status page shows a 3D fix. Fourth, the displayed position agrees with your known position on the apron. And fifth, the settings are correct: the units, the CDI scaling, and the map datum, {WGS-84|W G S eighty-four}.

The database check matters because airspace, frequencies and aerodromes change every few months. And the receiver will present an out-of-date boundary with the same confidence as a current one. The datum check matters because coordinates on a different datum describe a different point on the ground.

In flight, watch for integrity alerts, check the fix quality, and cross-check with visual fixes.

IFR operations require a RAIM prediction. That is a forecast of whether enough satellites, in good geometry, will be available along the route, and the AIP sets the requirement. VFR pilots should still check NOTAMs for GNSS outages, and for interference testing, before relying on the receiver.

## The human factors trap

GNSS is accurate and easy. And those are exactly the qualities that make it hazardous to people. Here are the traps, one at a time.

Automation complacency comes first. A display that is right a thousand times in a row trains you to stop checking it. You stop looking out for the fixes. You stop timing the legs. And you become a passenger, following a line.

Head-down time is the physical cost. Programming a flight plan, or a diversion, in flight takes your eyes inside for many seconds at a time. And it does so in the very airspace where a lookout matters. So programme on the ground. In flight, keep entries short, and break them up with a lookout scan.

Data entry errors are inevitable. One wrong letter in an identifier, or one wrong digit in a coordinate, can give a waypoint hundreds of miles away, as the worked example showed.

Mode confusion is not knowing what the receiver is doing. OBS mode left on, so it never sequences. Or a Direct-To that has dropped the flight plan you thought was active.

Loss of situational awareness follows when the unit fails, and you have no independent idea of where you are. And over time, skills fade if GNSS is always used. Map reading and dead reckoning are skills that need practice.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Its best practice answers are never trust the receiver, or programme it in flight to stay current. They are these. Plan on the chart. Programme and check on the ground. Cross-check visually at every fix. And be ready to continue without it.

## The rules for pilots and equipment

For most private VFR flying, GNSS is an aid to visual navigation, not a sole means. That includes a non-approved handheld. You must still be able to fix your position by visual reference to the ground or water. And you must be ready to continue if the receiver fails. When operating at or below 2,000 ft above ground or water, Australian VFR rules expect navigation by visual reference, rather than reliance on GNSS.

The exact conditions under which GNSS may be the primary means of navigation for VFR are in the navigation section of the VFR guide, the Part 91 Manual of Standards, and the AIP. They cover an approved receiver, pilot training, and position checks. Check the current text, rather than relying on memory. GNSS used as a primary means, and for IFR, requires an approved, TSO-certified, installation. And it requires pilot training and recency, as Part 61 requires. The AIP sets out the equipment standards.

The logic behind the rules is the logic of this whole lesson. A handheld unit has no certified integrity monitoring. Its antenna may be masked inside the cabin. And its battery and cable can fail. An approved installation has been tested to a standard, and monitors its own integrity. And a trained pilot knows its modes and warnings. So the more you rely on GNSS, the more of both the rules require.

## Bringing it together

You plan a VFR flight from Jandakot to Narrogin, with a tablet and a panel GNSS. At home, you draw the route on the VNC, pick the fixes, and work out the headings and times. You check NOTAMs, and find a GNSS interference test in the north of the state, nowhere near your route.

At the aeroplane, you confirm the database is current. The receiver has a 3D fix, and shows you on the right apron. You enter the flight plan, checking each leg's track and distance against your log.

In flight, at each fix, you compare the receiver with the ground and the clock. Over the hills east of Perth, a RAIM warning appears in a turn, and clears when you roll level. That is masking. Twenty minutes later, the panel unit shows loss of integrity, and this time it does not clear. You note the time. You confirm your position against a town and a railway on the chart. And you continue on heading and time, as planned, using the receiver only as a cross-check.

You arrive at Narrogin on time. Because the plan you made on the chart never depended on the receiver.

Question. A VFR pilot with a handheld receiver loses the signal at 1,500 ft above the ground. What should already be in place, and why?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: a position fixed on the chart, and the planned heading and time to the next fix. The handheld is only an aid, and the pilot must be able to navigate by visual reference, especially low down. With those in place, the loss is an inconvenience, rather than an emergency.

## Key points

Let's recap.

Position comes by trilateration from pseudoranges. Four satellites for a 3D fix: three for position, and one for the receiver clock. Five for RAIM fault detection. Six to exclude a faulty satellite. The errors are atmospheric delay, the largest natural one, clock and ephemeris errors, multipath, receiver noise, masking and interference. And poor geometry, a high DOP, magnifies them all. GNSS height is not pressure altitude.

The habits. Plan on the chart first. Check every waypoint's coordinates, and every leg, against the plan. Cross-check visually at each fix. Keep the chart open. And revert to other navigation on any integrity or dead reckoning alert.

Know your receiver. Direct-To, the flight plan, OBS or hold, Nearest, and the groundspeed, ETA and cross-track error fields.

Waypoint passage. The waypoint alert. TO flips to FROM. The distance counts down, then up. And the receiver sequences to the next leg: earlier with turn anticipation, and not at all in OBS mode.

The checks. Before flight: the self-test, a current database, a 3D fix, the correct position on the apron, and the correct settings, with the {WGS-84|W G S eighty-four} datum. In flight: integrity, fix quality, and visual cross-checks. And check NOTAMs for outages.

The human factors. Complacency, head-down time, data entry errors, mode confusion, loss of situational awareness, and fading skills.

And the rules. For private VFR flying, GNSS is an aid to visual navigation. Primary-means and IFR use need an approved installation, and pilot training and recency. Verify the current text.

A final exam tip. Four satellites for a 3D fix, five for RAIM fault detection, and six to exclude a faulty satellite. Poor geometry, a high DOP, increases the error even when plenty of satellites are in view. For the procedure and human factors questions, choose the answer that keeps you navigating visually, with GNSS as the cross-check. And remember that a current database, and a position check on the ground, come before every flight.
