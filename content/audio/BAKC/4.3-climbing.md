This is BAKC 4.3, Climbing. By the end you will be able to tell the rate of climb from the angle of climb, say what sets each one and which speed gives the best of each, Vy or Vx, turn feet per minute into feet per nautical mile, and say what a headwind or a tailwind does to each. There are worked examples and a few questions along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (BAKC 4.3)

The exam wants one thing from you, with four parts. Explain how the rate of climb differs from the angle of climb: what each one measures, what sets it, the speed that gives the best of each, and what wind does to each.

## Why this matters

How well does it climb? That question has two answers, and they are not the same.

Departing a farm strip near York, with a line of gums at the far fence, you care about how much height you gain before you reach the trees. That is angle. Climbing out of Jandakot to get on top of the haze before the hills, you care about how many feet you gain each minute. That is rate.

The aeroplane gives its best of each at a different speed, and the flight manual, the POH, lists both. Choosing the wrong one at the wrong moment has put aeroplanes into trees with the engine working perfectly. The exam checks that you can tell the two apart, pick Vx or Vy for a situation, convert between feet per minute and feet per nautical mile, and say what a headwind or tailwind does to each. This lesson builds both from the forces in a climb, then does the arithmetic.

## Two questions about one climb

Picture two aeroplanes leaving the same runway at the same moment. After one minute, the first is 700 ft up and 1.5 nautical miles down the track. The second is 650 ft up, but only 1 nautical mile down the track. Which climbs better?

The first has gained more height per minute. It has the better rate of climb, measured in feet per minute, and shown on the vertical speed indicator. The second has gained more height per mile. It has the better angle of climb, the steepness of its path. That is measured as a gradient: in feet per nautical mile, as a percentage, or in degrees. Now put a 600 ft hill 1.2 nautical miles from the runway. Only the second aeroplane is above it when it gets there, even though the first is climbing faster.

Hold onto that picture. Rate goes with time. Angle goes with distance. Everything else in this lesson follows from it.

A gradient can be given three ways, and the exam uses all of them. Feet per nautical mile is the most practical for pilots, because charts and obstacles are in nautical miles. A percentage is the height gained per 100 units of distance. A nautical mile is about 6,076 ft, so 1 percent is about 61 ft per nautical mile. And degrees is the angle of the path itself. For the shallow climbs of a light aeroplane, 1 degree is about 106 ft per nautical mile.

Picture a climb path rising 450 ft over 1 nautical mile, about 6,076 ft, drawn with the height stretched so you can see it. 450 ft per nautical mile is about 7.4 percent, and a little over 4 degrees. That is much flatter than it feels from the cockpit. And the conversions again: 1 percent is about 61 ft per nautical mile, and 1 degree is about 106.

Question. Which instrument shows the rate of climb, and in what units? Which shows the angle?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the vertical speed indicator shows the rate, in feet per minute. No instrument shows the angle over the ground directly. You work it out from the rate and the groundspeed, or judge it from the view of the obstacles ahead.

## What makes an aeroplane climb

In level flight, thrust balances drag, and lift balances weight, as the lesson on basic aerodynamics showed. Now tilt the flight path upwards by a climb angle, called gamma. Lift still acts at right angles to the flight path, and drag still acts back along it. But weight still points straight down, so it is no longer at right angles to the path.

So split the weight into two parts. One part acts across the flight path, and lift balances it. That part is the weight times the cosine of the climb angle. In a shallow climb the cosine is very nearly 1, so lift is only a little less than weight. The other part, the weight times the sine of the climb angle, acts backwards along the flight path, exactly like extra drag. To hold a steady climb, thrust must overcome both. Thrust equals drag, plus the weight times the sine of the climb angle.

[[diagram:forces-in-a-climb]]
Picture a climbing aeroplane, nose up along a sloping path. Thrust points forward along the path, and drag points back along it. Lift stands at right angles to the path. Weight points straight down. Now split the weight arrow into two. One part lies across the path, and lift balances it. The other part points back down the path, beside the drag. So thrust has to match drag plus that backward part of the weight.

Rearrange that, and you have the angle of climb. The sine of the climb angle equals thrust minus drag, divided by weight. So the steepness of the climb depends on the excess thrust, thrust minus drag, as a fraction of the weight. More thrust, from full power and dense air, steepens the climb. So does less drag, from a clean configuration and the right speed. More weight flattens it. This is the key result. The angle of climb is set by excess thrust.

The rate of climb is how fast the aeroplane gains height. The aeroplane moves along the path at its speed, V, and the vertical part of that is V times the sine of the climb angle. So the rate of climb equals V, times thrust minus drag, divided by weight.

Thrust times speed is power, the work done each second. So V times the excess thrust is the power available, minus the power needed to fly level. That is the excess power. The rate of climb is set by excess power. It is the same idea in different clothes. Excess thrust tells you how steep. Excess thrust times speed tells you how fast upwards.

Question. The angle of climb depends on excess thrust, and the rate depends on excess power. What is the difference between the two?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: excess power is excess thrust multiplied by the speed. The same surplus thrust lifts the aeroplane up the slope faster if it is moving along the slope faster. So a slightly faster climb can give a better rate, even though its angle is a little flatter.

## Vx and Vy: two best speeds

Both excess thrust and excess power change with airspeed, and they peak at different speeds.

For a propeller aeroplane, thrust is greatest at low speed, and falls as speed rises. Total drag is the U-shaped curve from the lesson on lift and drag. The gap between them, the excess thrust, is largest at a fairly low speed, a little above the stall. That speed gives the steepest climb, the most height per unit of distance. It is Vx, the best angle of climb speed.

Fly a few knots faster than Vx, and the excess thrust shrinks a little, so the path flattens. But the aeroplane is now covering more ground each minute. And the product of the two, the excess power, keeps rising for a while. It peaks at a higher speed. That is Vy, the best rate of climb speed, the most height per minute.

[[diagram:climb-rate-vs-airspeed]]
You can find both on one graph. Picture the rate of climb plotted up the side, against airspeed along the bottom. The curve rises to a hump and falls again. Vy is simply the top of the hump, about 75 knots for this example trainer. Now draw a straight line from the bottom-left corner, the origin, so that it just touches the curve. The slope of that line is rate divided by speed, which is height per distance. And the touching point has the steepest possible slope. That point is Vx, about 60 knots. It is always to the left of the top. So Vx is always lower than Vy, for a propeller aeroplane.

Typical figures for a two-seat trainer are a Vx of about 60 kt, and a Vy of about 75 kt. But those are only an example. Use the figures in your own flight manual. The flight manual also tells you which flap setting it means, because flap usually costs climb performance. And it tells you how the speeds change with weight.

[[diagram:vx-vs-vy]]
Picture two climb paths leaving the same point. The Vx path is steeper. It gains the most height per distance, so it clears an obstacle close ahead. The Vy path is flatter, but the aeroplane is going faster along it. Mark where each aeroplane is after one minute, and after two. At each mark, the Vy aeroplane is higher. It gains the most height per minute.

So the choice is about what you are trying to beat.

Use Vx when the problem is distance. An obstacle close to the end of the runway, a short strip with trees, or rising ground ahead. You accept a lower rate to get the steepest path. But Vx is close to the stall, and the engine is working hard at a low cooling airspeed. So you use it only until the obstacle is cleared, then lower the nose to Vy.

Use Vy when the problem is time. Getting to cruise height, getting above the weather or terrain in the least time, or a normal climb-out. Many pilots climb en route a little faster than Vy, which is called a cruise climb, for engine cooling and a better view over the nose.

So, side by side. The rate of climb is height per unit of time. It is in feet per minute, on the vertical speed indicator. It is set by excess power, it is best at Vy, the higher speed, and you use it to reach height in the least time. The wind does not change it, because rate is relative to the air. The angle of climb is height per unit of horizontal distance. It is in feet per nautical mile, percent or degrees. It is set by excess thrust, it is best at Vx, the lower speed, and you use it to clear obstacles close to the runway. And the wind does change it over the ground: a headwind steepens it, and a tailwind flattens it.

Everything that cuts thrust or power, or adds weight or drag, worsens both. A hot day at Northam, a heavy load, or a dirty wing all reduce excess thrust and excess power. So both angle and rate fall. The lesson on take-off and landing performance covers density height.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. It offers Vy for clearing an obstacle, because it is the best climb speed. But Vy gives the best rate, which is height per minute, not per mile. For obstacles, the question is how high you are when you reach them. And that is the best angle: Vx.

Question. Why is Vx always slower than Vy in a propeller aeroplane?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: Vx is where the excess thrust is greatest, and for a propeller aeroplane that happens at low speed. Going faster loses a little excess thrust, but covers ground faster. So the excess power, thrust times speed, keeps rising until Vy. The top of the rate curve is therefore always at a higher speed than the steepest-angle point.

## Converting rate into angle

The rate of climb comes from the vertical speed indicator, or from the flight manual. The angle you need comes from the chart: how high is the obstacle, and how far away? To compare them, convert the rate into a gradient over the ground, using the groundspeed.

At a given groundspeed in knots, you cover the groundspeed divided by 60 nautical miles each minute. In that minute, you gain the rate of climb in feet. So here is the formula. The gradient, in feet per nautical mile, equals the rate of climb in feet per minute, times 60, divided by the groundspeed in knots.

Let's work one in still air. The rate of climb is 600 feet per minute, and the groundspeed is 80 kt, with no wind. What is the gradient? Pause if you'd like to try it first.
[[pause 5]]
The distance each minute is 80 over 60, which is 1.33 nautical miles. The gradient is 600 times 60, divided by 80, which is 450 ft per nautical mile. So every nautical mile you fly, you gain 450 ft. A 300 ft obstacle, 1 nautical mile from the start of the climb, is cleared by about 150 ft.

Now the same steps with wind. One climb, three winds, one obstacle. The aeroplane climbs at Vy, at 700 feet per minute and 90 kt true airspeed. The obstacle is 400 ft high, 1.5 nautical miles from where the climb starts. Work out the clearance in still air, with a 15 kt headwind, and with a 15 kt tailwind. This one takes a moment.
[[pause 8]]
First, the gradient you need: 400 divided by 1.5 is 267 ft per nautical mile, plus a margin.

In still air, the groundspeed is 90 kt. 700 times 60, divided by 90, is 467 ft per nautical mile. At 1.5 nautical miles, that is 700 ft, clearing the obstacle by 300 ft.

With a 15 kt headwind, the groundspeed is 75 kt. 700 times 60, divided by 75, is 560 ft per nautical mile. At 1.5 nautical miles, you are at 840 ft, clearing it by 440 ft.

With a 15 kt tailwind, the groundspeed is 105 kt. 700 times 60, divided by 105, is 400 ft per nautical mile. At 1.5 nautical miles, you are at 600 ft, clearing it by only 200 ft.

So all three clear the obstacle: by 300 ft in still air, 440 with the headwind, and 200 with the tailwind. And the vertical speed indicator read 700 feet per minute every time. The wind changed only how much ground you covered while gaining that height.

Sometimes the unknown is the rate you need. Try this one. A departure from a short strip needs at least 300 ft per nautical mile to clear the ridge. Your groundspeed in the climb will be 70 kt. What rate of climb must the aeroplane achieve?
[[pause 6]]
Rearrange the formula. The rate equals the gradient, times the groundspeed, divided by 60. 300 times 70, divided by 60, is 350 feet per minute. Check it another way. In one minute at 70 kt you fly 1.17 nautical miles. And 1.17 nautical miles at 300 ft per nautical mile is 350 ft. Now check the flight manual climb chart for the day's density height. If the aeroplane cannot make 350 feet per minute with a margin, you do not go.

## What wind does, and what it does not

The rate of climb is measured relative to the air. The aeroplane climbs through the air mass at the same feet per minute, whether that air mass is still, or moving across the ground at 20 kt. So wind does not change the rate of climb. And the flight manual's Vx and Vy, which are airspeeds, remain the speeds to fly.

What wind changes is the path over the ground. A headwind cuts the groundspeed. So in each minute of climbing, you cover less ground, and the path over the ground is steeper. A tailwind raises the groundspeed, and flattens the path. Obstacles are fixed to the ground. So the angle that matters for clearing them is the angle over the ground. And that is why you take off into wind.

[[diagram:climb-gradient-wind]]
Picture the same aeroplane climbing three times, at 600 feet per minute and 80 kt true airspeed, towards a 300 ft obstacle 1 nautical mile away. In still air, at 80 knots over the ground, the path is 450 ft per nautical mile, and it clears the obstacle by about 150 ft. With a 20 knot headwind, the groundspeed is 60 kt, and the path steepens to 600 ft per nautical mile, clearing it by 300 ft. With a 20 knot tailwind, the groundspeed is 100 kt, and the path flattens to 360 ft per nautical mile. It clears the obstacle by only about 60 ft. The still-air margin of 150 ft has doubled with the headwind, and shrunk to less than half with the tailwind. Same aeroplane, same power, same reading on the vertical speed indicator.

Near the ground, the wind usually strengthens with height, because friction slows it at the surface. Climbing into a headwind that grows as you climb helps a little. The airspeed tends to rise, and the aeroplane can be held in a slightly steeper attitude. Climbing out with a tailwind that grows does the opposite. And a sudden loss of headwind on climb-out is wind shear, which drops the airspeed, as the lesson on basic aerodynamics explained.

Here's another trap the exam likes to set. A headwind improves the climb, it says. That is half right. A headwind improves the angle over the ground, not the rate. If an option says a headwind increases the rate of climb, it is wrong. If it says the rate is unchanged and the angle over the ground increases, it is right.

Question. Climbing at a steady 500 feet per minute, you turn from into wind to downwind. What happens to the reading on the vertical speed indicator, and to your clearance over the hills ahead?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the vertical speed indicator still reads about 500 feet per minute, because the rate is measured relative to the air, and the wind carries the whole air mass. But the clearance shrinks. The groundspeed is now higher, so you cover more ground for each foot gained, and the path over the ground is flatter.

## Bringing it together

You are leaving a 900 m strip near Toodyay on a warm morning, with two on board. The trees at the far fence are about 60 ft tall, and the land rises beyond them towards a ridge.

The windsock shows a light breeze straight down the strip, so you take off into it. The lower groundspeed shortens the run, and steepens your path over the ground.

After lift-off you hold Vx, and accept a modest rate on the vertical speed indicator, because the trees are a distance problem. Once they are well below, you lower the nose to Vy. Now the problem is time: getting high enough above the ridge, and the turbulence over it, as quickly as possible.

Had the wind been a tailwind, the vertical speed indicator would have shown the same rate at each speed. But the path over the trees would have been flatter. And the right decision would have been to use the other end of the strip, or to wait.

## Key points

Let's recap.

The rate of climb is height per unit of time. It is in feet per minute, on the vertical speed indicator. It is set by excess power, and it is best at Vy.

The angle of climb is height per unit of distance. It is in feet per nautical mile, percent or degrees. It is set by excess thrust, and it is best at Vx, which is always the lower speed.

Use Vx to clear obstacles close ahead, and Vy to gain height in the least time.

The gradient in feet per nautical mile equals the rate in feet per minute, times 60, divided by the groundspeed in knots.

And wind changes the angle over the ground, not the rate. A headwind steepens the path, and a tailwind flattens it.

A final exam tip. Remember: rate goes with time, and angle goes with distance. Vy and feet per minute are for time. Vx and feet per nautical mile are for obstacles. Expect one conversion from feet per minute and groundspeed to feet per nautical mile: multiply by 60, and divide by the groundspeed. Expect one choice between Vx and Vy. And expect one wind question, where the right answer is: rate unchanged, angle over the ground changed.
