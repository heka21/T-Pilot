This is BAKC 6.2, Take-off and landing performance. By the end you will be able to tell pressure height from density height, get both from the altimeter and from a little arithmetic, and say what wind, heat, pressure, elevation, ground effect, wind shear and frost do to your take-off, your climb and your landing. There are worked examples you can try in your head, so pause whenever you want a moment to think.

## What the exam expects (BAKC 6.2)

The exam wants five things from you. First, the difference between pressure height and density height. Second, how to use the altimeter on the ground to find the local QNH, the pressure height of the aerodrome, and its elevation. Third, the calculations: pressure height from elevation and QNH, and density height from pressure height and temperature. Fourth, what headwind and tailwind, temperature, QNH, elevation, ground effect, wind shear and frost do to the take-off, the landing and the climb. And fifth, the maximum structural take-off and landing weights, and the climb weight limit.

## Why this matters

A training aeroplane that lifts off comfortably at Jandakot on a cool June morning can need half as much runway again at Northam on a 40 degree February afternoon. And once it is airborne, it can climb at a fraction of the rate. Nothing about the aeroplane has changed. The air has.

Pilots who don't understand that run off the end of strips every summer, or clear the fence and then can't out-climb the rising ground. The exam cares a great deal. CASA lists working out density height and pressure height among the most common errors in the RPLA exam, and every performance chart in the workbook starts with those two numbers. So this lesson gives you the physical picture first, then the arithmetic, then what each factor does to the distances.

## Three heights for one aerodrome

Imagine standing on the apron at an inland aerodrome. There are three different heights you could attach to the spot you are standing on. And performance depends on the third one.

The first is elevation. That is the surveyed height of the aerodrome above mean sea level. It is printed in ERSA, and it never changes.

[[diagram:height-altitude-elevation]]
Picture a side view, with the sea on the left and the aerodrome sitting 300 ft above it. That 300 ft is the elevation, the ground's height above mean sea level. An aeroplane in the circuit, with the QNH set, reads 1,300 ft on its altimeter, so it is 1,000 ft above the aerodrome. Fly the same altitude over a 900 ft ridge, and it is only 400 ft above the ground. The elevation never changes. What the altimeter reads depends on what you set on its subscale.

The second height is pressure height, also called pressure altitude. It answers a different question. In the International Standard Atmosphere, the model atmosphere that performance charts are drawn for, at what height would the pressure be the same as the pressure here today?

If today's sea-level pressure is standard, {1013.25 hPa|ten thirteen point two five hectopascals}, the pressure height equals the elevation. But if a trough has pulled the QNH down to {1000 hPa|one thousand hectopascals}, the air here is thinner than standard, and the aerodrome is behaving as if it were higher than its elevation. In the lower atmosphere, pressure falls about 1 hectopascal for every 30 ft of height. So each hectopascal below {1013|ten thirteen} adds about 30 ft to the pressure height.

The third height is density height, also called density altitude. It asks the question the wing, the propeller and the engine actually care about. At what height in the standard atmosphere would the air be as thin as it is here today?

Density depends on pressure, and on temperature. The standard atmosphere has a temperature of 15 degrees at sea level, falling 2 degrees for every 1,000 ft. Warm air is less dense than standard air at the same pressure. So a hot day pushes the density height above the pressure height, by about 120 ft for every degree above standard.

Put it together. A 1,300 ft aerodrome on a 35 degree day with a low QNH might have a density height of 4,000 ft. The aeroplane has no idea what the survey says. It performs exactly as it would at 4,000 ft on a standard day. That single idea is the whole of this subtopic. Pressure height is the stepping stone. Density height is the answer.

Question. An aerodrome's elevation is 1,000 ft, and today its pressure height is also 1,000 ft. What does that tell you about the QNH?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the QNH is standard, {1013|ten thirteen} hectopascals. Pressure height equals elevation only when the sea-level pressure is standard. A lower QNH would put the pressure height above 1,000 ft, and a higher one below.

## Reading the heights off the altimeter

The altimeter is a barometer with a scale in feet, and an adjustable datum called the subscale. Whatever pressure you set on the subscale, the altimeter shows your height above the level where that pressure exists. So on the ground, one instrument gives you three readings.

First, the local QNH. Turn the subscale until the altimeter reads the known aerodrome elevation. The number now in the window is the local QNH: the sea-level pressure that makes the altimeter read true height above mean sea level. That is how you get a QNH at a strip with no ATIS or AWIS.

Second, the pressure height. Set {1013|ten thirteen} on the subscale. The altimeter now reads the aerodrome's pressure height directly, because that is exactly what pressure height means.

Third, the elevation. Set the aerodrome QNH from the tower, the ATIS or the AWIS. The altimeter should now read the aerodrome elevation. That doubles as the pre-flight altimeter check. With the correct QNH set, a reading within 100 ft of the elevation is acceptable for VFR. A bigger error makes the altimeter unserviceable. Check that figure against the current AIP.

[[widget:altimeter-subscale]]
Picture turning the subscale knob and watching the needle. Each hectopascal you wind on moves the reading about 30 ft. That is the same 30 ft per hectopascal as in the pressure height rule. And it is why a 10 hectopascal error on the subscale puts you 300 ft from where you think you are.

Question. You are parked at a strip with no weather service, and you want the local QNH. What do you do with the altimeter?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: wind the subscale until the altimeter reads the strip's elevation from ERSA. The subscale then shows the local QNH. Set {1013|ten thirteen} instead, and the altimeter reads the pressure height.

## The calculations

There are two formulas, used one after the other. First get the pressure height. Then correct it for temperature to get the density height.

Pressure height comes from the elevation and the QNH. Pressure height equals the elevation, plus {1013|ten thirteen} minus the QNH, times 30. If the QNH is below {1013|ten thirteen}, the bracket is positive, and the pressure height is above the elevation. Thin air, worse performance. If the QNH is above {1013|ten thirteen}, the bracket is negative, and the pressure height is below the elevation. Keep the sign.

Density height comes from the pressure height and the temperature, in two steps. First, find what the temperature should be at the pressure height on a standard day. That is 15, minus 2 degrees for every 1,000 ft of pressure height. Then add 120 ft for every degree the actual temperature is above that, or subtract if it is below.

Let's work one, a hot afternoon inland. The elevation is 1,700 ft, the QNH is {1009|ten oh nine}, and the temperature is 38 degrees. Pause if you'd like to try it first.
[[pause 8]]
Step one, the pressure height. {1013|ten thirteen} minus {1009|ten oh nine} is 4 hectopascals. 4 times 30 is 120 ft. So the pressure height is 1,700 plus 120, which is 1,820 ft.

Step two, the standard temperature at 1,820 ft. Two degrees per thousand feet, times 1.82, is 3.6 degrees. 15 minus 3.6 is 11.4 degrees.

Step three, how far above standard it is today. 38 minus 11.4 is 26.6 degrees.

Step four, the density height. 26.6 times 120 is 3,192 ft. Add that to the 1,820 ft pressure height, and the density height is about 5,010 ft.

So the strip is at 1,700 ft, but the aeroplane will take off and climb as though it were at 5,000 ft on a standard day. That is roughly a 15 percent loss of engine power, and a much longer run.

Now a mild morning at Jandakot. The elevation is 99 ft, the QNH is {1018|ten eighteen}, and the temperature is 24 degrees.
[[pause 8]]
The pressure height first. {1013|ten thirteen} minus {1018|ten eighteen} is minus 5. Minus 5 times 30 is minus 150 ft. 99 minus 150 is minus 51 ft. Call it minus 50 ft. The high pressure has put the aerodrome below sea level, in standard terms.

The standard temperature at minus 50 ft is a fraction warmer than at sea level: 15.1 degrees. Today's 24 degrees is 8.9 degrees above that. 8.9 times 120 is 1,068 ft. Add the minus 50, and the density height is about 1,020 ft.

So a sea-level aerodrome on a 24 degree day is already performing like a 1,000 ft one. Warmth alone does that.

Here is one for you to try. The elevation is 1,300 ft, the QNH is {1004|ten oh four}, and the temperature is 33 degrees. Find the density height.
[[pause 10]]
The pressure height: {1013|ten thirteen} minus {1004|ten oh four} is 9 hectopascals, times 30 is 270 ft. 1,300 plus 270 is 1,570 ft. The standard temperature there is 15 minus 3.1, which is 11.9 degrees. Today is 33, so that is 21.1 degrees above standard. 21.1 times 120 is 2,532 ft. Add that to 1,570, and the density height is about 4,100 ft. If you rounded the standard temperature to 12 degrees, you got 4,090, which is also fine.

[[widget:density-height]]
Notice how the two corrections compare. Low QNH and heat both push the density height up. But heat is the big one. Every degree above standard adds 120 ft, while every hectopascal of low pressure adds only 30.

Two practical notes. The CASA take-off and landing charts in the workbook are entered with pressure height and temperature, and the chart does the density correction for you. So for a chart question, you stop after the pressure height, and read the temperature on the chart's own scale. And when a question gives you the pressure height directly, skip the first step. The exam often does that.

[[workbook:fig3]]
Picture the take-off weight chart from the workbook, figure 3. The dashed example line starts on the left, at the pressure height, where the chart says start here. Then it crosses to the temperature lines. There is no density height scale anywhere on the chart.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set, and it comes in three parts. One: low QNH raises the pressure height, so subtract the QNH from {1013|ten thirteen}, not the other way round. Two: the standard temperature is worked out at the pressure height, not at the elevation. And three: a temperature below standard gives a density height below the pressure height, so the correction can be negative. Keep the signs, work to the nearest 10 ft, and round only at the end.

Question. The QNH is {1025|ten twenty-five}. Is the pressure height above or below the elevation, and by how much?
[[pause 5]]
The answer: below, by 360 ft. {1013|ten thirteen} minus {1025|ten twenty-five} is minus 12, and minus 12 times 30 is minus 360. High pressure means denser air than standard, so the aerodrome behaves as if it were 360 ft lower than it is.

## What the day does to the aeroplane

Now the second half of the subtopic. For each factor the syllabus names: does it make the take-off and landing distances longer or shorter, does it make the climb better or worse, and why? Almost everything comes back to one of two things. Air density, or groundspeed.

Start with density, which covers temperature, QNH and elevation. Thin air hurts three times over. The engine breathes less oxygen per stroke, so a normally aspirated engine loses roughly 3 percent of its power for every 1,000 ft of density height. The propeller bites less air, so thrust falls further. And the wing needs a higher true airspeed to make the same lift at the same indicated airspeed. So the aeroplane must be accelerated to a higher groundspeed before it can lift off, with less thrust to do it.

[[diagram:power-loss-with-altitude]]
Picture a line sloping down as height increases: full-throttle power, as a percentage of sea-level power. It loses roughly 3 percent for every 1,000 ft. At 5,000 ft the engine has about 85 percent of its sea-level power. By about 7,000 to 8,000 ft, a typical trainer can only make about 75 percent.

So in thin air, the take-off distance grows. The landing distance grows too, because the touchdown true airspeed is higher, so there is more energy to stop. And the climb, which depends on the surplus of power over what is needed to fly level, shrinks fastest of all. Every factor that raises the density height lengthens both distances and flattens the climb. Higher temperature. Lower QNH. Higher elevation. They are really one factor, wearing three hats.

Now wind. Wind does not change the air the wing flies through. It changes how fast the aeroplane moves over the ground while it reaches flying speed. The aeroplane lifts off at a fixed airspeed. Into a headwind, part of that airspeed is supplied by the wind before the aeroplane has even moved. So lift-off happens at a lower groundspeed. And the energy that has to be built up, or on landing, taken away by the brakes, goes with the square of the groundspeed. So the saving is bigger than the wind looks.

Let's work it. The lift-off airspeed is 60 kt. Compare a 10 kt headwind with a 10 kt tailwind, looking at the ground roll only.
[[pause 5]]
With the headwind, the groundspeed at lift-off is 60 minus 10, which is 50 kt. 50 over 60, squared, is 0.69. So the roll is about 30 percent shorter. With the tailwind, the groundspeed at lift-off is 60 plus 10, which is 70 kt. 70 over 60, squared, is 1.36. So the roll is about 36 percent longer.

The tailwind costs more than the headwind saves. That is why the charts punish tailwinds steeply. And it is why the usual rule of thumb says a tailwind of 10 percent of the lift-off speed adds roughly 20 percent to the whole take-off distance. The same arithmetic applies to the landing roll.

For the climb, wind changes the angle over the ground, but not the rate. The aeroplane climbs at the same feet per minute whatever the wind. Into a headwind, it covers less ground each minute, so it is higher when it reaches the fence or the trees.

[[diagram:climb-gradient-wind]]
Picture the same aeroplane climbing at 600 ft per minute, three times over. In still air, at 80 kt over the ground, it gains 450 ft for every nautical mile. With a 20 kt headwind, the path is steeper: 600 ft per nautical mile. With a 20 kt tailwind, it is flatter: only 360. Put a 300 ft obstacle 1 nautical mile ahead. In still air you clear it by about 150 ft. With the tailwind, by only about 60 ft.

A crosswind contributes only its headwind component to performance. Its crosswind component is a control problem, not a performance one.

[[widget:crosswind-component]]
To split a wind into its two parts, picture the angle between the wind and the runway. The crosswind part grows fast at first. Just 30 degrees off the runway already puts half the wind across it. At 60 degrees, nine-tenths of it is across, while the headwind part is down to half. Compare the crosswind with the demonstrated figure in the flight manual.

Next, ground effect. Within about a wingspan of the ground, the wingtip vortices are squeezed, and induced drag falls. So the aeroplane will fly in ground effect at a speed and angle of attack at which it could not fly clear of it. On take-off, that means an aeroplane dragged off early will lift off, climb to 10 or 15 ft, run out of ground effect, and then either settle back or hang there unable to climb while the trees get closer. On landing, it means an aeroplane that is a little fast will float, using up runway. Ground effect shortens the ground run, but it can wreck the climb-out. That is why you accelerate to the flight manual speed before you rotate.

Then wind shear, a change in wind speed or direction over a short distance. On climb-out, a sudden loss of headwind drops the indicated airspeed and the lift at once. That can happen climbing through a wind gradient, or into a sea breeze front at Jandakot. The aeroplane sinks, or stalls, with the attitude unchanged. CASA names wind shear as a weak area in the RPLA exam. Your protection is speed in hand, and an attitude you can lower.

And frost. Even a thin, barely visible frost on the wing, on a winter morning at Northam or Narrogin, roughens the surface and distorts the leading edge. The airflow separates earlier, the maximum lift coefficient falls, and the stall speed rises. So the aeroplane needs a longer run to reach a speed that will lift it, and it may not fly at all at the normal rotation speed. The climb suffers too, because the wing is making less lift for more drag. All frost comes off before flight. There are no exceptions for a thin layer.

[[diagram:takeoff-landing-factors]]
So let's sort the factors. Picture a table of arrows. Only one factor shortens both distances: a headwind. A tailwind lengthens both, markedly. Higher temperature, lower QNH and higher elevation lengthen both, through lower density, and they worsen the climb. Ground effect can shorten the ground run, but the aeroplane may not climb out, and on landing it floats. Wind shear is variable, and dangerous on the climb. Frost lengthens the take-off, and may prevent it altogether.

The charts also handle three factors the syllabus does not list here. Weight: heavier lengthens both distances, and reduces the climb. Surface: grass, gravel and wet surfaces lengthen both. And slope.

[[diagram:runway-slope-take-off-and-landing]]
Picture two side views of a sloping runway. Taking off uphill, part of the thrust is spent climbing the slope, so the run is longer. A 2 percent upslope can add around 10 percent to the take-off distance. Landing uphill, the slope helps you slow down, so the roll is shorter. And landing downhill, gravity fights the brakes. So with a light wind, land uphill and take off downhill.

Question. A 10 kt tailwind and a 10 kt headwind are not equal and opposite in their effect on the take-off roll. Why not?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because the energy the aeroplane must build up goes with the square of its groundspeed at lift-off. The tailwind raises that groundspeed, and the square makes the increase bigger than the decrease a headwind gives. Tailwinds cost more than headwinds save.

Question. Why is ground effect a hazard on take-off, if it shortens the ground run?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because the aeroplane can fly in ground effect at a speed at which it cannot climb out of it. Dragged off early, it lifts to a wingspan or so, loses the reduced induced drag, and either settles back, or hangs there unable to climb while the obstacles get closer.

## Weight limits

The syllabus ends with two kinds of weight limit. The difference matters, because on a hot day the lower one wins.

The maximum structural take-off weight, MTOW, is the heaviest weight at which the airframe is certified to begin a take-off. The maximum structural landing weight, MLW, is the heaviest at which it is certified to land, when the undercarriage and its attachments take the loads. Both are set by the strength of the structure. They are printed in the limitations section of the flight manual, and they do not change with the weather.

The climb weight limit is different. It is the heaviest weight at which the aeroplane can still achieve the required climb gradient after take-off, at the pressure height and temperature of the day. It is sometimes called the performance-limited weight, or the WAT limit, for weight, altitude and temperature. On a cool day at sea level, it is usually above the structural MTOW, and it never bites. At Northam at 38 degrees, it can be well below it. And then the climb weight limit is the limit.

[[workbook:fig6]]
Picture the landing chart from the workbook, figure 6. The bottom right panel gives the climb weight limit from the pressure height alone. And a note on the chart says the landing weight must not exceed it, however long the runway.

So the take-off weight you may actually use is the lowest of four numbers. The structural MTOW. The climb weight limit for the day. The weight the runway length allows, from the take-off chart. And the weight that will have burned down to the MLW by the time you land.

Question. What is the difference between the maximum structural take-off weight and the climb weight limit?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the structural limit is fixed by the airframe's strength, and never changes. The climb weight limit is the heaviest weight at which the aeroplane can still make the required climb gradient at today's density height. So it falls as the day gets hotter or the aerodrome higher. And when it is lower than the structural limit, it is the one that applies.

## Bringing it together

You plan to leave Northam at two in the afternoon in late January, with three friends and full fuel. ERSA gives the elevation. The AWIS gives a QNH of {1006|ten oh six} and a temperature of 39 degrees.

Working it through, the pressure height is a couple of hundred feet above the elevation. And the density height comes out more than 3,000 ft above the strip. The engine is down to roughly 87 percent power, the propeller is pulling thin air, and the wing needs a higher true airspeed to lift off. The sea breeze has not arrived, so the wind is a light northerly, a slight tailwind for the runway you would otherwise choose.

The take-off chart says the runway is long enough, just. But the take-off weight chart says the climb weight limit is 60 kg below your planned weight. Nothing about the structure stops you. The climb gradient over the rising ground does.

The answers are the ones this lesson leads to. Leave fuel behind. Leave a passenger behind. Wait for the sea breeze and the cooler air. Or depart early tomorrow morning, when at 20 degrees the density height will be more than 2,000 ft lower.

## Key points

Let's recap.

Elevation is surveyed and fixed. Pressure height is the elevation corrected for QNH, at 30 ft per hectopascal. Density height is the pressure height corrected for temperature, at 120 ft per degree from standard. And density height is what the aeroplane feels.

On the ground, wind the subscale until the altimeter reads the elevation, and you have the local QNH. Set {1013|ten thirteen}, and it reads the pressure height. Set the aerodrome QNH, and it should read the elevation, within 100 ft for VFR.

The formulas. Pressure height equals elevation, plus {1013|ten thirteen} minus the QNH, times 30. The standard temperature is 15, minus 2 for every 1,000 ft of pressure height. Density height equals pressure height, plus 120 times the difference between the actual and the standard temperature. Keep the signs.

The factors. A headwind shortens both distances and steepens the climb. A tailwind lengthens both, markedly. Higher temperature, lower QNH and higher elevation all mean lower density: longer distances, and a poorer climb. Ground effect lets you lift off before you can climb. Wind shear drops the indicated airspeed. Frost raises the stall speed and lengthens the run.

And the weights. Structural MTOW and MLW are fixed by the airframe. The climb weight limit is the heaviest weight that still gives the required climb gradient today. The lowest limit that applies is the one that governs.

A final exam tip. There is always a pressure height or density height calculation. Often there is a trap in the sign of the QNH correction, or the standard temperature is taken at the elevation instead of the pressure height. Work to the nearest 10 ft, and round at the end. Answers of 4,090 and 4,100 ft for the same problem are both within the tolerance of a numeric question. And for the factors, remember that only a headwind shortens both distances. Everything else the syllabus lists lengthens them, or leaves the aeroplane unable to climb.

That's the end of BAKC 6.2.
