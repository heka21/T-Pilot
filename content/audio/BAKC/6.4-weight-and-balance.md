This is BAKC 6.4, Weight and balance. By the end you will know the language of weight and balance, where each loading figure lives, how to convert fuel between litres and kilograms, how to work out whether an aeroplane is within its weight and centre of gravity limits, and what happens when it is not. There are several worked examples you can try in your head, so pause whenever you want a moment to think.

## What the exam expects (BAKC 6.4)

The exam wants four things from you. First, the terms: datum, arm, moment, station, centre of gravity and its limits, empty weight, operating weight, maximum take-off weight, zero fuel weight and maximum zero fuel weight, and maximum landing weight. Second, how to find and use the loading information for an aircraft: its maximum take-off weight, the capacity and arm of each baggage locker, the fuel's capacity, arm, grade and specific gravity, and the position and arm of each seat. Third, how to work out whether the aircraft is loaded within its centre of gravity limits. And fourth, what is likely to happen if the weight limits are exceeded.

## Why this matters

Three friends, their overnight bags, and full tanks, for a weekend at Rottnest or Busselton. It is the flight every new pilot wants to make. And it is the one most likely to put a four-seat trainer over its maximum weight, or behind its aft CG limit. CG is the centre of gravity.

Nothing about the aeroplane looks different when it is overloaded, or tail-heavy, on the apron. The difference shows up on the take-off roll, in the climb over the trees, or in a stall that will not recover. A weight and balance calculation is the only way to know before you start.

The exam always includes one. And CASA lists the loading systems among the areas candidates get wrong most often. Usually it is from reading the wrong line on a graph, or converting fuel the wrong way. This lesson builds the method from the physics of a lever, so that every step makes sense, and every check has a reason.

## The lever: why position matters as much as weight

You already know the physics, from a playground seesaw. A light child far from the pivot can balance a heavy adult close to it. What matters is not the weight alone, but the weight multiplied by its distance from the pivot. That is the turning effect.

A 20 kg child 2 m out balances a 40 kg adult 1 m in. That is because 20 times 2 is 40, and 40 times 1 is also 40.

An aeroplane is a seesaw lying along its length. Every item in it pushes down at its own position. The engine, the pilot, the passengers, the fuel, the bags in the back. And the aeroplane balances at one point, the centre of gravity. That is the point at which its whole weight can be thought of as acting.

The wing's lift has to be balanced around that point by the tailplane. And the aeroplane is only controllable and stable when the CG lies within a narrow range that the designers have tested. Weight and balance is the arithmetic of finding that point.

The analogy breaks down in one place. A seesaw pivots at a fixed point. An aeroplane does not pivot about any point you choose. So instead of measuring from a pivot, we measure from an agreed reference, and work out where the balance point is relative to it.

## The language of weight and balance

There are nine terms to know. We'll take them in turn.

The first is the datum. The datum is a reference plane, chosen by the manufacturer, from which all horizontal distances are measured. It is often the firewall, or a point ahead of the nose. It is arbitrary. The CG comes out at the same physical place whichever datum you use. But every figure in a loading system must be measured from the same one. Putting it ahead of the nose has a convenience: every distance is then positive.

The second is the arm. The arm is the horizontal distance from the datum to the centre of gravity of an item, in millimetres or inches. It is positive aft of the datum. A pilot's seat with an arm of 2,000 mm means the pilot's weight acts 2,000 mm behind the datum.

The third is the moment. The moment is weight multiplied by arm. It is the turning effect of that item about the datum, the seesaw's weight times distance. In metric loading systems, its unit is the kilogram millimetre. Because the numbers get big, some loading systems divide moments by a fixed amount, and call the result index units. The logic is unchanged. You'll find those in the PPL lesson on loading.

The fourth is the station. A station is a location in the aircraft, identified by its arm. For example, station 2,100 for the rear seats. The loading system lists the stations: the front seats, the rear seats, each baggage locker, and the fuel tanks.

The fifth is the centre of gravity, and its limits. The CG is the point at which the total weight is considered to act. It is the total moment divided by the total weight. The forward and aft CG limits are the range within which the aircraft is controllable and stable. On many aeroplanes, the forward limit moves aft at higher weights. So the limits form an envelope, of weight against CG, rather than two fixed numbers.

The sixth is empty weight, or basic empty weight. That is the weight of the aircraft with its fixed equipment, unusable fuel and full oil, as weighed, with its own arm. It is different for each individual aeroplane. That is why the weight and balance data in the POH must be for this airframe.

The seventh is operating weight. That is the empty weight, plus the crew and crew items, and on some systems the oil. It is the starting point when the crew is the same on every flight.

The eighth is the group of structural weights. MTOW, the maximum take-off weight, is the structural limit for take-off. You'll find why the weight you may actually use on a hot day can be lower in the lesson on take-off and landing performance. MLW, the maximum landing weight, is the structural limit for landing, where the undercarriage and its attachments take the impact. On light trainers, it often equals MTOW.

And the ninth is zero fuel weight, with its limit, the MZFW. Zero fuel weight is the weight with everything on board except usable fuel. The maximum zero fuel weight exists because of where the load sits. Fuel in the wings hangs where the lift is made, so it bends the wing very little. People and bags in the fuselage hang from the wing roots, and bend the wing upward. So the MZFW limits the wing-bending load from the fuselage contents. Beyond it, any additional load must be fuel.

Question. Why does the maximum zero fuel weight limit the load in the cabin, but not the fuel in the wings?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because fuel in the wing tanks sits where the lift is generated, so it adds little bending load at the wing roots. Weight in the fuselage hangs from the roots, and bends the wings upward. The MZFW caps that fuselage load. Any weight above it must be fuel.

## Finding the loading information

The figures you calculate with are not things you remember. They belong to the aircraft. They are in section 6 of its POH, or in the workbook's loading system. Knowing where each one lives, and what each one means, is half of this part of the syllabus. There are four kinds.

First, the MTOW, and the MLW and MZFW where they exist. Those are in the limitations. Every calculation ends by comparing your total with them.

Second, the seats. Each seat row has an arm. Front seats are usually close to the CG range. Rear seats are well aft of it. That is why rear passengers move the CG aft so strongly. Some aeroplanes give an adjustable seat a range of arms. Use the one the loading system specifies.

Third, the baggage lockers. Each locker has an arm, and a capacity. The capacity is a maximum weight, set by the strength of its floor and tie-downs. A locker's limit is a limit in its own right. A load can be inside the CG envelope and under MTOW, and still be illegal, because one locker is overloaded. Lockers are usually the furthest aft station in a light aeroplane. So a heavy bag there has a large moment.

And fourth, the fuel. The loading system gives the usable capacity in litres, the arm of the tanks, the grade of fuel the engine needs, and the specific gravity to use. The grade matters for the engine, not for the arithmetic. AVGAS {100LL|one hundred low lead} is dyed blue, and AVGAS {100/130|one hundred, one thirty} is green. A fuel sample of the wrong colour, or a clear one, means the wrong fuel, or water.

[[diagram:fuel-sample-colours]]
Picture three clear fuel testers side by side. AVGAS {100LL|one hundred low lead} is blue. AVGAS {100/130|one hundred, one thirty} is green. Jet A-1 is clear to straw coloured. A sample with clear globules, or a separate layer at the bottom, contains water. And a clear sample from an AVGAS aeroplane means water or jet fuel. Do not fly until the cause is found.

The specific gravity, often shortened to SG, is the fuel's density compared with water. A litre of water has a mass of 1 kg. A litre of AVGAS has a mass of about 0.71 to 0.72 kg.

Fuel is measured in litres, and loaded in kilograms. So there are two conversions. Kilograms equal litres times the SG. And litres equal kilograms divided by the SG. Litres to kilograms is multiply. Kilograms to litres is divide.

Here is a quick sense check. Fuel is lighter than water. So the number of kilograms must be smaller than the number of litres. Use the SG given in the question, or in the loading system.

Let's work one, in both directions. The SG is 0.72. First, how many kilograms is full tanks of 150 L? Second, the load sheet shows you can carry 95 kg of fuel before reaching MTOW. How many litres is that? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 8]]
First, litres to kilograms, so multiply. 150 times 0.72 is 108 kg. That is fewer kilograms than litres, so the direction is correct.

Second, kilograms to litres, so divide. 95 divided by 0.72 is {131.9|one hundred and thirty-one point nine}. So 131 L. You round down, because rounding up would put you over.

The answers again: 108 kg, and 131 L. The second direction is the one people get wrong under exam pressure, because multiplying feels natural. So check the answer against the rule. Litres must come out larger than kilograms.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Multiplying when you should divide. If converting kilograms to litres gives you fewer litres than kilograms, you have multiplied by the SG instead of dividing. And the same slip in the other direction makes 150 L of fuel weigh 208 kg.

## Finding the CG: why moment divided by weight works

Now the method. Imagine replacing everything in the aeroplane, every item at its own arm, with a single weight equal to the total, placed at one point. For that single weight to have the same turning effect about the datum as all the items together, one thing must be true. The total weight, times the CG arm, must equal the sum of all the moments.

That point is the CG. Rearrange it, and the CG equals the total moment divided by the total weight.

The method is the same for every loading system. It has five steps.

First, list each item with its weight and arm. The empty aircraft. The pilot and front passenger. The rear passengers. The baggage, locker by locker. And the fuel.

Second, convert the fuel in litres to kilograms, using the SG.

Third, multiply each weight by its arm to get its moment. Then add the weights, and add the moments.

Fourth, divide the total moment by the total weight, to get the CG.

And fifth, check the total weight against MTOW, and against MZFW and MLW where they apply. And check the CG against the limits at that weight.

Let's work one: four people, bags, and full fuel. The trainer has an empty weight of 680 kg, at an arm of 2,050 mm. Its MTOW is 1,050 kg. Its CG limits are 2,000 to 2,120 mm aft of the datum. The front seats are at 2,000 mm. The rear seat is at 2,900 mm. Baggage is at 3,500 mm. And fuel is at 2,300 mm, with an SG of 0.72.

The load is a pilot and front passenger together weighing 160 kg. A rear passenger of 70 kg. A 20 kg bag. And full tanks of 150 L. Is it within limits? This one takes a while, so pause as long as you like.
[[pause 10]]
Start with the moments, item by item.

The empty aircraft. 680 kg at 2,050 mm is 1,394,000 kilogram millimetres.

The pilot and passenger. 160 kg at 2,000 mm is 320,000.

The rear passenger. 70 kg at 2,900 mm is 203,000.

The baggage. 20 kg at 3,500 mm is 70,000.

And the fuel. 150 L times 0.72 is 108 kg. At 2,300 mm, that is 248,400.

Now add them. The total weight is 1,038 kg. That is under the 1,050 kg MTOW. The total moment is 2,235,400 kilogram millimetres.

Then divide. 2,235,400 divided by 1,038 puts the CG at 2,154 mm.

And check it against the limits. 2,154 mm is aft of the 2,120 mm limit, by 34 mm.

So the answer: the weight is legal, at 1,038 kg, but the CG is at 2,154 mm, aft of the limit. The aeroplane is tail-heavy, and must not fly loaded like this.

[[diagram:moment-arm-seesaw]]
Picture that load as a balance beam, measured from the datum on the left. Each load pushes down at its own arm. Far out to the right are the rear passenger at 2,900 mm and the bag at 3,500 mm. The total weight is 1,038 kg and the total moment is 2,235,400, so the balance point lands at 2,154 mm. That is just past the right-hand end of the marked limits, which run from 2,000 to 2,120 mm.

The bag and the rear passenger sit far behind the limits. Their moments pull the balance point aft, out of all proportion to their weight.

So the load must change, and each change has to be worked through. There are three to try.

The first is moving the 20 kg bag, from the locker at 3,500 mm to the front at 2,000 mm. That removes 20 times the difference between 3,500 and 2,000, which is 30,000 kilogram millimetres. The new total moment is 2,205,400. Divided by 1,038, that is 2,125 mm. Still just aft.

The second is removing the baggage altogether. The moment is 2,165,400, and the weight is 1,018. That gives 2,127 mm. Also aft. And notice, it is further aft than moving the bag. That is because taking the bag away also made the divisor smaller.

So the rear passenger's 70 kg at 2,900 mm is the load that has to change. With the front seats full, there is nowhere further forward to put them. So the third option is leaving them behind, with the bag still in the locker. The moment is 2,235,400 minus 203,000, which is 2,032,400. The weight is 1,038 minus 70, which is 968. And 2,032,400 divided by 968 is 2,100 mm. That is inside the limits.

That is the lesson of the example. Re-check the CG after every change. Changing a weight changes the divisor as well as the moment, and the result is not always what intuition says.

Now the same aeroplane again, with heavier people in the front. The load is the same, but the pilot and front passenger together weigh 170 kg instead of 160. Check the weight, and the CG.
[[pause 8]]
First, the total weight. 680 plus 170 plus 70 plus 20 plus 108 is 1,048 kg.

Second, against MTOW. 1,050 minus 1,048 is 2 kg under. So it is legal on weight, with almost no margin. A slightly heavier bag, or a top-up of fuel, would put it over.

Third, the moment. The extra 10 kg at 2,000 mm adds 10 times 2,000 to the moment. So the total is 2,255,400 kilogram millimetres. Divided by 1,048, the CG is at 2,152 mm. Still aft of the limit.

So the answer: 2 kg under MTOW, and still tail-heavy at 2,152 mm. A weight check and a CG check are separate tests, and a load has to pass both.

Here is one for you to try. The same aeroplane, flown solo. The pilot weighs 85 kg, in the front seat at 2,000 mm. There is no rear passenger. There is a 30 kg bag in the 3,500 mm locker. And there are 120 L of fuel at an SG of 0.72, in the tanks at 2,300 mm. Is it within limits at take-off? And again after you have burned 60 L? Take your time.
[[pause 15]]
Here is the working. First, the fuel. 120 times 0.72 is 86.4 kg.

Now the moments. The empty aircraft, as before, is 680 kg and 1,394,000. The pilot, 85 kg at 2,000 mm, is 170,000. The baggage, 30 kg at 3,500 mm, is 105,000. And the fuel, 86.4 kg at 2,300 mm, is 198,720.

The total weight is {881.4|eight hundred and eighty-one point four} kg. The total moment is 1,867,720. Divide, and the take-off CG is 2,119 mm.

That is inside the limits of 2,000 to 2,120 mm. But it is only 1 mm from the aft limit. A solo pilot with a heavy bag is a tail-heavy case. There is no front passenger to balance the bag.

Now after burning 60 L. That is 60 times 0.72, which is 43.2 kg. The weight becomes {881.4|eight hundred and eighty-one point four} minus 43.2, which is {838.2|eight hundred and thirty-eight point two} kg. The fuel's moment that comes off is 43.2 times 2,300, which is 99,360. So the moment becomes 1,867,720 minus 99,360, which is 1,768,360. Divide by {838.2|eight hundred and thirty-eight point two}, and the CG is at 2,110 mm.

So the fuel tanks, at 2,300 mm, are aft of the CG. And burning fuel moves the CG forward. It is still inside.

The answer: legal at take-off, at 2,119 mm, and legal after the burn, at 2,110 mm. But moving the bag forward would give a much more comfortable margin.

Each load you add moves a point on the graph. That point must stay inside the envelope at take-off, and after the fuel has burned off.

That is why you check at more than one weight. As fuel burns, the loading point moves along a line on the envelope. Both ends of that line, the take-off point and the landing point, must be inside it.

Whether burning fuel moves the CG forward or aft depends on where the tanks are, relative to the CG. Tanks aft of the CG, as here, move it forward as they empty. Tanks forward of it move it aft. And where the loading system gives an MZFW, the zero fuel point must also be within limits. That is where the CG ends up if you burn everything.

The CASA workbook's loading systems, ALPHA, BRAVO and CHARLIE, use index units and graphs instead of raw moments. But the logic is identical. They are covered in detail in the PPL lesson on loading. And the exam will give you the workbook.

Question. You remove a bag from the rear locker, and the CG moves forward less than you expected. Why?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because removing the bag reduces both the total moment and the total weight. The CG is moment divided by weight, so the smaller divisor partly cancels the smaller moment. That is why you re-calculate after every change.

Question. The fuel tanks are aft of the CG. Does burning fuel move the CG forward or aft, and why?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: forward. Taking weight away from a station aft of the CG removes a large moment relative to its weight. So the balance point moves away from it, forward. If the tanks were forward of the CG, burning fuel would move the CG aft.

## What happens when the limits are exceeded

The limits are not paperwork. Each one protects something physical. And exceeding it has predictable results.

Start with over MTOW. Everything that depends on weight gets worse at once.

The wing must make more lift at every speed, so the stall speed rises. Stall speed goes with the square root of weight. The aeroplane needs a higher speed to lift off, and more distance to reach it. So there is a longer take-off run and distance. The surplus of power over that needed to fly level shrinks. So the rate and angle of climb fall. Cruise speed and range drop. The aeroplane is less manoeuvrable. It lands faster, and needs a longer landing distance.

And the structural loads go beyond those the airframe and undercarriage were certified for, especially in turbulence or a hard landing. The limit load factor is a multiple of the certified weight. So an aeroplane 10 percent over MTOW puts its 3.8 g design load on the structure at about 3.8 divided by 1.1. That is about 3.5 g. And it is also illegal.

Then over MLW. The undercarriage, and the structure around it, take the impact of touchdown. Above MLW, they can be damaged on landing. In a trainer with MLW equal to MTOW, this is rarely the issue. But on larger aeroplanes, fuel may have to be burned off before landing.

The CG limits protect controllability and stability. To see why, remember how the aeroplane balances in pitch.

The CG normally sits ahead of the wing's centre of lift. So the nose wants to drop, and the tailplane holds it up by pushing down. That tail download is what makes the aeroplane stable. If the nose pitches up, the tail's angle of attack changes, and the tail pushes it back. How hard the tail has to push, and how much leverage it has, both depend on where the CG is.

[[diagram:tail-download-cg]]
Picture two side views of the same aeroplane, one above the other. In the first, the CG is well ahead of the centre of lift. The tail must push down hard. So the wing must carry the weight plus that download. It flies at a higher angle of attack, and stalls at a higher speed. But the long distance from the CG to the tail makes it very stable. In the second, the CG is close to the centre of lift. The tail pushes down only lightly, and the wing carries less, so the stall speed is lower. But the tail's leverage over the CG is short, and the restoring moment is weak. It is less stable, and light and over-sensitive in pitch.

Now take each limit in turn.

CG forward of the limit. The nose is heavy, so the tail must push down harder. The wing has to carry that extra download as well as the weight. So it flies at a higher angle of attack. That means a higher stall speed, and more drag from the trim, so less range. The aeroplane is very stable. That sounds good, but it means it resists your pitch inputs. The serious problem comes at the flare. At low speed the elevator is less effective. And with a nose-heavy aeroplane, you may need more elevator to flare than you have. You land nosewheel first.

CG aft of the limit. The opposite. The tail needs to push down less, so the stall speed is lower. But the CG is now close to the centre of lift. So the tail's leverage over the CG is short, and the restoring moment is weak. That is reduced longitudinal stability. The controls feel light, and over-sensitive in pitch. It is easy to over-rotate, or to overstress the aeroplane.

And most dangerous of all, the elevator may not have the leverage to unstall the wing. Stall and spin recovery may be impossible. An aft-CG aeroplane can enter a flatter spin, from which the tail cannot recover it.

Picture the two ways the CG can be wrong, side by side. Forward of the limit: nose heavy. A higher stall speed. More elevator needed to flare, and possibly not enough. Higher drag from trim. And very stable. Aft of the limit: tail heavy. Light and over-sensitive pitch control. Reduced longitudinal stability. A lower stall speed, but poor stall and spin recovery, which may be impossible.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. An aft CG gives a lower stall speed. And the exam may offer that as if it were a benefit. It is not. The price is reduced stability, and possibly no stall or spin recovery. A forward CG is the safer of the two errors. But it still raises the stall speed, and it can leave you without enough elevator to flare.

Question. Why does a forward CG raise the stall speed?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because the tailplane must push down harder to hold the nose up. And the wing has to make enough lift to support that download, as well as the aeroplane's weight. More lift needed at the same speed means a higher angle of attack. So the wing reaches its critical angle at a higher speed.

## Bringing it together

It is Friday afternoon at Jandakot. You are planning Saturday's trip to Busselton, with two friends and weekend bags.

You take the empty weight and arm from this aircraft's POH. Not from the club's other one of the same type. The front seats, the rear seat and the rear locker each have their arm. The locker has its own weight limit. And the fuel goes in at its arm, after converting litres to kilograms by multiplying by 0.72.

The first sum comes out under MTOW, but aft of the limit. The bags and the rear passenger are all well behind the CG range. So you move the heaviest person to the front, and re-calculate, moment and weight both. Now it is inside.

Then you check it again, with the fuel you expect to have at Busselton. The tanks are aft of the CG, so the CG moves forward as you burn fuel. The landing point is inside too.

You also notice something else. With the full tanks you planned, you are 15 kg under MTOW, on a summer afternoon. And the climb weight limit, from the lesson on take-off and landing performance, may not allow that.

The calculation took ten minutes. The alternative is finding out on the take-off roll.

## Key points

Let's recap.

The terms. The datum is the reference for all distances. The arm is the distance from the datum, positive aft. The moment is weight times arm. A station is a location identified by its arm. The CG is the total moment divided by the total weight, and it must lie within the forward and aft limits, which form an envelope. Empty weight includes unusable fuel and full oil. Operating weight adds the crew. MTOW and MLW are structural limits. And MZFW caps the fuselage load that bends the wing.

The loading information. Seats, lockers and tanks each have an arm. Lockers also have a maximum weight. Fuel has a usable capacity, a grade and an SG. Litres times the SG gives kilograms. Kilograms divided by the SG gives litres.

The method. List the weights and arms. Find the moments. Add them. Divide. Check the weight against MTOW, and the CG against the limits, at take-off and after the fuel burn. And re-calculate after every change, because the divisor changes too.

The consequences. Over MTOW: a higher stall speed, a longer take-off and landing, a poorer climb, structural overload, and it is illegal. Over MLW: damage on landing. A forward CG: nose heavy, a higher stall speed, and possibly not enough elevator to flare. An aft CG: unstable and over-sensitive, with stall and spin recovery possibly impossible.

A final exam tip. Work in the units of the loading system, and do not round until the end. CASA lists the loading systems among the areas candidates get wrong most often. Usually it is from reading the wrong line on a graph, or converting fuel the wrong way. Litres to kilograms is multiply by the SG. Kilograms to litres is divide. And expect a question with a load under MTOW, but a CG outside the limits. The answer is to change the distribution of the load, not to take off because the weight is fine.
