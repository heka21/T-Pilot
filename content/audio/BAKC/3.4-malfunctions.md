## What the exam expects (BAKC 3.4)

The exam wants nine things from you. First, for a list of components, what the cockpit shows when each one fails, what you do, and what follows if you cannot fix it. The list is the alternator, the magneto, the battery, the ignition switch, a blocked fuel vent and the fuel or booster pump, the oil cooler and cowl flaps, the vacuum pump, and the hydraulic brakes. Second, the same for the gauges: oil temperature and pressure, cylinder head temperature, fuel pressure, the tachometer, the ammeter or loadmeter, the voltmeter, and engine icing. Third, how you check the carburettor heat before take-off. Fourth, the conditions that produce throttle ice, fuel evaporation ice and impact ice, and how severe each is. Fifth, the danger of opening the throttle bit by bit when icing has not been recognised. Sixth, using carburettor heat to prevent ice, to remove it, and on the ground. Seventh, how alternate air differs from carburettor heat. Eighth, what carburettor heat does to performance and the engine instruments. And ninth, the symptoms of fuel vaporisation, and how to fix it.

## Why this matters

Engines rarely fail without warning. A needle creeps, a light flickers, the RPM sags. The pilot who reads those signs early can fix the problem, or pick a field while the engine still runs.

This lesson takes each system from the lesson on piston engines and asks three questions. What do you see? What do you do? And what happens if it cannot be fixed? The second half is about carburettor ice, which can form on a mild, humid morning over Rottnest. That is where the exam's questions cluster.

## When a component fails

There are eight components to cover, in order: the alternator, the magneto, the battery, the ignition switch, the fuel vent and pumps, the oil cooler and cowl flaps, the vacuum pump, and the brakes.

The alternator first. When the alternator stops, the battery quietly takes over. The ammeter shows a discharge. The voltmeter sags. Many aeroplanes show a warning light. And later, the radios fade. Check the alternator switch and the field breaker, and cycle the alternator once, if the flight manual allows. If it stays off, reduce the electrical load to essentials. The battery may last perhaps 30 to 60 minutes. Then you lose the radios, the transponder, electric flaps, the fuel pump and the lights. So land before that. The engine runs on regardless.

The magneto. A failed magneto shows as rough running and lost RPM, or as an excessive drop on the magneto check. Select each magneto in turn, and stay on the one that runs smoothly. You keep an engine, with less power and no redundancy. Land as soon as practicable.

[[diagram:dual-ignition-system]]
Picture an engine with two magnetos, one on each side, each driven by the engine. Every cylinder has two spark plugs. Each magneto feeds one plug in every cylinder, through its own leads. So if one magneto fails, the engine keeps running on the other, with a small loss of power. The ignition switch stops a magneto by earthing it. And a broken earth lead leaves that magneto live.

The battery. A failing battery shows as an engine that will not crank, weak electrical services, or a discharge even with the alternator working. Charge it, or replace it. And do not hand-swing the propeller unless you are trained and approved. A flat battery is no reserve, and it cannot absorb the alternator's surges, so electrical reliability suffers.

The ignition switch. A faulty switch, or a faulty P-lead, shows as the engine still running at off on the dead cut, or as a magneto that cannot be selected. Report it, and treat the propeller as live. A live magneto fires the engine if the propeller is moved by hand, and that kills people.

The fuel vent and the pumps. A blocked vent, or a failed pump, shows as falling fuel pressure, then rough running, then a stopped engine. A blocked vent can also buckle the tank. Follow the fuel's path. Booster pump on, because it takes over from a failed engine pump. Change tanks, because the other vent is probably clear. And mixture rich. Otherwise, fuel starvation means a forced landing.

[[diagram:fuel-system-schematic]]
Picture the fuel's path from top to bottom. Two wing tanks, each with a vent at the top and a drain at its lowest point, feed down to a fuel selector. From the selector, the fuel passes through the strainer, which has its own drain at the lowest point of the whole system. Then it goes through the electric booster pump, where one is fitted, and the engine-driven pump, to the carburettor. Water is heavier than fuel and settles at the low points. That is why the drains are there, and why each one is sampled before flight.

The oil cooler and cowl flaps. Cowl flaps are doors that let more cooling air out of the cowling. A blocked oil cooler, or cowl flaps stuck shut, traps heat. You see a rising oil temperature and cylinder head temperature, and then a falling oil pressure as the oil thins. Open the cowl flaps, enrich, reduce power, and lower the nose. Unresolved, overheating leads to detonation and failure.

The vacuum pump. A failing vacuum pump shows as low suction, perhaps a warning flag, and attitude and heading indicators that slow, drift and topple. Cross-check with the turn coordinator, the compass and the pressure instruments, and stay visual. With no reliable attitude reference, do not enter cloud. The lesson on flight instruments explains why.

And the hydraulic brakes. A brake leak shows as a spongy or sinking pedal, a pull to one side, or fluid on the wheel. If you find it on the ground, get it fixed. If you find it on landing, pump the pedal, and use the good brake with rudder and aerodynamic braking. Choose a long runway. Otherwise you may run off the end.

So here is the pattern across all eight. Electrical failures, the alternator and the battery, show on the ammeter and voltmeter, and the answer is to shed load and land before the battery dies. Ignition failures, the magneto and the switch, show as roughness or a live propeller. Fuel failures, the vent and the pumps, show as falling fuel pressure, and the answer is booster pump, change tanks, mixture rich. Cooling failures show as rising temperatures, and the answer is more air, more fuel and less power. The vacuum pump takes the gyros, so stay out of cloud. And a brake leak means a long roll, so pick a long runway.

Question. Why is a magneto failure a reason to land soon, even though the engine is still running smoothly on the other one?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because the redundancy is gone. Dual ignition exists so that one failure does not stop the engine. With one magneto out, a single further failure in the remaining system, a lead or the magneto itself, stops it.

## When a gauge looks wrong

Now the gauges. A needle is out of place. What could cause it, and what do you do? There are eight readings to know.

The first is oil pressure low with oil temperature rising. Together, these mean the engine is losing oil, or failing to circulate it. That could be low oil quantity, a leak, a pump failure or a blocked cooler. Less oil carries the same heat, so the temperature rises as the pressure falls. Reduce power, enrich, and land as soon as possible. If the pressure falls but the temperature stays steady, the gauge itself may be at fault. But treat it as real until you are on the ground. Unresolved, the engine seizes.

The second is oil temperature high on its own, with normal pressure. That is usually the way you are operating, rather than a fault: a hot day, a long slow climb, or cowl flaps closed. Lower the nose, reduce power, and open the cowl flaps, or the oil breaks down.

The third is a high cylinder head temperature. The cylinders are too hot. The cause could be a lean mixture, high power at low airspeed, cowl flaps closed, or detonation already happening. Enrich, reduce power, increase the airspeed, and open the cowl flaps. Otherwise, detonation and engine damage follow.

The fourth is low fuel pressure. Fuel is not reaching the engine properly. The cause could be a failed pump, a blocked vent, an empty tank, or vapour lock. Booster pump on, change tanks, mixture rich. Otherwise the engine stops.

The fifth is the tachometer, which can be wrong in two directions. RPM falling with the throttle unchanged, in a fixed-pitch aeroplane, points to carburettor ice first, then a magneto problem or a propeller fault. Apply carburettor heat, then check the magnetos. RPM over the red line is usually a fixed-pitch propeller in a dive. Throttle back and raise the nose. Unresolved, these lead to engine failure, or over-stress.

The sixth is the ammeter or loadmeter. A discharge, or zero load, means the alternator has stopped. A very high charge that does not settle after start means a battery fault or a voltage regulator fault, and a runaway charge can overheat the battery. Reduce load, check the breakers, and cycle the alternator.

The seventh is the voltmeter. Low voltage means the alternator is not charging. High voltage means the regulator has failed and the alternator is overcharging. Switch the alternator off, as the flight manual directs, before it damages the battery and the avionics.

And the eighth is engine icing. It shows as a gradual loss of RPM with a fixed-pitch propeller, or of manifold pressure with a constant-speed one, often with rough running. Apply full carburettor heat, and leave it on until the engine recovers. Unresolved, the engine stops. The rest of this lesson is about this one.

Question. Oil pressure is falling and oil temperature is rising together. Why is that combination more serious than a high oil temperature alone?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: together, they point to the engine losing its oil, or its circulation. Less oil is carrying the heat, so it gets hotter. And less is reaching the bearings, so the pressure falls. A high temperature alone usually just means a hot day or a slow climb. The combination means seizure is coming. Land as soon as possible.

## How carburettor ice forms

Recall the carburettor from the lesson on piston engines. Air speeds up through the venturi, a narrow throat, and its pressure drops. Fuel sprays into it. And the throttle butterfly, above the venturi, sets how much mixture reaches the cylinders. Two things happen to that air that make it cold.

The first is expansion. When air's pressure drops, its temperature drops with it. The air in the venturi, and the air squeezing past a partly closed butterfly, is at lower pressure, so it is colder.

The second is evaporation. Liquid fuel turning into vapour takes heat from its surroundings, the way sweat cools your skin. The fuel evaporating into the airstream takes that heat from the air.

Together, these can lower the air temperature by 20 to 30 degrees. Air entering at 20 degrees can be below freezing by the time it passes the butterfly. If that air is humid, its water vapour condenses and freezes onto the cold metal. That narrows the passage, and chokes the engine of mixture. So carburettor ice does not need freezing weather. It needs moisture, and a temperature drop. And the carburettor supplies the temperature drop itself.

## Three kinds of engine ice

The ice forms in three places, under different conditions, with different severity. Throttle ice, fuel evaporation ice, and impact ice.

Throttle ice forms on and just behind the partly closed throttle butterfly, where the pressure drop, and so the cooling, is greatest. It is most likely at low power, when the butterfly is nearly closed: in the descent, while taxiing, at idle, and on the glide approach. It needs high humidity, and outside temperatures of roughly minus 5 to plus 20 degrees. It is the most common, and the most dangerous kind. Around a nearly closed butterfly the gap is tiny, so a small amount of ice blocks it. And an engine at low power has little heat to resist.

Fuel evaporation ice, also called refrigeration ice, forms in the venturi, and in the induction passage downstream of the fuel jet, where the fuel evaporates. Evaporation happens at any throttle setting, so this ice can form at any power, including cruise. It needs high humidity, at outside temperatures of roughly minus 10 to plus 30 degrees. It is serious. It is the one that catches you in cruise, when you are not expecting ice.

Impact ice forms on the air intake, the filter and the intake ducting. It forms when visible moisture, such as cloud, rain or sleet, at or below freezing, strikes them and freezes on contact, just as ice forms on a wing. It can block the air supply completely. It affects fuel-injected engines as well as carburettor engines, and the alternate air source exists to bypass it.

[[diagram:carburettor-ice-locations]]
Picture a carburettor drawn in cross-section, with air coming in at the bottom and flowing up. At the bottom, at the air intake and filter, is impact ice, in visible moisture at or below freezing. In the middle, in the venturi and just above the fuel nozzle, is fuel evaporation ice, where the evaporating fuel takes heat from the air. And at the top, on and behind the partly closed throttle butterfly, is throttle ice, where the pressure drop is largest. That top one is the most common and the most dangerous. Off to one side, a muff around the exhaust supplies the hot air for carburettor heat.

The CASA carburettor icing chart plots the risk against outside air temperature and humidity, which it shows as dew point. It shows serious icing at any power is possible from well below freezing to about 30 degrees, when the humidity is high. And it shows serious icing at descent power over an even wider range. High humidity is the common factor. That makes coastal Western Australia in the cooler months, and any day with showers about, a carburettor icing environment.

[[diagram:carburettor-ice-temperature-ranges]]
Picture a temperature scale running left to right, with freezing marked near the left, and three bars laid against it. Throttle ice runs from roughly minus 5 to plus 20 degrees, at low power. Fuel evaporation ice is the longest bar, from roughly minus 10 to plus 30, at any power. And impact ice sits only at or below freezing, in visible moisture. The point to notice: in humid air, every kind except impact ice is possible well above freezing, up to 20 or 30 degrees.

So, to say the figures again. Throttle ice: low power, humid, roughly minus 5 to plus 20, and the most dangerous. Fuel evaporation ice: any power, humid, roughly minus 10 to plus 30, and serious. Impact ice: visible moisture at or below freezing, and it can block the intake.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. The distractor says carburettor ice is most likely on cold, dry days near freezing. It is the opposite. It needs moisture, and the most dangerous kind is most likely at low power, in humid air, at mild temperatures. A clear, frosty, bone-dry winter morning at Merredin is a lower risk than a grey, drizzly 15 degree morning at Jandakot.

Question. Why is throttle ice most likely during a glide approach, rather than in cruise?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: in the glide, the throttle butterfly is nearly closed. So the air squeezing past it has its largest pressure drop and cooling, and the gap is small enough for a little ice to block it. The engine at idle also has little heat to melt it.

## The danger of chasing the RPM

You are cruising in a fixed-pitch trainer, and you notice the RPM is a little lower than you set. Nothing else seems wrong, so you nudge the throttle forward, and the RPM comes back. Ten minutes later it has crept down again, and you nudge it again.

Behind the panel, ice is growing in the carburettor and narrowing the passage. So less mixture reaches the cylinders, and the RPM falls. Opening the throttle wider lets more mixture past the ice, so the RPM recovers. But the ice keeps growing. Each throttle increment hides the symptom while the cause gets worse. Eventually the throttle is fully open, and there is nothing left to give. The ice keeps building, and the engine stops. By then, the engine has little heat left to melt so much ice.

[[diagram:chasing-the-rpm]]
Picture a graph of RPM against time, shaped like a saw blade. The line slopes slowly down as ice builds. Each time the pilot opens the throttle, it jumps back up for a moment, then starts sloping down again, while the ice keeps building behind the more open butterfly. The steps repeat until the throttle is fully open. Then there is nothing left, and the line falls to zero. The engine has stopped.

So the rule is this. Any unexplained loss of RPM, or of manifold pressure, is carburettor ice until proven otherwise. Apply full carburettor heat, and leave it on. Only then think about the throttle.

## Checking the carburettor heat before take-off

The check uses one fact: hot air is less dense, so an engine breathing it makes less power.

During the run-up, at the flight manual's check RPM, often 1,700 to 1,800 RPM, apply full carburettor heat and watch the tachometer. There are four things to look for.

First, the RPM should drop, typically by about 100 to 150 RPM in a trainer, although your flight manual may give its own figure. The heated air is less dense. So a drop proves hot air is reaching the carburettor.

Second, leave the heat on for several seconds. If the RPM then rises while the heat is still on, there was ice in the carburettor. The hot air has melted it, and the engine is breathing more freely.

Third, return the control to cold. The RPM should return to its original value, or a little higher if ice was cleared.

And fourth, no drop at all means the heat is not working. That could be a disconnected control, or a damaged hot-air duct or flap. Do not take off into conditions where carburettor ice is likely.

[[diagram:carb-heat-check-rpm]]
Picture the RPM traced against time through the run-up. When full heat goes on, the line steps down, because hot air is less dense. If ice was present, the line then climbs back up a little while the heat is still on, as the ice melts. When the heat goes back to cold, the line returns to where it started. And if the line never stepped down at all, the heat is not working.

Let's work one. The run-up is at 1,700 RPM, on a mild, humid morning, and you apply full heat. The RPM falls to 1,580. You hold the heat on for ten seconds, and the RPM stays at 1,580. Then you return to cold, and it comes back to 1,700. What does that tell you? Pause if you'd like to work it out.
[[pause 5]]
The drop is 1,700 minus 1,580, which is 120 RPM. That is within the normal 100 to 150, so hot air is reaching the carburettor. With the heat held on, there was no rise, so there was no ice. And on cold, the RPM returned to 1,700, the starting value. So the system works, and the carburettor was clear. Select cold before take-off, so full power is available.

Now two variations for you to try. In the first, the same run-up: heat on, and the RPM falls to 1,560. Then it climbs to 1,610 with the heat still on. Back on cold, it settles at 1,740. In the second, heat on, and the RPM stays at 1,700. What does each one mean?
[[pause 8]]
The first. The drop is 1,700 minus 1,560, which is 140 RPM, so the system is working. The rise, 1,610 minus 1,560, is 50 RPM with the heat still on. That means ice was present, and has melted. Back on cold, the RPM is 40 higher than when you started, because the starting figure was already reduced by the ice. Taxiing at low power in humid air iced the carburettor. Expect more, and use heat as the flight manual requires.

The second. No drop means hot air is not reaching the carburettor. The carburettor heat is not working. The aeroplane is unserviceable for any flight where carburettor ice is possible. And on a humid morning, that is every flight.

So, three results to know. A drop that stays: heat works, no ice. A drop, then a rise with the heat still on: there was ice. No drop: the heat is not working.

## Using carburettor heat

There are three uses to cover: preventing ice, removing it, and the ground.

Preventing ice is called anti-icing. Use carburettor heat before ice has a chance to form. That means before reducing power for a descent or a long low-power approach, and when flying in high humidity, as the flight manual directs. And use full heat or none. Partial heat, without a carburettor air temperature gauge, can warm air that was well below freezing up into the icing range. That makes ice more likely, not less.

Removing ice that has already formed is called de-icing. When you suspect ice, apply full heat and keep it on. Expect the engine to run rougher, and the RPM to fall further at first. The hot air is less dense, which is the normal drop. And the melting ice is going through the engine as water. Do not turn the heat off because of the roughness. Hold it on. As the ice clears, the RPM rises and the engine smooths out. And when you return to cold, the RPM settles higher than before you applied the heat.

[[diagram:carburettor-heat-decision]]
Picture two RPM traces, one above the other. The top one has ice. The RPM has already crept down from {2400|twenty-four hundred} to {2300|twenty-three hundred}. Full heat goes on, and it falls to {2200|twenty-two hundred}, and the engine runs rough for a moment. Then the RPM climbs to {2350|twenty-three fifty} as the ice melts. Back on cold, it settles at {2400|twenty-four hundred}, higher than before. The bottom trace has no ice. The RPM drops slightly, stays there, and returns to its original value on cold.

And on the ground. Carburettor heat air is taken from around the exhaust, and it is normally unfiltered. On the ground, especially on a dusty strip, it draws dust and grit straight into the engine, where it wears the cylinders and rings. So use it only for the pre-take-off check, unless the flight manual says otherwise. And select cold before take-off, so full power is available.

Question. You apply full carburettor heat for suspected ice, and the engine immediately runs rougher. Do you leave the heat on, and why?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: yes. The roughness is the melted ice passing through the engine, plus the expected power loss from less dense hot air. Turning the heat off leaves the ice to keep building. Hold full heat until the engine smooths out and the RPM recovers.

## Alternate air and carburettor heat

The two controls look alike, and both bring in a different air source. But they solve different problems.

Carburettor heat is fitted to carburettor engines. It brings hot air from a muff around the exhaust into the carburettor, to prevent or melt carburettor ice inside it.

Alternate air is fitted to fuel-injected engines. Those cannot form carburettor ice, because they have no venturi. Their risk is a blocked air intake: impact ice on the filter, dust, or a collapsed filter. Alternate air opens a second air source, from inside the cowling, that bypasses the blocked intake. That air is unfiltered. On many engines the alternate air door opens automatically, when the intake blocks and the suction behind it rises.

So: carburettor heat melts ice inside a carburettor. Alternate air gives an injected engine a way around a blocked intake.

## What carburettor heat costs

Hot air is less dense. So the engine draws in less mass of air on each stroke, and makes less power. The RPM drops with a fixed-pitch propeller, and the manifold pressure drops with a constant-speed one. The carburettor delivers about the same fuel for the same volume of air, so the less dense air makes the mixture richer. Expect a slight loss of performance, higher fuel consumption for the power produced, and slightly rougher running. Lean as the flight manual permits, if the heat must stay on for long.

That is why carburettor heat is not used for take-off, unless the flight manual requires it. You lose take-off power. And hot intake air, on an engine already working hard at full power, raises the risk of detonation. Select cold before take-off, and before a go-around.

Question. Why does carburettor heat make the mixture richer?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the hot air is less dense. So a given volume of it contains less mass of air, and less oxygen. But the carburettor meters about the same fuel for that volume. Same fuel, less air: a richer mixture.

## Fuel vaporisation

Fuel stays a liquid only as long as it is cool enough for its pressure. Heat it, or lower the pressure on it, and it boils into vapour. That happens most on hot days, and especially after a hot engine has been shut down and restarted. With no cooling air flowing, heat soaks from the engine into the fuel lines and the pump, and the fuel in them boils. A pump designed to move liquid cannot move bubbles. So the supply to the engine becomes intermittent. This is vapour lock.

The symptoms are what an intermittent fuel supply looks like. Fluctuating fuel pressure and fuel flow. Rough running. Surging. And difficulty starting.

[[diagram:vapour-lock]]
Picture two sketches of the fuel line. On the left, after a hot engine is shut down, heat soaks into the lines and the pump, and the fuel boils into bubbles. The pump cannot move them, so the fuel pressure needle flickers, and the engine surges or will not start. On the right, the electric booster pump is on. It raises the pressure in the lines, pushing the vapour through or collapsing it back into liquid, and the pressure needle steadies in the green.

So, to fix it, switch on the electric booster pump. It raises the pressure in the lines, which pushes the vapour through or collapses it back into liquid, and restores the flow of liquid fuel. Enrich the mixture if needed, and follow the flight manual's hot-start procedure. On fuel-injected engines, that procedure usually includes keeping the mixture at idle cut-off until the engine fires, so that the vapour is cleared without flooding the engine. The procedure for your type takes precedence over any generic one.

## Key points

Let's recap.

Each component failure has a cockpit sign, an action and a consequence. The alternator: a discharge, so shed load and land before the battery dies. The magneto: rough running, so stay on the good one, with no redundancy. The battery: no crank, and never hand-swing untrained. The ignition switch: a live propeller. The vent or pump: falling fuel pressure, so booster pump on and change tanks. The cooler or cowl flaps: overheating. The vacuum pump: the gyros topple, so stay visual. And the brakes: a spongy pedal, so choose a long runway.

The gauges. Low oil pressure with a rising temperature means losing oil, so land. A high cylinder head or oil temperature: enrich, reduce power, more airflow. Low fuel pressure: booster pump and tanks. Falling RPM: carburettor heat first. A discharge or low volts: the alternator has failed. High volts: the regulator has failed, so switch the alternator off.

The carburettor heat check, at the flight manual RPM. A drop, typically about 100 to 150 RPM, proves the heat works. A rise with the heat on means ice was present. No drop means the heat is not working.

The three kinds of ice. Throttle ice: low power, humid, roughly minus 5 to plus 20 degrees, and the most dangerous. Fuel evaporation ice: any power, humid, roughly minus 10 to plus 30. Impact ice: visible moisture at or below freezing, and it blocks the intake.

Opening the throttle to recover lost RPM hides the ice while it grows, until full throttle leaves nothing, and the engine stops.

Using the heat. Anti-ice before low power, with full heat or none. De-ice with full heat, held on through the roughness. Minimise its use on the ground, because the air is unfiltered, and select cold for take-off.

Carburettor heat prevents or melts ice in a carburettor. Alternate air gives a fuel-injected engine an unfiltered path around a blocked intake. Carburettor heat reduces power, so the RPM or manifold pressure drops, and it richens the mixture.

And vapour lock: fluctuating fuel pressure and flow, rough running, surging and hard starting. Booster pump on, enrich, and follow the hot-start procedure.

A final exam tip. Two facts are reliable exam material. Carburettor ice is most likely at low power, in humid air, at moderate temperatures, not in freezing weather. And the correct response to an unexplained RPM loss is full carburettor heat, left on, before you touch the throttle. Know the three results of the carburettor heat check, a drop, a drop then a rise, and no drop, and what each one means. And know that alternate air is for fuel-injected engines and a blocked intake.
