This is BAKC 2.4, Units of measurement. By the end you will know which unit goes with which quantity in Australian aviation, you will be able to tell height, altitude and elevation apart, and you will be able to turn litres of fuel into kilograms and back. There are worked examples and questions along the way, so pause whenever you want a moment to think.

## What the exam expects (BAKC 2.4)

The exam wants two things from you. First, the unit for vertical distance, which is feet, and the difference between height, altitude and elevation. And second, the units used in Australia for runway dimensions, temperature, atmospheric pressure, weight, volume of liquids and visibility.

## Why this matters

Australian aviation measures in a mixture of units that grew up in different places. Feet from British and American practice. Metres and kilograms from the metric system. Nautical miles and knots from the sea.

In one pre-flight you will read a runway length in metres from ERSA, the En Route Supplement. A cloud base in feet from the TAF. A QNH in hectopascals. A temperature in degrees Celsius. A fuel load in litres. And a maximum take-off weight in kilograms.

Each one sits in its own unit for a reason, and mixing them up has real consequences. Fuel entered in litres where the weight and balance wants kilograms makes the fuel look almost 40 percent heavier on paper than it is. And kilograms entered as litres hide real weight the other way. A cloud base above the aerodrome, read as above sea level, puts you in cloud over Northam.

The exam tests the units directly. And it tests the three vertical words, height, altitude and elevation, which are not interchangeable. This lesson gives each unit its job, and shows the two conversions you will make on every flight.

## Vertical distance is measured in feet

Everything vertical in aviation is in feet. Circuit height. Cruising levels. Cloud base. Aerodrome elevation. Obstacle heights on the chart. And the altimeter itself.

Feet were the unit of the countries that built the early aviation system. The world has kept them for vertical distance, so that a pilot and a controller anywhere share the same numbers. Australia follows that convention, even though it is otherwise metric.

Here is a useful size to have in your head. 1,000 ft, a typical circuit height, is about 300 m. Thousands are written with a comma, 1,000 ft, and in radio calls they are read as one thousand.

## Height, altitude and elevation

Three words describe vertical distance. They differ in what you measure from, and in what you are measuring. Picture an aeroplane flying the circuit at an aerodrome that sits on a low rise, with a ridge a few miles away.

The first word is altitude. Altitude is the vertical distance of the aeroplane above mean sea level. It is written feet AMSL, above mean sea level, or just feet on its own. It is what the altimeter shows when you have the local QNH set on its subscale. That is why QNH is the setting you use for flying below 10,000 ft in Australia. Cruising at 4,500 ft means an altitude.

The second word is elevation. Elevation is also measured above mean sea level. But it is the word for a point on, or fixed to, the Earth's surface: an aerodrome, a runway threshold, the top of a hill. An aerodrome's elevation is surveyed and printed in ERSA, and it never changes. Aerodrome elevation 99 ft is Jandakot's.

The third word is height. Height is the vertical distance above a specified datum, usually the ground or the aerodrome directly below. It is written feet AGL, above ground level, or above aerodrome level. Circuit height 1,000 ft is a height: 1,000 ft above the aerodrome, wherever the aerodrome happens to be.

So height and altitude describe where the aircraft is. Elevation describes where the ground is. And the three are linked by a single sum, which you will use on every circuit away from the coast. Altitude equals the elevation of the ground below, plus the height above that ground.

[[diagram:height-altitude-elevation]]
Picture a side view, with the vertical scale stretched. The sea is on the left. An aerodrome sits on the ground at an elevation of 300 ft above mean sea level. An aeroplane flies its circuit with QNH set, and the altimeter reads an altitude of 1,300 ft. So its height above the aerodrome is 1,000 ft. Further right, the ground rises to a 900 ft ridge. Over the ridge, at the same altitude, the aeroplane is only 400 ft above the ground. Altitude is measured from sea level, elevation is the ground's altitude, and height is what is left between them.

Let's work that one through. Your altimeter, set to the local QNH, reads 1,300 ft while you fly the circuit at an aerodrome with an elevation of 300 ft. A ridge nearby has an elevation of 900 ft. How high are you above the aerodrome, and above the ridge? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 5]]
Above the aerodrome: 1,300 minus 300 is 1,000 ft above ground level. That is circuit height. Above the ridge, at the same altitude: 1,300 minus 900 is only 400 ft. The altimeter has not changed, but the ground under you has risen 600 ft. Altitude is fixed to sea level. Height goes up and down with the terrain.

Now an inland aerodrome. You overfly an aerodrome of elevation 1,200 ft, with the altimeter reading 2,500 ft on QNH. How high are you above it?
[[pause 5]]
2,500 minus 1,200 is 1,300 ft above ground level. An altitude that would be a comfortable 2,500 ft above the beach at Rockingham is only 1,300 ft above this aerodrome. That is barely above circuit height, so you are in the way of the circuit traffic.

Now turn it round, with the altitude as the unknown. You arrive at a country aerodrome of elevation 650 ft, and you want to fly the circuit at 1,000 ft above it. What should the altimeter read?
[[pause 5]]
Altitude equals elevation plus height. 650 plus 1,000 is 1,650 ft on QNH. The circuit height is fixed above the aerodrome. So at every aerodrome, the altimeter figure is the elevation plus 1,000.

Now the altimeter and its datum. The altimeter is a barometer with a scale in feet. Whatever pressure you set on its subscale becomes its zero. Set the local QNH, and it reads altitude above mean sea level. Set the standard pressure, {1013.2 hPa|ten thirteen point two hectopascals}, and it reads pressure height. You will use that for performance calculations, in the lesson on take-off and landing performance. The instrument has no idea what is under it. That is why height above terrain always needs the elevation from the chart.

[[widget:altimeter-subscale]]
Imagine winding the subscale knob and watching the needle. Each hectopascal of difference between the subscale and the real QNH moves the reading about 30 ft.

For completeness, a flight level. A flight level is a pressure altitude: the altimeter reading with {1013.2 hPa|ten thirteen point two hectopascals} set, expressed in hundreds of feet. So {FL125|flight level one two five} is 12,500 ft on the standard setting. Flight levels are used above the transition altitude, which you will meet in the altimetry and flight rules units.

Next, cloud heights, and which datum they use. The difference between height and altitude matters most when you read cloud bases. In a TAF or a METAR, cloud heights are above the aerodrome. They are heights. In the GAF, the area forecast, they are above mean sea level. They are altitudes.

At Jandakot, 99 ft above the sea, the two are nearly the same. But at an aerodrome 1,000 ft up, a cloud base of 2,500 ft in the TAF is at 3,500 ft above mean sea level. And the same base in the GAF would read 3,500.

[[diagram:cloud-height-datum]]
Picture one layer of cloud above an aerodrome that sits on raised ground. Two measuring sticks reach up to the cloud base. The GAF's stick starts down at sea level, so it is the longer one. The TAF and METAR stick starts at the aerodrome, so it is shorter. The difference between them is the aerodrome elevation. The same cloud base reads lower in the TAF than in the GAF.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Elevation is always for something attached to the ground. If the question is about where an aircraft is above sea level, the word is altitude. Above the ground, it is height. A wrong answer will offer elevation for the aircraft, or height for an altimeter reading on QNH.

Question. You fly at 3,000 ft on QNH from the coast to an aerodrome of elevation 1,000 ft. What happens to your altitude, and to your height?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: your altitude stays at 3,000 ft, because it is measured from sea level and the altimeter reading has not changed. But your height falls, from about 3,000 ft over the beach to 2,000 ft over the aerodrome, because the ground has risen 1,000 ft.

## The other units, one at a time

The second part of the subtopic is a list of six quantities, each with its unit. Rather than memorise the list, attach each one to the place you meet it. We'll take them in order: runways, temperature, pressure, weight, liquids, and visibility.

First, runway dimensions, in metres. Runway lengths and widths are in metres. ERSA lists each runway in a form like runway {12/30|one two, three zero}, 1,400 m long by 30 m wide. Runways were surveyed and built under the metric system. And the take-off and landing charts in your POH, and in the CASA workbook, compare the distance you need, in metres, with the distance available. A useful size: 1,000 m is about 3,300 ft, a little over half a nautical mile.

Second, temperature, in degrees Celsius. Air temperature, dew point and engine temperatures in Australian reports are all in degrees Celsius. The METAR shows something like {28/12|twenty-eight, twelve}: temperature 28, dew point 12. The International Standard Atmosphere, which every performance calculation is built on, starts at 15 degrees Celsius at sea level. Some imported aeroplanes have engine gauges in Fahrenheit. But the weather is always Celsius.

The two temperature numbers in a METAR earn their place. The air temperature drives density, and therefore take-off performance. And the gap between the temperature and the dew point tells you how close the air is to forming fog or cloud, and how likely carburettor ice is.

Third, atmospheric pressure, in hectopascals. You will hear QNH {1018|ten eighteen}. A pascal is a tiny unit of pressure, and a hectopascal is a hundred of them. One hectopascal equals one millibar. So older books that talk about millibars use exactly the same numbers. Standard sea-level pressure is {1013.2 hPa|ten thirteen point two hectopascals}, often rounded to {1013|ten thirteen}. Some imported aircraft have altimeters with a second subscale in inches of mercury. In Australia, you set hectopascals.

Fourth, weight, in kilograms. Aircraft weights, the maximum take-off weight, passenger and baggage weights, and the weight and balance are all in kilograms. Maximum take-off weight 1,111 kg, for example. Strictly, a kilogram measures mass, not weight. But aviation uses weight for both, and the exam does too. Weight is one of the numbers that moves everything else. The take-off distance, the climb rate and the stall speed all rise with it. That is why every kilogram in the cabin and the tanks is accounted for.

Fifth, volume of liquids, in litres. Fuel and oil are loaded and measured by volume, in litres. Fuel on board 140 L, for example. The bowser meter reads litres, the fuel gauges are calibrated in litres, and fuel consumption is quoted in litres per hour.

And sixth, visibility, in metres and kilometres. Visibility is reported in metres for lower values, and in kilometres for higher ones. Visibility 3,000 m. Visibility 10 km. That matches the lesson on distance, speed and velocity. Visibility is the odd one out from navigation distance, which is in nautical miles. So when you compare a visibility with a distance on the chart, convert. 1 km is 0.54 NM.

Let's work one. One report says visibility 3,500 m. Another says 5 km. What are those in nautical miles? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
3,500 m is 3.5 km. 3.5 times 0.54 is 1.9 NM. And 5 km, times 0.54, is 2.7 NM. So in 5 km visibility, you will see a town on your track only once you are less than 3 NM from it. That is a minute and a half at 120 kt.

Question. Why is 1 hectopascal the same as 1 millibar, and what does that mean for an old textbook that gives a QNH of {1015|ten fifteen} millibars?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the hectopascal and the millibar are the same size of unit under different names, so the number does not change. {1015|Ten fifteen} millibars is {1015|ten fifteen} hectopascals, and you set {1015|ten fifteen} on the subscale.

## Litres and kilograms: converting fuel

Here two of the units have to talk to each other. The weight and balance is done in kilograms, but fuel goes in by the litre. To put fuel into a weight calculation, you need the weight of one litre.

That comes from the fuel's specific gravity: how heavy the liquid is compared with the same volume of water. Water weighs 1 kg per litre. For avgas, the specific gravity is about 0.72. So a litre of avgas weighs about 0.72 kg, and 100 L of avgas weighs about 72 kg. Check the figure your flight manual uses, because the specific gravity changes a little with temperature and fuel grade.

Getting this conversion wrong is not a paper error. In {1983|nineteen eighty-three}, an airliner in Canada ran out of fuel in flight. The fuel load had been worked out in pounds, where the aircraft's systems expected kilograms. It carried less than half the fuel the crew believed. The same kind of slip in a light aeroplane, litres treated as kilograms, either overloads the aeroplane on paper or leaves the tanks short.

The conversion is one multiplication, or one division. Kilograms equals litres times the specific gravity. And litres equals kilograms divided by the specific gravity.

[[diagram:units-conversion-ladder]]
Picture three pairs of rulers laid side by side, so you can read straight across from one unit to the other. Distance: 1 nautical mile lines up with 1.852 km. Speed: 110 kt lines up with 204 km/h. And fuel: avgas weighs about 0.72 kg per litre, so 100 L lines up with about 72 kg, and 120 L with 86.4 kg. On the fuel ruler, the kilograms are always the smaller number.

Let's work one. You have 120 L of avgas on board, at a specific gravity of 0.72. Later, you fill the tanks to 180 L. What do those weigh? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
120 L times 0.72 is 86.4 kg. And 180 L times 0.72 is {129.6|one hundred and twenty-nine point six} kg. That is the number that goes into the weight and balance. Entering 180 in the kilograms column would overstate the fuel by about 50 kg.

Now the other way. The weight and balance shows you can carry only 90 kg of fuel with your passengers on board. The specific gravity is 0.72. How many litres do you ask for?
[[pause 6]]
90 divided by 0.72 is 125 L. So you tell the refueller 125 litres. For avgas, the litres are always the bigger number, because a litre weighs less than a kilogram.

[[widget:fuel-litres-to-kg]]
Imagine a converter with litres on one side and kilograms on the other. Whatever you put in, the kilograms of avgas come out at about three-quarters of the litres. Multiply litres by 0.72. Divide kilograms by 0.72.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Multiply litres by the specific gravity to get kilograms. Divide kilograms by the specific gravity to get litres. If your answer in kilograms is bigger than the litres you started with, you divided when you should have multiplied.

Question. Why does fuel need converting at all, before it goes into the weight and balance?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because the weight and balance is in kilograms, but fuel is loaded and measured by volume, in litres. The specific gravity, about 0.72 kg per litre for avgas, turns one into the other.

## Bringing it together

You are planning a solo flight from Jandakot to an inland strip of elevation 1,100 ft. ERSA lists its runway as 1,200 m long. That is metres.

The METAR at the nearest reporting station gives {32/08|thirty-two, zero eight}. That is 32 degrees Celsius, a hot day, which will lengthen your take-off run. The QNH is {1009 hPa|ten oh nine hectopascals}, which you will set so your altimeter reads altitude.

The TAF gives broken cloud at 3,000 ft. That is above the aerodrome, so the base is at about 4,100 ft above mean sea level. A cruise at 3,000 ft keeps you more than 1,000 ft below it.

You will fly the circuit at the strip at 1,100 plus 1,000, which is 2,100 ft on QNH. You ask for 120 L of avgas. At a specific gravity of 0.72, that is 86.4 kg in the weight and balance, comfortably under the maximum take-off weight in kilograms. The forecast visibility is 8 km, about 4.3 NM.

Eight quantities, eight units, each in its own place.

## Key points

Let's recap.

Vertical distance is in feet. Altitude is the aircraft above mean sea level, read on the altimeter with QNH set. Elevation is a point on the ground, above mean sea level. Height is the aircraft above a datum, usually the ground. Altitude equals elevation plus height. And a flight level is the altitude on {1013.2 hPa|ten thirteen point two hectopascals}, in hundreds of feet.

The other units. Runway dimensions in metres. Temperature in degrees Celsius. Pressure in hectopascals, where 1 hectopascal is 1 millibar, and standard is {1013.2|ten thirteen point two}. Weight in kilograms. Fuel and oil in litres. And visibility in metres and kilometres.

Avgas weighs about 0.72 kg per litre. Multiply litres by the specific gravity for kilograms. Divide kilograms by the specific gravity for litres.

A final exam tip. Here is the split to remember. Feet for anything vertical. Metres for runways and visibility. Nautical miles for navigation distance. Knots for speed. Hectopascals for pressure. Kilograms for weight. Litres for fuel and oil. And degrees Celsius for temperature. Expect one question on the three vertical words, where the aircraft has altitude or height, and the ground has elevation. Expect a subtraction of elevation from altitude. And expect a fuel conversion with a specific gravity of 0.72.

That's the end of BAKC 2.4.
