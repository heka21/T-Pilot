This is BAKC 6.3, Speed limitations. By the end you will be able to explain load factor and the limit and design loads, define the manoeuvring speed, the turbulence penetration speed, the normal operating speed and the never exceed speed, say why the manoeuvring speed falls with weight, and recognise the situations that push an aeroplane past its limits. There are worked examples and questions along the way, so pause whenever you want a moment to think.

## What the exam expects (BAKC 6.3)

The exam wants two things from you. First, the speed and structural limits: the normal operating speed, VNO; the never exceed speed, VNE; the manoeuvring speed, VA; the turbulence penetration speed, VB; and the limit and design load factors. And second, the situations in which an aeroplane can be pushed past its speed or load factor limits.

## Why this matters

On a hot afternoon, descending from the hills towards Jandakot, the air over the Darling Scarp is often rough. And the aeroplane, nose down with cruise power still on, accelerates into the yellow arc almost without you noticing.

Nothing breaks. But you are now flying in a region where a sharp gust could load the structure past what it was designed for. And the protection the designers built in depends on you knowing where that region starts.

Speed limits are the line between flight the aeroplane was built for, and flight it was merely not tested beyond. The exam asks you to define each V-speed, to know the load factors for the normal category, to explain why VA falls with weight, and to recognise the situations that break limits. All of those follow from one idea: lift and gust loads grow with speed. So this lesson starts there.

## What actually breaks an aeroplane: load factor

The wing has to carry the weight of the aeroplane. In straight and level flight, lift equals weight, so the wing carries exactly one weight's worth of load.

Whenever the flight path curves upward, the wing has to make more lift than the weight. That happens in a pull-up, a level turn, or a sharp upward gust. And then the spar, the wing attachments, and everything bolted to them carry that extra load.

The load factor is the ratio of lift to weight. Load factor equals lift divided by weight. It is measured in g. Level flight is 1 g. At 2 g, the wing is making twice the weight in lift, and everything in the aeroplane, including you, feels twice as heavy. A load factor can also be negative, when the wing pushes downward, as in a sharp push-over.

The most common way to raise the load factor is a level turn. Banking tilts the lift. To keep the vertical part of it equal to the weight, the total lift must grow. You'll find that in the lesson on turning. The result depends only on the bank angle. The load factor equals one over the cosine of the bank angle.

[[widget:bank-angle]]
Picture the load factor climbing as the bank steepens. It creeps up slowly at first, to 1.41 at 45 degrees. It doubles by 60 degrees. And it reaches the normal category limit near 75 degrees. The stall speed rises too, with the square root of the load factor.

Let's work one. What is the load factor in a level turn at 45 degrees of bank, and at 60 degrees? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
At 45 degrees, the cosine is 0.707. So the load factor is 1 divided by 0.707, which is 1.41 g.

At 60 degrees, the cosine is 0.5. So the load factor is 1 divided by 0.5, which is 2.0 g.

So going from 45 to 60 degrees adds 15 degrees of bank, and 0.59 g. And the curve steepens fast beyond 60. The answers again: 1.41 g at 45 degrees, and 2 g at 60. A 60 degree steep turn, which you will fly in training, already puts twice the aeroplane's weight on the wing.

## Limit and design load factors

The designers cannot make an aeroplane infinitely strong, because strength costs weight. So they choose the load the structure must carry in service, and build in a margin above it.

The limit load factor is the maximum the aeroplane is designed to take in service without permanent deformation. Up to the limit load, the structure flexes and springs back.

For a light aeroplane, the limit depends on the category it is certified in. And that depends on what it is built to do. There are three categories, and the pattern is that each step up is stronger.

The normal category has a positive limit of plus 3.8 g, and a negative limit of minus 1.52 g. The utility category has plus 4.4 g, and minus 1.76 g. And the aerobatic category has plus 6.0 g, and minus 3.0 g.

The design load factor, also called the ultimate load factor, is the limit load multiplied by a safety factor of 1.5. It is the load at which the structure may fail. So the ultimate loads are 5.7 g for normal, 6.6 g for utility, and 9.0 g for aerobatic.

Between the limit and the ultimate load, the structure is not expected to break. But it bends permanently. A wing spar can take a set. Rivets can pull. And the aeroplane is damaged, even though it landed. Beyond the ultimate load, it breaks.

[[diagram:load-factor-limits]]
Picture a vertical scale of load factor. For the normal category, the limit sits at plus 3.8 g and minus 1.52 g. Above the limit, a band runs up to the ultimate load, 5.7 g, where the structure bends permanently. Beyond 5.7 g, it breaks. Low on the scale, a 60 degree turn sits at 2 g, and a 70 degree turn at 2.9 g. So a 60 degree turn already uses 2 of the normal category's 3.8 g.

The safety factor is not spare strength for you to use. It covers the things the design cannot control exactly. Variations in materials and manufacture. Ageing, corrosion and fatigue. An aeroplane that has been taken past its limit load once may be weaker the next time. That is why an over-stress must be reported and inspected. You'll find that in the lesson on structural damage.

Let's work one. A utility category aeroplane has a limit load factor of plus 4.4 g, and a safety factor of 1.5. What is its ultimate load factor, and how wide is the band where the structure bends permanently?
[[pause 6]]
First, the ultimate load factor. 4.4 times 1.5 is 6.6 g.

Second, the margin between limit and ultimate. 6.6 minus 4.4 is 2.2 g. That is the band in which the structure bends permanently.

So the answer: an ultimate load of 6.6 g, and a 2.2 g band of permanent bending. Many training aeroplanes are certified in both categories, utility at reduced weight. Loaded heavily into the normal category, the same aeroplane's limit drops to 3.8 g.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. The limit load is the one you must not exceed in flight. Not the ultimate load. Reaching 4 g in a normal category aeroplane does not break it. But it has gone past the 3.8 g limit, and it may now be permanently deformed. It didn't break is not the same as it is undamaged.

Question. In a normal category aeroplane, what happens to the structure at 3 g, at 5 g, and at 6 g?
[[pause 5]]
The answer: at 3 g it is within the 3.8 g limit, and it flexes elastically. At 5 g it is past the limit but below the 5.7 g ultimate load, so it may be permanently bent. And at 6 g it is past the ultimate load, and it may fail.

## The manoeuvring speed, V<sub>A</sub>

Here is the most useful idea in the subtopic.

A wing can only make so much lift at a given speed. Pull back hard, and the angle of attack rises until the wing reaches its critical angle, and stalls. After that, the lift stops rising. You'll find that in the lesson on stalling, spinning and spiral dives.

The maximum lift available grows with the square of the airspeed. So the maximum load factor the wing can produce before it stalls also grows with the square of the speed. The maximum load factor equals the airspeed divided by the 1 g stall speed, all squared. At the stall speed, the wing can make just 1 g. At twice the stall speed, it can make 4 g before it stalls.

So at low speed, the stall protects the structure. However hard you pull, the wing stalls before it can make enough lift to overload anything. At high speed, it does not. The wing can make more lift than the structure can carry, and a hard pull reaches the limit load before the stall.

The crossover is the manoeuvring speed, VA. It is the highest speed at which full, abrupt control deflection will stall the wing before the limit load factor is reached. Below VA, a full control input stalls the wing first. Above VA, a full control input, or a sharp gust, can overstress the airframe.

Set the maximum load factor equal to the limit load, and you get VA directly. VA equals the stall speed, times the square root of the limit load factor.

[[diagram:v-n-diagram]]
Picture a graph, with airspeed along the bottom and load factor up the side. This is the V-n diagram, for a normal category aeroplane with a 52 knot stall speed. On the left, a curved line rises from the stall speed, getting steeper as it goes. That is the stall: the most load factor the wing can make at each speed. Across the top runs a flat line at plus 3.8 g, the limit. The curve meets the flat line at VA, about 101 knots. Below the axis, the negative limit is minus 1.52 g. And further right along the speed scale are VNO and then VNE.

Inside that envelope, the aeroplane is safe. Above the curve, it is stalled. Above the flat top, it is overstressed. So for an aeroplane that stalls at 52 kt, VA is 52 times the square root of 3.8. The square root of 3.8 is 1.95. And 52 times 1.95 is about 101 kt.

Now the part that surprises people. VA is lower at lower weight. So take it slowly.

A lighter aeroplane stalls at a lower speed, because it needs less lift to support it. So its stall curve on the V-n diagram sits further left. And it meets the 3.8 g line at a lower speed.

Put another way. At any given speed, a lighter aeroplane can be pulled to a higher load factor before the wing stalls, because the same maximum lift is shared over less weight. So it reaches the limit load factor at a lower speed.

Stall speed varies with the square root of weight, and so does VA. The new VA equals the VA at maximum weight, times the square root of the actual weight divided by the maximum weight.

[[diagram:va-vs-weight]]
Picture the same graph with two stall curves. The right-hand one is the aeroplane at maximum weight, stalling at 52 kt. It meets the 3.8 g line at about 101 kt. The left-hand one is the same aeroplane at 80 percent of maximum weight. It stalls at a lower speed, so its curve sits to the left. And it meets 3.8 g at about 90 kt. Between 90 and 101 kt, a full pull stalls the heavy aeroplane first, but can overstress the light one.

Let's work one. The POH gives VA as 100 kt, at the maximum weight of 1,050 kg. Today you are flying solo with part fuel, at 840 kg. What is your manoeuvring speed? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 8]]
Step one, the weight ratio. 840 divided by 1,050 is 0.80.

Step two, the square root. The square root of 0.80 is 0.894.

Step three, VA at 840 kg. 100 times 0.894 is 89 kt.

So your VA is 89 kt. Solo and light, your protection from a full control input runs out 11 kt earlier than the placard says. Many POHs give VA at more than one weight for exactly this reason. Use the one for the weight you are at, or the lower one.

Here is one for you to try. A normal category aeroplane stalls at 48 kt at its present weight. What is its manoeuvring speed?
[[pause 8]]
The answer: VA equals the stall speed times the square root of 3.8. That is 48 times 1.949, which is 93.6. So about 94 kt. At or below about 94 kt, a full, abrupt elevator input will stall the wing before it reaches 3.8 g. Above it, the same input could overstress the structure.

Two more points about VA. First, it is not marked on the airspeed indicator. It is in the POH limitations section, and often on a placard.

Second, VA protects you against a single full deflection, in one axis. Full deflections in more than one axis at once, or rapid reversals of a control, can overload parts of the structure even below VA.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. VA decreases as weight decreases. The tempting answer is the opposite, because a lighter aeroplane feels as though it has strength to spare. It has the same structure. But its wing can make the limit load at a lower speed, because it needs less lift for each g.

Question. Why does flying below the manoeuvring speed protect the structure from a full, abrupt pull on the control column?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because below VA, the wing reaches its critical angle and stalls before it can make enough lift to reach the limit load factor. The stall caps the load. Above VA, the wing can generate the limit load before it stalls.

## Gusts, V<sub>NO</sub> and V<sub>B</sub>

You do not have to pull the control column to load the structure. The air can do it for you.

Picture the aeroplane cruising level when it flies into a rising current of air. For an instant, the air is coming at the wing from slightly below, as well as from ahead. So the angle of attack jumps, and so does the lift.

How much? The extra angle depends on the gust speed compared with your airspeed. And the lift that angle produces depends on the square of your airspeed. Put the two together, and the extra load from a given gust grows in proportion to your airspeed. Fly through the same bump twice as fast, and it hits the structure twice as hard.

[[diagram:gust-load-vs-airspeed]]
Picture the V-n diagram again, with straight gust lines fanning out to the right from 1 g at zero speed. Each line rises in proportion to airspeed. An example gust that adds 1.0 g at 100 kt, making 2.0 g in all, adds 1.3 g at 130 kt, making 2.3 g. The strongest design gust line stays inside the plus 3.8 g limit up to VNO, and crosses it just beyond VNO. At low speed, the same gust line runs into the stall curve first. There, the wing stalls momentarily, instead of overloading the structure. The speeds on the picture are schematic.

That is why the aeroplane has a speed above which it should only be flown in smooth air. The normal operating speed, VNO, is also called the maximum structural cruising speed. It is the top of the green arc on the airspeed indicator. Up to VNO, the structure can take the design gust loads. Those are the strongest gusts the designers assumed you would meet in turbulence.

So do not exceed VNO except in smooth air, and then only with caution. Between VNO and VNE is the yellow arc, the caution range, for smooth air only.

The turbulence penetration speed is VB. Strictly, it is the design speed for maximum gust intensity. It is the recommended speed in rough air. It is low enough that a strong gust stalls the wing momentarily, rather than overloading the structure. And it is high enough to leave a margin above the stall, so the aeroplane remains controllable in the bumps.

VB is often not published for light aeroplanes. In that case, the POH gives a turbulence penetration speed near VA. And that is the speed you slow to.

## The never exceed speed, V<sub>NE</sub>

At the top of the scale is the red line, the never exceed speed, VNE. Beyond it, structural failure, flutter, or control problems are possible.

Flutter is a vibration in which a control surface or a wing begins to oscillate, fed by the airflow itself. It grows until something breaks, sometimes within seconds. The designers have shown that the aeroplane is free of it up to a speed above VNE, with a margin. Above the red line, that margin is gone.

VNE is a limit, not a target. The yellow arc below it is for smooth air. And the red line is never crossed, in any air.

[[diagram:asi-colour-arcs]]
Picture the airspeed indicator dial, with its coloured arcs in order of speed. First a white arc, from the full-flap stall speed up to VFE, the maximum flap extended speed. That is the flap operating range. Then a green arc, from the clean stall speed up to VNO. That is the normal operating range. Then a yellow arc, from VNO up to VNE, for caution, smooth air only. And at the end, a red line at VNE. Notice what is missing. VA is not marked on the dial.

So there are four speeds to hold together. VA, the manoeuvring speed, is not marked on the airspeed indicator. A full, abrupt control deflection below it stalls the wing before the limit load, and it is lower at lower weight. VB, the turbulence penetration speed, is not marked either. It is the recommended rough-air speed, near VA if it is not published. VNO, the normal operating speed, is the top of the green arc. It protects against the design gust loads, and above it is smooth air only. And VNE, the never exceed speed, is the red line. Beyond it lie structural failure, flutter and control problems.

Question. You are cruising in the yellow arc when the air becomes bumpy. What do you do, and why does it help?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: reduce power, and slow to the turbulence penetration speed, well below VNO. That is VB, or the POH speed near VA. The load a gust puts on the structure is proportional to airspeed, so slowing reduces every bump's load. And at VA or below, a strong gust will stall the wing momentarily, rather than overstress it.

## How the limits get exceeded

Every way of breaking these limits is a version of one of two things. Going too fast. Or pulling too hard at speed. Knowing the situations lets you see them coming. There are seven.

The first is spiral dives, and other nose-low upsets. In a spiral dive, the wing is not stalled. The aeroplane is in a steepening, descending turn, with the nose low. The speed rises quickly through VNO towards VNE. And the bank makes the natural instinct, pulling back, tighten the spiral, adding load factor on top of the excess speed.

The recovery order matters for exactly that reason. Close the throttle. Roll the wings level. Then ease out. Rolling level first points the lift upward, so the pull to recover is not adding to a turn.

[[diagram:spin-vs-spiral-dive]]
Picture two columns, a spin on the left and a spiral dive on the right. In the spin, both wings are stalled. The airspeed is low and roughly steady, and the rate of descent is high. You recover with the throttle closed, ailerons neutral, full opposite rudder, and the control column forward until the rotation stops. Then you centralise the rudder and ease out. In the spiral dive, the wings are not stalled. The airspeed is high and increasing, and the rate of descent is very high. You recover by closing the throttle, rolling level with aileron and coordinated rudder, easing out gently, then restoring power. The airspeed is the tell: low in the spin, high and rising in the spiral.

The second situation is steep turns at high speed, and rapid pull-ups. That includes a harsh recovery from a dive, or from an accelerated stall. Each adds load factor by curving the flight path sharply. Above VA, nothing stops that reaching the limit.

The third is turbulence above VA. A vertical gust adds angle of attack and load factor in proportion to the speed. So the faster you are, the harder each bump. And above VNO, the design gust loads are no longer covered.

The fourth is flying in the yellow arc in anything but smooth air. The yellow arc is legal, but only for smooth air. A descent into turbulent air at a yellow-arc speed is the classic way to meet a gust bigger than the structure was designed for at that speed.

The fifth is flap extended above VFE, or operated above VFO, and retractable gear above its limits. Flaps and undercarriage are built for lower speeds than the airframe. Their limits are separate speed limits in the POH.

[[diagram:flap-speeds-vfo-vfe]]
Picture a speed scale with the white arc on it. The arc starts at VSO, the full-flap stall speed at maximum weight, and runs up to VFE, the maximum speed with flap extended. On some aeroplanes there is one more mark, below VFE. That is VFO, the maximum speed for moving the flap. So slow to VFO before selecting flap. Then VFE is the limit for flying with it out.

The sixth is descending with high power, and not watching the airspeed. Lowering the nose with cruise power set turns height into speed, on top of the engine's thrust. It is the easiest way to drift through VNO without noticing. Especially on a long descent into Jandakot from the hills.

And the seventh is over-controlling above VA. For example, a full aileron and rudder input in a wake turbulence encounter. The instinct to fight an unexpected roll with everything you have is exactly the full deflection that VA protects against below it. Above it, that can overstress the structure.

The defences follow from the causes. Slow to VA, or the turbulence penetration speed, in rough air. Recover from any nose-low attitude by rolling level and reducing power before easing out. Keep the speed in the green arc unless the air is smooth. Plan descents with reduced power. And use the controls smoothly at speed.

Question. In a spiral dive, why do you roll the wings level before easing out of the dive?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because while the aeroplane is banked, back pressure tightens the turn and adds load factor without raising the nose much. And that is on top of a speed that is already high. Rolling level first points the lift upward, so the pull-out does the job with the least load factor. And closing the throttle stops the speed building further.

## Bringing it together

You are returning from Northam, solo and light, at 840 kg. Over the scarp the air is rough. And you are descending towards Jandakot with cruise power still set.

The speed creeps up through the top of the green arc, VNO, and into the yellow. That is a range built for smooth air only. Each bump now loads the structure in proportion to your speed.

Your POH placard gives VA at maximum weight. But at your light weight, it is about 11 kt lower. So you are further above it than the placard suggests. A sharp gust, or a reflex full control input, could reach the 3.8 g limit before the wing stalls. Past 3.8 g, the structure may bend permanently. Past 5.7 g, it may fail.

The fix is the one the lesson leads to. You reduce power, raise the nose gently, and slow to the turbulence penetration speed for your weight. Then you continue the descent at a lower rate. The bumps feel the same. But the loads they put on the wing are much smaller.

## Key points

Let's recap.

Load factor is lift divided by weight, in g. A level turn gives one over the cosine of the bank angle. That is 2 g at 60 degrees.

The limit load is the most the structure takes without permanent deformation. Normal category, plus 3.8 and minus 1.52 g. Utility, plus 4.4 and minus 1.76. Aerobatic, plus 6.0 and minus 3.0. The design, or ultimate, load is 1.5 times the limit, which is 5.7 g for the normal category. That is where the structure may fail.

VA, the manoeuvring speed. A full, abrupt deflection below it stalls the wing before the limit load. It is not on the airspeed indicator. And it is lower at lower weight.

VB is the rough-air penetration speed, near VA if it is not published. VNO is the top of the green arc, covering the design gust loads. Above it, smooth air only. And VNE is the red line. Beyond it, flutter, structural failure and control problems.

The limits are broken by seven things. Spiral dives and nose-low upsets. Steep turns and harsh pull-ups at speed. Turbulence above VA. The yellow arc in rough air. Flap or gear above their limits. Power-on descents without watching the speed. And over-controlling above VA. So slow down in turbulence. And roll level and reduce power before easing out.

A final exam tip. VA decreases as weight decreases. Candidates often guess the opposite. The reason is that a lighter aeroplane generates the same limit load at a lower speed, because it needs less lift to reach the same g. Have these by heart. Normal category, plus 3.8 g and minus 1.52 g. Ultimate, 1.5 times the limit. 2 g in a 60 degree turn. VNO at the top of the green arc. VNE, the red line. And VA, not marked on the dial.
