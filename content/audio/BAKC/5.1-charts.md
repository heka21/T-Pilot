This is BAKC 5.1, Charts. By the end you will be able to name the main features printed on the visual charts, read the three chart scales, and say which charts show controlled airspace and restricted areas, and which do not. There are a few questions and a worked example along the way, so pause whenever you want a moment to think.

## What the exam expects (BAKC 5.1)

The exam wants two things from you. First, to pick out and name the main features printed on the visual charts: relief, water, towns and transport, obstacles, aeronautical information, and the grid of latitude and longitude. And second, to say which charts show controlled airspace and prohibited, restricted and danger areas, and which do not.

## Why this matters

On your first navigation exercise out of Jandakot, you will have a chart folded on your knee. Everything you see through the windscreen has to be matched against something printed on it. The Avon River at Northam. The railway running east to Merredin. The power line that crosses your track. The hill with a spot height of 1,800-odd feet.

Before that, at the planning table, the same charts tell you where the Perth Class C steps start, where the restricted areas sit, and which frequency to be on.

Choose the wrong chart, and you can navigate perfectly, straight into controlled airspace, without knowing it was there. That is because one of the most-used charts shows no airspace at all.

The exam tests two things here. Whether you recognise the symbols, and whether you know which chart carries airspace. This lesson explains what each chart is for, why it shows what it shows, and how to read its features.

## A family of charts, each drawn for a job

Every chart is a compromise between coverage and detail. A sheet of paper is only so big. So a chart that covers half of Western Australia cannot also show every farm track. And a chart that shows every farm track around Perth cannot reach Kalgoorlie.

The compromise is fixed by the scale. That is the ratio between a distance on the chart and the same distance on the ground.

At {1:1,000,000|one to one million}, one centimetre on the chart is a million centimetres on the ground, which is 10 km. At {1:500,000|one to five hundred thousand}, one centimetre is 5 km. And at {1:250,000|one to two hundred and fifty thousand}, one centimetre is 2.5 km.

A larger second number means a smaller-scale chart. More country on each sheet, and less detail on each square centimetre. Keep that inverse relationship clear, because large scale and small scale are easy to swap. The {1:250,000|one to two hundred and fifty thousand} chart is the large-scale one. It shows a small area in a lot of detail.

[[diagram:chart-scale-comparison]]
Picture two panels. On the left, the same 10 kilometres on the ground drawn on three charts. It is 1 centimetre on the WAC, 2 centimetres on the VNC, and 4 centimetres on the VTC. On the right, three sheets of the same size. At each step from WAC to VNC to VTC, the sheet covers a quarter of the area. So the smaller the number after the colon, the larger the scale. More detail, and less country.

Airservices Australia publishes the charts you will use. Each one exists for a job. There are six to know.

The first is the WAC, the World Aeronautical Chart, at {1:1,000,000|one to one million}. It is a topographic chart. It shows terrain, towns, roads, railways, rivers, aerodromes, obstacles, the magnetic variation, and the latitude and longitude grid. And it shows no airspace. Its job is visual navigation over long distances. It is the chart the RPLA and PPLA exams are built around, because it covers the whole country in a consistent way. It is the one you measure tracks and distances on.

The second is the VNC, the Visual Navigation Chart, at {1:500,000|one to five hundred thousand}. It has twice the detail of the WAC. And it adds what the WAC leaves out. Controlled airspace, with its lower and upper limits. PRD areas, meaning prohibited, restricted and danger areas. Frequencies. And reporting points. VNCs cover the capital cities and the busy regions around them. There, the airspace is complicated enough that a VFR pilot needs both the ground and the airspace on one sheet.

The third is the VTC, the Visual Terminal Chart, at {1:250,000|one to two hundred and fifty thousand}. It is the most detailed of the visual charts. It is drawn around major aerodromes such as Perth and Jandakot. It shows detailed topography, together with the CTA steps, the control zones, PRD areas, VFR routes and lanes of entry, reporting points and frequencies. CTA is short for controlled airspace, and the CTA steps are its layers around the airport. The VTC is the chart you fly with in and out of the Perth basin, because the airspace changes every few miles.

The fourth is the ERC-L, the En Route Chart Low, at various scales. It is built for IFR pilots, those flying by instruments. It shows air routes, navigation aids, frequencies, airspace and PRD areas. And it shows no topography. A VFR pilot uses it as the authoritative picture of airspace boundaries en route, outside the areas the VNC and VTC cover.

The fifth is the PCA, the Planning Chart Australia. It is a small-scale chart of the whole country. It shows which WAC covers which area, the forecast area boundaries, time zones, and summary PRD information. You plan with it, and choose your other charts from it. You do not navigate with it.

And the sixth is the TAC, the Terminal Area Chart, at various scales. It shows the airspace detail in the terminal areas. It is mostly an IFR chart, but it does show CTA boundaries.

[[diagram:chart-features-panel]]
Picture four chart panels side by side. The first, the WAC, has topography, towns, roads, railways, rivers and aerodromes, and no airspace. The second, the VNC, has the topography plus controlled airspace, PRD areas, frequencies and reporting points. The third, the VTC, has detailed topography with control zones, CTA steps, PRD areas, VFR routes and frequencies. And the fourth, the ERC-L, has airspace, air routes, navigation aids, frequencies and PRD areas, with no topography at all.

The pattern is worth seeing as one idea. The visual charts drawn for the busy places, the VNC and the VTC, carry both the ground and the airspace. The chart drawn for long distances, the WAC, carries only the ground. And the chart drawn for IFR, the ERC-L, carries only the airspace.

ERSA, the En Route Supplement Australia, is a book rather than a chart. But it goes with them. It carries the aerodrome entries, and the PRD listings that explain what the charts show. You'll find it in the lesson on documentation.

So, the scales once more. The WAC is {1:1,000,000|one to one million}: ground but no airspace, for long-distance navigation and the exam. The VNC is {1:500,000|one to five hundred thousand}: ground and airspace. The VTC is {1:250,000|one to two hundred and fifty thousand}: detailed ground and airspace, with VFR routes. The ERC-L and the TAC come in various scales. The PCA is small scale, with outlines only, for planning.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Large scale means a large ratio of chart to ground. And that is the small second number. The {1:250,000|one to two hundred and fifty thousand} VTC is the large-scale chart, with the most detail. The {1:1,000,000|one to one million} WAC is the small-scale chart, with the least. So if a question asks which chart shows the most detail, look for the smallest number after the colon.

Question. On a {1:500,000|one to five hundred thousand} VNC, how far on the ground is 4 cm on the chart? And is a VNC larger or smaller in scale than a WAC?
[[pause 6]]
The answer: at {1:500,000|one to five hundred thousand}, one centimetre is 5 km, so 4 cm is 20 km. And the VNC is the larger scale of the two. It is half the ratio of the WAC, so it has twice the detail, over a quarter of the area per sheet.

## Reading the ground: relief and water

Most of what you navigate by is the shape of the land, and the water on it. So the chart spends most of its ink there.

Relief is how the chart shows height. Three devices work together.

The first is contour lines. They join points of equal elevation. Where they crowd together, the ground is steep. Where they spread apart, it is gentle.

The second is hypsometric tints. These are the colour bands between the contours. They darken, or change hue, with height. So you can see at a glance where the high ground is, without reading a single number.

The third is spot heights. A spot height is a dot with a number beside it. It is the surveyed elevation of a hilltop or other point, in feet above mean sea level.

On the VNC and the VTC, there is one more. A large maximum elevation figure is printed in each grid square. It is the height of the highest terrain or obstacle in that square, rounded up with a margin. With it, you can pick a safe altitude for a whole area without hunting for every spot height.

Why give you three ways of showing the same thing? Because each answers a different question. Tints answer, where is it high? Contours answer, how steep, and which way? And spot heights and maximum elevation figures answer, how high exactly? When you plan a route along the Darling Scarp, you use all three.

Then there is hydrography, which is the water. Coastlines, rivers and lakes are blue. A river that does not always run is an intermittent river, and it is drawn with a dashed line. The same convention applies to intermittent lakes and salt lakes. They are drawn differently from permanent ones.

This matters in Western Australia more than almost anywhere. In February, most of the lakes in the Wheatbelt are white salt pans, and most of the rivers are dry beds. The chart is telling you that by the way it draws them. And a dry salt lake is still an excellent landmark, often better than a permanent one, because its shape is distinctive and its colour stands out.

Question. A blue line on the WAC is drawn dashed rather than solid. What is it telling you, and does it still make a useful landmark?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: it is an intermittent watercourse, one that does not always flow. It may be a dry bed on the day. So look for the line of vegetation, or the shape of the bed, rather than water. It is still a usable feature, but less conspicuous than a permanent river.

## Reading the ground: what people have built

Culture is the chart's word for human-made features.

Towns and cities are yellow areas or circles, and the symbol grows with the size of the place. A built-up area like Perth is drawn to its real shape. A small town is a circle.

Roads are red or brown, depending on their class. Railways are black lines with cross ticks along them. That pattern is easy to recognise, and it is good to know, because a railway and a road often run side by side out of a Wheatbelt town. Power lines are drawn as lines with their own symbol. And pipelines, prominent buildings, mines, towers and masts each have a symbol of their own.

Linear features are the visual navigator's best friend. A road, a railway, a power line or a pipeline gives you a line to cross at a time you can predict, and that checks your groundspeed. Or it gives you a line to follow, and that checks your track. So before you fly, find the features that cross your track at right angles, and the ones that run along it.

Obstacles are tall structures that could hit you. Masts, towers, chimneys and wind turbines. The obstacle symbol carries two figures. The height above ground, and the elevation of the top above sea level. You need both.

The elevation is what you compare with your altimeter set to QNH, the local sea-level pressure setting, because the altimeter then reads height above sea level. The height above ground tells you how much the obstacle stands out from the land around it. And a lit obstacle has a flash symbol beside it.

Picture a sheet of sample symbols, like a chart legend. Contour lines and hypsometric tints. A spot height. Rivers, with the intermittent ones dashed. Lakes, towns and roads. Railways with their cross ticks. Power lines. Obstacles with their two figures, height above ground and elevation, and a lit obstacle with its flash. Aerodromes. Isogonals, as dashed lines labelled with the variation. And the latitude and longitude graticule. Notice that nowhere on a WAC does airspace appear.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. The figure to compare with your altimeter is the obstacle's elevation above mean sea level, not its height above the ground. A 150 ft mast on a 1,200 ft ridge reaches about 1,350 ft on your QNH altimeter. The way out is to read the legend of the chart you are using, to see which of the two figures is which.

## Reading the aeronautical information

Over the ground picture, the chart prints what a pilot needs that a road map does not.

Aerodromes have symbols that tell you whether they are civil or military, and whether their runways are sealed. Navigation aids have their own symbols, with an identifier and a frequency. Reporting points are named places where you report your position. And on the VTC, VFR routes and lanes of entry show the paths you are expected to follow in and out of the terminal area.

Isogonals are dashed lines joining places of equal magnetic variation. Magnetic variation is the angle between true north and magnetic north. Each isogonal is labelled with the variation in degrees, east or west.

Why do they exist? Because tracks are measured on the chart against true north, from the graticule. But you fly them by the magnetic compass. The isogonal nearest your route gives the correction. You'll find that in the lesson on direction of flight. In Western Australia the variation is small, roughly 1 to 2 degrees west around Perth. That is why it is easy to forget, and why the exam likes to test it.

The graticule is the grid of latitude and longitude lines. On the WAC they are usually every 30 minutes, with tick marks for each minute along the lines. You use it for three things. To plot a position from coordinates, since an ERSA entry gives each aerodrome's position. To measure true track against a meridian. And to measure distance. One minute of latitude is one nautical mile, so the ticks along a meridian are a ready-made distance scale.

Finally, on the charts that show it, there is airspace. The boundaries of control zones and CTA steps, each labelled with its lower and upper limits. And the boundaries of PRD areas, each labelled with its identifier and limits. That brings us to the second thing the exam wants.

Question. Why does the chart print isogonals at all, when you could just draw tracks magnetic?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because the chart's grid is the latitude and longitude graticule, which is aligned with true north. So tracks are measured true. The compass points to magnetic north, and the variation changes from place to place. The isogonal tells you the variation along your route, so you can convert the true track to the magnetic one you fly.

## Which charts show controlled airspace and PRD areas

Now the question the exam asks most directly.

Controlled airspace is airspace in which air traffic control provides a service, and a clearance is needed to enter. PRD areas are prohibited, restricted and danger areas, each with its own rules. You'll find what the letters mean in the lesson on documentation. You need to know where both are before you fly.

Controlled airspace is shown on the VTC, the VNC and the ERC-L. The TAC shows CTA boundaries too. And it is not shown on the WAC. So a WAC alone cannot keep you out of controlled airspace. Plan any flight near a controlled aerodrome, or under CTA steps, with the VNC or VTC for that area. Elsewhere, use the ERC-L.

[[diagram:airspace-cross-section]]
Picture a slice through the airspace, seen from the side. At a major airport, a Class C control zone starts at the surface. Around it, Class C steps sit above, with lower limits that rise the further you go from the airport. At a regional tower there is a smaller Class D control zone. Class E sits above the steps, and Class A at high level. Underneath the steps is Class G, non-controlled airspace. And off to one side is a restricted area. The VNC and the VTC draw these boundaries with their limits. The WAC does not.

The chart has to show vertical limits as well as boundaries, because controlled airspace is layered. Around Perth, the Class C steps have lower limits that rise as you move away from the airport. So you can fly under a step, in Class G, without a clearance, as long as you stay below its base. The VTC prints that base on each step. On a WAC, you would see only the ground beneath it.

PRD areas are shown on the VTC, the VNC and the ERC-L, with their identifier and their vertical limits. An identifier looks like {R155|R one five five}. The chart shows where the area is, and how high it goes. It does not tell you when it is active, who controls it, or whether you are likely to get a clearance through it. For that, the chart sends you to ERSA's PRD section, and to the part of the AIP that covers prohibited, restricted and danger areas. Those give the full description, the controlling authority, the hours of activity, and any conditions. The PCA carries a summary.

Let's work one. You are planning a VFR flight from Jandakot, east over the Darling Scarp, to Northam. You depart through the Jandakot control zone, and pass under the Perth Class C steps. Which charts do you need, and what does each one give you? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
First, the departure and the first part of the track lie in the Perth terminal area, where the airspace changes every few miles. So you need the Perth VTC. It gives you the Jandakot control zone, the step bases, the VFR route out of the basin, and the reporting points.

Second, the rest of the route lies under and beyond the outer steps. So you need the VNC covering Perth. It shows the outer step bases, and any PRD areas along the way.

Third, for the long-distance navigation picture, and for the exam-style track and distance measurement, you use the WAC. You know that it shows none of the airspace you have just checked.

And fourth, for anything the charts flag but do not explain, such as a restricted area beside the track, you use ERSA's PRD section, and the NOTAMs.

So the answer: the VTC, the VNC and the WAC, with ERSA and the NOTAMs behind them. The WAC alone would let you navigate to Northam accurately, and give you no warning of the controlled airspace you cross on the way.

Now one for you to try. You are planning a flight across the Wheatbelt, far from any controlled aerodrome, and you have only the WAC. A friend says you need nothing else, because there is no airspace out there. What do you check, and on what?
[[pause 8]]
The answer: the WAC cannot tell you whether there is controlled airspace or a PRD area on the route, because it shows none. So check the route on the ERC-L, which shows airspace and PRD areas everywhere. Or on the VNC, if one covers the area. Then look up any PRD area you find in ERSA's PRD section, for its hours and conditions. And check the NOTAMs for temporary restricted areas. There is no airspace out there is a conclusion you reach from those, not from the WAC.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. The WAC is the navigation chart the exam is built around, so it is tempting to answer WAC to every chart question. But for controlled airspace and PRD areas, the answer is the VTC, the VNC or the ERC-L. Never the WAC. And a chart tells you only where a restricted area is. When it is active comes from ERSA and the NOTAMs.

Question. A VNC shows a restricted area beside your track, with its identifier and limits. What does the chart not tell you, and where do you find it?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the chart does not give the hours of activity, the controlling authority, or the conditional status, meaning whether a clearance is likely. Those are in ERSA's PRD section and in the AIP. And the NOTAMs may change them on the day.

## Bringing it together

You are planning a Saturday flight from Jandakot to Cunderdin and back.

On the PCA, you confirm which WAC covers the route, and that the whole flight is in one time zone.

On the WAC, you draw the track. You measure the true track against a meridian, and the distance against the latitude ticks. And you apply the 1 to 2 degrees west variation from the nearest isogonal.

Along the line, you mark the features you will cross. The scarp. The Avon valley and its river, solid near the coast, and dashed further inland, where it does not always flow. The Great Eastern Highway, and the railway beside it. A power line. And the maximum elevation figures that set your lowest safe altitude.

Then you put the WAC aside, and open the Perth VTC and VNC. The WAC has shown you none of the airspace. Now you see the Jandakot control zone, the Perth step bases you must stay under on the way out, and a restricted area whose identifier you note.

ERSA's PRD section tells you when that area is active. And the NOTAMs tell you whether anything has changed. Four documents, each answering the question it was drawn for.

## Key points

Let's recap.

Visual charts show six kinds of feature. Relief: contours, hypsometric tints, spot heights, and maximum elevation figures on the VNC and VTC. Water, with intermittent rivers and lakes drawn differently. Culture: towns in yellow, railways with cross ticks, roads and power lines. Obstacles, with their height above ground and their elevation above sea level, and lit ones with a flash. Aeronautical information: aerodromes, navaids, reporting points and isogonals. And the graticule.

The scales. The WAC is {1:1,000,000|one to one million}. The VNC is {1:500,000|one to five hundred thousand}. The VTC is {1:250,000|one to two hundred and fifty thousand}. The smaller the number after the colon, the more detail.

Controlled airspace and PRD areas are shown on the VTC, the VNC and the ERC-L, and CTA on the TAC. They are never shown on the WAC. The chart shows where an area is, and its limits. ERSA's PRD section and the AIP give its hours, its controlling authority and its status.

A final exam tip. The trap is the WAC. It is the navigation chart for the exam, but it shows no airspace at all. If a question asks which chart identifies controlled airspace or restricted areas, the answer is the VTC, the VNC or the ERC-L, never the WAC. Know the three scales. And be able to recognise four symbols. A railway, with its cross ticks. An intermittent river, dashed. An isogonal, dashed and labelled with the variation. And a maximum elevation figure, the large number in a VNC or VTC grid square, giving the highest terrain or obstacle in that square, with a margin.

That's the end of BAKC 5.1.
