This is BAKC 5.2, Documentation. By the end you will be able to read an aerodrome's runway data in ERSA, read the listing for a prohibited, restricted or danger area, and work out when a restricted area is active in Western Australian time. There are worked examples and questions along the way, so pause whenever you want a moment to think.

## What the exam expects (BAKC 5.2)

The exam wants three things from you. First, to look up an aerodrome in ERSA and read its runway data: the designators, length, width, surface, slope, strength, lighting, and the remarks that go with them. Second, to read the data for a prohibited, restricted or danger area: its type, its limits, its controlling authority and its conditional status. And third, to work out from ERSA when a restricted area is active, converting the times from UTC to local time.

## Why this matters

Before you land at a country strip you have never seen, say Cunderdin or York, everything you know about it comes from one entry in ERSA. How long the runway is. Whether it is sealed or gravel. Which way it slopes. Whether it has lights. Which way the circuit goes. And what frequency to call on.

Before you route past a military or industrial area, ERSA tells you whether that red-hatched shape on the chart is a firm keep out, or a call and ask. And because ERSA writes its times in UTC, you have to convert them before they mean anything in Perth.

The exam gives you an ERSA extract, or lets you bring your own current ERSA, and asks you to find things in it. So this lesson is about reading. Where each item lives. What each abbreviation means. And how to turn the printed times into local time, without getting the conversion backwards.

## How ERSA is organised

ERSA, the En Route Supplement Australia, is the book of facts that do not fit on a chart. It is published by Airservices Australia and amended on a regular cycle, so the copy you use must be current.

Two sections matter here. The FAC section, short for facilities, has an entry for every listed aerodrome, alphabetically by state. The PRD section lists the prohibited, restricted and danger areas, with their details. The introduction and legend at the front explain the abbreviations. Read it once, because almost every field in an entry is abbreviated.

ERSA is not the only document you need. Part of reading it well is knowing what it does not tell you. ERSA is the permanent picture. Temporary changes come by NOTAM, a notice to airmen. A runway closed for works, or a restricted area activated for an exercise, are examples. A NOTAM overrides ERSA for as long as it is in force.

## Reading an aerodrome entry, block by block

Every FAC entry follows the same layout. So once you can read one, you can read them all. We'll walk through an invented aerodrome, Example Creek in Western Australia, from the top.

[[diagram:ersa-entry-anatomy]]
Picture an ERSA entry for Example Creek, laid out in blocks from top to bottom, with a label pointing at each. At the top, the header, with the elevation, position, variation, time zone and operator. Below it, the runway table: designators, length and width in metres, surface, slope, strength and lighting. Then the communications and navigation aids. And at the bottom, the remarks and local traffic regulations. Notice that the runway lengths are in metres, while the elevation up in the header is in feet.

The header identifies the aerodrome, and gives the facts about the place as a whole. For Example Creek, it gives the name and state, a time zone of UTC plus 8, an elevation of 1,050 ft, a position in latitude and longitude, a magnetic variation of 1 degree west, and the operator with a phone number.

Take those one at a time. The elevation is in feet above mean sea level. It is the number you set your altimeter to when you have no QNH. And it is the starting point for the pressure height and density height calculations. You'll find those in the lesson on take-off and landing performance.

The time zone tells you how local time relates to UTC. Western Australia is UTC plus 8. The magnetic variation is the same quantity the isogonals show on the chart, which you met in the lesson on charts. And the operator is who you phone for permission, or for anything the entry does not answer.

The runway table is the heart of the entry. Each runway has a line, and each line has the same six fields, in order.

First, the designators, such as {06|zero six} and {24|two four}. A runway is named by its magnetic direction, rounded to the nearest 10 degrees, with the last zero dropped. So runway {06|zero six} points roughly 060 degrees magnetic. And runway {24|two four}, the same strip used the other way, points roughly {240|two four zero}. The two ends always differ by 18.

Second, the length and width, in metres. A length and width of 1,200 by 30 means 1,200 m long and 30 m wide. This is the one place on the page where the unit changes from feet to metres. And the exam knows it.

Third, the surface: sealed, gravel, grass or natural. Surface changes the take-off and landing distances. Gravel and grass lengthen both. So you need it before you open the performance chart.

Fourth, the slope, as a percentage and a direction. For example, 0.5 percent, up to the east. That means the runway rises towards the east. A runway with no slope given is effectively level.

Fifth, the pavement strength. That tells the operator of a heavy aircraft whether the surface will carry it. For a light trainer, it is rarely limiting.

And sixth, the lighting, if any. A runway without lighting listed is not lit. You cannot use it at night.

Where they are published, the runway distances supplement gives four declared distances for each runway. TORA, the take-off run available, is the length of runway you can roll on. TODA, the take-off distance available, is the run plus any clearway beyond the end, over which you can climb. ASDA, the accelerate-stop distance available, is the run plus any stopway. And LDA, the landing distance available, can be shorter than the runway, if the threshold is displaced. For a small country strip, the runway table is usually all there is.

The aerodrome diagram shows the layout. The runways, the taxiways, the apron, and the circuit direction where it is non-standard. Use it to picture the place before you arrive.

The communications and navigation aids block lists the frequencies. At a non-controlled aerodrome, that is the CTAF, the common traffic advisory frequency. At a controlled one, it is the tower frequencies. It also lists an AWIS frequency or phone number, for automatic weather, if there is one, and any navigation aids. At Example Creek there is a CTAF, an AWIS, and an NDB.

And the remarks and local traffic regulations block is where the rules peculiar to this aerodrome live. It is the block pilots skip, and then regret skipping. At Example Creek, the remarks give four things. Right-hand circuits for runway {24|two four}. Noise abatement. Operating hours. And PPR, which means prior permission required: you must phone the operator before you arrive. They also say AVGAS is available.

Let's work one. What are the length, width, surface, slope and lighting of runway {24|two four} at Example Creek? And is there anything special about using it? Pause if you'd like to think it through.
[[pause 6]]
Step one. Find the line in the runway table that contains {24|two four}. It is the line for {06|zero six} and {24|two four}, because those are the two ends of the same runway.

Step two, the length and width. 1,200 m by 30 m, in metres, from the length and width column.

Step three, the surface. Sealed.

Step four, the slope. 0.5 percent, up to the east. Runway {24|two four} points roughly {240|two four zero} degrees magnetic, towards the west-south-west. So you take off from the eastern, higher end, and roll downhill. Over 1,200 m, a 0.5 percent slope is a rise of 1,200 times 0.005, which is 6 m. That is about 20 ft from end to end.

Step five, the lighting. The table notes lighting for runway {06|zero six} and {24|two four}. So runway {24|two four} is lit.

Step six, the remarks. Right-hand circuits for runway {24|two four}. If you join for {24|two four}, you fly the circuit to the right, not the standard left.

So the answer: 1,200 by 30 m, sealed, sloping down the way you take off, lit, with a right-hand circuit. Everything the runway table gave you goes straight into the take-off and landing charts. And the remark changes how you join.

Now one for you to try. What can you find out about runway {30|three zero} at Example Creek? And could you use it for a night landing? The second line of the runway table is for runways {12|one two} and {30|three zero}: 800 m by 18 m, gravel, with no slope given.
[[pause 6]]
The answer: runway {30|three zero} is 800 m by 18 m, gravel, with no slope given, so treat it as level. The table lists lighting only for {06|zero six} and {24|two four}. So {12|one two} and {30|three zero} are unlit, and cannot be used at night.

Being gravel, and 400 m shorter, it will also give you a longer take-off and landing distance on a shorter strip. So check the performance chart before planning to use it. And the right-hand circuit remark applies to runway {24|two four}, not to {30|three zero}. So the circuit on {30|three zero} is the standard left-hand one.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Runway lengths and widths in ERSA are in metres. But the elevation in the same entry is in feet. A 1,200 m runway is about 3,900 ft long. Mix the two, or read the length as feet, and a short strip looks generous, or a generous one looks short.

Question. An ERSA runway line reads: runways {14|one four} and {32|three two}, 950 by 18, natural, 1.2 percent up to the north-west. Which way do you roll downhill?
[[pause 5]]
The answer: the runway rises towards the north-west. Runway {32|three two} points roughly {320|three two zero} degrees magnetic, towards the north-west. So taking off on {32|three two} is uphill. Runway {14|one four} points south-east, away from the high end. So taking off on {14|one four} is downhill.

## Reading the PRD areas

Some airspace is set aside because something dangerous or sensitive happens in it. A naval base. A weapons range. A parachuting zone. Blasting at a mine.

Rather than draw the whole story on the chart, the chart shows the shape, the identifier and the limits. ERSA's PRD section, together with the AIP, gives the rest. And the letter at the start of the identifier tells you the kind of area, and how strictly you are kept out. There are three letters.

P is prohibited. Flight is not permitted at any time, for any reason. These areas are few, over sensitive installations.

R is restricted. Flight is not permitted unless the controlling authority gives you a clearance, with whatever conditions it attaches. Most restricted areas are not active all the time, which is why the hours matter.

D is danger. Flight is permitted, but a hazard exists. Gunnery, parachuting, gliding, or blasting. Nobody will stop you entering. But you are expected to know what the hazard is, and to avoid the area if that is practicable.

So: P, never. R, only with a clearance. D, permitted, but know the hazard.

Restricted areas carry one more piece of information that tells you how to plan. That is the conditional status. There are three levels.

{RA1|R A one}: a clearance can normally be obtained, and you may plan to fly through the area.

{RA2|R A two}: do not flight plan through the area. ATC may give a clearance tactically on the day, but do not count on it. Plan to avoid it.

{RA3|R A three}: no clearance will be available. Plan around it.

The ERSA PRD listing for a restricted area gives five things, in order. The identifier and name. The vertical limits, for example from the surface to 5,000 ft, or up to FL125. The hours of activity. The controlling authority, with its contact details. And the conditional status.

A typical listing reads like this. {R155|R one five five} Garden Island. Surface to 5,000 ft. {H24|H twenty-four}. Controlling authority, Navy. {RA3|R A three}. That is an illustration of the format. Read the real area's limits, hours and status from the current ERSA.

Let's work one. Here is the listing again. {R155|R one five five} Garden Island. Surface to 5,000 ft. {H24|H twenty-four}. Controlling authority, Navy. {RA3|R A three}. What does each part tell you, and how do you plan? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
First, the letter R. It is a restricted area. Entry needs a clearance from the controlling authority.

Second, the vertical limits, from the surface to 5,000 ft. Below 5,000 ft over the island, you are inside it.

Third, the hours, {H24|H twenty-four}. It is active continuously. There is no quiet time to wait for.

Fourth, the controlling authority, Navy. That is who would issue a clearance, and who to contact with questions.

And fifth, the conditional status, {RA3|R A three}. No clearance will be available.

So the conclusion. Plan your route around this area, either to the side of it, or above its upper limit with a margin. Do not plan to request a clearance through it.

Picture a flow of documents, each answering one question. Runway data comes from the ERSA FAC entry. For a prohibited, restricted or danger area, the VNC, the VTC or the ERC-L shows its identifier and vertical limits. The ERSA PRD section and the AIP give its hours, its controlling authority and its conditional status. The NOTAMs can activate it or change it. And when in doubt, you call the controlling authority.

Question. One restricted area beside your track is listed {RA1|R A one}, and another is listed {RA2|R A two}. How does that change your planning?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: you may plan through the {RA1|R A one} area, expecting a clearance to be normally available. You still need one before entering. You should plan to avoid the {RA2|R A two} area. You may not flight plan through it, and a tactical clearance on the day is not assured. And with {RA3|R A three}, there is no clearance at all.

## When is a restricted area active?

A restricted area that is not active is ordinary airspace. So the question that saves you a long detour, or keeps you out of trouble, is this. Is it active at the time you will be there? ERSA's hours of activity field answers it, with a small set of abbreviations.

Four of them are words in disguise. {H24|H twenty-four} means hours, 24. It is active continuously. HJ comes from the French jour, day. It is active during daylight, from sunrise to sunset. HN is night. It is active during the night. And HO is hours of operation. It is active during the hours of operation of the unit.

Then there is by NOTAM. That means the area sleeps until a NOTAM wakes it. So if an area is shown as NOTAM, and no NOTAM has activated it, it is inactive. An area active at times as notified also depends on a NOTAM, or on the controlling authority. Check the NOTAMs before flight.

And then there are specific times. Those are the ones you calculate.

ERSA writes them in UTC, Coordinated Universal Time, so that one book works across every Australian time zone. Western Australia has no daylight saving. So Western Standard Time, WST, is always UTC plus 8 hours. You'll find the conversion taught in full in the lesson on time. To read an ERSA time in Perth, add 8. If the answer passes {2400|twenty-four hundred}, subtract 24, and move to the next day.

[[diagram:utc-to-wst-activity]]
Picture two clock scales, one above the other. The top one is UTC. The bottom one is Western Standard Time. They are lined up so that each instant sits directly above itself. An area listed active {2200|twenty-two hundred} to {0600|oh six hundred} UTC shows as a bar across the top scale. Directly below it, the same bar runs from {0600|oh six hundred} to {1400|fourteen hundred} WST. Add 8 hours: the same moments, read on a different clock.

That is why the conversion can look strange, and still be right. The UTC clock and the WST clock are two labels for the same moments. An activity period that crosses midnight UTC, such as {2200|twenty-two hundred} to {0600|oh six hundred}, looks like an overnight period in UTC. But it sits entirely in the WST morning and early afternoon. That is because midnight UTC is {0800|oh eight hundred} in Perth.

Let's work one. ERSA lists a restricted area as active {2200|twenty-two hundred} to {0200|oh two hundred} UTC. You plan to pass it at {0930|oh nine thirty} WST. Is it active when you get there? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 8]]
Step one, the start of activity. {2200|Twenty-two hundred} plus 8 is {3000|thirty hundred}. That is past {2400|twenty-four hundred}, so subtract 24. It starts at {0600|oh six hundred} WST, on the next calendar day from the UTC date.

Step two, the end of activity. {0200|Oh two hundred} plus 8 is {1000|ten hundred} WST.

Step three. So the area is active from {0600|oh six hundred} to {1000|ten hundred} WST, all within one WST morning, even though the UTC period crossed midnight.

Step four. {0930|Oh nine thirty} WST is inside that window. So the area is active when you get there.

The answer: yes, it is active, from {0600|oh six hundred} to {1000|ten hundred} WST. So either plan around it, or ask the controlling authority for a clearance, depending on its conditional status. Or time your flight to pass after {1000|ten hundred} WST.

Now a second one. ERSA lists an area as active {0000|zero zero zero zero} to {0800|oh eight hundred} UTC, Monday to Friday. Is it active on a Wednesday afternoon at {1530|fifteen thirty} WST?
[[pause 8]]
Step one, the start. {0000|Zero zero zero zero} plus 8 is {0800|oh eight hundred} WST.

Step two, the end. {0800|Oh eight hundred} plus 8 is {1600|sixteen hundred} WST.

Step three, the days. {0000|Zero zero zero zero} UTC on a Monday is {0800|oh eight hundred} WST on the same Monday. So the days line up. The area is active from {0800|oh eight hundred} to {1600|sixteen hundred} WST, Monday to Friday.

Step four. {1530|Fifteen thirty} WST on a Wednesday is inside the window. So it is active. Arrive after {1600|sixteen hundred}, and it is not.

A working-hours area in Perth is a working-hours area in UTC, shifted by eight.

Here is one for you to try. An area is listed active {1400|fourteen hundred} to {2200|twenty-two hundred} UTC. When is it active in WST, and is it active at {0500|oh five hundred} WST?
[[pause 8]]
The answer. The start is {1400|fourteen hundred} plus 8, which is {2200|twenty-two hundred} WST. The end is {2200|twenty-two hundred} plus 8, which is {3000|thirty hundred}. Minus 24 gives {0600|oh six hundred} WST the next day. So the area is active from {2200|twenty-two hundred} WST to {0600|oh six hundred} WST, overnight in Perth. {0500|Oh five hundred} WST falls inside that window, so the area is active at {0500|oh five hundred}. This time, the WST period crosses local midnight, even though the UTC one did not.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. The conversion goes one way. UTC to WST, add 8. Subtracting gives a time sixteen hours out, and in the exam that is usually one of the distractors.

Watch the day too. {2200|Twenty-two hundred} UTC on Monday is {0600|oh six hundred} WST on Tuesday. So an area listed for certain UTC days can fall on different WST days. When a listing crosses midnight UTC, and the day matters, confirm with the NOTAMs or the controlling authority.

Reading the hours is not the end of the check. A restricted area can be activated, deactivated, or have its limits changed, by NOTAM. So read the NOTAMs for the area before every flight. If you are still in doubt, call the controlling authority listed in ERSA. And remember that an active restricted area needs a clearance. Without one, you stay out.

Picture four steps, in order, from left to right. First, read the chart, the VTC, VNC or ERC-L, for the boundaries and limits. Second, read ERSA's PRD section and the AIP for the hours of activity and the controlling authority. Third, check the NOTAMs for activations, changes, and temporary restricted areas. Question. An ERSA listing shows a restricted area's hours as NOTAM. You find no NOTAM for it. Is it active?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: no. An area activated by NOTAM is inactive unless a current NOTAM has activated it. Check the NOTAMs on the day of flight, because the answer can change. And call the controlling authority if anything is unclear.

## Bringing it together

On a weekday, you plan a flight from Jandakot to Example Creek, arriving about {1100|eleven hundred} WST.

The FAC entry for Example Creek gives an elevation of 1,050 ft. That is your QNH reference, and the start of your density height sum. Runway {06|zero six} and {24|two four} is 1,200 m, sealed and lit, with a 0.5 percent slope rising to the east. Runway {12|one two} and {30|three zero} is 800 m, gravel, and unlit.

The remarks say PPR, so you phone the operator. They also say right-hand circuits on {24|two four}. With a westerly forecast, you plan a right-hand circuit on to {24|two four}, landing slightly downhill.

On the chart, a restricted area sits beside your track. ERSA's PRD section lists it as active {0000|zero zero zero zero} to {0800|oh eight hundred} UTC, Monday to Friday, with {RA2|R A two} status. That is {0800|oh eight hundred} to {1600|sixteen hundred} WST on weekdays. So it will be active when you pass. A clearance is unlikely at short notice, and you plan to fly around it. The NOTAMs show no change.

Two sections of one book, and the NOTAMs, and the plan is complete.

## Key points

Let's recap.

Runway data is in the ERSA FAC entry. The elevation, in feet, and the time zone are in the header. The runway table gives the designators, which are the magnetic direction to the nearest 10 degrees. Then the length and width, in metres. Then the surface, slope, strength and lighting. And the circuit direction, PPR, and other local rules are in the remarks.

PRD areas. P means never. R means only with a clearance from the controlling authority. D means permitted, but know the hazard. {RA1|R A one}, plan through. {RA2|R A two}, plan to avoid. {RA3|R A three}, no clearance. The ERSA PRD listing gives the identifier, the limits, the hours, the controlling authority and the status.

Hours of activity. {H24|H twenty-four}, HJ, HN, HO, or specific times in UTC. Add 8 for WST. An area by NOTAM is inactive unless a NOTAM activates it. Always check the NOTAMs, and call the controlling authority if in doubt.

A final exam tip. ERSA times are in UTC. A restricted area listed as active {2200|twenty-two hundred} to {0600|oh six hundred} is active from {0600|oh six hundred} to {1400|fourteen hundred} Western Standard Time. Getting the conversion backwards is a common way to lose this mark. In runway questions, the length and width are in metres. Read the whole runway line, including the slope direction. Then check the remarks for anything that applies to the runway you were asked about.
