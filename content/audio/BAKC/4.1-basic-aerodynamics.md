This is BAKC 4.1, Basic aerodynamics. By the end you will be able to describe the aeroplane's energy and its inertia, say exactly what the angle of attack and the relative airflow are, name the four forces and which way each one acts, tell the centre of pressure from the centre of gravity, and define chord, span, camber and the stall. There are worked examples and a few questions along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (BAKC 4.1)

The exam wants three things from you. First, the aeroplane's energy state, in terms of kinetic energy, potential energy and inertia. Second, the meaning of aerofoil, angle of attack and relative airflow, of centre of pressure and centre of gravity, and of the four forces: lift, weight, thrust and drag. And third, the chord, span and camber of an aerofoil, and what an aerodynamic stall is.

## Why this matters

Every lesson on flight from here on is written in the words this one defines. Your instructor at Jandakot might say: you're slow and low, you've got no energy. Or: the angle of attack is too high on that turn. Or: watch the CG with three up. Each phrase is a precise idea, and the exam uses the same words with the same precision.

Most wrong answers in this subtopic come from one confusion. Treating the angle of attack as if it were the nose attitude, or lift as if it always pointed straight up. This lesson builds each term from a picture you already have. Then the lift formula, the climb and the stall, which come in later lessons, all rest on solid ground. The exam asks for definitions here. Usually they come as: the angle between which two lines? Which way does lift act? And what does inertia do in wind shear?

## Energy: speed and height are stored work

Picture a training aeroplane on a long final at 70 kt and 500 ft. It has two things that took fuel to get: speed and height. Both are energy, the capacity to do work. And the pilot spends them, stores them, and swaps one for the other on every flight. The physics is in an earlier lesson. Here is what it means for an aeroplane.

Kinetic energy is the energy of motion. Kinetic energy equals a half, times the mass, times the speed squared. Because the speed is squared, a little extra speed is a lot of extra energy. 10 percent fast carries 21 percent more kinetic energy. And that energy has to go somewhere when you land.

[[diagram:kinetic-energy-speed-squared]]
Picture a curve that starts gently and then bends steeply upward: kinetic energy plotted against speed. At 1.1 times a reference speed, the energy is 1.21 times as much. That is the 10 percent fast, 21 percent more energy. And at double the speed, the curve is at four times the energy.

Potential energy is energy stored by height in the Earth's gravity. Potential energy equals the mass, times the acceleration due to gravity, times the height. Double the height, and you double the potential energy. Height is energy in the bank. In a glide with the engine stopped, it is the only energy you have left to buy distance with.

The energy state of the aeroplane is the sum of the two, at any moment. A high energy state is fast, or high, or both. A low energy state is slow and low: close to the stall, with no height to recover in. That is where most loss-of-control accidents begin.

The pilot manages the energy state with two controls. The throttle adds energy to the system. Or, closed, it lets drag take energy away. The elevator does not add any energy at all. It only shares the existing energy between speed and height. Pull back without adding power, and you trade speed for height. Push forward, and you trade height for speed.

[[diagram:energy-trade-zoom]]
Picture an aeroplane at 100 kt that raises its nose without adding power, and zooms up until it has slowed to 60 kt. Beside it stand energy bars. The kinetic energy bar shrinks, and the potential energy bar grows, but the total stays the same. Ignoring drag, the speed it lost has become about 280 ft of height.

Let's work one: how much height does a zoom buy? An aeroplane at 90 kt raises the nose without adding power, and slows to 60 kt. Ignore drag. How much height does it gain? This one is tough to do in your head, so just follow along if you prefer.
[[pause 6]]
First, convert the speeds to metres per second. Multiply by 0.514. 90 kt is 46.3 metres per second, and 60 kt is 30.9.

Second, the kinetic energy lost for each kilogram. That is a half, times the difference of the squares. 46.3 squared is 2,144. 30.9 squared is 955. The difference, halved, is 594 joules per kilogram.

Third, the height that energy buys. Divide by g, 9.81, and you get 60.6 m.

And fourth, in feet. 60.6 times 3.28 is about 200 ft.

So a zoom from 90 kt to 60 kt buys about 200 ft. Notice that the mass cancels out, so the answer is the same for a Jabiru or a Boeing. In real air, drag takes its share, and you get a little less. But this is why a go-around begun with speed in hand starts climbing at once. And it is why an aeroplane that is low and slow has nothing to trade.

Compare it with the zoom in the picture, from 100 kt to 60 kt, which bought about 280 ft. Starting 10 kt faster bought 80 ft more, because the energy goes with the square of the speed.

Question. You raise the nose with the throttle fixed, and the airspeed falls. Has the total energy changed?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: ignoring drag, no. The elevator only moves energy between kinetic, the speed, and potential, the height. Only the throttle adds energy, and drag removes it. So a climb that holds its speed needs more power.

## Inertia: the aeroplane keeps doing what it was doing

Stand in a bus that brakes hard, and you keep moving forward. That reluctance to change motion is inertia. It is Newton's first law. A body stays at rest, or keeps moving at the same speed in the same direction, unless a force acts on it. Inertia depends only on mass. The heavier the body, the more force it takes to change its motion, or the longer a given force takes to do it.

For an aeroplane, inertia shows up in three places.

The first is take-off and landing. A heavy aeroplane takes longer to accelerate to lift-off speed with the same thrust, and longer to stop with the same brakes. That is one reason the take-off and landing distances grow with weight, as the lesson on take-off and landing performance shows.

The second is changes of flight path. When you change the attitude, the flight path does not change at once. The aeroplane carries on along its old path for a moment, until the changed lift has had time to bend it. On a fast, steep approach, this catches pilots out. You begin the round-out at the usual height. But the descent has momentum, the path is slow to flatten, and the aeroplane arrives on the runway harder than you meant.

The third is wind shear, and this is the one the exam likes. Imagine you are on final with a 20 kt headwind. You descend through a layer where the headwind drops to 5 kt. The air around you has changed speed. But the aeroplane, because of its inertia, has not. For a moment it keeps its old groundspeed. Groundspeed equals airspeed minus headwind. So losing 15 kt of headwind means losing about 15 kt of airspeed. Lift falls with the airspeed, and the aeroplane sinks. Only thrust and time, or a lower nose, can rebuild the speed. And a heavier aeroplane, with more inertia, is slower to regain its airspeed after the shear.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Inertia holds the aeroplane's speed over the ground, not its airspeed. That is why a sudden change in the wind shows up as a sudden change in indicated airspeed. A decreasing headwind, or an increasing tailwind, drops the indicated airspeed. An increasing headwind raises it, for a moment.

Question. On climb-out, the headwind suddenly increases by 10 kt. What does the airspeed do at first, and why?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: it rises, by about 10 kt. Inertia keeps the aeroplane's groundspeed for a moment, and more headwind at the same groundspeed means more air flowing past the wing. The aeroplane balloons upward, until it settles at its trimmed speed again.

## The aerofoil and the air it meets

Hold your hand out of a car window, flat and level, and you feel only a push backwards. Tilt the front edge up a little, and your hand is pushed up as well. Curve your hand slightly, and the upward push grows for the same tilt. That is a wing in miniature.

An aerofoil is a shape designed to produce a useful aerodynamic force, mostly at right angles to the airflow, for as little drag as possible, as it moves through the air. The wing is the obvious one. But the tailplane, the fin and each propeller blade are aerofoils too. A typical light-aeroplane wing section is rounded at the front edge, the leading edge. It is curved on top, flatter underneath, and tapered to a sharp trailing edge.

The relative airflow is the air as the aerofoil sees it. If the aeroplane moves forward through still air at 90 kt, the wing meets air coming at it at 90 kt, from exactly the opposite direction. So the relative airflow is equal in speed, and parallel and opposite to the flight path. Notice that this has nothing to do with the horizon. In a climb, the aeroplane is going up the slope, so the relative airflow comes from ahead and slightly above. In a descent, it comes from ahead and slightly below.

[[diagram:relative-airflow-climb-descent]]
Picture three side views, one above the other. In level flight, the relative airflow comes from straight ahead. In a climb, the aeroplane is moving up a slope, so the airflow arrows come down that slope at it, from ahead and slightly above. In a descent, they come up the slope, from ahead and slightly below. In every case the airflow runs straight back down the flight path, whatever the horizon or the nose attitude.

Now the angle of attack. It is the angle between the wing's chord line and the relative airflow. The chord line is the straight line from the leading edge to the trailing edge, and there is more on it later. The angle of attack is the single most important angle in flying, because it decides how hard the wing is working. More angle of attack makes more lift at the same speed, up to the stall.

[[diagram:aerofoil-terminology]]
Picture a wing section, flying to the right, with the rounded leading edge on the right and the sharp trailing edge on the left. A straight line joins them: the chord line. A curved line runs halfway between the upper and lower surfaces: the mean camber line. The relative airflow arrives from the right, and the angle between it and the chord line is the angle of attack. Lift acts through the centre of pressure, at right angles to the relative airflow. In a small inset, the wing is seen from above, showing the span from tip to tip and the chord from front to back.

The angle of attack is not the pitch attitude. Pitch attitude is the angle between the aeroplane's longitudinal axis and the horizon: the thing you see out of the windscreen. The two are the same only in level flight, and even then only roughly, because the wing is bolted on at a small angle to the fuselage. Climb, descend or turn, and they come apart, because the relative airflow follows the flight path, and the flight path is no longer level.

Let's work one: the angle of attack in a climb. The nose attitude is 10 degrees above the horizon. The flight path is climbing at 6 degrees. Ignore the small rigging angle between the chord and the fuselage. What is the angle of attack? Pause if you'd like to try it first.
[[pause 5]]
The relative airflow comes from directly along the flight path. So it is inclined 6 degrees above the horizontal, ahead. The angle of attack is the attitude minus the flight path angle: 10 minus 6, which is 4 degrees. The nose looks high, but the wing is working gently, well below the stall.

[[diagram:angle-of-attack-vs-attitude]]
Picture that climb from the side. The nose points 10 degrees above the horizon. The flight path, and the airflow coming back down it, sit 6 degrees above the horizon. And the gap between them, the angle of attack, is only about 4 degrees. Measure it from the flight path, never from the horizon.

Now try one the other way round. On a powered approach, the nose is 2 degrees below the horizon, and the aeroplane is descending along a 5 degree path. What is the angle of attack?
[[pause 6]]
The relative airflow comes up the descent path, from 5 degrees below the horizontal. The chord is 2 degrees below the horizontal. So it sits 5 minus 2, which is 3 degrees, above the relative airflow. The nose is below the horizon, yet the wing still has a positive angle of attack, because the air is coming at it from further below.

The same reasoning tells you that a wing can have a large angle of attack with the nose on, or below, the horizon. If the aeroplane is sinking fast, the relative airflow comes steeply from below.

[[widget:attitude-and-angle-of-attack]]
Imagine a wing with small streamers along its top surface. Hold the nose level, but let the aeroplane sink faster and faster. The airflow swings round to come from further below, the angle of attack grows, and past the critical angle the streamers break away. It happens whatever the attitude, and whatever the airspeed.

Question. The angle of attack is the angle between which two lines?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the chord line of the aerofoil, and the relative airflow. Not the horizon, and not the longitudinal axis. The relative airflow comes from wherever the aeroplane is going, so the angle of attack follows the flight path, not the view out of the windscreen.

## The four forces

In flight, four forces act on the aeroplane. Two of them come from the air, one from the engine, and one from the Earth.

The first is lift. Lift is the part of the total aerodynamic force on the wing that acts at right angles to the relative airflow. It comes from the difference in pressure between the lower and upper surfaces. The curved upper surface and the angle of attack speed the air up over the top, and lower its pressure. The air beneath is slowed, and its pressure raised. In level flight the relative airflow is horizontal, so lift points straight up. In a climb, a descent or a turn, it does not.

The second is drag. Drag is the part of the aerodynamic force that acts parallel to the relative airflow, in the same direction as the airflow. That means it opposes the aeroplane's motion through the air. It is the price of moving, and of making lift. The next lesson, on lift and drag, takes it apart.

The third is thrust. Thrust is the forward force from the propeller, acting along the thrust line. The propeller blades are aerofoils, and the lift they make points forward.

The fourth is weight. Weight is the force of gravity on the aeroplane's mass. It always acts vertically downward, towards the centre of the Earth, whatever the aeroplane's attitude.

In steady straight and level flight, at constant speed and constant height, nothing is accelerating. So the forces balance. Lift equals weight, and thrust equals drag. If thrust exceeds drag, the aeroplane accelerates, or climbs. If lift exceeds weight, it starts to climb, or turn.

[[diagram:four-forces-level-flight]]
Picture the aeroplane from the side, with four arrows. Lift points up, through the centre of pressure. Weight points down, through the centre of gravity. Thrust points forward along the thrust line, and drag points back. The lift arrow sits a little behind the weight arrow, so together they try to tip the nose down. And a small downward force on the tailplane balances that.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Lift is defined as perpendicular to the relative airflow. Not perpendicular to the ground, and not perpendicular to the wing's chord. Drag is parallel to the relative airflow. And weight is the only one of the four that always points the same way: straight down.

## Centre of pressure and centre of gravity

The forces in that picture are drawn as single arrows, but they are really spread out. Every square centimetre of wing has its own small pressure difference. Every kilogram of fuel, seat and pilot has its own small weight. To work with them, we replace each spread-out force with one arrow, acting at one point.

The centre of pressure, the CP, is the point on the chord line through which the total aerodynamic force, and so the lift, can be taken to act. On a conventional cambered wing, it is not fixed. As the angle of attack increases through the normal flight range, the pressure peak over the top surface moves forward, and the centre of pressure moves forward. At the stall, the flow over the rear of the wing breaks away, and the centre of pressure moves rapidly rearward. That is part of why the nose drops at the stall.

[[diagram:cp-movement-with-aoa]]
Picture three wing sections, one above the other, all flying to the right, with a dot marking where the lift acts. At a small angle of attack, the dot is well back along the chord. At a larger angle, still below the critical angle, it has moved forward. And beyond the critical angle, with the flow broken away over the rear of the wing, the dot jumps rapidly back again.

The centre of gravity, the CG, is the point through which the whole weight of the aeroplane acts. It is the balance point. It depends on how you load the aeroplane. Two heavy people in the back seats and full baggage move it aft. And burning fuel from tanks ahead of it, or behind it, moves it as the flight goes on. Weight and balance calculations exist to keep it between the limits, as a later lesson shows.

The centre of pressure and the centre of gravity are almost never in the same place. In a conventional design, the centre of pressure is a little behind the centre of gravity. Lift pushes up behind the balance point, while weight pulls down at it. So the pair forms a couple: two equal and opposite forces that are not in line. That couple tends to pitch the nose down. The tailplane balances it, with a small downward force at the tail.

The arrangement is deliberate. An aeroplane that is nose-heavy, with a tail holding it up, tends to lower its nose and gain speed if it slows down. That is the start of the pitch stability you will meet in the lesson on flight controls.

Question. What is the difference between the centre of pressure and the centre of gravity?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the centre of pressure is where the lift, the total aerodynamic force, acts. It moves forward as the angle of attack increases. The centre of gravity is where the weight acts, and it moves only with loading and fuel burn. With the CP behind the CG, lift and weight form a nose-down couple, balanced by the tailplane.

## The shape of a wing: chord, span and camber

Look at a wing from the end, and you see the aerofoil section. Look at it from above, and you see its plan. Three measurements describe the shape: chord, span and camber.

The chord line is the straight line joining the leading edge to the trailing edge of the section. Its length is the chord. On a tapered wing, the chord is longer at the root than at the tip, so we speak of the average, or mean, chord. The chord line is the reference from which the angle of attack is measured.

The span is the distance from wing tip to wing tip. Span divided by the average chord is the aspect ratio. A glider's long, slender wing has a high aspect ratio. A short, broad wing has a low one. High aspect ratio wings make less induced drag for the same lift, because a smaller share of the wing is near the tips, where the air spills round. The lesson on lift and drag explains why.

Camber is the curvature of the aerofoil section. Draw a line halfway between the upper and lower surfaces, from the leading edge to the trailing edge. That is the mean camber line. Camber is how far the mean camber line bows away from the chord line. A symmetrical aerofoil, the same shape top and bottom, has none. More camber gives more lift at the same angle of attack. And that is exactly how a plain or slotted flap works. Lowering it increases the camber of the inner wing, so that it makes the lift it needs at a lower speed.

Question. What does lowering flap change about the aerofoil, and what does that do?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: it increases the camber, and usually the chord. More camber makes more lift at the same angle of attack. So the wing can support the aeroplane at a lower speed, which is what you want for approach and landing.

## The aerodynamic stall

Go back to the hand out of the car window. Tilt it further and further, and at some point the upward push suddenly fades, and your hand starts to buffet. The air can no longer follow the curve of the back of your hand. It breaks away and tumbles.

A wing does the same. As the angle of attack increases, lift increases, until the airflow over the upper surface can no longer follow its curve. It separates, first near the trailing edge, and then further forward, leaving a turbulent wake over the top of the wing. Lift falls sharply, and drag rises sharply. The angle at which this happens is the critical angle of attack. It is about 15 to 18 degrees for a typical light-aeroplane wing. A wing beyond it is in an aerodynamic stall.

[[diagram:stall-sequence]]
Picture three wing sections, left to right, at increasing angles of attack. On the left, the airflow is attached over the whole upper surface. In the middle, it is starting to separate near the trailing edge. On the right, past the critical angle, the flow over the top is separated and turbulent: the stall. Below them run the warning signs, in the usual order. First, a low and decreasing airspeed with a high nose attitude. Then sloppy controls and a quieter airflow note. Then the stall warning, 5 to 10 knots above the stall. Then buffet. And then the stall itself: a nose drop or a wing drop, and a high rate of descent.

Because the stall is caused by the angle of attack, a wing can stall at any airspeed, and any attitude. It stalls at a higher speed in a steep turn or a hard pull-out, because the wing needs a larger angle of attack to make the extra lift. And it can stall with the nose below the horizon, if the aeroplane is sinking fast. The stall speed in the flight manual is only the speed at which the critical angle is reached, in one particular condition. Everything about recognising and recovering from the stall is in the lesson on stalling. For this subtopic, you need the definition, and its cause.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. A stall is not flying too slowly, and it is not the engine stopping. It is the wing exceeding its critical angle of attack. Low speed is the most common way to get there in level flight. But an exam answer that defines the stall by speed, or by attitude, is wrong.

## Bringing it together

You are on base at Jandakot, at 80 kt and 800 ft, and the tower asks you to extend downwind for traffic. The terms in this lesson describe everything that happens next.

Your energy state is moderate: some speed, some height. As you turn final, the sea breeze, a 15 kt headwind at circuit height, weakens to 5 kt near the ground. Inertia keeps your groundspeed for a moment, and the indicated airspeed sags by about 10 kt.

To hold the glide path, you raise the nose. That raises the angle of attack. That moves the centre of pressure forward, and asks the tailplane for a little more down force. The aeroplane is now slower, with the wing working harder and nearer its critical angle. And the nose attitude looks quite ordinary, because the descent path brings the relative airflow from below.

The right response comes from the energy picture. Add power, to put energy back into the system, rather than using the elevator to borrow height you do not have.

## Key points

Let's recap.

Energy. Kinetic energy goes with the speed squared. Potential energy goes with the height. Together they are the energy state. The throttle adds energy, and the elevator only trades speed for height. Inertia keeps the aeroplane's groundspeed and path for a moment, so wind shear shows up as a sudden change of airspeed.

The angle of attack is the angle between the chord line and the relative airflow. The relative airflow is parallel and opposite to the flight path, not the horizon.

The four forces. Lift acts at right angles to the relative airflow, and drag parallel to it. Thrust acts along the thrust line, and weight straight down. In steady level flight, lift equals weight, and thrust equals drag.

The centre of pressure, where lift acts, moves forward as the angle of attack rises. The centre of gravity, where weight acts, moves with loading. The centre of pressure behind the centre of gravity makes a nose-down couple, which the tailplane balances.

The shape. Chord: leading edge to trailing edge. Span: tip to tip, and span over chord is the aspect ratio. Camber: the curvature of the section, and more camber gives more lift.

And the stall: the angle of attack exceeding the critical angle, about 15 to 18 degrees, at any speed and any attitude.

A final exam tip. Expect definitions phrased as: the angle between which lines? The answer is the chord line and the relative airflow. Lift acts which way? Perpendicular to the relative airflow. And the centre of pressure? It moves forward with increasing angle of attack, until the stall. For inertia, the exam's scenario is a decreasing headwind on approach. The airspeed drops, because the groundspeed is held. And any option that ties the stall to a speed or an attitude, rather than an angle of attack, is the distractor.
