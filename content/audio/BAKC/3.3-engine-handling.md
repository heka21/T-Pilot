This is BAKC 3.3, Engine handling. By the end you will know how an engine gets hurt, and how each limit and check in your flight manual stops that happening: detonation, plug fouling, the oil and temperature limits, the magneto check, the dead cut, and what the colour of the exhaust smoke tells you. There are a few questions along the way, and two magneto checks to work through, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (BAKC 3.3)

The exam wants four things from you. First, what causes detonation, and what it does to the engine. The causes are the wrong use of the mixture control, the wrong combination of manifold pressure and RPM, and fuel of too low an octane. Second, what prolonged idling does to an engine, and what flying with the wrong mixture does. Third, the reasons behind a long list of limits and checks: oil pressure, oil temperature, cylinder head temperature and RPM, the ignition checks before take-off and at shutdown, the limit on the starter motor, not using pitot heat on the ground, and keeping the engine warm on long descents. And fourth, what blue or black exhaust smoke tells you.

## Why this matters

The limits in section 2 of your flight manual, the POH, and the checks in section 4 can look like a list of numbers to memorise. But every one of them is there because an engine was damaged, or a pilot was hurt, by doing the opposite.

The lesson on piston engines showed how the engine works. This one shows how you can hurt it, and how the checklist stops you. You will use it on every flight. In the run-up bay at Jandakot, reading the magneto drop. On a long glide back from the training area, keeping a little power on. And on a hot climb out of Northam, watching the cylinder head temperature creep up. The exam asks for the cause-and-effect pairs. What leads to detonation. Why the dead cut check exists. And what blue smoke means.

## Detonation: the engine hitting itself

Start with what normal combustion looks like. When the spark plugs fire, the mixture does not explode. A flame front starts at each plug, and spreads across the cylinder in a fraction of a second, burning the mixture progressively. Pressure in the cylinder rises smoothly. It peaks just after the piston passes the top, and pushes the piston down in a firm, even shove. That smooth rise is what the engine is designed for.

Now, what goes wrong. As the flame front advances, the unburnt mixture ahead of it is squeezed and heated by the expanding gas behind it. That unburnt mixture is called the end gas. Normally the flame reaches the end gas before it gets hot enough to ignite on its own. But if the end gas reaches its ignition temperature first, it does not burn progressively. It explodes, all at once. That is detonation. Instead of a firm push, the piston and the cylinder head receive a sharp hammer blow. It is a pressure spike far above normal, many times a second.

[[diagram:detonation-vs-normal-combustion]]
Picture two cylinders side by side. On the left, normal combustion: a smooth flame front spreading out from the spark plug, and underneath it a smooth, rounded rise in cylinder pressure. On the right, detonation: the flame has started from the plug in the same way, but the remaining charge on the far side explodes, and the pressure trace shows a sharp, jagged spike. The difference between the two is the whole story. One shoves the piston. The other hammers it.

So here is the pattern behind every cause. Anything that makes the end gas hotter, puts it under higher pressure, or gives it more time before the flame arrives, makes detonation more likely. And so does anything that makes the fuel less resistant to igniting by itself. The syllabus names three causes, and each one fits that pattern.

The first cause is a mixture that is too lean at high power. A lean mixture burns hotter, because it has less spare fuel to soak up heat as it evaporates. It also burns more slowly. So the end gas is hotter, and the flame takes longer to reach it. That is why you take off and climb at full rich, as the lesson on piston engines explained. The extra fuel is coolant.

The second cause is high manifold pressure with low RPM. On an aeroplane with a constant-speed propeller, you set manifold pressure with the throttle, and RPM with the propeller lever. High manifold pressure packs a dense charge into the cylinder, so the pressure at the top of the stroke is high. Low RPM means the piston moves slowly, so the end gas sits at that high pressure for longer. High pressure plus time is the recipe. That is why the flight manual gives approved combinations. And it is why the standard sequence is to increase RPM before manifold pressure when you add power, and to reduce manifold pressure before RPM when you take it off. That way the engine is never over-boosted.

The third cause is fuel of too low an octane. The octane rating measures how well a fuel resists igniting by itself under heat and pressure. An engine built for 100 octane has a compression ratio that relies on it. Lower-octane fuel lets the end gas ignite on its own, at pressures the engine was designed to tolerate. The lesson on fuels and oils covers that.

On top of those three, a high cylinder head temperature, from any cause, makes every one of them worse. The classic set-up is a steep, slow climb on a hot day with the mixture leaned too far. High power, little cooling air, hot end gas.

So what does detonation do, and what do you do about it? The effects follow from the hammer blow. First, rough running. Second, a sharp rise in cylinder head temperature, because the shock waves scrub away the thin layer of cooler gas that normally insulates the cylinder walls. Third, loss of power. And if it continues, burnt valves, holed pistons, cracked cylinder heads, and engine failure. In a light aeroplane engine, detonation is often hard to hear over the noise. So the cylinder head temperature gauge, and the feel of the engine, are your warnings.

The remedy reverses the causes. Enrich the mixture, for cooler end gas. Reduce power, for lower cylinder pressure. Lower the nose, to increase the airspeed and the cooling airflow. And open the cowl flaps, if they are fitted.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Detonation is not the same as pre-ignition. Pre-ignition is the mixture being lit before the spark, by a glowing hot spot such as a carbon deposit or an overheated plug. Detonation happens after the spark, when the unburnt end gas explodes. Both cause rough running and high temperatures, and one can lead to the other. But the exam's list of causes is the list for detonation: a lean mixture, high manifold pressure with low RPM, and low octane.

Question. Why does leaning the mixture too far at climb power make detonation more likely?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: a lean mixture burns hotter and more slowly, and it has less excess fuel to absorb heat. So the unburnt end gas is hotter, and it waits longer for the flame to reach it. That makes it more likely to reach its self-ignition temperature, and explode.

## Idling too long and the wrong mixture in flight

This section has two parts: idling on the ground, and the wrong mixture in the air.

First, prolonged idling. On a busy Saturday at Jandakot, you can sit at the holding point at idle for ten minutes behind a queue of departures. At idle the engine is running slowly, cool, and very rich. The carburettor's idle system deliberately supplies extra fuel so the engine will idle smoothly, and the cylinders are too cool to burn it all. The unburnt fuel leaves carbon on the spark plugs. And the lead in the AVGAS, the aviation gasoline, leaves lead deposits. That is plug fouling. A fouled plug cannot spark properly. So the engine may run rough, and typically the next magneto check fails, with an excessive drop on one side. Idling for long periods also keeps the oil cool, which does it no good either.

Many flight manuals give a remedy. Before the run-up, or if a magneto check shows a fouled plug, run the engine at a higher RPM, with the mixture leaned, for a short period. That burns the deposits off. Then repeat the check. During long waits on the ground, some flight manuals also recommend leaning the mixture at idle, to reduce fouling. Follow your POH.

Now the wrong mixture in flight. The lesson on piston engines explained what each mixture setting does. Here is what it does to you, if you get it wrong for long. There are two ways to get it wrong.

The first is too rich in cruise, typically from forgetting to lean at altitude. The engine loses some power, and may run rough. The plugs foul. And above all, fuel consumption rises. A flight planned on the flight manual's leaned fuel flow will burn noticeably more than planned. The fuel reserve you planned on is no longer there. And cooler running also means more deposits.

The second is too lean, typically from leaning at high power, or from forgetting to enrich before a climb. The cylinder head temperature and the exhaust gas temperature rise. Power falls. The engine roughens. And at high power, the stage is set for detonation and engine damage.

[[diagram:mixture-rich-to-lean]]
Picture a graph with the mixture along the bottom, full rich on the left, getting leaner as you move right. The power curve rises to a peak near the correct mixture, then falls away on both sides. The exhaust gas temperature climbs as you lean. On the rich side, on the left, the engine runs cool, burns a lot of fuel, fouls its plugs, and can make black smoke. On the lean side, on the right, it runs hot, with high cylinder head and exhaust temperatures, and it invites detonation. Either way, you lose power.

Question. You sat at idle for 15 minutes waiting for a departure slot. The magneto check now shows a large drop on the left magneto. What is the likely cause, and what do you do?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: plug fouling, from prolonged rich idling, on a plug fed by the left magneto. Run the engine at the higher RPM, with the mixture leaned, as the flight manual directs, to burn off the deposits. Then repeat the check. If the drop is still outside the limit, the aeroplane does not fly.

## Why the limits exist

This is the longest section, so here is the order. First the oil pressure. Then the oil temperature and the cylinder head temperature. Then maximum RPM. Then the two ignition checks, before take-off and at shutdown. Then the starter motor and the pitot heater. And last, keeping the engine warm on long descents.

Start with the minimum oil pressure. Oil pressure is the push that drives oil into the bearings. Below the minimum, oil is no longer reaching the crankshaft and camshaft bearings, and the cylinder walls, in the quantity they need. Metal starts to touch metal. The bearings overheat, and the engine seizes. So the minimum oil pressure is the edge of engine failure. A falling oil pressure in flight is a reason to land as soon as possible.

Next, oil temperature, which has a minimum and a maximum.

The minimum first. Cold oil is thick. It flows slowly through the narrow passages, so some parts are starved of oil, even though the pressure gauge may read high. The pump is pushing hard against thick oil, and that is what the gauge sees. Thick oil also flows poorly between the piston rings and the cylinder walls. Apply high power to a cold engine, and those parts take the load before the oil reaches them. That is why you warm the engine until the oil temperature reaches the flight manual minimum, before take-off or full-power checks.

Then the maximum. Hot oil thins. A thin oil film can break through under load, so lubrication fails. Very hot oil also breaks down chemically, and loses its ability to carry heat away. And a high oil temperature is a symptom too. It tells you the engine as a whole is running too hot.

The cylinder head temperature also has a minimum and a maximum.

The minimum: a cylinder that runs too cold burns its mixture incompletely. So deposits build up, and plugs foul. Many engines also have a minimum cylinder head temperature for applying high power, for the same reason as the oil.

The maximum: the cylinder heads are aluminium alloy, which loses strength rapidly as it gets hot. Above the maximum, the metal is weakened, and valves can burn. And the high temperature itself encourages detonation, which raises the temperature further. The maximum cylinder head temperature is the line beyond which the engine starts to damage itself.

Now maximum RPM. Everything that moves up and down in the engine, the pistons, the rods and the valves, is stopped and restarted twice every revolution. The force needed to do that rises with the square of the RPM. So 10 percent more RPM is about 21 percent more load. Exceed the red line, and those loads can overstress the crankshaft, the connecting rods and the valve gear. The valves may float, which means they fail to close in time.

The propeller is limited too. Its tips are moving fastest. As they approach the speed of sound, they lose efficiency and become noisy, and the blades face very high stresses. In a fixed-pitch aeroplane, the commonest way to overspeed is a dive with power on. That is why you throttle back when the nose goes down.

Next, the ignition check before take-off: the magneto check. It confirms that both ignition systems work independently. With the engine at the flight manual's check RPM, you select the left magneto alone and note the RPM. You return to both. Then you select the right magneto alone. Running on one magneto, each cylinder has only one spark. The burn is slower, and the RPM drops a little.

The check asks two questions. The first: is each drop within the limit? An excessive drop, or rough running, on one side means a fault on that side. That could be a fouled plug, a bad lead, or a faulty magneto. The second: is the difference between the two drops within its limit? A big difference means one side is weaker than the other.

Typical figures for a trainer are a maximum drop of about 125 to 175 RPM on either magneto, and a maximum difference of about 50 RPM between them. But the only numbers that count are the ones in your POH.

There is a third result to watch for, and that is no drop at all. A healthy engine always loses a little RPM on one magneto. If selecting left gives no drop, the right magneto is probably not being earthed. That means a broken P-lead, or a faulty switch. So the engine is still running on both, and that magneto will be live when the switch says off.

Picture three bars of RPM, side by side. On the left, the RPM on both magnetos. In the middle, a little lower, the left magneto alone. On the right, a little lower again, the right magneto alone. Each single-magneto bar may sit below the first by no more than the flight manual limit, typically about 125 to 175 RPM. And the two drops must be close to each other, typically within about 50 RPM.

Let's work one. Your flight manual allows a maximum drop of 125 RPM on either magneto, and a maximum difference of 50 RPM. The check RPM on both is 1,800. On the left magneto alone, it reads 1,710. On the right alone, 1,650. Does it pass? Pause if you'd like to try it first.
[[pause 6]]
The left drop: 1,800 minus 1,710 is 90 RPM. That is within 125. The right drop: 1,800 minus 1,650 is 150 RPM. That is over the 125 limit. And the difference between the drops: 150 minus 90 is 60 RPM. That is also over the 50 limit.

So the right ignition system has a problem, probably a fouled plug. Try the flight manual's clearing procedure: a higher RPM, leaned, briefly. Then repeat the check. If it still fails, taxi back. The aeroplane is unserviceable. In short: a right drop of 150 RPM and a difference of 60 RPM, so it fails.

Here is one for you to try. The same limits: 125 RPM drop, 50 RPM difference. On both, 1,800 RPM. Left alone, 1,720. Right alone, 1,690. Does it pass?
[[pause 6]]
The left drop: 1,800 minus 1,720 is 80 RPM. The right drop: 1,800 minus 1,690 is 110 RPM. Both are within 125. The difference: 110 minus 80 is 30 RPM, within 50. So it passes, provided the engine ran smoothly on each magneto.

Now the ignition check at shutdown: the dead cut. Recall from the lesson on piston engines that the ignition switch stops a magneto by connecting it to earth, through a wire called its P-lead. A broken P-lead leaves that magneto live, with the switch at off. You would never know from the cockpit. The engine runs normally on both, and the fault only shows itself when someone moves the propeller.

The dead cut check finds it. At idle, just before shutdown, you switch the ignition briefly to off. The engine should falter and begin to stop. You switch straight back to both before it does, then shut down normally with the mixture. If the engine keeps running with the switch at off, a magneto is still live. The aeroplane is then dangerous to anyone who touches the propeller. It must be reported, and fixed, before anyone goes near it.

[[diagram:dead-cut-check]]
Picture two wiring sketches side by side, each with a magneto, a P-lead and the switch at off. On the left, the P-lead is intact. It connects the magneto to earth, the magneto makes no spark, and the engine falters. The switch works. On the right, the P-lead is broken. The magneto is left unearthed, it keeps sparking with the switch at off, and the engine keeps running. That magneto is live, and anyone who moves the propeller could start the engine.

Next, prolonged use of the starter motor. The starter is a small, powerful electric motor, built for short bursts. Cranking draws a very large current, and the motor heats quickly. It has no cooling to cope with continuous running. Crank too long, and it overheats and can burn out. The battery runs flat too. So the flight manual gives a cranking limit, a number of seconds, and a cooling period before the next attempt. If the engine will not start within that limit, something else is wrong. It might be over-priming, a flat battery, or the fuel turned off. More cranking will not fix it.

Then pitot heat on the ground. The pitot heater is an electric element inside the pitot tube that keeps ice out of it in flight. It is powerful, because in flight a fast, cold airflow is carrying its heat away. On the ground there is no airflow. So the element overheats and can burn out. And the probe gets hot enough to burn your hand on the walk-round. Check that it works by feeling the probe warm after a brief switch-on, as the flight manual directs. Then switch it off.

And last, keeping the engine warm on long descents. A long glide from 4,500 ft back to the circuit at idle power is pleasant for you, and hard on the engine. There are three reasons.

The first is shock cooling. At idle the cylinders are making little heat, while the airflow over them, at a fast descent speed, is removing a lot. The cylinder heads cool rapidly. Different parts cool at different rates, and that sets up stresses that can crack the cylinders and distort them. A rapidly cooled engine can also glaze its cylinder bores.

The second is plug fouling. A long period at idle is a long period rich and cool. Those are exactly the conditions that foul plugs on the ground.

The third is carburettor ice. Low power means a nearly closed throttle butterfly, and that is where carburettor ice forms most readily. An engine at idle also has little heat to spare to melt it. The lesson on malfunctions covers carburettor ice in full.

The answer is to keep some power on, and descend at a moderate rate rather than glide. Descend in stages if you can. And apply carburettor heat, as the flight manual requires, before reducing power. Some flight manuals also recommend briefly opening the throttle every so often during a long glide, to warm the engine and clear the plugs.

[[diagram:shock-cooling-descent]]
Picture a graph of cylinder head temperature against time, from the top of descent on the left to the circuit on the right. One line drops steeply. That is the throttle closed to idle for a long glide. The engine makes little heat, the fast airflow removes a lot, and the parts cool at different rates. It ends up low and cold, rich and prone to fouling and carburettor ice. The other line slopes down gently, in steps. That is a descent with some power kept on, in stages. The temperature falls slowly, and the engine stays warm.

So let's pull the reasons together, one line each. The minimum oil pressure: below it the bearings and cylinder walls are not being lubricated or cooled, and the engine will seize. The minimum oil temperature: cold oil is thick and does not flow properly, so parts are starved of oil, while the pressure may read high. The maximum oil temperature: hot oil thins and breaks down, lubrication and cooling suffer, and it signals the engine is running too hot. The cylinder head temperature limits: too cold means incomplete combustion and fouling, and too hot means detonation, weakened metal and valve damage. Maximum RPM: the loads on the crankshaft, rods and valve gear rise with the square of the RPM, and the propeller tips approach the speed of sound.

Then the checks. The magneto check before take-off confirms both systems and all the plugs work. The dead cut at shutdown confirms the switch earths both magnetos. The starter is a short-duty motor that overheats if cranked too long. The pitot heater needs airflow to cool it. And a long idle descent risks shock cooling, fouled plugs and carburettor ice, so keep some power on.

Question. Why can the oil pressure read high just after a cold start, and why is that not good news?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: cold oil is thick, so the pump has to push hard against it, and the gauge shows a high pressure. But thick oil flows slowly through the narrow passages. So the bearings and cylinder walls are actually getting less oil than they need. That is why you wait for the minimum oil temperature before high power.

## Reading the exhaust smoke

The colour of the smoke from the exhaust tells you what is burning that should not be. There are two colours to know: blue and black.

Blue smoke is oil, being burnt in the cylinders. Oil is getting past a seal that should keep it out of the combustion chamber. There are two usual culprits. Worn piston rings, where the oil control ring is no longer scraping the cylinder wall clean. Or worn valve guides, where oil runs down the valve stem into the cylinder. An over-filled sump can do it too, by throwing too much oil onto the cylinder walls. Expect high oil consumption, and oil-fouled plugs. A puff of blue on start-up, after the aeroplane has been sitting, can be oil that has seeped past the rings of the lower cylinders overnight. But continuous blue smoke in flight is a maintenance problem.

Black smoke is unburnt fuel, leaving as soot. The mixture is too rich. So check the things that make it rich. First, the mixture control: perhaps it was not leaned when it should have been. Second, the primer: if it is not locked, it can keep feeding raw fuel into the cylinders. And third, the carburettor heat. Hot air is less dense, so the same fuel meets less air, and the mixture richens. The lesson on malfunctions explains that. A rich engine will also show fouled plugs, and higher fuel consumption.

So: blue is oil, black is fuel.

Question. After a flight with continuous blue exhaust smoke, the dipstick shows the engine used far more oil than usual. What is the likely cause?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: oil is reaching the combustion chambers and burning, most likely past worn piston rings or valve guides. That explains both the blue smoke and the high consumption. It is a maintenance item before the next flight.

## Bringing it together

It is a summer afternoon. You have waited 12 minutes at idle behind traffic at the Jandakot holding point. On the run-up, the left magneto drops 160 RPM, against a flight manual limit of 125, and the engine is rough on that side. The idle has fouled a plug.

You lean, run at the clearing RPM for the flight manual's period, and repeat the check. Now the drop is 95, and the difference is within limits.

Departing, you climb at full rich, at a speed that keeps air flowing over the cylinders, and you watch the cylinder head temperature. A lean mixture at climb power on a 38 degree day is detonation waiting to happen.

Coming home from the training area, you plan a powered descent rather than an idle glide, with carburettor heat as the flight manual requires. The cylinders stay warm, the plugs stay clean, and the throttle butterfly is not sitting nearly closed in humid coastal air.

At shutdown, the dead cut makes the engine falter, just as it should. Every one of those actions is a limit or a check from this lesson, doing its job.

## Key points

Let's recap.

Detonation is the unburnt end gas exploding after the spark. The causes: too lean at high power, high manifold pressure with low RPM, and too low an octane, all made worse by a high cylinder head temperature. The effects: rough running, a sharp rise in cylinder head temperature, power loss, and damage. The remedy: enrich, reduce power, lower the nose, and open the cowl flaps.

Prolonged idling runs cool and rich, and fouls the plugs, which shows up as a failed magneto check. Too rich in flight wastes fuel and fouls plugs. Too lean runs hot, and invites detonation.

The limits. Minimum oil pressure protects the bearings. The oil and cylinder head temperature limits keep the oil flowing and the metal strong. Maximum RPM protects the moving parts and the propeller. The magneto check proves both ignition systems. The dead cut proves the switch earths both magnetos. The starter and pitot heat limits stop them overheating. And a warm engine on descent avoids shock cooling, fouling and carburettor ice.

And the smoke. Blue smoke is oil burning, from worn rings or guides, or an over-filled sump. Black smoke is too rich a mixture.

A final exam tip. The examined pairs are cause and effect. A lean mixture at high power leads to detonation. Prolonged idling leads to plug fouling. Cold oil reads high pressure, but lubricates poorly. A long idle descent risks shock cooling and carburettor ice. And the dead cut finds a live magneto. For the magneto check, quote the flight manual limits. Typical trainer values are a drop of about 125 to 175 RPM, and a difference of about 50 RPM.
