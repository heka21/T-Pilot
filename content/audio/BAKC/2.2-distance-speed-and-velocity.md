This is BAKC 2.2, Distance, speed and velocity. By the end you will know which unit goes with which job, what a knot is, how to read a wind, and how the four airspeeds lead from the needle on the dial to your speed over the ground. There are worked examples and questions along the way, so pause whenever you want a moment to think.

## What the exam expects (BAKC 2.2)

The exam wants four things from you. First, the units for distance over the ground: nautical miles for navigation, and metres and kilometres for visibility. Second, the definition of the knot as the unit of aircraft speed. Third, what wind velocity means, and how to read it the way it is written. And fourth, to tell apart indicated airspeed, calibrated airspeed, true airspeed and groundspeed, and to say what turns each one into the next.

## Why this matters

Plan your first cross-country from Jandakot to Northam, and you will meet every idea in this lesson before you start the engine. The distance on the chart is in nautical miles. The visibility in the forecast is in kilometres. The wind is written as something like {250/15|two five zero at 15}. And the speed you will use to work out the flight time is not the one on the airspeed indicator.

Get them muddled and the flight time is wrong. So the fuel is wrong. So the reserve you thought you had is not there.

The exam asks short, precise questions here. Which unit goes with which job. What a knot is. Which way a wind of {090/25|zero nine zero at 25} blows. And the order of the four airspeeds. It also expects you to do the one-line arithmetic that links them. This lesson builds the four speeds from the instrument outwards, because once you see what each correction is for, the order looks after itself.

## Distance over the ground: the nautical mile

Look at the edge of any aeronautical chart and you will find the latitude scale. It is marked in degrees, and each degree is divided into 60 minutes. That scale is the reason aviation measures distance in nautical miles.

The Earth is close to a sphere. A full circle around it, through the poles, is 360 degrees of latitude, each of 60 minutes. A nautical mile was chosen to be one minute of latitude. The modern definition fixes it at exactly 1,852 metres, about 6,076 ft. That is one minute of latitude, to within any accuracy you will ever need.

That choice pays off every time you plan. Want to know how far 18 NM is on the chart? Open your dividers to 18 minutes on the latitude scale beside the route, and you have it. On any chart, at any scale. And because one degree of latitude is 60 minutes, it is also 60 NM. So Perth to Geraldton, about 3 degrees of latitude further north, is roughly 190 NM in a straight line north-south.

[[diagram:nautical-mile-latitude]]
Picture two drawings side by side. On the left, a slice through the Earth from pole to pole. One degree of latitude, measured at the centre, is divided into 60 minutes, and each minute of arc along the surface is one nautical mile. So one degree is 60 NM. On the right, the latitude scale on the side of a chart, with a pair of dividers opened to 18 minutes. That is 18 NM, about Rottnest to Jandakot. The point is that the latitude scale is also a distance scale, on any chart.

All navigation distances are in nautical miles. Distances between places. DME ranges, the distance-measuring equipment readouts. Radius limits around an aerodrome. And the lengths of the legs on your nav log. Rottnest is about 18 NM from Jandakot is the normal way to say it.

So there are three jobs and their units. Navigation distances are in nautical miles. Visibility is in metres and kilometres, such as visibility 5,000 m, or visibility 10 km. And runway length and width are in metres, which you will meet in the lesson on units of measurement.

There are three conversions you need. One nautical mile is 1,852 m, or 1.852 km. One kilometre is 0.54 NM. And one statute mile is 0.87 NM, though you will rarely meet statute miles in Australia.

[[diagram:units-conversion-ladder]]
Picture three pairs of rulers laid side by side, each pair with one unit along the top and the other along the bottom, so you can read straight across. Distance: 1 nautical mile lines up with 1.852 km, 1 km with 0.54 NM, and 1 statute mile with 0.87 NM. Speed: 110 kt lines up with 204 km/h, and 60 km/h with 32 kt. And fuel: avgas weighs about 0.72 kg per litre, so 100 L lines up with about 72 kg, and 120 L with 86.4 kg.

## Visibility: metres and kilometres

Visibility, the distance you can see and identify objects, is the odd one out. It is measured and reported in metres for lower values, and in kilometres for higher ones. Visibility 3,000 m. Visibility 10 km.

That is because visibility is a weather observation. It is made by observers and instruments on the ground, using the metric system. And the VMC minima, the weather you need to fly visually, which you will learn in the flight rules, are written in the same units.

The practical consequence is that you will often hold two different units in your head at once. The forecast says visibility 5 km in showers, and the next town on your route is 8 NM away. When you need to compare them, convert one into the other. A kilometre is a little over half a nautical mile. So the visibility in nautical miles is a little over half the number of kilometres.

Let's work one. The forecast visibility is 8 km. The next landmark on track is 5 NM ahead. Will you see it straight away? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
First, the visibility in nautical miles. 8 times 0.54 is 4.3 NM. Now compare. 4.3 is less than 5, so the landmark is beyond the visibility when you start looking. You will not see it until you are about 4 NM from it. So you hold heading and time, rather than search for it.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. It offers kilometres for navigation and nautical miles for visibility, the wrong way round. And statute miles appear in American material. In Australia, navigation is in nautical miles, and visibility is in metres and kilometres.

Question. Why is the nautical mile a convenient unit for navigation?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because one nautical mile is one minute of latitude. So the latitude scale on the side of every chart is a ready-made distance scale, and one degree of latitude is 60 NM.

## The knot

Once distance is in nautical miles, speed follows. A knot is a speed of one nautical mile per hour. Airspeeds, groundspeeds and wind speeds in Australian aviation are all in knots.

The per hour is built into the word. So a pilot never says knots per hour. That would be an acceleration, not a speed. The name comes from ships, which once measured their speed by counting knots on a line paid out over the stern in a fixed time.

The knot is to the nautical mile what the kilometre per hour is to the kilometre. So converting between them uses the same 1.852. Kilometres per hour equals knots times 1.852. And knots equals kilometres per hour divided by 1.852.

Let's work one. You cruise at 110 kt. And you want to compare a speed limit of 100 km/h with your own speeds. Convert each one. Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
110 kt in kilometres per hour is 110 times 1.852. That is {203.7|two hundred and three point seven}, or about 204 km/h. And 100 km/h in knots is 100 divided by 1.852, which is 54 kt. So a light trainer lifts off at a speed a car on the freeway would recognise, and cruises at about twice it.

Now the number you will use most. In flight you rarely think in hours. Divide the speed by 60, and you get the nautical miles covered each minute. That is the number that makes mental navigation easy. At 60 kt you cover 1 NM a minute. At 90 kt, 1.5 NM a minute. And at 120 kt, 2 NM a minute. So at 120 kt, a 25 minute leg covers 25 times 2, which is 50 NM. And a town 10 NM ahead is 5 minutes away.

The general formula for time on a leg is the distance divided by the speed over the ground, turned into minutes. Time in minutes equals the distance in nautical miles, divided by the groundspeed in knots, times 60.

The speed in that formula is the groundspeed, because the distance is over the ground. Why that is not the number on the airspeed indicator is the rest of this lesson.

Question. What is wrong with saying an aeroplane cruises at 100 knots per hour?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: a knot is already one nautical mile per hour. So knots per hour would mean a change of speed each hour, an acceleration. The correct phrase is simply 100 knots.

## Wind velocity

A speed is how fast something moves. A velocity is how fast, and in what direction. Wind is a velocity. 15 kt of wind blowing straight down the runway, and 15 kt blowing straight across it, are the same speed but very different winds. So wind velocity is given as a direction and a speed together.

The convention is that the direction is where the wind blows from, as a three-figure group, followed by the speed in knots. So {270/15|two seven zero at 15} means a 15 kt wind blowing from the west, towards the east. A wind from {090|zero nine zero} is an easterly.

That matches how you feel wind. You face into a westerly wind when you face west. And the sea breeze at Perth is a south-westerly, because it comes off the sea from the south-west.

[[diagram:wind-velocity-from]]
Picture an arrow of wind coming in from the left, the west, and blowing towards the right, the east. That is {270/15|two seven zero at 15}: from the west at 15 knots. An aeroplane flying west, into it, has a headwind. An aeroplane flying east, with it, has a tailwind.

Naming the wind for where it comes from makes the headwind question simple. If the wind direction is the same as your heading, the wind is coming straight at your nose: a pure headwind. If it is the reciprocal of your heading, it is a pure tailwind. Anything in between has a headwind or tailwind part, and a crosswind part. And a runway wind is usually some of each.

[[widget:crosswind-component]]
Imagine swinging the wind round from straight down the runway towards straight across it, and watching the two parts change. The point to notice is that just 30 degrees off the runway already puts half the wind across it. Compare that crosswind with the demonstrated figure in the POH.

The direction reference matters, and it connects with the lesson on direction of flight. Winds in forecasts, the TAF, the GAF or the area forecast, and winds in METAR and SPECI reports, are given in degrees true. That is because they are weather products drawn on true-north maps. Surface winds passed by ATC or on an ATIS are in degrees magnetic, so you can compare them directly with the runway number. Around Perth the difference is only a degree or two. Elsewhere it can be ten.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. A wind is named for where it comes from, not where it goes. A westerly, {270|two seven zero}, blows towards the east. It is a headwind for an aeroplane flying west, and a tailwind for one flying east. The exam's wrong answer is the reverse.

Question. The ATIS at Jandakot gives a wind of {240/12|two four zero at 12}, and runway 24 is in use. What is the wind doing to the take-off?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: it is a 12 kt headwind, straight down the runway. The wind comes from {240|two four zero} magnetic, and the runway points {240|two four zero} magnetic. Both figures are magnetic, which is exactly why ATIS winds are given that way.

## Four airspeeds: IAS, CAS, TAS and GS

Now the speed of the aeroplane itself. There are four numbers, and each answers a different question. The way to keep them straight is to start with the instrument and correct it, one error at a time, until you arrive at the speed over the ground.

[[diagram:airspeed-chain-ictg]]
Picture four boxes in a row, joined by arrows, reading left to right. Indicated airspeed, then calibrated, then true, then groundspeed. The first arrow corrects for instrument and position error. The second corrects for air density. The third corrects for wind. Under the boxes are two examples. At 8,000 ft, an IAS of 100 kt is about 116 kt TAS. And a TAS of 105 kt into a 20 kt headwind gives 85 kt over the ground. Always I, C, T, G.

Let's take them one at a time. First, indicated airspeed, IAS: what the instrument shows.

The airspeed indicator does not measure speed directly. A pitot tube, facing forward into the airflow, feels the air rammed into it. A static vent, on the side of the fuselage, feels the still pressure of the surrounding air. The airspeed indicator measures the difference. That difference is the dynamic pressure, the extra pressure caused by the aeroplane's motion through the air. Dynamic pressure grows with the speed, and with the density of the air. Formally, it is a half, times the air density, times the speed squared. The dial is marked in knots on the assumption that the air has standard sea-level density.

[[diagram:pitot-static-system]]
Picture the pipework behind the panel. Out at the front, the pitot tube faces the airflow and senses pitot pressure, the total pressure. It feeds only one instrument, the airspeed indicator. On the side of the fuselage, the static vent senses the ambient pressure. It feeds all three pressure instruments: the airspeed indicator, the altimeter and the vertical speed indicator. The airspeed indicator measures the difference between the two. And an alternate static source inside the cockpit can replace a blocked static vent.

The reading on the dial is the indicated airspeed. It is the speed the wing feels, because the wing's lift depends on the same dynamic pressure. That is why the aeroplane stalls, rotates and reaches its structural limits at the same IAS whatever the altitude. And it is why every speed in the POH, and every coloured arc on the ASI, is an indicated speed. You fly the aeroplane on IAS.

Second, calibrated airspeed, CAS: correcting the instrument's errors.

No pitot-static installation is perfect. The static vent sits on the side of a fuselage that disturbs the air around it. As the angle of attack and the flap setting change, the pressure it senses changes slightly. That is position error, also called installation error. The instrument itself, with its capsule and gearing, has a small instrument error as well.

Correct the IAS for those two, and you have the calibrated airspeed: the IAS the instrument would show if it were perfect. The correction comes from the airspeed calibration table in section 5 of the POH. In a light aeroplane it is usually only a few knots, largest at low speed with flap down.

Third, true airspeed, TAS: correcting for density.

Now climb. The air thins. So at the same speed through it, the pitot tube receives less dynamic pressure, and the needle reads lower. Hold the same IAS, and the aeroplane must actually be moving faster through the thinner air to produce the same dynamic pressure.

The real speed of the aeroplane through the air mass is the true airspeed. It is CAS corrected for air density, which means for the pressure altitude and the temperature. In practice you do it with the flight computer.

A rule of thumb gives you the size of the effect. TAS exceeds IAS by about 2 percent per 1,000 ft of altitude. At 8,000 ft, an IAS of 100 kt is roughly 100 times 1.16, which is 116 kt TAS. At sea level on a standard day, the two are the same. TAS is the speed you use for navigation. It is how fast the aeroplane is really moving through the air, before the wind is considered.

And fourth, groundspeed: correcting for wind.

The air mass itself is moving over the ground. The groundspeed is the aeroplane's actual speed over the ground. It is the TAS combined with the wind velocity. Flying straight into a headwind, the wind is subtracted. Straight downwind, it is added. With the wind at an angle, only part of it acts along your track, and you will work those cases on the flight computer in the navigation unit. Groundspeed is the speed for time, distance and fuel: how long the leg will take.

[[diagram:groundspeed-headwind-tailwind]]
Picture two bars drawn to scale, one above the other. Both start from the same TAS of 105 knots. The top one has a 20 knot headwind cut off its end, leaving a groundspeed of 85 knots. The bottom one has a 20 knot tailwind added, reaching 125 knots.

So, the four speeds once more, with their jobs. Indicated airspeed is what the airspeed indicator shows. You read it directly, and you fly the aeroplane on it: the stall, the rotate speed, the limits. Calibrated airspeed is IAS corrected for instrument error and position error, using the correction from the POH table. It is the input to the TAS calculation. True airspeed is the actual speed through the air mass. It is CAS corrected for air density, that is, pressure altitude and temperature, and you use it for navigation and flight planning. And groundspeed is the actual speed over the ground. It is TAS combined with the wind velocity, and you use it for time, distance and fuel.

Let's work one. Your TAS is 105 kt. First you track directly into a 20 kt headwind. Then the same aeroplane has a 20 kt tailwind. What is the groundspeed each time?
[[pause 5]]
With the headwind, 105 minus 20 is 85 kt. With the tailwind, 105 plus 20 is 125 kt. The same aeroplane, at the same power, covers the ground almost 50 percent faster one way than the other. That is why the return leg of a there-and-back flight in a strong wind is never the same length.

Now a flight time. Your TAS is 95 kt, there is a 15 kt headwind straight on the nose, and the leg is 48 NM. How long will it take? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
First the groundspeed. 95 minus 15 is 80 kt. Then the time. 48 divided by 80 is 0.6, and 0.6 times 60 is 36 minutes. In still air, the same leg would take 48 divided by 95, times 60, which is about 30 minutes. So the headwind has added 6 minutes, and 6 minutes of fuel.

Now try the whole chain yourself. You are at 6,000 ft. Your airspeed indicator shows 100 kt, and the POH table says CAS is 2 kt less than IAS at that speed. You have a 20 kt tailwind straight along your track. Using the 2 percent rule of thumb, roughly what is your groundspeed? This one needs a moment.
[[pause 8]]
CAS first. 100 minus 2 is 98 kt. Then TAS. 6,000 ft is 6 lots of 2 percent, which is 12 percent. 98 times 1.12 is {109.8|one hundred and nine point eight}, about 110 kt. Then groundspeed. 110 plus 20 is about 130 kt. Each step corrected one thing: the instrument, then the density, then the wind. The answer is about 130 kt.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. TAS is higher than IAS at altitude, not lower. The needle reads low in thin air; the aeroplane is going faster than it shows. And the order never changes: I, C, T, G. A wrong answer that corrects IAS for wind to get TAS has skipped two steps, and mixed up the last one.

Question. Why do you fly the approach on IAS, but plan the flight on TAS and groundspeed?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the wing's lift and the stall depend on dynamic pressure, which is exactly what the airspeed indicator measures. So the right approach speed is the same IAS at any altitude. Navigation needs the real speed through the air, the TAS, and then over the ground, the groundspeed, to work out time and fuel.

## Bringing it together

Jandakot to Northam is about 48 NM on the chart. That is distance in nautical miles. The forecast gives visibility 10 km, a little over 5 NM, so you will see Northam from about 5 NM out.

The GAF wind at your planned 4,500 ft is {060/15|zero six zero at 15}, in degrees true. Your track is about {056|zero five six} true, so the wind is almost straight on the nose.

Your POH gives a cruise of 87 kt indicated at that level. The 2 percent rule adds 4.5 lots of 2 percent, which is 9 percent. That turns 87 times 1.09 into about 95 kt TAS. And the 15 kt headwind takes that down to a groundspeed of about 80 kt. 48 NM at 80 kt is 36 minutes, against about 30 in still air.

On the ground at Northam, the AWIS, the automatic weather station, gives a surface wind in degrees magnetic, ready to compare with the runway number. Every unit and every speed in this lesson has done a job.

## Key points

Let's recap.

Navigation distance is in nautical miles. One nautical mile is 1,852 m, which is one minute of latitude. Visibility is in metres and kilometres, and 1 km is 0.54 NM.

A knot is one nautical mile per hour. Knots times 1.852 gives kilometres per hour. And the speed divided by 60 gives nautical miles per minute, so 120 kt is 2 NM a minute.

Wind velocity is a direction and a speed, written direction then speed, with the direction the wind blows from. {270/15|two seven zero at 15} is a 15 kt westerly. Forecast and METAR winds are true. ATC and ATIS winds are magnetic.

IAS is the dial. CAS corrects instrument and position error. TAS corrects for density, about 2 percent per 1,000 ft. And groundspeed corrects for wind. Fly on IAS. Navigate on TAS and groundspeed.

A final exam tip. Have these by heart. One nautical mile is 1,852 m. A knot is 1 NM per hour. Wind is named for where it comes from. And the order is I, C, T, G: instrument and position error, then density, then wind. Expect a knots to kilometres per hour conversion, a question on what {090/25|zero nine zero at 25} means, and a groundspeed question that makes you apply the 2 percent rule before adding or subtracting the wind. Read whether the question gives you IAS or TAS before you start.
