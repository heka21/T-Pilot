This is BAKC 2.3, Time. By the end you will be able to write any time of day as a four-figure group, and convert between Western Standard Time and UTC in either direction, with the date right, even when midnight gets in the way. There are worked examples and questions along the way, so pause whenever you want a moment to think.

## What the exam expects (BAKC 2.3)

The exam wants three things from you. First, to write any time of day as a four-figure group on the 24-hour clock. Second, to convert a local standard time, which for you is Western Standard Time, into UTC, including the date. And third, to convert a UTC time back into local standard time, again including the date.

## Why this matters

Almost every time you read in aviation is in UTC. And almost every time you live by is in Western Standard Time, WST.

The TAF for Perth that tells you when the fog will lift. The NOTAM that closes a runway at Jandakot for works. The activity times of a restricted area on the way to Bunbury. The time you put on your flight notification. All of those are in UTC. Your alarm clock, last light at York, and the time your passengers turn up are in WST.

Every pre-flight briefing involves converting between the two. And the mistake that matters is rarely the hours. It is the date. A NOTAM that closes an aerodrome from {2300|twenty-three hundred} UTC on the 3rd closes it at 7 o'clock on the morning of the 4th in Perth.

The exam asks exactly these conversions, usually with a midnight in the way, and it expects the four-figure format throughout.

## The 24-hour clock as a 4-figure group

The ordinary clock uses the numbers 1 to 12 twice a day, and leans on {am|a.m.} and {pm|p.m.} to tell the two halves apart. Spoken over a radio, written in a hurry or read on a printout, those two letters are the easiest thing in the world to lose. And losing them puts you 12 hours out.

Aviation removes the problem by counting the hours straight through the day, from 0 to 23. There is then only one 7 o'clock. {0700|Oh seven hundred} is the morning. {1900|Nineteen hundred} is the evening.

The time is written as a four-figure group. Two figures for the hours, then two for the minutes. No colon, and no {am|a.m.} or {pm|p.m.}. The day starts at {0000|zero zero zero zero}, midnight, the beginning of the day. Its last minute is {2359|twenty-three fifty-nine}. Leading zeros are always written, so that every time has four figures. Five past seven in the morning is {0705|oh seven oh five}, not 705. Spoken, it is zero seven zero five.

The conversion from the 12-hour clock follows from how the day is counted. There are three cases.

First, morning times keep their hours, with a leading zero. {7:05 am|Seven oh five in the morning} is {0705|oh seven oh five}.

Second, from 1 in the afternoon onward, add 12 to the hour. {3:45 pm|Three forty-five in the afternoon} is 15 and 45, so {1545|fifteen forty-five}.

And third, the two awkward hours, the ones with 12 in them. Noon is {1200|twelve hundred}. And {12:30 pm|twelve thirty in the afternoon} is {1230|twelve thirty}, with nothing to add, because that is already the twelfth hour. But the hour after midnight is hour zero. So {12:20 am|twelve twenty in the morning}, just after midnight, is {0020|zero zero twenty}.

At the other end of the day, {11:59 pm|eleven fifty-nine at night} is {2359|twenty-three fifty-nine}.

Let's work some. A briefing is at {9:40 pm|nine forty at night}. A fuel delivery is at {12:15 am|twelve fifteen in the morning}. And last light is printed as {1925|nineteen twenty-five}. Convert each one. Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
{9:40 pm|Nine forty at night} is after 1 in the afternoon, so add 12 to the hour. 9 plus 12 is 21, giving {2140|twenty-one forty}. Next, {12:15 am|twelve fifteen in the morning} is in the hour after midnight, hour zero. So it is {0015|zero zero fifteen}. And {1925|nineteen twenty-five} is more than {1259|twelve fifty-nine}, so subtract 12 from the hour. 19 minus 12 is 7, giving {7:25 pm|seven twenty-five in the evening}. Each answer in the 24-hour form has exactly four figures, and no {am|a.m.} or {pm|p.m.}.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. The hour after midnight is zero zero, not 12 and not 24. {12:20 am|Twelve twenty in the morning} is {0020|zero zero twenty}. And never drop the leading zero. 705 on its own is not a valid time group.

Now adding times, where the minutes carry at 60. Flight planning constantly adds a flight time to a clock time. Departure at {1545|fifteen forty-five}, flight time 1 hour 35 minutes: when do you land?

The catch is that a four-figure group looks like an ordinary number, but it is not one. The last two figures are minutes, and they run only to 59. So add hours to hours and minutes to minutes, separately. When the minutes reach 60 or more, take 60 away and carry one hour across. Never add the two groups as if they were whole numbers. {1545|Fifteen forty-five} plus 135 gives {1680|sixteen eighty}, and that is not a time.

Let's work that one properly. Departure is {1545|fifteen forty-five}, and the flight time is 1 hour 35 minutes. Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
Minutes first. 45 plus 35 is 80 minutes, which is 1 hour 20 minutes. So write 20, and carry 1 hour. Then the hours. 15, plus 1, plus the 1 you carried, is 17. So the landing time is {1720|seventeen twenty}.

The same rule, run backwards, gives the latest departure time for a fixed arrival. When the minutes would go negative, borrow 60 minutes from the hours.

Question. Why does aviation write times as four-figure groups, instead of using {am|a.m.} and {pm|p.m.}?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because {am|a.m.} and {pm|p.m.} are easily missed or misheard, and a mistake puts you 12 hours out. Counting the hours from zero zero to 23 gives each time of day exactly one number. And the fixed four figures make a missing digit obvious.

## UTC: one clock for the whole world

The sun is overhead at noon somewhere different every moment. The Earth turns 360 degrees in 24 hours, which is 15 degrees of longitude every hour. Local clocks follow the sun, roughly. So when it is noon in Perth, it is about 2 in the afternoon in Sydney, early morning in London, and the middle of the night in New York.

That is fine on the ground. But aviation is an international network. A forecast, a NOTAM or a flight plan written in local time would mean different things to the forecaster in Melbourne, the controller in Perth, and the pilot flying across a time-zone boundary. So aviation uses one clock for everyone.

That clock is UTC, Coordinated Universal Time. It is the time at zero degrees of longitude, through Greenwich in England. It is kept by atomic clocks, and it is never adjusted for summer. Flight notifications, NOTAMs, forecasts such as the TAF and the GAF, METARs, ATC logs and SARTIMEs are all in UTC. It is often marked with a Z, for Zulu. So {0530Z|oh five thirty zulu} means {0530|oh five thirty} UTC.

Every local time zone is defined as an offset from UTC. Perth is at about 116 degrees east. The zone that covers Western Australia is set at 120 degrees east. 120 divided by 15 is 8, so the zone is 8 hours of the Earth's turn ahead of Greenwich. Western Standard Time is UTC plus 8.

There are three zones across Australia. Western Standard Time, in Western Australia, is UTC plus 8. Central Standard Time, in South Australia and the Northern Territory, is UTC plus 9 and a half. And Eastern Standard Time, in Queensland, New South Wales, the Australian Capital Territory, Victoria and Tasmania, is UTC plus 10.

Plus means ahead. The sun rises over Australia long before it rises over Greenwich, so Australian clocks are ahead of UTC. When it is {0800|oh eight hundred} in Perth, it is still midnight UTC. Hold on to that picture, and the direction of every conversion follows from it.

Western Australia does not use daylight saving. So WST is UTC plus 8 all year round. Some eastern and southern states move their clocks an hour forward in summer. That changes their offset from UTC, and their difference from Perth, for part of the year. You need to remember that if you read times from those states, or fly there. For the exam, the conversions are to standard time.

[[diagram:utc-and-wst-strip]]
Picture two time strips, one above the other, lined up so the same moment sits in the same column. UTC is on top, and WST underneath, always 8 hours further along. Because of that offset, the WST date changes at {1600|sixteen hundred} UTC. Two examples sit on the strips. {1530|Fifteen thirty} WST is {0730|oh seven thirty} UTC, the same day. And {0545|oh five forty-five} WST on Tuesday 7 October is {2145|twenty-one forty-five} UTC on Monday 6 October. Early-morning WST is still yesterday in UTC.

Question. Is Perth ahead of UTC or behind it, and how can you remember which?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: ahead, by 8 hours. The sun rises in Australia first. When Perth is already at {0800|oh eight hundred} on its working day, the UTC day is only just beginning, at {0000|zero zero zero zero}.

## Local standard time to UTC

Perth is 8 hours ahead. So to go from WST to UTC, you go back 8 hours: subtract 8. If the subtraction drops below {0000|zero zero zero zero}, you have gone back past midnight. Add 24 to the hours to bring the time back onto the clock, and move the date back one day.

Let's work two easy ones first. A planned departure from Jandakot is at {1530|fifteen thirty} WST, and a briefing is at {1115|eleven fifteen} WST. What are they in UTC?
[[pause 5]]
{1530|Fifteen thirty} minus 8 hours is {0730|oh seven thirty} UTC, the same date. And {1115|eleven fifteen} minus 8 hours is {0315|oh three fifteen} UTC, the same date. Both WST times are after {0800|oh eight hundred}. So subtracting 8 hours leaves a positive time, and the date does not change.

Now one across midnight. A dawn departure is at {0545|oh five forty-five} WST on Tuesday 7 October. What is that in UTC? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
{0545|Oh five forty-five} minus 8 hours is negative. You have gone back past midnight. So add 24 hours to the starting time, which makes it {2945|twenty-nine forty-five}, and subtract again. {2945|Twenty-nine forty-five} minus 8 hours is {2145|twenty-one forty-five}. Then move the date back one day. The answer is {2145|twenty-one forty-five} UTC on Monday 6 October. You take off on Tuesday morning, but the flight notification shows Monday night.

Here is one for you to try. A night VFR instructor plans to depart at {0300|oh three hundred} WST on Saturday. What is that in UTC, with the day?
[[pause 6]]
{0300|Oh three hundred} minus 8 hours is negative, so add 24. That gives {2700|twenty-seven hundred}, and minus 8 hours is {1900|nineteen hundred}. You crossed midnight going backwards, so the day moves back. The answer is {1900|nineteen hundred} UTC on Friday. And the sanity check: any WST time before {0800|oh eight hundred} is the previous day in UTC.

## UTC to local standard time

Going the other way, you go forward 8 hours: add 8. If the result is {2400|twenty-four hundred} or more, you have gone forward past midnight. Subtract 24 to bring the time back onto the clock, and move the date forward one day.

[[diagram:utc-to-wst-activity]]
Picture two clocks for the same moments: a UTC scale on top, and a WST scale underneath, lined up so each instant sits directly above the other. A restricted area is listed as active from {2200|twenty-two hundred} to {0600|oh six hundred} UTC. Read straight down, and it is active from {0600|oh six hundred} to {1400|fourteen hundred} WST. Add 8 hours, and notice that the start falls on the next WST date.

Let's work two on the same date. A TAF for Perth becomes valid at {0000|zero zero zero zero} UTC, and a SIGMET, a warning of hazardous weather, expires at {1430|fourteen thirty} UTC. What are those in WST?
[[pause 5]]
{0000|Zero zero zero zero} plus 8 hours is {0800|oh eight hundred} WST, the same date. And {1430|fourteen thirty} plus 8 hours is {2230|twenty-two thirty} WST, the same date. Both results are below {2400|twenty-four hundred}, so the date stays the same.

Now across midnight. A NOTAM is effective from {2300|twenty-three hundred} UTC on 3 October. When does it start in Perth? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
{2300|Twenty-three hundred} plus 8 hours is {3100|thirty-one hundred}, which is past {2400|twenty-four hundred}. Subtract 24 hours, and you get {0700|oh seven hundred}. Then move the date forward one day. So it starts at {0700|oh seven hundred} WST on 4 October. If you read only the date on the NOTAM, you would think the restriction started on the 3rd, a day early.

Try one yourself. A NOTAM closes a runway from {1700|seventeen hundred} UTC on Thursday. When does it start in Perth?
[[pause 6]]
{1700|Seventeen hundred} plus 8 hours is {2500|twenty-five hundred}. Subtract 24 to get {0100|oh one hundred}, and move the day forward. The answer is {0100|oh one hundred} WST on Friday. The sanity check: any UTC time from {1600|sixteen hundred} onward is the next day in WST.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. The hours are rarely wrong. The date is. Any WST time before {0800|oh eight hundred} is the previous day in UTC. And any UTC time from {1600|sixteen hundred} onward is the next day in WST. Every time you add or subtract 8, ask whether you crossed midnight. If you did, change the date in the same direction.

Now, checking your answer. Two habits catch nearly every conversion error.

The first is to convert back. If {1700|seventeen hundred} UTC Thursday became {0100|oh one hundred} WST Friday, then {0100|oh one hundred} WST Friday minus 8 hours must give {1700|seventeen hundred} UTC Thursday. It does, so the answer stands.

The second is to picture the two boundaries where the date changes. Perth's midnight is {1600|sixteen hundred} UTC. And the UTC midnight is {0800|oh eight hundred} in Perth. Between {0800|oh eight hundred} and {2359|twenty-three fifty-nine} WST, the two clocks share a date. Between {0000|zero zero zero zero} and {0759|oh seven fifty-nine} WST, Perth has already moved on to tomorrow while UTC is still on today, so the WST date is one ahead. Put any time on that picture before you calculate, and you know whether the date will change before the arithmetic tells you.

Question. A TAF is valid from {2000|twenty hundred} UTC on the 21st. Without working it out fully, is the WST start time on the 21st or the 22nd, and why?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the 22nd. Any UTC time from {1600|sixteen hundred} onward, plus 8 hours, passes {2400|twenty-four hundred}. So it falls on the next WST date.

## Other Australian time zones

The same method works for any zone. Add the offset to go from UTC to local. Subtract it to go from local to UTC. And watch midnight. You will meet eastern times in national NOTAMs, in area forecasts covering the Nullarbor, and whenever you plan a flight across the border.

Let's work one. A NOTAM for Brisbane says {2200|twenty-two hundred} UTC. A Sydney briefing says {2330|twenty-three thirty} UTC. Both cities are on Eastern Standard Time, UTC plus 10. What are the local times? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
Brisbane first. {2200|Twenty-two hundred} plus 10 hours is {3200|thirty-two hundred}. Take away {2400|twenty-four hundred}, and it is {0800|oh eight hundred} Eastern Standard Time, the next day. Sydney: {2330|twenty-three thirty} plus 10 hours is {3330|thirty-three thirty}. Take away {2400|twenty-four hundred}, and it is {0930|oh nine thirty}, the next day. The eastern states are 10 hours ahead of UTC, so their date changes at {1400|fourteen hundred} UTC, two hours before Perth's.

Central Standard Time, in South Australia and the Northern Territory, is UTC plus 9 and a half. So conversions there carry half an hour. {0100|Oh one hundred} UTC is {1030|ten thirty} Central Standard Time. A NOTAM for Alice Springs starting at {2200|twenty-two hundred} UTC begins at {2200|twenty-two hundred} plus 9 and a half hours, which is {3130|thirty-one thirty}. That is past {2400|twenty-four hundred}, so it is {0730|oh seven thirty} Central Standard Time, the next day.

The half hour changes nothing about the method. It only makes the arithmetic less round, which is where slips happen. The difference between Perth and the eastern states is 2 hours on standard time, and more when daylight saving is in force there.

Question. It is {1000|ten hundred} WST in Perth. What standard time is it in Brisbane, and why?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: {1200|twelve hundred}, Eastern Standard Time. Both are measured from UTC. Perth is UTC plus 8, and Brisbane is UTC plus 10, so Brisbane is 2 hours ahead of Perth. Going through UTC: {1000|ten hundred} WST is {0200|oh two hundred} UTC, and plus 10 hours is {1200|twelve hundred}.

## Bringing it together

It is a Friday evening in December. You are planning a Saturday morning flight from Jandakot to Kalbarri, about 3 hours away.

The TAF for Kalbarri is valid from {1800|eighteen hundred} UTC on Friday. In Perth, that is {1800|eighteen hundred} plus 8 hours, which is {2600|twenty-six hundred}, so {0200|oh two hundred} WST on Saturday. It covers your whole flight.

A NOTAM closes the Kalbarri runway for works from {0100|oh one hundred} to {0300|oh three hundred} UTC on Saturday. Adding 8 hours, that is {0900|oh nine hundred} to {1100|eleven hundred} WST on Saturday. So if you take off at {0630|oh six thirty} and land at {0930|oh nine thirty}, you will arrive in the middle of the closure.

You bring the departure forward to {0540|oh five forty} WST. First light has long passed at that time of year. You will land at {0840|oh eight forty}, twenty minutes before the works start.

Now the flight notification. The take-off time is before {0800|oh eight hundred} WST, so in UTC it is the day before. {2940|Twenty-nine forty} minus 8 hours is {2140|twenty-one forty}, on Friday. The landing, {0840|oh eight forty} minus 8 hours, is {0040|zero zero forty} UTC, on Saturday. You set a SARTIME, the time search and rescue starts looking if you have not cancelled it, of {0910|oh nine ten} WST, which is {0110|oh one ten} UTC on Saturday.

One flight, on one Perth morning, and the paperwork spans two UTC dates. That is normal. And it is exactly where a careless conversion would have put you on a closed runway.

## Key points

Let's recap.

Times are four-figure groups on the 24-hour clock, from {0000|zero zero zero zero} to {2359|twenty-three fifty-nine}. Hours, then minutes. No colon, no {am|a.m.} or {pm|p.m.}, and leading zeros kept. From 1 in the afternoon, add 12 to the hour. And the hour after midnight is zero zero.

WST to UTC: subtract 8 hours. If you go below {0000|zero zero zero zero}, add 24 and move the date back a day. Any WST time before {0800|oh eight hundred} is the previous UTC date.

UTC to WST: add 8 hours. If you reach {2400|twenty-four hundred} or more, subtract 24 and move the date forward a day. Any UTC time from {1600|sixteen hundred} onward is the next WST date.

Western Australia has no daylight saving. Central Standard Time is UTC plus 9 and a half, and Eastern Standard Time is UTC plus 10.

A final exam tip. Expect a 12-hour to four-figure conversion, and at least one UTC conversion in each direction, often with the date in the options. Before you choose, run the sanity check. Perth is ahead of UTC, so the WST number is the bigger one, unless you crossed midnight. WST before {0800|oh eight hundred} is yesterday in UTC. UTC from {1600|sixteen hundred} is tomorrow in WST. And if the question involves another state, it means standard time, unless it says otherwise.
