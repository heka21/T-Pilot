This is BAKC 4.4, Wake turbulence. By the end you will know what makes a wing-tip vortex strong, why it is dangerous, which way it turns, where it goes in the cruise and near the runway, how wind moves it, and where to lift off and touch down to stay clear of it. There are a few questions and two crosswind examples along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (BAKC 4.4)

The exam wants four things from you. First, how the weight, speed and wing shape of the aircraft ahead change the strength of its vortices. Second, the main control hazard if you fly into one. Third, which way the air turns in each vortex, where the vortices are in still air behind an aircraft in the cruise and during take-off and landing, and the lift-off and touchdown points and flight paths that keep you clear of them. And fourth, what wind and atmospheric turbulence do to the strength, lifespan, position and movement of vortices.

## Why this matters

Behind every aeroplane in the sky trail two invisible, spinning tubes of air. And behind a large one, they are strong enough to roll a light trainer upside down.

You will meet them sooner than you think. The Royal Flying Doctor Service PC-12s and PC-24s operating out of Jandakot. A fire-bombing aircraft at a country strip in summer. A Dash 8 or a jet at a regional airport such as Geraldton, Kalgoorlie or Albany. A helicopter crossing the circuit. Most encounters happen on approach or just after take-off, when you are low and slow, and have no height to recover.

The good news is that the vortices behave predictably. You can work out where they are from the way they are made, and avoid them by choosing where you lift off and touch down. The exam tests exactly that. What makes them strong, which way they turn, where they drift, and where you should lift off or land behind a heavier aircraft.

## Where the vortices come from

Start with something you already know from the lesson on lift and drag. A wing making lift has higher pressure beneath it than above it. Along most of the span, the air cannot get from one side to the other. But at the wing tips, it can. The high-pressure air under the wing spills outward towards the tip, curls up round it, and flows inward over the top into the low-pressure region.

The aeroplane does not stay still while this happens. It moves forward, and leaves the swirling air behind it. The curl at each tip rolls up into a tight, rotating tube of air, trailing back from the wing tip. That is a wing-tip vortex. There are always two, one from each tip, and they turn in opposite directions. They are counter-rotating. Together, they are the aeroplane's wake turbulence. They are the visible face of induced drag. The same circulation that tilts the lift back and costs the aeroplane drag leaves this energy behind in the air.

A helicopter makes the same kind of wake from its rotor disc in forward flight, plus its rotor downwash when hovering. So the same rules of caution apply behind one.

[[diagram:wake-turbulence-vortices]]
Picture a heavy twin-jet airliner from behind. From each wing tip trails a spinning tube of air. Viewed from behind, the left one turns clockwise, and the right one anticlockwise. Between them, the air moves down. Outside them, it moves up. The pair sinks at about 500 feet per minute, and drifts with the wind. And a light aeroplane that flies into one of them is rolled, often faster than its ailerons can counter.

Question. Why does a wing that is making lift leave vortices behind it, while a wing making no lift does not?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the vortices come from the pressure difference between the lower and upper surfaces. High-pressure air spills round the tips into the low-pressure region on top. With no lift, there is no pressure difference. Nothing spills round the tips, and there are no vortices.

## What makes a vortex strong

If the vortices come from the pressure difference across the wing, they will be strongest whenever the wing is working hardest: making a lot of lift, at a high angle of attack. Three factors decide that: weight, speed, and wing shape.

First, weight. In steady flight, the wing must make lift equal to the weight. A heavier aircraft needs more lift, so a bigger pressure difference, so stronger vortices. A fully laden aircraft just after take-off is at its heaviest, and its wake is at its worst.

Second, speed. At a lower speed, the wing must fly at a higher angle of attack to make the same lift. The pressure difference is concentrated into a slower-moving flow, and the air left behind each second is turned more violently. So, for the same weight, slower means stronger. The aircraft ahead is slowest, and its wake strongest, just after lift-off and on final approach.

Third, wing shape and configuration. A clean wing, with flaps and gear up, concentrates the spill-over neatly at the tips, and rolls it into a tight, strong vortex. Extended flaps and other devices change the spread of lift along the span. They break the flow into several smaller vortices that mix and weaken sooner. And a short span for the weight carried also concentrates the vortex. The same lift, spread over less span, means a bigger pressure difference at the tips.

Put the three together, and the worst case is a heavy, clean, slow aircraft. The classic example is a large aircraft just after rotation: heavy with fuel, slow, with the flaps being retracted, or in the initial climb.

So, once more. Heavier means stronger, because more lift is needed. Slower means stronger, because the wing needs a high angle of attack. And clean means stronger, because flaps spread and break up the vortex, while a clean wing concentrates it at the tips.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Intuition says a fast aircraft leaves the worst wake. It is the opposite. For the same weight, the slower aircraft is at a higher angle of attack, and makes stronger vortices. Heavy, clean and slow is the answer the exam wants. Fast, and flaps extended, are the distractors.

Question. An airliner is landing with full flap, at its maximum landing weight. Another departs clean, at its maximum take-off weight. Which wake is likely to be worse, and why?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the departing aircraft's. It is heavier, because take-off weight is higher than landing weight. And once the flaps come up in the initial climb, it is clean, and still slow. Both make the wing work harder, and concentrate the vortices.

## The hazard: induced roll

What happens if you fly into one? The vortex is a rotating tube of air. Behind a large aircraft, its diameter can be comparable to the whole wingspan of a light aeroplane. If your aeroplane enters it near the centre, one of your wings is in air moving up, and the other is in air moving down. The vortex pushes one wing up and the other down, and the aeroplane rolls, in the direction the vortex is turning.

The primary hazard is that induced roll. The vortex can roll a following aircraft faster than its ailerons can counter. A light aeroplane, with a small span sitting almost entirely inside the vortex, can be rolled through the vertical and past it before full opposite aileron has any effect. At height, that is a frightening upset you can recover from. Near the ground, on final or just after take-off, there is no height to recover in. That is why wake encounters on approach and departure are so dangerous.

There are secondary effects too. Between the two vortices, the air is moving down. That is the downwash. So an aeroplane flying along the wake between them may lose height, or be unable to climb. Just outside them, the air moves up. And crossing a wake at an angle gives a series of sharp jolts. Those can load the structure, and throw unsecured objects around the cabin.

Question. Why does the same vortex threaten a light trainer more than a larger aircraft following the same heavy?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the trainer's short span fits inside the vortex. So one wing is pushed up and the other down, and the whole aeroplane is rolled, faster than its small ailerons can counter. A larger aircraft spans across and beyond the core, so the rolling push is partly cancelled. And it has more roll control to oppose it.

## Which way the air turns

The flow round each vortex follows directly from how it was made. Outward under the wing, upward around the tip, inward over the top.

Stand behind the generating aircraft, and look forward along its path. Take the left wing first. The air goes outward, to your left, underneath. Then up round the tip. Then inward, to your right, on top. That is a clockwise rotation. The right wing is the mirror image. Its vortex rotates anticlockwise. Between the two vortices, both are moving air down. Outside them, both are moving air up.

So, from behind: left clockwise, right anticlockwise, downwash between.

Question. Viewed from behind, which way does the right wing-tip vortex rotate? And is the air between the two vortices going up, or down?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: anticlockwise. Outward, to the right, under the wing, up round the right tip, and inward over the top. Between the vortices, both push the air down. So the space directly behind the aircraft is a region of downwash.

## Where the vortices go in still air

There are two situations: the cruise, and take-off and landing.

First, the cruise. Vortices are made whenever the wing makes lift, which in flight means all the time. Once shed, the pair does not stay at the generating aircraft's level. The vortices push air down between them, and by the same token, they push themselves downward. They sink, typically at a few hundred feet per minute, about 300 to 500 ft per minute. They drift slowly apart as they go, and level off somewhere around 500 to 1,000 ft below the generating aircraft's flight path.

So in the cruise, the danger area is behind and below the other aircraft. An aircraft crossing ahead of you at the same level, or a little above, is the one to watch. Its wake sinks across your path. Passing above its flight path, or waiting until it is well clear, keeps you out of it.

[[diagram:wake-sink-in-cruise]]
Picture a side view, with a large aircraft in the cruise flying to the right. Behind it, the pair of vortices slopes gently downward as it trails back, sinking at about 300 to 500 ft per minute. Some way back, it levels off, about 500 to 1,000 ft below the aircraft's path. The danger area is that region behind and below. Your safe choices are to pass above its path, or to wait until it is well clear.

Now take-off and landing. An aircraft's wake begins when its wing starts to make enough lift to fly, and it ends when the wing stops carrying the weight. On take-off, that is the moment the nosewheel lifts: rotation. On landing, it is when the nosewheel touches down. Before rotation, and after nosewheel touchdown, there is no significant wake on the runway.

Close to the ground, the vortices sink until they are a short height above the surface. Then, instead of sinking further, they spread outward, sideways from the runway centreline. Each one moves away at a few knots in still air. Calm air leaves them on or beside the runway for some time.

[[diagram:wake-turbulence-avoidance]]
Picture two side views of a runway. In the top one, a heavier aircraft has just departed. Its vortices start at its rotation point and lie along and below its climb path. The light aeroplane behind lifts off before that point, and climbs above the heavy's path. In the bottom one, a heavier aircraft has just landed. Its vortices lie along and below its approach path, and stop at its touchdown point. The light aeroplane stays at or above that approach path, and touches down beyond that point.

## Flight paths that stay clear

The wake exists only between the other aircraft's rotation and touchdown points, and it sinks below its flight path. Knowing that, you can choose your own points to stay out of it. There are five standard cases, and each one follows from that picture.

First, you are taking off after a heavier aircraft took off. Rotate before its rotation point, and climb above its flight path. Turn away, upwind of its path, if possible. Or wait.

Second, you are landing after a heavier aircraft landed. Stay at or above its approach path, and touch down beyond its touchdown point.

Third, you are landing after a heavier aircraft took off. Touch down well before its rotation point.

Fourth, you are taking off after a heavier aircraft landed. Rotate beyond its touchdown point.

And fifth, en route, with a heavier aircraft crossing ahead. Avoid flying below and behind it. If in doubt, pass above, or wait.

Take the first case in more detail. The heavy aircraft's wake starts at its rotation point, and lies along and below its climb path. If you lift off before that point, your light aeroplane is airborne and climbing before you reach the place where the wake begins. And with your steeper initial climb, you stay above its path. If you cannot do that, because the runway is short or your aeroplane needs more distance, the answer is to wait.

Now the second. The heavy aircraft's wake lies along and below its approach path, and ends where its nosewheel touched down. If you fly at or above its approach path, you stay above the sinking vortices. And if you touch down beyond its touchdown point, you land where there is no wake on the runway.

Finally, allow time. Vortices decay with time, so waiting a few minutes behind a heavy aircraft is the simplest protection of all. Air traffic control, ATC, applies wake turbulence separation minima, in time and distance, between categories of aircraft. The current figures and the aircraft categories are published in the AIP. Check the current AIP for the numbers, rather than memorising figures from a textbook. And remember: when you accept a visual approach, or are told to maintain own separation, the responsibility to avoid wake turbulence is yours.

Question. A heavier aircraft has just taken off from the runway you are about to use. Where should you aim to lift off, and how should you climb?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: before the point where it rotated, because its wake begins there. Then climb above its flight path, turning away from its track if you can. If you cannot lift off before its rotation point, wait for the wake to decay.

## Wind and turbulence

Everything so far assumed still air. Real air moves and churns, and the vortices go with it. There are three effects to know: on strength and lifespan, on movement, and the special case of a light crosswind.

Strength and lifespan first. Vortices are organised, rotating flows, and anything that stirs the air breaks them up. Strong wind and atmospheric turbulence mix the vortex cores with the surrounding air, and make them decay faster. That turbulence might be mechanical, from wind over rough ground, or thermal, on a hot afternoon. In calm, stable air, typically early morning and evening, nothing disturbs them. They are at their strongest, and last longest, persisting for several minutes. So the most dangerous wake is the one left on a still, clear morning. Those are exactly the conditions in which a student pilot is most likely to be flying circuits.

Next, movement with the wind. The vortices are part of the air mass, so they drift with the wind. A crosswind carries both vortices downwind. A headwind or tailwind carries them along the runway. In the cruise, the danger area shifts downwind of the other aircraft's path, by the wind drift.

Now the light crosswind trap. Recall that near the ground, each vortex spreads outward from the centreline at a few knots. Add a light crosswind of about the same few knots. The downwind vortex now moves away twice as fast, and clears the runway quickly. But the upwind vortex's outward movement is cancelled by the wind blowing it back. It can sit almost stationary over the runway, near the touchdown zone, for minutes. A runway that looks clear can still have a vortex parked on it. And in this respect, a light crosswind is more dangerous than a strong one, which would carry both away.

[[diagram:wake-drift-in-crosswind]]
Picture the runway from behind a landing aircraft, with the two vortices just above the surface. In still air, they spread outward from the runway at a few knots each, one to each side. Now add a light crosswind from the right, about 2 knots. The right vortex, the upwind one, tries to move outward to the right, but the wind cancels its drift, and it sits over the runway. Meanwhile, the left vortex is carried away quickly to the left.

And a light tailwind. A light tailwind on the runway can carry the vortices of an aircraft that has landed forward, into your touchdown zone. And it can carry those of a departing aircraft back down the runway, towards your lift-off point.

Let's work one: a light crosswind behind a twin. You are landing on runway {21|two one}, in a light aeroplane the size of a Jabiru, after a heavier twin. The ATIS wind is {240|two four zero} at 4 knots. Which vortex is the problem, and what do you do? Pause if you'd like to try it first.
[[pause 6]]
The angle between the wind and the runway is {240|two four zero} minus {210|two one zero}, which is 30 degrees, from the right. The crosswind component is 4 times the sine of 30 degrees. That is 4 times 0.5, which is 2 kt from the right.

So the twin's right vortex, the upwind one, spreads outward to the right at a few knots. The 2 kt crosswind blows it back, so its drift slows or stops, and it may sit over the runway near the twin's touchdown point. The left vortex, the downwind one, is carried away to the left faster than in still air.

So fly at or above the twin's approach path, and touch down well beyond its touchdown point. If the runway length does not allow that, go around, or extend, to give the vortices time to decay. In short: a 2 kt crosswind from the right, and the right vortex may be parked on the runway.

The next case uses the same reasoning, with a stronger crosswind. The AIP gives no figures for how fast vortices drift, and neither does this lesson. So the answer is about direction, not distance.

You are landing on runway {24|two four}, behind a heavy aircraft that has just landed. The wind is {200|two zero zero} at 8 knots. Where are its vortices likely to be?
[[pause 6]]
The angle between the wind and the runway is {240|two four zero} minus {200|two zero zero}, which is 40 degrees, from the left. The crosswind component is 8 times the sine of 40 degrees. That is 8 times 0.64, which is 5 kt from the left. And the headwind component is 8 times the cosine of 40 degrees, about 6 kt.

The right vortex, the downwind one, spreads outward to the right, and the wind adds to that. It leaves the runway quickly.

The left vortex, the upwind one, tries to spread outward to the left at a few knots. But the 5 kt crosswind is pushing it right. Depending on how the two compare, it is held over the runway, or carried slowly back across it, towards and past the centreline.

And the headwind component carries both vortices back along the runway, away from the far end and towards the approach. So they lie along, and before, the heavy's touchdown point.

You cannot know exactly where the upwind vortex is. Only that it may be on the runway. So stay at or above the heavy's approach path, and touch down beyond its touchdown point. If that leaves too little runway, go around, or ask for a delay.

Question. A strong, gusty wind is blowing straight down the runway on a hot afternoon. Is the wake of a departing heavy aircraft more or less of a concern than on a calm morning, and why?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: less, though not zero. The wind and the turbulence mix the vortex cores with the surrounding air, so they decay sooner. And the wind carries them along the runway, rather than leaving them where they formed. On a calm morning, nothing breaks them up, or moves them.

## Bringing it together

It is a still winter morning at a regional airport. A heavy twin-engined aircraft has just departed ahead of you, and you are lined up for your own take-off.

The wake began at the point where its nosewheel lifted, about a third of the way down the runway. It lies along and below its climb path. The air is calm. So the vortices are at full strength, and they will last for minutes. With no wind, they will spread slowly outward from the centreline, rather than blow clear.

Your options come straight from this lesson. Lift off well before its rotation point, and climb above its path, turning away from its track as soon as you safely can. Or wait several minutes for the wake to decay. If the tower offers you an immediate take-off, you are entitled to ask for time. And if a light crosswind of a few knots springs up, the upwind vortex is the one that will stay on the runway.

## Key points

Let's recap.

Vortices are strongest behind an aircraft that is heavy, clean and slow. More weight needs more lift. Slower means a higher angle of attack. And a clean wing concentrates the vortex at the tips.

The primary hazard is induced roll, which can exceed the following aircraft's roll control. Near the ground, there is no height to recover.

Each vortex flows outward under the wing, up round the tip, and inward on top. Viewed from behind, the left one turns clockwise, the right one anticlockwise, with downwash between them.

In the cruise, they sink a few hundred feet per minute, to about 500 to 1,000 ft below the generating aircraft. So avoid below and behind. On take-off and landing, they exist from rotation to nosewheel touchdown, and spread outward near the ground. Rotate before the heavy's rotation point, and climb above its path. Land at or above its approach path, and beyond its touchdown point. And allow time.

Wind and turbulence break vortices up sooner. Calm, stable air keeps them strong and long-lived. Vortices drift with the wind. A light crosswind can hold the upwind vortex over the runway. And a light tailwind can carry them into your touchdown zone.

A final exam tip. Above and upwind is safe. Below and downwind is dangerous. On take-off, rotate before the heavy did. On landing, touch down after it did. The worst wake is from a heavy, clean, slow aircraft in calm air, and the trap answers are fast, and strong wind. For separation minima, the exam expects the principle: time and distance by category, published in the AIP. Not a number from a textbook. Check the current AIP before you rely on any figure.
