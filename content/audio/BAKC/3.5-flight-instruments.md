This is BAKC 3.5, Flight instruments. By the end you will know how each of the six primary instruments works, what the colours on the airspeed indicator mean, exactly how each instrument lies when a pitot tube or static vent blocks or a gyro loses its power, and how to check them all before flight. There are worked examples on the altimeter subscale, and a few questions along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (BAKC 3.5)

The exam wants four things from you. First, what each colour marking on the airspeed indicator means. Second, how the primary flight instruments, and the systems that feed them, work. Third, what goes wrong: what a pitot or static blockage does to each pressure instrument, what the alternate static source inside the cockpit does to them, what low suction and loss of electrical power do to the gyros, what makes a gyro topple and when it re-erects, and how, when and why you align the directional indicator with the compass. And fourth, how you check the instruments and the compass before flight.

## Why this matters

Six instruments in front of you answer the questions you ask on every flight. How fast? How high? Climbing or descending? Which way up? Which way pointing? Turning or not?

On a clear day at Jandakot you could fly the circuit without most of them. But on the day the cloud base comes down over the Darling Scarp, or the sun is low over the sea on a hazy evening, they are what keep you oriented. Each one can lie in a particular way. And a pilot who knows how it works can tell when it is lying.

The exam tests the colour arcs, the blockage effects, which are the most commonly examined part, the gyro power sources, and the altimeter subscale arithmetic.

## The pressure instruments and the pitot-static system

Three instruments work entirely by measuring air pressure: the airspeed indicator, the altimeter and the vertical speed indicator. They need no electrical power and no vacuum. That is why they are the ones you trust when everything else has failed.

They are fed by two sources. The first is the pitot tube, usually under the wing, pointing straight into the airflow. Air rams into it and stops. So the pressure inside is the pressure of the still air around you, plus the extra pressure of the air being brought to a halt. That is pitot pressure, also called total pressure. The second is the static vent, a small hole flush with the side of the fuselage, where the airflow runs smoothly past. It senses only the pressure of the surrounding air. That is static pressure. The difference between them, pitot minus static, is dynamic pressure. That is the pressure due to the aeroplane's motion through the air, and it equals a half, times the air density, times the speed squared.

[[diagram:pitot-static-system]]
Picture the plumbing. On the left, the pitot tube faces into the airflow, and a single pipe runs from it to the airspeed indicator, and nowhere else. On the side of the fuselage is the static vent, and its pipe branches to all three instruments: the airspeed indicator, the altimeter and the vertical speed indicator. A valve on the static line can switch it to an alternate static source inside the cockpit. Hold on to this picture, because the whole of the blockage section depends on it. Pitot pressure goes only to the airspeed indicator. Static pressure goes to all three.

Start with the airspeed indicator, the ASI. Inside it is a thin, flexible metal capsule, a diaphragm. Pitot pressure is piped into the capsule. Static pressure fills the sealed case around it. The static pressure acts on both sides and cancels out. So the capsule expands by an amount that depends only on the difference, the dynamic pressure. Gears turn that expansion into a needle movement, calibrated in knots. That reading is indicated airspeed, IAS.

Dynamic pressure depends on air density as well as speed. At altitude, where the air is thinner, the aeroplane must fly faster through the air to produce the same dynamic pressure, and the same indicated airspeed. In other words, a higher true airspeed. But dynamic pressure is exactly what the wing feels too. So the wing stalls at the same indicated airspeed at any height. That is why stall speeds and limit speeds are given as indicated speeds.

Next, the altimeter. The altimeter is a barometer. Inside its case is a stack of sealed aneroid capsules, with the air pumped out of them, and the case is filled with static pressure. As you climb, static pressure falls, the capsules expand, and gears drive the needles round. The scale is calibrated using the standard atmosphere, in which pressure falls by about 1 hPa for every 30 ft near sea level.

The subscale is the small window where you set a pressure in hectopascals. It sets the datum, the pressure level the altimeter treats as zero. Set the local QNH, and the altimeter reads altitude, which is height above mean sea level. Set {1013 hPa|ten thirteen hectopascals}, and it reads pressure height. Turn the subscale up by 1 hPa, and the reading goes up by about 30 ft.

[[diagram:height-altitude-elevation]]
Picture a side view, with the sea on the left. An aerodrome sits at an elevation of 300 ft above mean sea level. An aeroplane in its circuit, with QNH set, reads an altitude of 1,300 ft, so it is 1,000 ft above the aerodrome. Fly on at the same altitude over a 900 ft ridge, and it is only 400 ft above the ground. Height above the ground is altitude minus the elevation of whatever is beneath you.

That 30 ft rule links the two readings. Pressure height is about equal to the altitude, plus {1013|ten thirteen} minus the QNH, times 30 ft.

Let's work one, the wrong subscale setting on the ground. The aerodrome elevation is 500 ft. Today's QNH is {1003|ten oh three}. But the previous pilot left {1013|ten thirteen} set on the subscale. What does the altimeter read? Pause if you'd like to try it first.
[[pause 6]]
The subscale is too high by {1013|ten thirteen} minus {1003|ten oh three}, which is 10 hPa. 10 times 30 is 300 ft. So the altimeter reads 500 plus 300, which is 800 ft. It over-reads by 300 ft. It shows 800 ft while you are sitting on a 500 ft aerodrome. In flight with that setting, you would be 300 ft lower than indicated. Setting the QNH before take-off is what makes the reading true.

The pattern is simple. Any setting that is too high makes the altimeter read too high, by 30 ft per hectopascal. Set {1023|ten twenty-three} when the QNH is {1013|ten thirteen}, and it over-reads by 10 times 30, which is 300 ft. So you are 300 ft lower than it shows.

Now one for you to try, the pressure height version. An aerodrome sits at 1,200 ft elevation, and the QNH is {1008|ten oh eight}. What is the pressure height?
[[pause 6]]
{1013|ten thirteen} minus {1008|ten oh eight}, times 30, is 150 ft. 1,200 plus 150 is 1,350 ft. A QNH below {1013|ten thirteen} means the air here is at a lower pressure than standard, so the aerodrome behaves as if it were higher. With {1013|ten thirteen} set, the altimeter would read 1,350 ft.

The same error can happen in flight without anyone touching the subscale, when the pressure changes along your route. The pilot's saying is: from high to low, look out below.

Let's work one. You depart with a QNH of {1020|ten twenty} set, and fly towards a low-pressure trough where the QNH is {1005|ten oh five}, without resetting. How far off is your altimeter?
[[pause 6]]
The subscale is now too high by {1020|ten twenty} minus {1005|ten oh five}, which is 15 hPa. 15 times 30 is 450 ft. So if you hold an indicated 2,500 ft, you are actually at about 2,050 ft. You are 450 ft lower than indicated. Over rising ground, or under a lowering cloud base, that is the margin that disappears. Flying from low to high pressure does the opposite. The altimeter under-reads, and you are higher than it shows.

[[widget:altimeter-subscale]]
Picture turning the subscale knob and watching the needle. Each hectopascal between the subscale and the real QNH moves the reading about 30 ft. Set too high a number, or fly from high pressure into low, and you are lower than the altimeter says.

Last of the three, the vertical speed indicator, the VSI. It shows the rate at which static pressure is changing. Static pressure goes straight into a capsule. It also goes into the instrument case, but only through a tiny restriction, called a calibrated leak. In level flight the pressures are equal, and the needle reads zero. In a climb, static pressure falls. The capsule follows at once, but the case lags behind, because air can only escape through the leak slowly. So the case is left at a slightly higher pressure than the capsule. The capsule is squeezed, and the needle shows a climb, in feet per minute. The faster the climb, the bigger the difference.

Because it works on that lag, the VSI lags the real change by a few seconds. It shows the trend almost at once, but takes a few seconds to settle on the true rate. An abrupt pitch change can also show a brief reverse indication before it settles. So use it to confirm a trend, not to chase the needle.

[[diagram:asi-altimeter-vsi-cutaways]]
Picture the three instruments cut open, side by side. In every one, the case holds static pressure. In the airspeed indicator, on the left, the capsule is fed pitot pressure, so it moves with the difference, the dynamic pressure. In the altimeter, in the middle, the capsules are sealed and empty, and they expand as the static pressure in the case falls. In the vertical speed indicator, on the right, static pressure goes straight into the capsule, but into the case only through the calibrated leak. So in a climb or descent the case lags, and that difference shows the rate.

Question. Why does the wing stall at the same indicated airspeed at 7,000 ft as at sea level?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: both the wing and the airspeed indicator respond to dynamic pressure, which depends on density and speed together. At altitude the aeroplane must fly at a higher true airspeed to produce the same dynamic pressure. So the wing reaches its critical angle at the same indicated airspeed. The true airspeed at the stall is higher. The indicated one is not.

## The colours on the airspeed indicator

The face of the airspeed indicator carries coloured arcs. They let you see at a glance where the needle is, relative to the aeroplane's speed limits. Each arc begins or ends at a V speed from the flight manual, the POH.

[[diagram:asi-colour-markings]]
Picture the dial, with the speeds increasing clockwise and four colours around the edge.

The white arc, on the inside at the low-speed end, is the flap operating range. Its bottom is VSO, the stall speed in the landing configuration: full flap, power off, maximum weight. Its top is VFE, the maximum speed with flap extended. Above it, the air loads could damage the flaps.

The green arc is the normal operating range. Its bottom is Vs1, the stall speed clean, with the flaps up. Its top is VNO, the maximum structural cruising speed. That is the fastest you should fly in turbulence.

The yellow arc runs from VNO to VNE. It is the caution range: smooth air only, and no abrupt or large control inputs. A sharp gust at these speeds can overstress the airframe.

The red line is VNE, the never-exceed speed. Beyond it, structural failure or flutter is possible.

Twin-engine aeroplanes also carry a blue line. That is the best single-engine rate of climb speed.

So, the four colours once more. White: VSO to VFE, the flap range. Green: Vs1 to VNO, normal operations. Yellow: VNO to VNE, smooth air only. Red: VNE, never exceed.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. The manoeuvring speed, VA, is not marked on the airspeed indicator. It is placarded, or in the flight manual. And it reduces as the weight reduces, so a single number on the dial would be wrong most of the time. The exam offers the top of the green arc as a distractor. But that is VNO.

## The gyroscopic instruments

First, what a gyro does. A gyroscope is a heavy wheel, called the rotor, spinning fast. Spinning gives it two properties.

The first is rigidity in space. It resists any attempt to tilt its spin axis, the way a spinning bicycle wheel resists being turned. So it holds its orientation while the aeroplane moves around it.

The second is precession. If you do push on it, it responds not in the direction you pushed, but at 90 degrees to it, in the direction of rotation.

Each instrument uses one property or the other. The rotor is spun either by air, drawn through the instrument by the vacuum system, or by an electric motor.

[[diagram:gyro-rigidity-and-precession]]
Picture two sketches. On the left, rigidity in space. An aeroplane rolls around a spinning rotor, but the rotor's spin axis keeps pointing the same way. That is what the attitude and directional indicators use. On the right, precession. A push is applied to the rim of a spinning rotor, and the rotor responds as if the push had been applied 90 degrees further round, in the direction of rotation. That is what the turn coordinator uses.

The attitude indicator, or artificial horizon, uses a rotor spinning about a vertical axis, held rigid in space by its gimbals. The horizon bar is tied to the gyro. So it stays level with the real horizon, while the aeroplane, and the little aeroplane symbol fixed to the case, pitch and roll around it. In most trainers it is vacuum driven.

The directional indicator, the DI, also called the heading indicator, uses a rotor spinning about a horizontal axis. It is rigid in azimuth, which means it holds a fixed direction while the aeroplane turns around it, and a compass card attached to it shows the heading. Unlike the compass, it does not swing or lag in turns. But it has no magnetic sense at all. It shows whatever heading you set on it. In most trainers it is vacuum driven.

The turn coordinator uses precession. Its gyro is mounted so that when the aeroplane yaws or rolls, the gyro precesses and tilts a small aeroplane symbol, showing the rate of turn. Line the wing up with the marks, and you have a rate one turn. That is 3 degrees per second, a full circle in two minutes. Below it is the inclinometer, a ball in a curved, liquid-filled tube, which shows balance. In a balanced turn, the ball sits in the middle. The turn coordinator is normally electric, so that a vacuum failure does not take out all three gyros at once.

[[diagram:gyro-instruments-panel]]
On the left, the attitude indicator: a gyro spinning about a vertical axis, rigid in space, normally vacuum driven. In the middle, the directional indicator: a gyro spinning about a horizontal axis, rigid in azimuth, with no magnetic sense, reset to the compass about every 10 to 15 minutes, also normally vacuum driven. On the right, the turn coordinator: a gyro that precesses when the aeroplane yaws and rolls, showing rate of turn, with the ball showing balance, normally electric. One failure never takes out all three.

[[diagram:slip-and-skid-ball]]
Now picture three of those balls, all in a left turn. In the middle, the turn is balanced, and the ball is centred. On the left, the aeroplane is slipping: too much bank, or too little rudder. The ball sits to the inside of the turn, towards the low wing, so step on the left rudder. On the right, the aeroplane is skidding: too much rudder. The ball sits to the outside, towards the high wing, so step on the right rudder. Either way, step on the ball.

Last, the magnetic compass. It is a magnet assembly with a card attached, floating in a damping liquid, and it aligns itself with the Earth's magnetic field. It needs no power, and it is the reference everything else is checked against. It has well-known errors. Variation: magnetic north is not true north. Deviation: magnetic fields from the aeroplane's own metal and electrics, recorded on a deviation card beside the compass. Dip: the field lines slope into the Earth, and tilt the magnets. And from dip come the turning and acceleration errors. The important point here is that the turning and acceleration errors only disappear in straight, level, unaccelerated flight.

[[diagram:true-magnetic-compass]]
Picture three arrows fanning out from one point, each called north. True north runs along the meridian. Magnetic north is offset from it by the variation. And compass north is offset again, by the deviation from the aeroplane itself. For example, a track of {120|one two zero} degrees true, with a variation of 3 degrees east, gives {117|one one seven} magnetic. A deviation of 2 degrees west then gives {119|one one nine} compass.

Question. Why is the turn coordinator usually electric, when the attitude and directional indicators are vacuum driven?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: so that a single failure cannot take out every gyro instrument. If the vacuum pump fails, the turn coordinator still shows rate of turn and balance. If the electrical system fails, the attitude and directional indicators keep running.

## When the instruments lie

Every blockage effect follows from two facts. Which pressure each instrument uses. And what happens to that pressure once it is trapped. There are four cases: two pitot blockages, a static blockage, and the alternate static source.

The first case: the pitot tube is blocked, but its drain hole is clear. The pitot tube has a small drain hole to let water out. If ice blocks the front of the tube, but the drain is open, the trapped pitot pressure leaks away through the drain until it equals static pressure. Dynamic pressure falls to nothing. So the airspeed indicator falls to zero. The altimeter and the VSI use only static, so they are unaffected.

The second case: the pitot tube and its drain are both blocked. Now the pitot pressure is trapped, at whatever it was when the blockage happened. Static is still live. So the airspeed indicator reads the trapped pitot pressure, minus the live static. Climb, and static pressure falls, so the difference grows. The airspeed indicator over-reads, showing an increasing speed even if you are slowing. Descend, and static rises, so it under-reads. In level flight it stays stuck at the same reading, whatever your real speed. The airspeed indicator has become an altimeter. That is the dangerous case. A pilot who climbs to fix an apparently rising airspeed is slowing towards the stall.

The third case: the static vent is blocked. Now static pressure is trapped, and it feeds all three. The altimeter freezes at the height where the blockage occurred, because its case pressure no longer changes. The VSI reads zero, because the capsule and case pressures are both trapped, and equal. And the airspeed indicator reads the live pitot pressure minus the trapped static. That is correct only at the height where the vent blocked. Climb, and the real static pressure falls while the trapped one does not. So the airspeed indicator subtracts too much, and it under-reads. Descend, and it over-reads. That can take you towards VNE while the needle looks safe.

The fourth case: the alternate static source inside the cockpit. Many aeroplanes have a valve that lets you take static pressure from inside the cabin instead. In an unpressurised aeroplane, the air flowing over the cabin makes the cabin pressure slightly lower than the outside static pressure. Lower static pressure looks like more height and more speed. So the altimeter reads slightly high. The airspeed indicator reads slightly high. And the VSI shows a brief climb at the moment you select it, then settles. The instruments are usable, but less accurate, and the flight manual gives the corrections.

[[diagram:pitot-static-blockages]]
Picture a grid: the three instruments across the top, and the faults down the side. In the pitot rows, only the airspeed column changes. With the drain blocked, it over-reads in a climb and under-reads in a descent, like an altimeter. With the drain clear, it falls to zero. In the static row, every column changes. The altimeter is frozen, the VSI reads zero, and the airspeed indicator under-reads in a climb and over-reads in a descent. And in the alternate static row, the altimeter and airspeed read slightly high, and the VSI shows a brief climb when it is selected.

So here is the recap the exam draws on. Pitot and drain blocked: the airspeed indicator over-reads climbing, under-reads descending, and sticks in level flight, while the altimeter and VSI are normal. Pitot blocked with the drain clear: the airspeed falls to zero, and the others are normal. Static vent blocked: the altimeter is frozen, the VSI reads zero, and the airspeed under-reads climbing, over-reads descending, and is correct only at the height where it blocked. Alternate static: the airspeed and altimeter read slightly high, and the VSI gives a brief climb when selected.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. It is mixing up which fault hits which instruments. A pitot fault affects only the airspeed indicator, because only the airspeed indicator uses pitot pressure. A static fault affects all three. If the altimeter has frozen and the VSI reads zero, it is the static vent. And selecting the alternate static source is the cure.

Question. With the static vent blocked, you descend from 4,500 ft. What do the altimeter, the VSI and the airspeed indicator show?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the altimeter stays at 4,500 ft, and the VSI at zero, because both are fed with the trapped static pressure. The airspeed indicator over-reads. The real pitot pressure rises as you descend, while the trapped static does not, so the difference it measures is too large.

Now the gyros, starting with low suction and loss of electrical power. When the vacuum pump weakens, less air spins the rotors of the attitude and directional indicators. They slow down, lose rigidity, and begin to drift, and eventually topple. Often that happens slowly, and without a flag. The instruments do not tell you they are failing. The suction gauge does, and that is why it is part of your scan.

An electrical failure takes out the turn coordinator, and an electric attitude indicator, on aeroplanes that have one. But it leaves the vacuum instruments running. Knowing which instrument runs on which power source is what tells you which ones to trust.

Next, toppling and re-erection. A gyro's rotor is held in gimbals. These are rings that let the case move around the rotor, and each gimbal has a mechanical stop. Push the aeroplane far enough in pitch or roll, and a gimbal hits its stop. The force then acts on the rotor itself. It precesses violently, and the display tumbles. The gyro has toppled. Aerobatics will topple most attitude indicators. And a rotor that has slowed, from low suction, or just after start, loses rigidity, and can topple at much smaller angles.

A toppled gyro is useless until it re-erects. Its self-erecting mechanism gradually nudges the rotor back to its proper axis. That happens once the aeroplane is in straight and level flight, and the rotor is up to speed, usually within a few minutes. Some instruments have a caging knob, which locks the gyro upright to reset it quickly.

Last, synchronising the directional indicator. The DI has no idea where north is. So it must be set to the magnetic compass. And because it drifts, it must be reset regularly. It drifts for two reasons. First, friction in its bearings and gimbals slowly precesses the rotor. And second, the Earth rotates beneath a gyro that is holding a fixed direction in space. That second one is called apparent drift.

So the rule has three parts. How: read the compass, and set the DI to agree, using the setting knob. When: about every 10 to 15 minutes, and before any turn onto a new heading. And why straight and level: only in straight, level, unaccelerated flight is the compass free of its turning and acceleration errors. Set the DI from a compass that is swinging in a turn, and you copy the compass's error into an instrument that would otherwise have been more accurate.

[[diagram:di-drift-and-reset]]
Picture a graph of the directional indicator's heading error against time. It climbs steadily, from friction and from the Earth turning underneath. Every 10 to 15 minutes, a reset to the compass in straight and level flight drops it back to zero, so the line looks like a row of saw teeth. But reset during a turn, and the line drops to the compass's turning error instead of to zero.

Question. Why must you not reset the DI during a turn?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: in a turn, the magnetic compass suffers turning errors, and does not show the correct heading. Setting the DI then copies that error across. Reset it only in straight, level, unaccelerated flight, when the compass is reading correctly.

## Checking the instruments before flight

Each check tests a mechanism you have just learnt. There are three stages: before start, after start and during the taxi, and after take-off.

Before start, four checks. The airspeed indicator reads zero, or roughly the wind speed if you are parked facing a strong wind. The pitot cover is off, and the static vents are clear, because a blocked vent will not show on the ground. The altimeter: set the aerodrome QNH, and it should read the aerodrome elevation within the permitted tolerance. That is within 100 ft for a VFR flight, and 60 ft for IFR. Check your operator's requirement and the current AIP. The subscale should turn smoothly. The VSI reads zero on the ground. And the magnetic compass is full of fluid, with no bubbles, the deviation card present, and a sensible heading for the direction you are parked.

After start and during the taxi, four more. The attitude indicator erects within a few minutes and shows level, with the aeroplane level, and the suction gauge is in the green. The turn coordinator: in a taxi turn, the aeroplane symbol shows a turn in the direction of turn, and the ball moves to the outside, because the aeroplane is skidding on the ground. The directional indicator is set to the compass, and it should turn the correct way in taxi turns. And the compass swings freely, turns the correct way, and reads the runway direction when you line up.

After take-off, confirm the airspeed rises on the take-off roll. That is the airspeed alive call. Confirm the altimeter and the VSI show the climb. And confirm the DI agrees with the compass, once you are established in straight and level flight.

## Bringing it together

You depart Jandakot on a cool morning, with a QNH of {1021|ten twenty-one} set, heading for Narrogin. The forecast QNH there is lower, ahead of a trough.

In cloud-free air, you notice the airspeed creeping up during a cruise climb, even though the attitude has not changed. The altimeter and the VSI behave normally. Only the airspeed indicator is wrong, so it is a pitot fault. And an airspeed that rises in the climb means the pitot and its drain are both blocked. So you fly attitude and power, switch on the pitot heat, and ignore the needle.

Later, 15 minutes after you last set the DI, you check it against the compass in level flight, and correct a few degrees of drift.

Approaching Narrogin you get the area QNH: {1009|ten oh nine}. Your altimeter has been set 12 hPa too high. So it has been over-reading by about 360 ft over the rising wheatbelt. From high to low, look out below. You reset it before descending.

## Key points

Let's recap.

The colours. White arc: VSO to VFE, the flap range. Green: Vs1 to VNO, normal. Yellow: VNO to VNE, smooth air only. Red line: VNE. And VA is not marked.

How they work. The airspeed indicator measures pitot minus static, the dynamic pressure, so it shows indicated airspeed. The altimeter is an aneroid barometer whose subscale sets the datum, at about 30 ft per hectopascal. The VSI measures the rate of static change through a calibrated leak, and it lags. The attitude indicator and the DI use rigidity, and are usually vacuum driven. The turn coordinator uses precession, and is usually electric. And the compass is self-contained.

What goes wrong. Pitot blocked: the airspeed only. With the drain clear it falls to zero, and with the drain blocked it acts as an altimeter. Static blocked: the altimeter is frozen, the VSI reads zero, and the airspeed under-reads climbing and over-reads descending. Alternate static: everything reads slightly high, with a brief climb on the VSI. Low suction topples the vacuum gyros, and electrical failure loses the turn coordinator. Gyros topple at their gimbal limits, or at low rotor speed, and re-erect in straight and level flight. Reset the DI to the compass every 10 to 15 minutes, in straight, level, unaccelerated flight.

And the checks. On the ground: airspeed zero, covers off, the altimeter reading the elevation with QNH set, the VSI at zero, and the compass full with no bubbles. In the taxi: gyros erect and turn the right way, and the ball goes to the outside of turns. And on take-off: airspeed alive.

A final exam tip. The blockage questions are the most common. A blocked static vent freezes the altimeter and zeroes the VSI. A pitot blocked with its drain acts as an altimeter. Know which gyro is driven by which system: the attitude indicator and DI by vacuum, the turn coordinator by electricity. Know why the DI is set only in straight and level flight. And know the subscale arithmetic: 30 ft per hectopascal, and from high to low, look out below.
