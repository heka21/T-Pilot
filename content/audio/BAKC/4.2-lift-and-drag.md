This is BAKC 4.2, Lift and drag. By the end you will be able to say what happens to lift and drag when the airspeed or the angle of attack changes, name the families of drag and where each comes from, and explain why total drag is least at one speed and why flying slower than that needs more power. There are worked examples and a few questions along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (BAKC 4.2)

The exam wants three things from you. First, whether an aerofoil's lift and drag go up or down when the airspeed changes, and when the angle of attack changes. Second, the families of drag on a subsonic aeroplane: parasite drag, also called zero-lift drag, made up of form drag, interference drag and skin friction drag; and induced drag, also called lift-dependent drag. And third, how total drag changes with airspeed.

## Why this matters

On every flight you trade lift against drag without thinking about it. Slow down on downwind at Jandakot, and you raise the nose to hold height. Fly a long, flat final at Murrayfield, and you find you need more power to go slower. Trim for the best glide after an engine failure over the wheatbelt, and you are choosing the bottom of a drag curve. Each of those is one line of this lesson.

The lift formula tells you what you can change to make more or less lift, and what the day decides for you. The drag curve tells you why the aeroplane needs the least power to go far at one particular speed, and why, slower than that, it starts to work against you. The exam asks about this with direct increase or decrease questions, with a speed-squared calculation, and with the classic trap of swapping parasite and induced drag.

## The lift formula, term by term

In the lesson on basic aerodynamics, you met lift as the force at right angles to the relative airflow, made by a pressure difference across the wing. How big that force is depends on four things, and the lift formula puts them in one line. Lift equals a half, times rho, times V squared, times S, times the lift coefficient. Take it from left to right, as a story about air hitting a wing.

Rho is the air density: the mass of air in each cubic metre. Denser air means more air molecules arriving at the wing every second, so more lift. The pilot does not choose it. It falls with height, with heat, and with low pressure, which is the whole subject of density height in the lesson on take-off and landing performance. The day controls the density.

V is the true airspeed, the speed of the wing through the air. It appears squared, because faster air brings more molecules each second, and hits each one harder. Double the speed, and both effects double, so lift goes up four times. The pilot controls V, with power and attitude.

S is the wing area. It is fixed by the designer, although flap that extends backwards adds a little. For a given aeroplane, treat it as constant.

And the lift coefficient is a number that says how good the wing is at turning dynamic pressure into lift, at its present angle of attack. It depends on the aerofoil shape, on the flap setting, and above all on the angle of attack. The pilot controls the lift coefficient through the angle of attack, with the elevator.

The first group, a half rho V squared, has a name of its own: dynamic pressure. It is the pressure the air would exert if you brought it to rest against a flat plate. And it is exactly what the pitot tube and the airspeed indicator measure. That is why the airspeed indicator is so useful. Whatever the density and the true airspeed, the same indicated airspeed means the same dynamic pressure. And that means the wing will make the same lift at the same angle of attack.

[[diagram:lift-formula-factors]]
Picture the formula written out, with each term labelled underneath by who controls it. Under the lift coefficient: the pilot, with angle of attack and flap. Under V squared: the pilot, with power and attitude. Under rho: the day, with height and temperature. And under the wing area: fixed. A small example sits beside it. At a constant angle of attack, slowing from 100 to 70 knots leaves only 0.49 of the lift.

Drag follows exactly the same pattern. Drag equals a half, rho, V squared, times S, times a drag coefficient in place of the lift coefficient.

In level flight, lift must equal weight. The weight is fixed for the moment. So if one term in the lift formula goes up, another must come down. That simple balance answers most of the questions in this subtopic.

## Lift and drag with airspeed

Hold the angle of attack constant, so the lift and drag coefficients are fixed, and change the speed. Both lift and drag change with the square of the speed. Double the speed, and lift and drag are four times as great. Halve it, and they are a quarter.

But in level flight you cannot simply let lift quadruple. The aeroplane would climb. So in practice, when you speed up, you lower the nose. The angle of attack falls, and the lift coefficient comes down, to keep lift equal to weight. When you slow down, you raise the nose, and the lift coefficient goes up. Every speed in level flight has its own angle of attack. High speed, small angle. Low speed, large angle, with the largest at the stall speed.

Let's work one: what doubling the speed does to lift. An aeroplane in level flight at 60 kt accelerates to 120 kt, with the air density unchanged. At the same angle of attack, how much lift would it make, and what must happen to the lift coefficient? Pause if you'd like to try it first.
[[pause 5]]
The speed ratio is 120 over 60, which is 2. At the same angle of attack, lift goes with the square, so 2 squared is 4 times as much lift. But to stay level, lift must still equal weight. So the lift coefficient must fall to a quarter of its value, and that means a much smaller angle of attack. That is why the nose sits well down in a fast cruise, and well up in slow flight. The pilot is holding lift constant, by trading angle of attack against the speed squared.

The day can change the balance too. Climb high, and the density falls. To hold the same lift with the same lift coefficient, rho times V squared must stay the same.

Let's work one more. In the standard atmosphere, the ISA, the density is about half its sea-level value at around 22,000 ft. You hold the same angle of attack, and so the same lift coefficient, and the same weight. At sea level the aeroplane flies at 100 kt. What true airspeed does it need up there, and what does the airspeed indicator show?
[[pause 6]]
Lift must not change, so rho times V squared must not change. Rho has halved, so V squared must double. So V must rise by the square root of 2, which is 1.41. The true airspeed is 100 times 1.41, which is 141 kt. But the dynamic pressure, a half rho V squared, is unchanged. So the indicated airspeed is still 100 kt.

So the wing needs the same indicated airspeed at any height, for the same angle of attack. Only the true airspeed rises. That is why the indicated stall speed does not change with altitude. And it is why a high, hot strip needs a longer run. Lift-off happens at the same indicated airspeed, but at a higher true airspeed, and a higher groundspeed.

Now one for you to try. An aeroplane cruising level at 100 kt slows to 70 kt, at the same density and weight. By how much must the lift coefficient change?
[[pause 6]]
At the same angle of attack, the lift would be 70 over 100, squared, which is 0.49 of what it was. To keep lift equal to weight, the lift coefficient must rise by 1 over 0.49, which is 2.04. So, roughly double. The pilot does it by raising the nose to a higher angle of attack. That is why slow flight looks nose-high.

Question. At a constant angle of attack, the speed rises by 10 percent. What happens to lift and drag?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: both rise, by about 21 percent, because both go with the square of the speed. 1.1 squared is 1.21. To stay level, you would lower the nose slightly, to bring the lift back to the weight.

## Lift and drag with angle of attack

Now hold the speed constant, and change the angle of attack. As the wing is tilted further into the airflow, the air over the top is accelerated more, the pressure difference grows, and the lift coefficient rises. Over the normal range it rises almost in a straight line. Each extra degree adds about the same amount of lift.

That continues up to the critical angle, about 16 degrees on a typical light-aeroplane wing. There, the lift coefficient reaches its maximum. Beyond it, the flow separates from the upper surface, and lift falls away. That is the stall.

[[diagram:cl-vs-angle-of-attack]]
Picture a graph with the angle of attack along the bottom and the lift coefficient up the side. The line climbs straight and steady from the left, until the critical angle, about 16 degrees. There it rounds over into a peak. And beyond the peak it drops away, as the airflow separates and the wing stalls.

Drag behaves differently. It increases with angle of attack all the way. Slowly at small angles, then faster as the angle grows, because both the wing's frontal area and the lift-dependent drag grow. Near and beyond the stall, drag rises sharply as the separated wake gets bigger. So at the stall you lose lift and gain drag at the same time. That is why the aeroplane sinks so quickly when it stalls.

So here is the whole pattern. Change the airspeed at a constant angle of attack, and lift and drag move together: both up with more speed, by the square, and both down with less. Increase the angle of attack below the critical angle, and both increase. The one exception is past the critical angle. There, lift decreases sharply, while drag increases sharply.

Question. You increase the angle of attack from 2 degrees to 8 degrees, at a constant airspeed. What happens to lift, and to drag?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: both increase. Below the critical angle, more angle of attack raises the lift coefficient almost in proportion. And drag rises too, slowly at first, and faster as the angle grows. Only past the critical angle does lift fall while drag keeps rising.

## Parasite drag: the cost of pushing through the air

Drag on a subsonic aeroplane comes in two families. The first is the drag the aeroplane would have even if it made no lift at all: the cost of pushing an object through air. It is called parasite drag, or zero-lift drag. And it has three parts: form drag, skin friction drag, and interference drag.

Form drag, also called pressure drag, comes from the shape of the object. Air flowing around a blunt shape cannot follow it closely at the back. It separates, and leaves a low-pressure wake behind. The high pressure on the front and the low pressure behind push the object backwards. Put your hand flat out of the car window, and the push is mostly form drag. Streamlining, with rounded fronts and long tapering tails, lets the air close in smoothly behind, and shrinks the wake. Wheel spats, cowlings and a tapered fuselage all exist for this.

Skin friction drag comes from the air's viscosity, its stickiness. The layer of air touching the skin is dragged to a stop. And the thin boundary layer above it is sheared between the stationary skin and the moving air. The rougher the surface, the thicker and more turbulent the boundary layer, and the higher the friction. Dirt, squashed insects, rough paint, protruding rivet heads and frost all increase it. A clean, polished wing is faster than a dirty one, for the same power.

Interference drag comes from junctions. Where the wing meets the fuselage, where a strut meets the wing, or where an undercarriage leg meets the wheel fairing, the airflows around the two parts meet and disturb each other. The total drag of the joined parts is more than the sum of their drags separately. Smooth fillets and fairings at the junctions reduce it.

All three grow with dynamic pressure. So parasite drag rises with the square of the airspeed. Double the speed, and parasite drag is four times as great.

[[diagram:parasite-drag-types]]
Picture three small sketches. In the first, form drag: a flat plate on the left trails a large, swirling low-pressure wake, while a streamlined shape on the right leaves only a small one. In the second, skin friction: a close-up of the skin, where the air touching it is brought to rest, and the boundary layer above it is sheared, thicker over a rough surface. In the third, interference: a high, strut-braced wing seen from the front. The flows disturb each other where the wing meets the cabin roof, and where each strut meets the wing, and a fillet smooths the wing root. All three grow with the square of the speed.

## Induced drag: the price of making lift

The second family exists only because the wing is making lift. It is called induced drag, or lift-dependent drag.

A wing making lift has higher pressure below it than above. Along most of the span, the air cannot get from one to the other. But at the tips it can. The high-pressure air underneath spills outward, and round the tip, into the low-pressure region on top. As the aeroplane moves forward, this circulation is left behind as two rotating wing-tip vortices. They are the same vortices that cause wake turbulence, the subject of the lesson on wake turbulence.

The vortices push the air behind the wing downward. This is called downwash. It means the wing is flying in air that is moving slightly down, so the effective relative airflow over the wing is tilted. Lift acts at right angles to that tilted airflow, so the lift itself tilts backwards. And its rearward part is a drag force. That is induced drag.

[[diagram:induced-drag-downwash]]
Picture a wing section flying to the right. The free airflow comes from straight ahead. But at the wing, the downwash makes the air move slightly down, so the effective airflow arrow is tilted, coming from a little above. Lift stands at right angles to that tilted arrow, so it leans backwards. Split it into an upward part and a rearward part, and the rearward part is induced drag. In a small inset, seen from behind, the two tip vortices turn inward over the top, pushing the air between them down. The angles are exaggerated.

The harder the wing works, the bigger the pressure difference, the stronger the vortices, and the bigger the tilt. So induced drag is greatest at a high angle of attack. That means at low speed, at high weight, and in a turn, where the wing must make more lift. At high speed the wing needs only a small angle of attack, the vortices are weak, and induced drag is small. Roughly, induced drag falls with the inverse square of the airspeed. Double the speed, and it falls to about a quarter.

Wing shape changes it too. A long, slender wing of high aspect ratio spreads the lift over a long span, so less of it is lost at the tips. That is why gliders have them. Tapered tips and winglets reduce it a little further.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Parasite drag goes up with speed. Induced drag goes down with speed. The exam options swap them. Induced drag is highest at low speed and high angle of attack, just before the stall. That is also when the wing-tip vortices, and the wake turbulence behind a large aeroplane, are strongest.

Question. Which type of drag is increased by frost on the wing, and which by flying slowly at a high angle of attack?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: frost roughens the surface, and increases skin friction drag, which is part of parasite drag. It also disturbs the airflow and lowers the maximum lift. Flying slowly at a high angle of attack increases induced drag, because the wing is working hard, and the tip vortices and downwash are strong.

## Total drag and the drag curve

Put the two families on one graph, against airspeed. Parasite drag starts small at low speed, and climbs steeply as speed rises. Induced drag starts large near the stall, and falls away as speed rises. Their sum, total drag, is a U-shaped curve. It is high at low speed, where induced drag dominates. High at high speed, where parasite drag dominates. And lowest in between.

[[diagram:drag-curves-vs-airspeed]]
Picture that graph, with airspeed along the bottom. The parasite drag line starts low on the left and sweeps up to the right. The induced drag line starts high on the left and falls away to the right. They cross in the middle. The total drag curve sits above both, shaped like a U, with its lowest point right above where they cross. Everything to the left of that low point is the back side of the curve.

The bottom of the U is where the two are equal. Below that speed, slowing down adds more induced drag than it saves in parasite drag. Above it, speeding up adds more parasite drag than it saves in induced drag. The speed for minimum total drag is also the speed of the best lift to drag ratio. In level flight, lift equals weight, and is fixed. So the least drag means the most lift for each unit of drag. For a light piston aeroplane, that speed is close to the best glide speed, and to the best range speed at low level. Later lessons cover both.

Let's work one, with illustrative round numbers: drag either side of the minimum. At 70 kt an aeroplane is at its minimum-drag speed, with parasite and induced drag each 600 newtons. Parasite drag goes with the speed squared. Induced drag goes with one over the speed squared. What is the total drag at 100 kt, and at 55 kt? Follow along, or pause and try it.
[[pause 6]]
At 100 kt, parasite drag is 600 times 100 over 70, squared. That is 600 times 2.04, which is 1,224 newtons. Induced drag is 600 times 70 over 100, squared. That is 600 times 0.49, which is 294 newtons. The total is 1,518 newtons.

At 55 kt, parasite drag is 600 times 55 over 70, squared. That is 600 times 0.617, which is 370 newtons. Induced drag is 600 times 70 over 55, squared. That is 600 times 1.62, which is 972 newtons. The total is 1,342 newtons.

Compare those with 1,200 newtons at 70 kt. Flying 15 kt slower costs about 12 percent more drag. So the aeroplane needs more thrust to fly slower. Faster or slower than the bottom of the curve, total drag goes up.

Now, the back of the drag curve. On the right-hand side of the curve, above the minimum-drag speed, the aeroplane is well behaved. If a gust slows it, drag falls, the surplus thrust accelerates it back, and the speed returns. If it speeds up, drag rises and slows it again. Speed looks after itself.

On the left-hand side, below the minimum-drag speed, the relationship is reversed. Slowing down increases the drag. That slows the aeroplane further, which increases the drag again. To fly slower, you need more thrust, not less. This region is called the back of the drag curve. It is also called the back side, or the region of reversed command.

It matters most on a slow, low final approach. Suppose you are a little low and a little slow, and you raise the nose to stretch the approach. The higher angle of attack adds induced drag. The speed falls, the drag rises further, and the aeroplane sinks faster, rather than going further. The pitch input that works on the front of the curve makes things worse on the back. The answer is power to hold the speed, with the attitude controlling the path. And the habit of flying the approach at the flight manual speed, so that you stay near the bottom of the curve, rather than sliding up its back.

Question. Why does an aeroplane need more power to fly slower than its minimum-drag speed?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: below that speed, induced drag is the larger part of total drag, and it grows as the speed falls and the angle of attack rises. So slowing down increases total drag, and more thrust is needed to hold the lower speed.

## Bringing it together

You leave the circuit at Jandakot and climb to 4,500 ft, to fly to Northam on a 30 degree afternoon. The air at height is thinner, and the day has made it thinner still. Rho is down. To hold your cruise indicated airspeed, the aeroplane must fly at a higher true airspeed. And the wing works at the same angle of attack as it does at that indicated airspeed anywhere.

Over Northam, you slow to circuit speed. The speed squared falls, so you raise the nose, and the lift coefficient rises. The tip vortices strengthen, and induced drag grows, while parasite drag shrinks.

On final you are a few knots slow, on the back of the drag curve. So a raised nose would cost speed and height together. Power holds the speed. The attitude holds the path.

## Key points

Let's recap.

Lift equals a half rho V squared, times S, times the lift coefficient. At a constant angle of attack, lift and drag both go with the speed squared. Below the critical angle, more angle of attack means more lift and more drag. Past it, lift falls sharply, and drag rises sharply. The pilot controls the speed and the lift coefficient. The day controls the density. And the same indicated airspeed means the same dynamic pressure.

Parasite drag, made of form, skin friction and interference drag, is the cost of moving through the air, and it rises with the speed squared. Induced drag comes from the tip vortices and downwash tilting the lift back. It is greatest at low speed, high angle of attack and high weight, and it is reduced by a high aspect ratio.

Total drag is U-shaped against airspeed. It is least where parasite drag equals induced drag. That is the best lift to drag speed, close to the best glide and best range speeds for a piston aeroplane. Slower than that is the back of the drag curve, where flying slower needs more thrust.

A final exam tip. Have the speed squared at your fingertips. Double the speed, and you get four times the lift and drag at the same angle of attack. At 70 percent of the speed, you get about half: 0.49. Expect a definition of interference drag, which is junctions. A question on when induced drag is greatest: slow, at a high angle of attack, and heavy. And a question on how total drag varies: it decreases to a minimum, then increases. If an option says parasite drag decreases with speed, it has swapped the two families.

That's the end of BAKC 4.2.
