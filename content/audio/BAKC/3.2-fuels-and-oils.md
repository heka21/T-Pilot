This is BAKC 3.2, Fuels and oils. By the end you will know where fuel contamination comes from and how the fuel drain catches it, the case for and against filling the tanks before the aeroplane is parked overnight, how to tell the fuel grades apart, and what happens when the wrong fluid or the wrong fuel goes in. There are a few questions along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (BAKC 3.2)

The exam wants four things from you, all about aviation fuels. First, where fuel contamination comes from. Second, the advantages and disadvantages of refuelling before parking the aeroplane overnight. Third, how to tell the grades of aviation fuel apart. And fourth, the hazards of mixing different hydraulic fluids, and of using the wrong grade of fuel.

## Why this matters

An engine will run on almost anything that burns, for a minute or two. That is exactly what makes bad fuel so dangerous. The run-up can be perfect, and the engine can still stop at 300 ft, when the contaminated fuel or the wrong fuel finally reaches the cylinders.

Fuel problems are a common cause of engine failure in light aeroplanes. And most of the contamination and wrong-fuel cases are caught by a careful fuel drain, and a look at the docket, before you leave the ground.

In Western Australia you will meet the full range. A bowser at Jandakot. A drum at a station strip in the Murchison. A hired aeroplane that sat outside through a wet winter week at Bunbury. The exam tests this subtopic with short recall questions. Where water comes from. What colour each fuel is. And what happens when the wrong fuel goes in.

## Where contamination comes from

Contamination is anything in the tank that is not the right fuel. Four kinds matter to a pilot: water, dirt, the wrong fuel, and microbial growth. And one of them, water, outweighs the rest.

Start with water. Think about a half-full wing tank on a winter evening. The space above the fuel is full of air, and that air carries water vapour. Overnight the wing cools, the tank walls cool, and the air inside cools with them. Cool air can hold less water vapour than warm air. So the excess condenses onto the cold metal as droplets, exactly as it does on the outside of a cold drink, and it runs down into the fuel.

One night does not make much. A week of cold nights with the tanks half empty can make a lot. This condensation is the main source of water in aircraft fuel, and the exam knows it.

Water also gets in more directly, in two ways. First, through filler caps and seals that leak, when it rains or when the aeroplane is washed. And second, with the fuel itself, from a contaminated supply. That might be a bowser or a drum that has water in it. Or a drum stored standing upright in the rain, so that water pools on the lid and is drawn past the bung as the drum breathes.

Why does water matter so much? It does not burn. A slug of water reaching the carburettor is, for as long as it lasts, a slug of nothing for the engine to run on. Rough running at best. A stopped engine at worst. And water can freeze in fuel lines and strainers, on a cold morning or at altitude.

The good news is that water is heavier than fuel. Left alone, it sinks to the lowest point it can reach. Aircraft fuel systems are designed around that fact. There is a drain at the lowest point of each tank. And there is another at the fuel strainer, the gascolator, which is the lowest point of the whole system. So you can draw the water off before it reaches the engine.

[[diagram:fuel-system-schematic]]
Picture two wing tanks, one on each side, each with a vent at the top and a drain at its lowest point. Pipes from both run down to a fuel selector. From there, fuel passes through the strainer, which has its own drain at the lowest point of the whole system, then through the pumps to the carburettor. Water is heavier than fuel and settles at those low points: the bottom of each tank, and the strainer. That is exactly where the drains are, and why each one is sampled before flight.

Next, dirt, sand and rust. Solid particles come in with the fuel. A dirty funnel. A dusty drum. A hose dragged across the ground. Rust from the inside of an old drum or tank. They block filters and jets, and they can stop the engine just as water can. On a red-dirt strip in the north-west, where everything you own is covered in fine dust, it pays to wipe the filler and the nozzle before the cap comes off.

Third, the wrong fuel. A tank full of clean, dry fuel of the wrong grade is still contaminated, as far as the engine is concerned. Jet fuel pumped into an AVGAS aeroplane is the classic case. The lower-octane cases come later in this lesson.

And fourth, microbial growth. Bacteria and fungi can live at the boundary between fuel and water at the bottom of a tank. They feed on the fuel and grow into a dark slime that blocks filters and corrodes the tank. It is more common in jet fuel, but it is possible in any tank that has had water left in it for a long time. The cure is the same as for water: do not let it sit there.

Now, the fuel drain: your defence against all four. Every pre-flight inspection includes taking a sample from each drain into a clear fuel tester, and looking at it. You are checking for four things.

First, water. It shows as clear globules, or as a separate clear layer at the bottom of the tester, with the fuel above it. Second, sediment: specks of dirt or rust. Third, cloudiness, which can be suspended water. And fourth, the right colour and smell for the grade the aeroplane uses.

If you find water, keep draining until the samples are clean. And two timing points matter. Let the fuel settle for a few minutes before you sample, so any water stirred up by refuelling or by moving the aeroplane has time to sink to the drain. And drain again after refuelling, and after the aeroplane has been parked in rain, because those are the moments new water is most likely to have arrived.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. A sample that looks clear and clean is not automatically good fuel. AVGAS is dyed. So a colourless sample from an AVGAS aeroplane is one of two things. Either it is water, and a whole tester of it means you have not yet reached the fuel. Or it is jet fuel. Neither is a reason to fly.

Question. Why are the fuel drains at the lowest points of the tanks and the strainer?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because water is heavier than fuel, and sinks. Placing the drains at the low points means the first fuel out of each drain comes from wherever water would have collected. So a sample from each one shows you the water before it reaches the engine.

## Fuelling before overnight parking

The condensation story suggests an obvious defence. Fill the tanks before you put the aeroplane away. A full tank has very little air space, so there is very little water vapour in it to condense as the night cools.

That is the main advantage: less water contamination. A second, practical advantage is that the aeroplane is ready to fly in the morning, without waiting for the fuel truck.

[[diagram:overnight-condensation]]
Picture two wing tanks side by side, overnight. On the left, a partly filled tank, with a large air space above the fuel. As that air cools overnight, its moisture condenses on the tank walls and runs down into the fuel as water. On the right, a full tank, with hardly any air, so hardly any water forms. Less air in the tank means less moisture to condense.

But it is not free. The disadvantages come from weight, and from heat. There are three.

First, weight. Full tanks may put tomorrow's flight over the maximum take-off weight, especially if the next pilot is taking two passengers. Taking fuel back out is slow, messy and wasteful. If you do not know who is flying next, or with what load, full tanks can be the wrong answer.

Second, expansion. Fuel expands as it warms. Tanks filled to the brim in the cool of the evening, or the early morning, will warm through the day in the sun. The expanding fuel has nowhere to go but out of the vents, onto the wing and the apron. On a WA summer day, that is a fire hazard as well as a waste.

And third, handling. A full aeroplane is heavier to push into the hangar, or to tie down.

So the decision is a trade-off, and many flying schools set their own rule. A common compromise is to fill the tanks overnight in cool, humid weather, when condensation is the bigger risk. And to fill to a known lesser quantity when the next flight's weight is tight, or the next day will be hot.

Question. Why does a partly filled tank collect more water overnight than a full one?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the air space above the fuel holds water vapour. As the tank cools overnight, that air cools, can hold less vapour, and the excess condenses on the tank walls and runs into the fuel. A full tank has little air space, so there is little vapour to condense.

## Telling the grades apart

You cannot judge fuel by eye alone unless someone has made it visible. So aviation fuels are dyed by grade. The colour is the quickest check you have. There are four fuels to know.

First, AVGAS {100LL|one hundred low lead}, where the two letters stand for low lead. It is dyed blue. It is the fuel almost every light piston aeroplane in Australia uses. The 100 is its octane rating: its resistance to detonation, which you will meet in the lesson on engine handling.

Second, AVGAS {100/130|one hundred, one thirty}. It is dyed green. It has more lead than {100LL|one hundred low lead}, and it is interchangeable with it in most engines. You are less likely to meet it, but you should know the colour.

Third, {Jet A-1|jet A one}. This is kerosene, the fuel for turbine engines. It is clear to straw coloured. It feels oily, rather than evaporating quickly from your fingers. And it has a distinct paraffin smell, quite unlike the sharper smell of AVGAS. It is for turbine engines only.

And fourth, mogas: motor gasoline, ordinary car petrol. Its colour varies, and it may be used only where the aircraft and engine have a specific approval to use it.

So the colours. {100LL|One hundred low lead}, blue. {100/130|One hundred, one thirty}, green. {Jet A-1|Jet A one}, clear to straw.

[[diagram:fuel-sample-colours]]
Picture a row of clear fuel testers, side by side. The first is blue: AVGAS {100LL|one hundred low lead}. The second is green: AVGAS {100/130|one hundred, one thirty}. The third is clear to straw coloured: {Jet A-1|jet A one}. Then a tester with clear globules, or a separate clear layer at the bottom: that one contains water. A clear sample from an AVGAS aeroplane means water or jet fuel. Do not fly until the cause is found.

Colour is only one layer of protection. Mis-fuelling is prevented by several checks that overlap, so that one missed check is caught by the next. There are four.

First, the placard beside each filler cap. It states the grade the aeroplane takes.

Second, the bowser and the delivery docket. They state the grade being delivered. Read the docket before you sign it.

Third, the nozzle. Jet fuel nozzles are wider than the AVGAS filler openings on most piston aircraft, so a jet nozzle will not go in easily. Treat that as a backstop, not a guarantee.

And fourth, your fuel sample, checked for colour and smell after refuelling.

[[diagram:misfuelling-defences]]
Picture four slices lined up one behind the other, each with holes in it. They are the placard at the filler, the bowser and its docket, the nozzle size, and your fuel sample after refuelling. A mistake that slips through the holes in one slice is stopped by the next. Notice that your own sample is the last slice before the engine.

The best defence is simply being there. If you are present when the aeroplane is refuelled, you can tell the refueller what it takes, and watch which nozzle comes off the truck. At a busy aerodrome, where the same refueller is fuelling turbine aircraft and piston trainers in the same hour, that is not paranoia.

Question. Your sample after refuelling, from a {100LL|one hundred low lead} aeroplane, is clear and smells of paraffin. What has happened, and what do you do?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: it is jet fuel. {100LL|One hundred low lead} is blue and smells sharp, while {Jet A-1|jet A one} is clear to straw and smells of kerosene. Do not start the engine. The tanks and lines must be drained, and the system flushed, before the aeroplane flies.

## Wrong fluids and wrong fuels

Four cases here. Mixed hydraulic fluids. A lower octane than specified. Jet fuel in a piston engine. And a higher octane than specified.

First, mixing hydraulic fluids. Your brakes use hydraulic fluid, and there is more than one kind. Two you should know. Mineral-based fluid is dyed red. Synthetic phosphate-ester fluid is dyed purple. They are chemically incompatible.

Mixing them, or putting the wrong one into a system, attacks the rubber seals in the master cylinders and brake units, and breaks down the fluid itself. The seals swell or soften, fluid leaks past them, and the brakes become spongy or fail. Only the fluid specified for the aeroplane goes into it. And topping up is an engineer's job, unless your operator says otherwise.

Second, a lower octane than specified. The octane rating is a fuel's resistance to detonation: the explosive burning of the mixture that hammers pistons and valves. An engine designed for 100 octane has a compression ratio that relies on that resistance.

Give it a lower octane and, at high power, the fuel detonates. That means rough running, high cylinder head temperatures, loss of power, and rapid damage to pistons and valves. The worst time is the take-off and climb, when power is highest and cooling airflow is lowest.

Third, jet fuel in a piston engine. This is the case the exam likes, because the sequence is so treacherous.

The fuel lines, the strainer and the carburettor still hold AVGAS from the last flight. So the engine starts normally, and passes the run-up. Then, a few minutes later, the jet fuel reaches the cylinders. Kerosene has very poor resistance to detonation in a spark-ignition engine, so it begins to detonate severely. Power falls, the engine runs very rough, and it may fail. Typically just after take-off, the worst possible moment.

The defence is entirely on the ground. The checks you have just heard, and a fuel sample after every refuel.

And fourth, a higher octane than specified. Going this way is usually harmless to the engine. A low-compression engine designed for a lower grade will run on higher-octane AVGAS. But the extra lead may increase lead fouling of the spark plugs. Use only the grades the POH approves.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. The engine started and ran up normally, so the fuel must be fine. Not so. With jet fuel in the tanks, a normal start and run-up is exactly what you should expect, because the engine is still running on the AVGAS in the lines. The failure comes after take-off. Only the sample and the docket protect you.

Question. Why does a lower-octane fuel cause trouble mainly at high power?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: octane is resistance to detonation, and detonation is driven by high cylinder pressure and temperature. At high power, especially in a climb with little cooling airflow, the cylinders are at their hottest and most highly pressurised. So a low-octane fuel is most likely to detonate then.

## Bringing it together

You arrive for an early lesson after a week of rain. The aeroplane was left with half tanks, and the refueller has just topped it up.

Before you go near the drains, you read the docket: {100LL|one hundred low lead}, the right grade. You wait a few minutes for the fuel to settle. Then you sample each tank drain, and the strainer.

The first tester from the left tank has a clear layer at the bottom, under the blue fuel. That is water. It came from a week of condensation in a half-empty tank, and possibly from a leaking cap. You keep draining until two samples in a row are clean blue fuel, with no globules and no specks. And you note the left cap seal for the engineer.

Had the sample been clear all the way through, and smelt of kerosene, the lesson would have been over before it started.

## Key points

Let's recap.

Water is the main contaminant. It comes mostly from condensation in partly filled tanks, and also from leaking caps and seals, and from a contaminated supply. Dirt, rust, the wrong fuel and microbial growth are the others. Water sinks, so drain each low point before flight, after refuelling and after rain, letting the fuel settle first.

Full tanks overnight mean less air space and less condensation, and an aeroplane ready to fly. The cost is possible overweight for the next flight, and fuel venting as it expands in the heat.

The grades. {100LL|One hundred low lead} is blue. {100/130|One hundred, one thirty} is green. {Jet A-1|Jet A one} is clear to straw, with an oily feel and a kerosene smell. Check the placard, the docket, the nozzle, and the sample.

And the wrong fluids. Mixing mineral hydraulic fluid, which is red, with phosphate-ester fluid, which is purple, destroys seals, and can fail the brakes. A lower octane causes detonation at high power. Jet fuel lets the engine start and run up normally, then detonate and fail after take-off. A higher octane is usually harmless, but may foul the plugs.

A final exam tip. Questions on contamination almost always come back to water from condensation in partly filled tanks. The defence is full tanks overnight, and a drain check before flight and after refuelling. Have the colours by heart: {100LL|one hundred low lead} blue, {100/130|one hundred, one thirty} green, {Jet A-1|jet A one} clear or straw. And remember that jet fuel in a piston engine fails it after take-off, not on the ground.
