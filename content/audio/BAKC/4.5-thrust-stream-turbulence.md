This is BAKC 4.5, Thrust stream turbulence: jet blast, propeller wash and rotor downwash. By the end you will be able to say how the danger from another aircraft's blast changes with the power it is using, and with your distance from it, and why. There are a few questions along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (BAKC 4.5)

The exam wants one thing from you, asked in several ways. How the danger from thrust stream turbulence changes as the engine power changes, and as you move closer to the source or further from it. That covers jet blast, propeller wash and rotor downwash.

## Why this matters

Wake turbulence is the hazard you meet in the air. You'll find it in the lesson on wake turbulence. Thrust stream turbulence is its cousin on the ground. It is the fast, turbulent air blown backwards by an engine, or downwards by a rotor.

A light aeroplane weighs very little, and it presents large, light surfaces to the air. So a blast that a parked airliner hardly notices can lift a wing, swing the tail, or flip a two-seat trainer onto its back.

You will meet it in plenty of places. Taxiing at a regional airport such as Karratha, Kalgoorlie or Geraldton, behind a jet with its beacon on. At the holding point, behind a turboprop about to line up. In the run-up bay at Jandakot, with another aircraft's propwash aimed at you. And near the helicopter pads, where rescue and police helicopters hover-taxi.

The exam asks one thing about it, in several ways. How does the hazard change with the power the other engine is using, and with your distance from it? This lesson builds that answer from how thrust is made.

## What thrust stream turbulence is

An engine makes thrust by throwing air backwards. That is Newton's third law. To push the aeroplane forward, the propeller or the jet engine must push a mass of air backwards. The faster it accelerates the air, and the more air it accelerates each second, the more thrust it gets.

The air it has thrown back does not stop when it leaves the engine. It carries on as a fast, turbulent stream behind the aircraft, until it has mixed with enough surrounding air to slow down. That stream is thrust stream turbulence. It comes in three forms.

The first is jet blast, the exhaust behind a jet engine. A jet accelerates a relatively small mass of air to a very high speed. So close behind the engine, the blast is extremely fast. And near the exhaust, it is also hot.

The second is propeller wash, also called prop wash, or slipstream. It is the air behind a propeller. A propeller accelerates a larger mass of air to a lower speed. So the stream is slower than jet blast, but still strong close behind. That is especially so when the aircraft is running up, or applying power to start moving.

The third is rotor downwash, the air blown down by a helicopter's rotor. A hovering helicopter's rotor must push down enough air every second to hold the whole weight of the helicopter up. That air hits the ground and spreads outward along the surface, in all directions. So unlike jet blast, it is not confined to the area behind the aircraft. It is strongest when the helicopter is hovering close to the ground, or hover-taxiing.

Here is the key contrast. Wing-tip vortices come from lift. Thrust stream turbulence comes from thrust. So it is mainly a hazard on the ground and close to it. On aprons and taxiways. At holding points and in the run-up bay. And at low level near a hovering helicopter.

Question. What is the difference in origin between wake turbulence and thrust stream turbulence?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: wake turbulence is the pair of wing-tip vortices left behind by a wing making lift. Thrust stream turbulence is the air accelerated backwards by an engine, or downwards by a rotor, making thrust. One comes from lift, the other from thrust. That is why thrust stream turbulence matters most on the ground, where engines run at high power while the wing makes little lift.

## Why a fast stream pushes so hard

To see why the hazard changes as it does, go back to the lift formula from the lesson on lift and drag. The force that moving air exerts on a surface goes with the dynamic pressure. That is half the air density, times the speed squared. It does not matter whether the surface is a wing making lift, or a tailplane being shoved sideways. The speed is squared.

So a blast twice as fast pushes four times as hard. And a blast at half the speed pushes only a quarter as hard.

That single fact explains both halves of the exam answer. Anything that makes the stream faster where you are makes the force on your aeroplane rise steeply. That means more power at the source, or being closer to it. And anything that makes the stream slower makes the force fall steeply. That means less power, or more distance.

Let's work one. These are illustrative speeds, not measured figures. You are behind a turboprop that is taxiing, and its propwash reaches you at 20 kt. Then it applies more power, and the stream reaching you rises to 30 kt. On another occasion, you are closer, and the stream reaches you at 40 kt. How much harder does each one push than the 20 kt stream? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
First, 30 kt against 20 kt. 30 over 20 is 1.5. And 1.5 squared is 2.25. So the push is 2.25 times as hard.

Second, 40 kt against 20 kt. 40 over 20 is 2. And 2 squared is 4. So the push is 4 times as hard.

So a stream only 50 percent faster pushes more than twice as hard. And doubling its speed quadruples the push. Your trainer's tail and wings feel the force, not the speed. That is why the hazard grows so quickly with power, and with closeness. The answers again: 2.25 times, and 4 times.

## How the hazard grows with engine power

The more power an engine is using, the more air it moves, and the faster it throws it. So the blast is stronger, and longer. The hazard increases with power. For the exam, and in practice, it helps to think of three settings.

The first is idle, with the aircraft stationary. That is the smallest hazard, but it is not zero. A large jet at idle still produces a strong blast close behind its engines. It is strong enough to blow a light aeroplane, or a person, over.

The second is breakaway thrust. An aircraft standing still has inertia, which you met in the lesson on basic aerodynamics. And its tyres have settled into the surface. So to start it moving takes considerably more power than to keep it rolling. Turning on the ground needs extra power too, often on one engine. That swings the blast sideways across the apron as the aircraft turns.

This breakaway thrust is considerably greater than idle. And it is used exactly where light aircraft are likely to be. On aprons. Leaving parking bays. At the holding point, as the aircraft starts to line up. So a stationary jet with its beacon on, about to move, is more dangerous to be behind than the same jet rolling steadily along a taxiway at idle.

The third setting is take-off power, or an engine ground run. That is the greatest hazard, and the blast extends a long way behind the aircraft. An aircraft lined up for take-off is producing as much thrust stream as it ever will. So is one doing a full-power engine run on the ground for maintenance.

So the pattern is a simple ladder. Idle is the smallest hazard, but not zero. Breakaway thrust is considerably greater than idle, and it is often used close to other aircraft on aprons. And take-off power, or a ground run, is the greatest, reaching a long way back.

Your clue is the anti-collision beacon, the red flashing or rotating light. It shows that engines are running, or about to start. It is switched on before the first engine turns. So a beacon on a stationary aircraft means one thing. Assume breakaway thrust is coming.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. A stationary aircraft is less dangerous than a moving one. That sounds sensible, and for thrust stream it is wrong. The aircraft that has stopped, and is about to move, will apply breakaway thrust. That is considerably more than the idle thrust of one already taxiing steadily. The exam's trap answer is that the taxiing aircraft is worse. The way out is to remember breakaway thrust.

Question. Why does breakaway thrust exceed the thrust needed to keep taxiing?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because the aircraft at rest must be accelerated from a standstill. It has its inertia to overcome, and the extra resistance of tyres settled on the surface. Turning on the ground needs extra power as well. Once it is rolling, much less thrust keeps it moving.

## How the hazard fades with distance

Once the stream leaves the engine, it starts to mix with the still air around it. Its edges drag the surrounding air along. So the stream widens, and the same momentum is shared among more and more air. The result is a stream that is wider but slower, the further it goes. The hazard decreases with distance from the source.

But it decreases gradually, not suddenly. A stream that is very fast close behind a large jet at high power still has plenty of speed after it has spread a long way. Behind a large jet at high power, the blast can still be strong enough to flip a light aircraft, or to blow debris and people over, well over a hundred metres back. There is no single safe distance to memorise here. Check the current AIP for any specified distances.

So, close behind the source, or close to a hovering helicopter, the hazard is highest. The speed is very high, the exhaust may be hot, and stones and debris fly. Further away, the hazard is lower. But it is still significant behind large aircraft at high power.

Power and distance work together. The hazard at your position depends on both. How hard the stream started out, and how much it has slowed by the time it reaches you. So a large jet at take-off power can be dangerous much further away than a light twin at idle close by. When you judge where to stop, ask both questions. What is that aircraft about to do with its power? And how far am I from it?

A helicopter is different in shape. Its downwash spreads outward around the helicopter, rather than streaming back. So the hazard is a ring around it, rather than a cone behind it. A larger, heavier helicopter must push more air down to hold itself up. So it produces a stronger and wider area of disturbed air. Distance helps in the same way. The outward flow weakens as it spreads. But it reaches further than you might expect, across a grass apron or a taxiway.

Question. On a busy apron, a light twin is idling 20 m ahead of you. A large jet is starting its take-off run 150 m ahead. Which stream is the bigger worry, and why?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: probably the jet's. The hazard at your position depends on power and distance together. The twin's idle wash has little speed to start with. The jet's take-off blast starts so fast that it is still strong after spreading and slowing over 150 m. Distance helps only gradually.

## What a blast does to a light aeroplane

A light aeroplane is a large, light structure, built to be pushed around by air. On the ground, with its engine at idle and its wings not carrying it, it has very little to resist a strong blast. The harms come in five kinds.

The first is being lifted, rolled or turned over. A blast from behind, or from the side, lifts a wing or the tail like a strong gust. The aeroplane can be weathercocked, tipped onto a wingtip, or turned over, while taxiing or parked. That is especially likely if a tail or a wing is presented broadside to the blast. A high-wing trainer is particularly easy to lift.

The second is loss of directional control. Taxiing, a sudden side blast swings the aeroplane off the taxiway faster than rudder and brake can hold it. And on the take-off roll behind an aircraft that has just departed, a blast or wash across the runway acts like a sharp crosswind gust, at the worst moment.

The third is control surface damage. A blast from behind drives the elevator, rudder and ailerons hard against their stops. That can damage hinges, cables and the stops themselves. So hold the controls firmly whenever you are exposed, as you would taxiing in a strong wind from the same direction.

The fourth is foreign object damage, often called FOD. The stream picks up stones, sand, gravel and loose items, and throws them. They chip propellers, crack windscreens, dent leading edges, and injure people. Unsealed aprons, and the edges of taxiways at country aerodromes, are the worst for it.

And the fifth is damage to doors and hangars. Open canopies and doors can be slammed, or torn off their hinges. And hangar doors and loose equipment can be blown about.

So, five kinds of harm. Lifted, rolled or turned over. Loss of directional control. Control surface damage. Foreign object damage. And damage to doors and hangars.

Question. Name three ways a jet blast can harm a taxiing light aeroplane.
[[pause 4]]
The answer: any three of these. It can lift a wing or the tail, and roll or turn the aeroplane over. It can swing the aeroplane off the taxiway faster than rudder and brakes can hold it. It can drive the control surfaces against their stops, and damage them. And it can throw stones and debris into the propeller, the windscreen and the structure.

## Avoiding it, and not causing it

Everything here follows from power and distance. There are four habits.

First, do not taxi or hold directly behind a large aircraft with its engines running. Look for the anti-collision beacon, which shows engines are running or about to start.

Second, allow extra distance behind a jet that is about to move, because it will use breakaway thrust. And allow extra distance behind aircraft at the holding point that will apply take-off power.

Third, position your aircraft at the holding point and in the run-up bay so that your own prop wash is not directed at other aircraft, open hangars or people.

And fourth, keep well clear of hovering and hover-taxiing helicopters, and avoid taxiing downwind of them. The AIP contains guidance on clearances from helicopters and jet aircraft on the ground. Check the current AIP for any specified distances.

Your own propeller is a thrust stream source too. In the run-up bay, at full or high power, your propwash will throw gravel at the aeroplane behind you. It will blow dust into an open hangar, and topple a person walking past. So turn the aeroplane so the wash points at clear ground before running up. And use only the power the check needs.

Let's work one. At the holding point, you are behind a regional jet that has been cleared to line up. Where should you be, and why? Pause if you'd like to think it through.
[[pause 5]]
First, as it moves off, it will apply breakaway thrust. That is a considerably larger blast than its idle.

Second, on the runway it will apply take-off power. That is the largest blast of all, extending a long way back.

Third, so the worst place to be is directly behind its tail. The best is to the side of its tail, and as far back as the taxiway allows.

And fourth, if you cannot get out of line, angle your aircraft so the blast does not hit the tail or a wing broadside. And hold the controls firmly.

The two highest-hazard power settings are both about to happen, and both point straight down the taxiway at you. So the answer: get to the side of its tail, and as far back as you can.

## Bringing it together

You land at a regional airport, and you are cleared to taxi to the general aviation apron. Ahead, a jet has just pushed back with its beacon on. And on the far side, a rescue helicopter is hover-taxiing towards its pad.

The jet is stationary now. But it is about to apply breakaway thrust, probably while turning, and that will swing the blast across your path. So you stop well short, and to the side, and wait until it is rolling steadily away.

The helicopter's downwash spreads in every direction from under it. So you hold your position, rather than taxi downwind of it, until it has settled.

When you reach the run-up bay, you turn so that your own propwash points at open grass, not at the aeroplane parked behind.

Three sources, and one rule. More power means more hazard. More distance means less. And you manage both.

## Key points

Let's recap.

Thrust stream turbulence is the fast, turbulent air thrown back by a jet or a propeller, or down and outward by a rotor. It comes from thrust, not lift, so it is mainly a hazard on the ground and close to it.

The hazard increases with power. It is least at idle, but not zero. It is considerably greater at breakaway thrust, the power needed to start moving from rest or to turn on the ground. And it is greatest at take-off power, or during an engine ground run.

The hazard decreases with distance. But it does so gradually, as the stream widens and slows. Behind a large jet at high power, it remains dangerous a long way back. Check the current AIP for any specified distances.

The force goes with the square of the stream's speed. So small increases in power, or small reductions in distance, make a large difference. A stream twice as fast pushes four times as hard.

Helicopter downwash spreads outward in all directions, as a ring, not a cone. It is stronger and wider from a heavier helicopter.

A final exam tip. The answer is nearly always one of two lines. More power means more hazard. More distance means less hazard, gradually. Breakaway thrust is higher than idle. So a stationary jet with its beacon on, about to move, is more dangerous than one taxiing steadily at idle. And for rotor downwash, remember it spreads outward in all directions, not just behind.
