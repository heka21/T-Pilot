This is BAKC 6.1, Airworthiness and aircraft equipment. By the end you will be able to name the documents that show whether an aircraft is serviceable, read a maintenance release before flight, tell the daily inspection from the pre-flight inspection, and say exactly how a defect is recorded and what happens next. There are worked examples and questions along the way, so pause whenever you want a moment to think.

## What the exam expects (BAKC 6.1)

The exam wants three things from you. First, the documents that tell you whether an aircraft is serviceable, and how to use them to decide whether it is. Second, how an aircraft is certified as fit for flight: the daily inspection, who may do it, how it is recorded, and how it differs from the pilot's pre-flight inspection. And third, how a defect is recorded on the maintenance release, the release to service document, and what happens to the aircraft afterwards.

## Why this matters

Early on a Saturday at Jandakot, you are handed the keys to a club aeroplane that someone else flew last night. You did not see it maintained. And you have no idea what the last pilot noticed.

The only way you can know it is fit to fly is through a short chain of documents and inspections. And the law makes you, the pilot in command, the person who must be satisfied that the chain is complete before you start the engine.

The same chain works in the other direction. When you find something wrong, the way you record it is what stops the next pilot flying it unknowingly.

The exam asks which document shows serviceability, who may sign a daily inspection, how it differs from a pre-flight, and what you do with a defect. All of those make sense once you see the chain as a whole. So this lesson builds it, link by link.

One caution. CASA has been moving these rules between the older Civil Aviation Regulations of {1988|nineteen eighty-eight} and the newer CASR Parts. So verify the current regulation numbers.

## A chain of certificates

Think about what has to be true for an aeroplane to be safe to fly today. There are six things.

Its design must be sound. This particular airframe must have been built to that design, and kept to it. It must be legally on the register, so that someone is accountable for it. Its maintenance must be up to date. Nothing must have gone wrong since the last maintenance. And nothing must be wrong right now, on this walk-around, with this fuel.

Each of those is answered by a different document or inspection. And each covers a different length of time. The design and conformity are certified once, and stay in force while the aircraft is maintained. The maintenance is certified for a period of months or hours. The daily inspection is certified for a day. And the pre-flight covers one flight. Lay them out in that order, and you have the whole of this subtopic.

The approved flight manual or POH, the pilot's operating handbook, with its weight and balance data. And the maintenance release, the key document. From the maintenance release come four checks before flight. It is current in hours and date. The daily inspection is certified today. There is no endorsed defect that grounds the aircraft. And no maintenance is due before the end of the flight.

## The documents that show serviceability

There are six documents to know. We'll take them in turn.

The first is the certificate of airworthiness. It confirms that the aircraft type meets an approved design standard, and that this individual aircraft conforms to it. It is the foundation. Without one, the aircraft cannot be flown as a normal certificated aircraft.

It does not expire on a date. It remains in force as long as the aircraft is maintained as required. That is the link to the next document. The moment the maintenance lapses, the certificate of airworthiness no longer means the aircraft is airworthy.

The second is the certificate of registration. It shows the aircraft is on the Australian register, under its VH marks, and who the registered operator is. It is a legal document, rather than an engineering one. It tells you the aircraft may lawfully operate in Australia, and who is responsible for it.

The third is the approved flight manual, or POH. The pilot's operating handbook must be the one for this aircraft, with its current amendments. And it must include the weight and balance data, or loading system, for this particular aircraft.

That last part matters. Two aeroplanes of the same type have different empty weights and CG positions, because their equipment differs. So a POH borrowed from the hangar next door gives you the wrong numbers. You'll find more on that in the lesson on weight and balance. The limitations section of the POH is also where the speed limits and the structural weights come from. Those are in the lesson on speed limitations.

The fourth is airworthiness directives, together with the maintenance schedule. An airworthiness directive, or AD, is a mandatory instruction to inspect or modify something, issued when a problem is found in a type. The maintenance schedule sets out the periodic inspections. Both are the engineer's business to track, not yours. But they reach you through the maintenance release. If the maintenance release shows an item overdue, the aircraft is unserviceable.

The fifth is the logbooks: the aircraft, engine and propeller logbooks. They hold the full maintenance history. They are not carried in the aircraft, and you do not normally inspect them before flight. For your purposes, their contents are summarised in the maintenance release.

And the sixth is the maintenance release itself. This is the key document. It is the one you actually check before every flight, and it is worth understanding in detail.

Question. The certificate of airworthiness has no expiry date. Why does that not mean the aircraft is airworthy for ever?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because it remains in force only while the aircraft is maintained as required. If the maintenance release has expired, or required maintenance is overdue, the aircraft no longer meets the condition the certificate rests on. So it is not airworthy, whatever the certificate says.

## The maintenance release: what it certifies and how you read it

After a periodic inspection, a licensed aircraft maintenance engineer certifies that the required maintenance has been done. The engineer then releases the aircraft to service for a stated period. The document that records this is the maintenance release. Its existence says: this aircraft was inspected and found fit, and its maintenance is current until the limits written on it.

The release is valid for a stated period. For a private or training aeroplane, that is commonly 100 hours or 12 months, whichever comes first. Verify that against the aircraft's maintenance schedule and the current regulations.

Both limits are written on it. An expiry date. And an expiry time in service, which is the aircraft's hours at which the release lapses. Whichever is reached first ends the release.

The maintenance release has more than one part, and each part has a job. First, the release itself, with the period of validity, and any maintenance due within it. That could be an item due at a certain hours figure, or an inspection required by an airworthiness directive, due by a date. Second, the daily inspection certifications, with a line for each day the aircraft flies. And third, the defect section, Part 2. That is where defects are endorsed, and where any permissible unserviceabilities are recorded, with their conditions.

[[diagram:maintenance-release-anatomy]]
Picture a simplified maintenance release, a schematic rather than a copy of the real form, in three parts. At the top, Part 1, the release itself. It gives the expiry date and the expiry time in service, whichever comes first, and any maintenance due within the period. Below it, Part 2, for defects and permissible unserviceabilities. And below that, Part 3, holding the daily inspection certifications. So the four questions you ask have four places to look.

Reading it before flight is a short routine with four questions.

The first question. Is it current in both hours and date? Compare today's date with the expiry date. And compare the aircraft's hours now with the expiry time in service.

The second. Has the daily inspection been certified for today?

The third. Is there an endorsed defect that grounds the aircraft? A defect that has not been cleared, or accepted as a permissible unserviceability, does ground it. And a permissible unserviceability may carry conditions that you must meet, for example a limit on the kind of flight.

And the fourth. Is any required maintenance due before the end of the flight you intend? Not just, is anything overdue now. But, will anything fall due while I am airborne? Add the planned flight time to the current hours, and compare.

A no to the first two, or a yes to the last two, means the aircraft is not serviceable for that flight.

Let's work one. Today is 3 October. The maintenance release was issued on 12 March this year, at {3,412.5|three thousand four hundred and twelve point five} hours time in service. It is valid for 100 hours or 12 months. The aircraft now shows {3,498.7|three thousand four hundred and ninety-eight point seven} hours. You plan a 1.5 hour flight. Today's daily inspection is certified. And Part 2 has one entry: a cabin light unserviceable, endorsed by an engineer as a permissible unserviceability, with no conditions relevant to a day VFR flight. Is the aeroplane serviceable for your flight? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 8]]
Step one, the expiry by hours. {3,412.5|three thousand four hundred and twelve point five} plus 100 is {3,512.5|three thousand five hundred and twelve point five} hours.

Step two, the expiry by date. That is 12 March next year. Today is well inside it.

Step three, the hours at the end of your flight. {3,498.7|three thousand four hundred and ninety-eight point seven} plus 1.5 is {3,500.2|three thousand five hundred point two} hours. That is below {3,512.5|three thousand five hundred and twelve point five}.

Step four, the daily inspection. It is certified today.

Step five, the defects. There is one, and it is accepted as a permissible unserviceability, with no condition that affects your flight.

All four answers are the right ones. So the aeroplane is serviceable for this flight. And notice that the hours limit, 12.3 hours after you land, is much closer than the date limit. On a busy training aeroplane, it usually is.

Now one for you to try. It is the same aeroplane, two weeks later. It now shows {3,511.0|three thousand five hundred and eleven point zero} hours. The daily is certified. And you plan a 2.0 hour cross-country to Northam and back. Can you go?
[[pause 8]]
The answer: no. The maintenance release expires at {3,512.5|three thousand five hundred and twelve point five} hours. You would land at {3,511.0|three thousand five hundred and eleven point zero} plus 2.0, which is {3,513.0|three thousand five hundred and thirteen point zero} hours. That is half an hour past the expiry. The release is current now, but required maintenance falls due before the end of the flight. So the aircraft is not serviceable for that flight. You could do a flight of under 1.5 hours. Or wait for the periodic inspection, and a new maintenance release.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Current is not enough. The question to ask of the maintenance release is whether it will still be current when you land, and whether any item of maintenance falls due during the flight. An aircraft that is legal on the ground can become one that should not be flying, halfway through a long cross-country.

## Certifying the aircraft for flight: the daily inspection

The maintenance release covers months. A lot can happen in a day. A bird's nest in the cowl. A tyre cut on a stone. A fuel cap left loose by a refueller. So on top of the periodic maintenance there is a daily check, and it is formally certified.

A daily inspection must be completed, and certified on the maintenance release, before the first flight of each day. It is a thorough inspection to the manufacturer's or operator's daily inspection schedule. That is a written checklist, not just a look around.

It is signed by an authorised person. That is either a licensed engineer, or a pilot who holds a pilot licence and is authorised by the operator to carry out daily inspections on that type. Once certified, it is valid for the rest of that day. So the second and third flights of the day do not need another one.

Why is the daily certified? Accountability. Someone has signed, on the document that travels with the aircraft, that the inspection was done to the schedule. If you are the first to fly the aeroplane today, and that line is blank, the aircraft is not ready to fly. It waits until an authorised person completes the daily.

## The pre-flight inspection: every flight, your responsibility

Separately, the pilot in command carries out a pre-flight inspection before every flight. This is the walk-around in the POH. Fuel quantity and a fuel sample. Oil. The control surfaces, and their full and free movement. Tyres. The propeller. The pitot cover off. The tie-downs off. It does not need to be certified. Nothing is written on the maintenance release.

What makes it binding is the pilot in command's wider responsibility. Under the Part 91 rules, the pilot in command is responsible for being satisfied of three things before flight. That the aircraft is airworthy. That it is correctly loaded. And that it has the required documents and equipment on board.

So the daily inspection may have been certified by someone else at {0700|oh seven hundred}. But if you find a fuel cap missing at {1400|fourteen hundred}, that responsibility makes it your problem, not theirs.

[[diagram:daily-vs-preflight-inspection]]
Picture two columns side by side. On the left, the daily inspection. Once a day, before the first flight. Done to the daily inspection schedule. By a licensed engineer, or an authorised pilot. Certified on the maintenance release. And valid for the rest of the day. On the right, the pre-flight inspection. Before every flight. The POH walk-around. By the pilot in command. And not certified.

So the two differ on every count. When: once a day, against every flight. To what: the schedule, against the walk-around. By whom: an engineer or authorised pilot, against the pilot in command. Recorded: certified on the maintenance release, against not certified. And valid for: the rest of the day, against that one flight.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. The daily inspection and the pre-flight inspection are different things. Doing one does not replace the other. An authorised pilot who does the daily before the first flight still has to do a pre-flight before each later flight. And the pre-flight is never written on the maintenance release.

Question. You are the third pilot to fly the club aeroplane today. Which inspection must you do, and what must you check on the maintenance release about the other one?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: you must do the pre-flight inspection yourself, because it is required before every flight. On the maintenance release, you check that today's daily inspection has been certified by an authorised person. It was done before the first flight, and it is valid for the rest of the day, so you do not repeat it.

## Recording a defect

Now the other direction. During the walk-around, you find the nosewheel tyre worn through to the cords. Or after landing, you realise the alternator warning light flickered for the last twenty minutes. Whatever the defect, the next pilot has no way of knowing about it, unless you write it down where they will look. That place is the maintenance release, the document they must check before they fly.

A defect found before or after a flight must be recorded on the maintenance release, in the defect section, Part 2. The entry has three parts. First, a clear description of the defect, written so that the next pilot and the engineer understand exactly what is wrong. Second, the date, and the aircraft time in service. And third, the signature and licence number of the person making the endorsement.

If the defect appears in flight, the pilot endorses it after landing.

Once the defect is endorsed, the aircraft cannot fly again until a licensed engineer does one of two things. Either rectifies the defect and signs it off. Or determines it is a permissible unserviceability that does not affect safety, and endorses it as such, with any conditions. A pilot in command must not fly with a known defect that has not been dealt with in this way.

[[diagram:defect-endorsement-flow]]
Picture a flow from top to bottom. At the top, a defect is found. Or it appears in flight, and then it is dealt with after landing. Next, it is endorsed in Part 2 of the maintenance release, with a description, the date and time in service, and the signature and licence number. Then the aircraft stops, grounded. Only two paths lead on. A licensed engineer rectifies it and signs it off. Or a licensed engineer endorses it as a permissible unserviceability, with any conditions.

The logic is simple once you see it. The endorsement is a lock. It is placed by whoever finds the problem, and that can be any pilot. It can be released only by an engineer. The engineer either fixes the problem, or takes professional responsibility for saying it does not matter for safety, perhaps with a condition attached. Neither the pilot who found it, nor the next pilot, can decide alone that it is probably fine.

Some defects are also reportable. Reporting a defect that affects safety is an obligation under the transport safety reporting rules. It is separate from endorsing it on the maintenance release. The endorsement protects the next pilot of this aircraft. The report lets the safety system see patterns across many aircraft.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Telling the next pilot, or the instructor on duty, is not recording the defect. The defect must be endorsed on the maintenance release, in Part 2. With a description, the date, the time in service, a signature and a licence number. Only that endorsement reaches every future pilot, through the document they are required to check.

Question. On your pre-flight, you find a crack in the exhaust pipe. What do you do, and when may the aeroplane fly again?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: do not fly. Endorse the defect in Part 2 of the maintenance release, with a clear description, the date, the time in service, and your signature and licence number. The aeroplane may fly again only when a licensed engineer has rectified it and signed it off. Or has endorsed it as a permissible unserviceability, with any conditions.

## Bringing it together

You arrive for a solo navigation exercise. The certificate of registration and the certificate of airworthiness are in order. The POH in the seat pocket is this aircraft's, with its own weight and balance data.

On the maintenance release, you check the expiry date, then the hours. The aircraft has 4 hours left on the release, and your flight is 2.5. So nothing falls due before you land. Today's daily inspection was certified at {0715|oh seven fifteen} by an authorised pilot. Part 2 shows one defect from yesterday, a sticking fuel gauge. It was rectified, and signed off by an engineer this morning.

Then you do your own pre-flight. And you find a cut in the left main tyre, deep enough to show the cords. That breaks the chain at the last link.

You endorse the defect in Part 2. And the flight waits until an engineer has replaced the tyre and signed it off. When that is done, the chain is complete again, and every link has a signature on it.

Question. Which single document do you check before every flight to know the aircraft's maintenance is current and its defects are dealt with? And what four things do you look for on it?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the maintenance release. It must be current in hours and date. It must have today's daily inspection certified. It must show no endorsed defect that grounds the aircraft, and any permissible unserviceability must have its conditions met. And it must have no maintenance falling due before the end of your flight.

## Key points

Let's recap.

The documents. The certificate of airworthiness, which stays in force while the aircraft is maintained. The certificate of registration. The approved flight manual or POH, with this aircraft's weight and balance data. And above all, the maintenance release. Airworthiness directives and the maintenance schedule reach you through it. The logbooks hold the history, and they are not carried.

On the maintenance release, check four things. Hours and date. Today's daily. Grounding defects. And maintenance due before you land. The release is commonly valid for 100 hours or 12 months, whichever comes first.

The daily inspection is done once a day, before the first flight, to the daily schedule. It is done by a licensed engineer or an authorised pilot. It is certified on the maintenance release, and valid for the rest of the day.

The pre-flight inspection is done before every flight, by the pilot in command, and it is not certified. The pilot in command must be satisfied the aircraft is airworthy, loaded correctly, and properly equipped.

A defect is endorsed in Part 2 of the maintenance release, with a description, the date and time in service, and the signature and licence number. The aircraft is then grounded until an engineer rectifies it, or endorses it as a permissible unserviceability, with any conditions. Defects that appear in flight are endorsed after landing.

A final exam tip. The daily inspection and the pre-flight inspection are different things. The daily is once a day, to a schedule, and certified on the maintenance release by an authorised person. The pre-flight is before every flight, by the pilot, and it is not certified. If a question asks which document shows maintenance status and outstanding defects, the answer is the maintenance release. Not the certificate of airworthiness, and not the logbooks. And remember the before the end of the flight test. Maintenance due during the flight makes the aircraft unserviceable for it.

That's the end of BAKC 6.1.
