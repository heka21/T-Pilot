This is BAKC 2.5, Basic physics. By the end you will be able to explain kinetic and potential energy in flying terms, say what the aircraft energy state is, and say what accelerating, slowing down, climbing and descending each do to it. On the way you will meet Newton's three laws, and how forces add. There are worked examples and questions along the way, so pause whenever you want a moment to think.

## What the exam expects (BAKC 2.5)

The exam wants three things from you. First, to explain kinetic and potential energy, and how each shows up in ordinary flying. Second, to explain what aircraft energy state means, in terms of kinetic and potential energy. And third, to explain what acceleration, deceleration, climbing and descending each do to the aircraft energy state.

## Why this matters

Every approach you fly is an exercise in energy. Turn final at Jandakot too high and too fast, and you have more energy than the runway can absorb. The aeroplane floats past the touchdown markers, and you go around. Turn final low and slow on a gusty afternoon, and you have too little. You get a sink you cannot stop with power, close to the stall.

The engine failure you will practise over the training area is the same problem without an engine. All you have is the height and the speed you had when it stopped. And the field you can reach depends on how you spend them.

The exam asks about this subtopic in words rather than numbers. What doubling the speed does to kinetic energy. Where the energy in a glide comes from. And what happens to the energy state in a zoom or a level acceleration. This lesson starts with the forces that move an aeroplane, because energy is what those forces do over a distance.

## Forces and Newton's three laws

Before energy, forces. A force is a push or a pull, measured in newtons. Four of them act on an aeroplane in flight. Lift, from the wings. Weight, from gravity. Thrust, from the propeller. And drag, from the air resisting the aeroplane's motion. How those four forces change the aeroplane's motion was set out by Isaac Newton in three laws. Each one has a flying example you already know.

The first law. An object keeps doing what it is doing, unless a net force acts on it. A body at rest stays at rest. And a body moving in a straight line at a steady speed keeps doing exactly that, unless the forces on it are out of balance. This tendency to resist a change of motion is called inertia.

In steady, straight and level cruise, the four forces balance. Lift equals weight, and thrust equals drag. There is no net force, so the aeroplane neither speeds up, slows down, climbs nor descends. Balance does not mean no motion. It means no change of motion.

The pen that floats off the glareshield in turbulence shows the same law. It carries on in a straight line, while the aeroplane drops away under it.

[[diagram:four-forces-level-flight]]
Picture an aeroplane side on, with four arrows. Lift points up, from the centre of pressure on the wing. Weight points down, from the centre of gravity. Thrust points forward along the thrust line, and drag points back. In steady straight and level flight, lift equals weight and thrust equals drag. One more detail: the centre of pressure is a little behind the centre of gravity, so lift and weight try to pitch the nose down, and a small force from the tailplane balances that. With no net force, the aeroplane holds its speed and its height.

The second law. A net force makes a mass accelerate. The acceleration is the net force divided by the mass.

Open the throttle at the start of the take-off roll. Thrust now exceeds drag and rolling friction, and the difference accelerates the aeroplane down the runway. The same law explains why a heavier aeroplane accelerates more slowly and needs more runway. The same excess thrust is pushing more mass.

A turn is an acceleration too. It is a change of direction rather than speed, so it also needs a net force. That is why you bank, so that part of the lift points towards the centre of the turn.

The third law. Every force has an equal and opposite reaction. You cannot push on something without it pushing back on you.

The propeller drives a mass of air backwards, so the air drives the propeller, and the aeroplane, forwards. The wing deflects air downwards behind it, so the air pushes the wing upwards. That is why a wing in flight leaves a downwash behind it. And it is why a large aeroplane leaves wake turbulence: the air it pushed down is still moving.

[[diagram:newtons-laws-in-flight]]
Picture three small panels, one for each law. In the first, an aeroplane in steady cruise, with lift equal to weight and thrust equal to drag: no net force, and no change of motion. In the second, an aeroplane on the take-off roll. 1,000 kg of aeroplane, 2,500 N of thrust against 1,000 N of drag and rolling friction, leaves a net force of 1,500 N. That gives an acceleration of 1.5 metres per second, every second. In the third, the propeller drives air backwards and the air drives the aeroplane forwards, while the wing deflects air downwards and the air pushes the wing up.

Let's work that second law example properly. An aeroplane of mass 1,000 kg is early in its take-off roll. Its thrust is 2,500 N, and drag plus rolling friction is 1,000 N. What is its acceleration? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
First the net force. 2,500 minus 1,000 is 1,500 N forward. Then the acceleration: the net force divided by the mass. 1,500 divided by 1,000 is 1.5 metres per second squared. Now carry 200 kg more. The same 1,500 N, divided by 1,200 kg, gives 1.25 metres per second squared. Slower acceleration, and a longer run to lift-off speed.

Question. In a steady climb at constant airspeed, is there a net force on the aeroplane?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: no. The speed and direction are constant, so by the first law the forces are in balance. The climb is not an acceleration. Thrust is simply larger than drag, by the amount needed to balance the part of the weight that acts back along the climbing path.

## Forces have direction: adding them tip to tail

A force has a size and a direction, and both matter. Quantities like that are called vectors. It is the same idea as wind velocity, from the lesson on distance, speed and velocity: a speed with a direction. You draw a vector as an arrow. Its length is the size, and it points in the direction.

To add two forces, you cannot just add the numbers, because they may not point the same way. Instead, draw the first arrow. Then draw the second one starting from the tip of the first. The arrow from the tail of the first to the tip of the second is the resultant: the single force that has the same effect as the two together. This is tip-to-tail addition.

There are three simple cases. Two forces in the same direction add as ordinary numbers. Two in opposite directions subtract. And two at right angles form the two short sides of a right-angled triangle, so the resultant is its long side.

[[diagram:vector-addition-tip-to-tail]]
Picture those three cases one above the other. In the first, two arrows of 300 N point the same way, end to end, and the resultant is 600 N. In the second, 300 N points forward and 300 N points back. The second arrow returns to where the first began, so the resultant is zero, and the motion does not change. In the third, the second arrow starts at the tip of the first, at right angles to it. The resultant runs from the first tail to the last tip, across the long side of the triangle.

A steady glide shows why this matters. With the engine at idle there is no thrust. Just lift, drag and weight. Lift acts at right angles to the flight path, and drag acts back along it. Added tip to tail, lift and drag make a resultant that points straight up, and exactly balances the weight.

That is how the aeroplane can be in equilibrium, with no net force, while it descends. The path is tilted so that the lift and drag together can hold the weight.

[[diagram:forces-in-a-glide]]
Picture an aeroplane gliding down a straight, sloping path, with no thrust. Lift points up and slightly forward, at right angles to the path. Drag points back along the path. Weight points straight down. Put lift and drag tip to tail, and their resultant is exactly equal and opposite to the weight. The glide angle is set by the ratio of drag to lift, so the flattest glide comes at the best ratio of lift to drag.

Let's put numbers on it. A 1,000 kg aeroplane, with a weight of about 9,810 N, is in a steady glide with a lift to drag ratio of 10. Lift is about 9,760 N, and drag about 976 N, at right angles to each other. What is their resultant?
[[pause 5]]
The two forces are at right angles, so use Pythagoras. Square each one, add the squares, and take the square root. The answer comes out at 9,809 N. That equals the weight, to within rounding, and it points straight up. That is the condition for a steady glide.

In a climb, the same idea runs the other way. Split the weight into two parts, one along the path and one across it. The part along the path acts backwards, like extra drag. So the engine must supply thrust to overcome drag plus that part of the weight. You will meet this again in the lessons on climbing and descending.

[[diagram:forces-in-a-climb]]
Picture an aeroplane climbing up a sloping path. Thrust points forward along the path, and drag points back along it. Lift is at right angles to the path, and weight points straight down. Now split the weight. One part lies back along the path, and adds to the drag. The other lies across the path, and lift balances it. So thrust must equal drag plus that backward part of the weight. And lift is a little less than the weight.

Question. Two forces of 300 N act on a body, one pointing forward and one pointing back. What is the resultant, and what happens to the body's motion?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: zero. Added tip to tail, the second arrow returns to where the first began. With no net force, the body keeps its present motion, whether that is at rest or moving steadily.

## Kinetic and potential energy

Energy is the capacity to do work. And work is done when a force moves something through a distance. Push a stalled car 10 m along a road, and you have done work. The car now has energy of motion that it did not have before.

An aeroplane in flight carries two forms of mechanical energy. The whole of energy management is about the balance between them. First, kinetic energy. Then, potential energy.

Kinetic energy is the energy of speed. It is the energy a body has because it is moving. Kinetic energy equals a half, times the mass, times the speed squared.

Two things follow. Kinetic energy is proportional to mass, so a heavier aeroplane at the same speed has more of it. And it goes with the square of the speed. Doubling the speed gives four times the kinetic energy. And even a small increase in speed adds a disproportionate amount.

For an aeroplane, the speed that matters for flying is its speed through the air. So kinetic energy is tied to airspeed. On the ground, during the landing roll, what the brakes have to absorb depends on the speed over the ground. That is why a tailwind landing is so much longer.

[[diagram:kinetic-energy-speed-squared]]
Picture a graph that curves upwards ever more steeply. Along the bottom is speed, as a multiple of some reference speed. Up the side is kinetic energy, compared with the energy at that reference speed. Go 10 percent faster, and the curve is already 21 percent higher, because 1.1 squared is 1.21. Double the speed, and it is four times as high.

Let's work one. The POH approach speed is 65 kt. First, you touch down 10 percent fast. Second, you touch down at 75 kt. How much more energy is there to get rid of, each time? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
The first case. 1.1 squared is 1.21, so there is 21 percent more energy to dissipate. The second case. The speed ratio is 75 divided by 65, which is 1.154. Squared, that is 1.33, so 33 percent more energy. Ten knots fast does not sound much. But the brakes, the tyres and the drag must absorb a third more energy. That is why excess approach speed lengthens the landing roll so much, and why it is a common cause of long landings.

Now potential energy, the energy of height. It is energy stored by height in the Earth's gravity. Potential energy equals the mass, times g, times the height. Here g is the acceleration due to gravity, 9.81 metres per second squared.

It took work to lift the aeroplane there. And it is all available to be given back as the aeroplane comes down. Potential energy goes up in a straight line with height. Double the height above the ground, and you double the potential energy. And like kinetic energy, it is proportional to mass.

The two kinds of energy are interchangeable. Lower the nose and gravity accelerates the aeroplane: height becomes speed. Raise the nose and the aeroplane slows as it climbs: speed becomes height. Without drag and thrust, the total stays the same, and only the share changes. This is the conservation of energy. And it is the most useful single idea in this subtopic.

Now, speed as stored height. Put the two formulas side by side, and ask a question. If all the kinetic energy at a given speed were turned into height, how much height would it buy? Set the kinetic energy equal to the potential energy. The mass appears on both sides, so it cancels. That means the answer is the same for a Jabiru and a jumbo jet. The height equals the speed squared, divided by twice g.

At 100 kt, which is 51.4 metres per second, that works out at 135 m, about 440 ft. Every aeroplane flying at 100 kt carries, in its speed, the equivalent of about 440 ft of height.

That is why pilots call airspeed stored height. It can be cashed in for altitude by raising the nose. And height can be cashed in for speed by lowering it.

Here is how aeroplanes use the two stores in ordinary flying. There are four cases.

First, take-off. The engine turns the chemical energy in the fuel into kinetic energy along the runway, accelerating to lift-off speed. Then into potential energy in the climb.

Second, a pull-up, or zoom. Raising the nose without adding power trades kinetic energy for potential energy. You gain height and lose airspeed.

Third, the glide. With the engine at idle, there is no thrust doing work. The aeroplane steadily gives up potential energy to do the work against drag. That is why a glide must descend.

And fourth, landing. All the kinetic energy at touchdown must be absorbed by the brakes, the tyres and drag.

Question. In a glide with the engine at idle, why does the aeroplane have to descend?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because drag is always taking energy away. With no thrust, the only store left to pay for it is potential energy. Holding a steady airspeed, the aeroplane converts height into the work done against drag. So it must lose height continuously.

## Aircraft energy state

Add the two stores together, and you have the aircraft energy state. It is the total mechanical energy the aeroplane has at a moment: kinetic energy, which is airspeed, plus potential energy, which is height. It describes how much margin the pilot has to work with.

Because the mass cancels in speed as stored height, it is often easiest to think of the energy state as a single height. The actual height, plus the extra height the speed would buy. Two aeroplanes at 1,000 ft, one at 60 kt and one at 100 kt, are in very different energy states. The faster one has the equivalent of roughly 280 ft more to spend.

A high energy state is fast, high, or both. It is useful when you need range or options, for reaching a forced-landing field or clearing terrain. But on approach it means too fast or too high. Floating, long landings and overruns.

A low energy state is slow, low, or both. Close to the stall, with little height to recover. Many loss-of-control accidents begin here, typically on the base turn or on final, where there is neither speed nor height to spare.

The pilot manages the energy state with three tools, and it pays to keep them separate in your mind.

First, the throttle. It adds energy to the system, or stops adding it. More power is the only way to raise the total.

Second, the elevator. It shares the existing energy between speed and height. It cannot create energy. It only moves it from one store to the other.

And third, drag. It is always present, flap increases it, and it removes energy. It is how you get rid of an excess.

[[diagram:powered-descent-vs-glide]]
Picture three descent paths fanning out from the same point, all flown at the same indicated airspeed. The top one, with power added, is the shallowest, with the lowest rate of descent and the nose highest. The middle one is the glide, with the power off. The bottom one, with flap lowered, is the steepest, with the highest rate of descent and the nose noticeably lower. Power adds energy and flattens the path. Flap adds drag, removes energy, and steepens it.

Question. You are on final, on the correct approach path, but 10 kt fast. Which tool removes the excess energy, and which only moves it?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: more drag, which means flap if you are within its limiting speed, or less power, removes the excess. Raising the nose with the elevator only moves the energy from speed into height. That would put you above the approach path instead.

## Accelerating, decelerating, climbing and descending

The third part of the subtopic asks what four simple manoeuvres do to the energy state. Each one is a change in one store, the other, or both. The question is always the same. Did the total change? And if so, where did the energy come from, or go?

First, accelerating at constant height. Kinetic energy rises, and potential energy stays the same. The total has gone up, so the energy came from outside. The engine has done more work than drag has removed. You opened the throttle.

Second, decelerating at constant height. Kinetic energy falls, and potential energy stays the same. The total has gone down, so drag has taken more than the engine provided. You reduced power, or extended flap.

Third, climbing at constant speed. Kinetic energy stays the same, and potential energy rises. The total has gone up, so the engine supplied it. Thrust exceeds drag by enough to lift the weight. This is a normal climb, and it is why climb performance depends on spare power.

Fourth, descending at constant speed. Kinetic energy stays the same, and potential energy falls. The total has gone down. You reduced power, and the energy lost from height has gone into overcoming drag.

And then the special case: a zoom climb, or a dive, with the power unchanged. Here one store rises while the other falls. Apart from what drag takes, the total is roughly constant. That is the trade, and it is the case the exam likes most.

So the pattern is this. Accelerating level, or climbing at a steady speed, raises the total, and the engine pays. Decelerating level, or descending at a steady speed, lowers the total, and drag takes it. A zoom or a dive, with the throttle fixed, keeps the total roughly constant. It only changes the share.

The energy formula, rearranged, tells you how much height a zoom can buy. Ignoring drag, the kinetic energy you give up between two speeds becomes height. The height equals the first speed squared, minus the second speed squared, all divided by twice g. The speeds go in metres per second, where 1 kt is 0.514 metres per second.

[[diagram:energy-trade-zoom]]
Picture an aeroplane at 100 knots raising its nose, with no change of power, and zooming up to 60 knots. Beside it, two energy bars. As it climbs, the kinetic bar shrinks and the potential bar grows by the same amount, so the total stays the same. Ignoring drag, the kinetic energy it loses between 100 and 60 knots reappears as about 280 ft of height.

Let's work that one. 100 kt is 51.4 metres per second. 60 kt is 30.9 metres per second. Take g as 9.81, and ignore drag. How much height does the zoom buy? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 8]]
First, the squares. 51.4 squared is 2,642. 30.9 squared is 955. The difference is 1,687. Divide by twice g, which is 19.6, and you get about 86 m. In feet, 86 times 3.28 is about 280 ft. In practice, drag makes the real gain smaller. But the example shows that airspeed is stored height.

Now the same 40 kt loss, from a higher speed: 120 kt down to 80 kt. 120 kt is 61.7 metres per second, and 80 kt is 41.2 metres per second.
[[pause 8]]
The squares. 61.7 squared is 3,807. 41.2 squared is 1,697. The difference is 2,110. Divided by 19.6, that is about 108 m. Times 3.28, about 350 ft. So the same 40 kt loss buys more height starting from a higher speed. That is because the energy goes with the square. The top 40 kt of a fast aeroplane's speed holds more energy than the bottom 40 kt. 280 ft from the slower zoom, and 350 ft from the faster one.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Climbing does not, by itself, mean the energy state has increased. If the airspeed fell while you climbed, with the power unchanged, you only traded speed for height, and the total is roughly the same. The total rises only if the engine supplied more work than drag removed. That means the speed held or rose while you climbed, or it rose while you held height.

Question. An aeroplane climbs at a constant airspeed with the throttle fixed. Has its total energy changed, and where did any change come from?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: it has increased. Kinetic energy is unchanged, and potential energy has risen. Nothing was traded from speed, so the extra energy must have come from the engine, through thrust exceeding drag.

## Bringing it together

You are cruising at 110 kt and 2,500 ft over farmland east of the Scarp, when the engine stops. Your energy state at that moment is everything you have. 2,500 ft of height, plus the height stored in 110 kt.

The POH best glide speed is 70 kt. That is the speed at which the aeroplane loses the least energy to drag for each mile covered. Holding 110 kt would waste energy on high drag. So you raise the nose, and let the speed decay to 70 kt, trading the excess kinetic energy for height.

How much is it worth? 110 kt is 56.6 metres per second, and 70 kt is 36.0. Ignoring drag, the squares are 3,204 and 1,297, and the difference divided by 19.6 is about 97 m. That is about 320 ft. Drag takes some of it, so the altimeter will show less. But it is height you would otherwise have thrown away.

From there, the glide spends potential energy at a steady rate, to pay for drag. The field you pick has to be within reach of that energy.

Over the field, you find you are high. Diving would only turn the excess height into excess speed, and a float. So instead you extend flap, to add drag and remove energy, and you touch down at the right speed.

Every part of this lesson was in that one forced landing. Speed as stored height. The total energy state. And what a climb, a descent and a deceleration each do to it. The POH procedure for your type governs the details.

## Key points

Let's recap.

Kinetic energy is the energy of speed: a half, times the mass, times the speed squared. Double the speed, and you have four times the energy. 10 percent fast, and you have 21 percent more. Potential energy is the energy of height: the mass, times g, times the height. Both grow with mass, and each can be traded for the other. Airspeed is stored height, about 440 ft at 100 kt.

The aircraft energy state is the total of kinetic and potential energy: speed plus height. A high energy state is fast or high, and it floats on approach. A low energy state is slow or low, near the stall, with little to recover with. The throttle adds energy. The elevator shares it. Drag removes it.

Accelerating level, and climbing at constant speed, raise the total, and the engine pays. Decelerating level, and descending at constant speed, lower it, and drag takes it. A zoom or a dive with fixed power only trades one store for the other.

And underneath all of it, Newton. Forces in balance mean no change of motion: that is the first law. A net force accelerates a mass: that is the second law. Every force has an equal and opposite reaction: that is the third. And forces add as vectors, tip to tail.

A final exam tip. The questions here are about the trade. If the speed falls while the aeroplane climbs with the throttle fixed, energy has been traded, not gained. If it climbs and the speed holds, the engine supplied the extra. In a glide, the energy to overcome drag comes from height. Have by heart that kinetic energy goes with the square of the speed, and potential energy goes in a straight line with height. And read every option for whether the total changed, or only the share.

That's the end of BAKC 2.5.
