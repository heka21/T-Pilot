This is BAKC 2.1, Direction of flight. By the end you will be able to give a direction three ways, as a three-figure group, as a runway number and by the clock, tell heading from track, and turn a true direction into a magnetic and then a compass one. There are worked examples and questions along the way, so pause whenever you want a moment to think.

## What the exam expects (BAKC 2.1)

The exam wants three things from you. First, to express a direction three ways: as a three-figure group, as a two-figure group, which is a runway number, and in the clock code. Second, to say what the aircraft's heading means, and how it differs from the direction you actually move. And third, to explain the difference between a direction quoted as true, as magnetic and as compass, and to convert between them.

## Why this matters

Every instruction you will ever get about direction uses one of these forms. Perth Tower clears you to runway 24. Jandakot Approach calls traffic two o'clock, two miles, a Cessna at 1,500 ft. And the nav log for your first cross-country to York lists a track you measured off the chart in degrees true, and a heading you will fly by the compass.

Each of those numbers is measured from a different reference. A pilot who mixes them up ends up a few degrees off track, looking at the wrong patch of sky for traffic, or lining up on the wrong runway.

The exam tests this subtopic with short recall questions and one-step conversions. Traffic positions by the clock. Runway designators. And the sequence from true to magnetic to compass, with its memory aid. It all rests on one simple idea, an angle measured clockwise from north, so that is where we start.

## Direction is an angle from north

Stand in the middle of a big field, facing north, and turn slowly to your right. As you turn, the angle between north and the way you are facing grows from zero. It passes 90 degrees when you face east, 180 degrees when you face south, and 270 degrees when you face west. After a full turn of 360 degrees you are facing north again.

That is all an aviation direction is. It is the angle, measured clockwise from north, between north and the direction you are describing. There are no words like north-north-east or south-west by west in the cockpit. There is a number from {001|zero zero one} to {360|three six zero}.

Clockwise matters. A direction of {045|zero four five} is half-way between north and east, not half-way between north and west. If you are ever unsure, picture the face of a clock laid on a map, with the 12 pointing north. The hands move the same way the numbers grow.

[[diagram:compass-rose-headings]]
Picture a compass rose, a circle with north at the top. Going clockwise round it, north is {360|three six zero}, east is {090|zero nine zero}, south is {180|one eight zero}, south-west is {225|two two five}, and west is {270|two seven zero}. A runway is drawn across the circle. One end points 214 degrees magnetic and is runway 21. The other end points 034 degrees and is runway 03. Notice the rule the picture makes: clockwise from north, always three figures, and the two ends of a runway differ by 18.

The reciprocal of a direction is the direction exactly opposite, 180 degrees away. You need it constantly. It gives you the other end of a runway, the way home along the same track, and the bearing of you from another aircraft. If the direction is less than 180, add 180. If it is 180 or more, subtract 180. So the reciprocal of {060|zero six zero} is {240|two four zero}. And the reciprocal of {300|three zero zero} is {120|one two zero}.

## The 3-figure group

Headings, tracks, bearings and wind directions are always written and spoken as three figures, with leading zeros where needed.

The reason is that a cockpit is full of numbers. Speeds, altitudes, frequencies and times. A direction of five could be mistaken for anything. But zero zero five can only be a direction. Three figures also make every direction the same length, so a misheard digit is more likely to be noticed.

So north is written {360|three six zero}, and spoken three six zero. East is {090|zero nine zero}, spoken zero nine zero. 5 degrees is {005|zero zero five}, spoken zero zero five. And south-west is {225|two two five}, spoken two two five.

Notice that north as a heading is {360|three six zero}, not zero zero zero. The scale runs from {001|zero zero one} to {360|three six zero}, so a direction of exactly north sits at the end of the scale, not at the start.

Question. Why is a heading of east written {090|zero nine zero}, and not just 90?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because directions are always given as three figures. That way they cannot be confused with a speed, an altitude or any other number, and a missing digit is obvious. Ninety degrees becomes {090|zero nine zero}, spoken zero nine zero.

## The 2-figure group: runway numbers

A runway is a long strip pointing in one direction. Pilots need to know that direction at a glance, from the air and from the chart. The runway designator gives it as a two-figure group.

Here is the recipe. Take the runway's magnetic direction. Round it to the nearest 10 degrees. Then drop the last zero.

A runway aligned 214 degrees magnetic rounds to 210, so it is runway 21. Its other end points the opposite way, {034|zero three four} degrees magnetic. That rounds to {030|zero three zero}, and becomes runway 03. Because the two ends are reciprocals, 180 degrees apart, their numbers always differ by 18.

Perth has two runways, 03 and 21, and 06 and 24. Without looking at a chart, you already know that runway 24 points roughly south-west, {240|two four zero} degrees magnetic. And you know a westerly sea breeze will be close to straight down it. The number is painted in large figures at the threshold, so it also tells you which end you are looking at.

The direction behind the designator is magnetic, not true. That is so the number on the runway can be compared directly with the direction indicator, and with the magnetic surface wind that ATC, air traffic control, gives you. Magnetic north wanders slowly over the years. So some runways are renumbered when their magnetic direction drifts across a rounding boundary.

Let's work one. A country strip is aligned 166 degrees magnetic. What are the numbers at its two ends? Pause if you'd like to try it first.
[[pause 6]]
First, round to the nearest 10 degrees: 166 becomes 170. Drop the last zero, and that end is runway 17. Now the reciprocal. 166 plus 180 is 346 degrees magnetic, which rounds to 350. So the other end is runway 35. Check it: 35 minus 17 is 18. So the strip is runway 17 and 35. Landing on 17 you are pointing roughly south. On 35, roughly north.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. The runway number is a magnetic direction. So a question that gives you a runway aligned so many degrees true is asking you to apply variation first. The other trap is the reciprocal. The two ends of a runway always differ by 18. So runway 06 pairs with 24, never with 26.

## The clock code

The three-figure group is perfect for navigation. But it is useless for telling someone where to look out of the window right now. For that, aviation uses the clock code.

Picture a clock face lying flat around your aeroplane, with 12 o'clock on the nose. Three o'clock is off the right wingtip. Six o'clock is straight behind. And nine o'clock is off the left wingtip. Each hour is 360 divided by 12, which is 30 degrees. The words high and low are added for traffic above or below your level, and same level if it is at your height.

The clock is fixed to the nose. Not to north, and not to your path over the ground. When ATC says traffic two o'clock, they mean turn your head 60 degrees right of where your nose points. In a crosswind, where the nose is angled into wind, that is still where you look.

[[diagram:clock-code-traffic]]
Picture an aeroplane heading {090|zero nine zero}, due east, with a clock face laid flat around it and 12 o'clock on the nose. Traffic at two o'clock is 60 degrees right of the nose, so it bears {150|one five zero}. Traffic at ten o'clock is 60 degrees left of the nose, so it bears {030|zero three zero}. The point to notice: 12 o'clock is the nose, not the track, and each hour is 30 degrees.

Turning a clock position into a direction from north is one sum. Hours to the right of 12 add to your heading. Hours to the left subtract. If the answer goes past 360, subtract 360. If it goes below zero, or to zero, add 360.

Let's work one. You are heading {090|zero nine zero}. Traffic is reported at two o'clock low, and later a second aircraft at ten o'clock. Which way does each one bear? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
Two o'clock is 2 hours right of the nose. 2 times 30 is 60 degrees right. So the first aircraft bears {090|zero nine zero} plus 60, which is {150|one five zero}, below your level. Ten o'clock is 2 hours left of the nose, so 60 degrees left. The second aircraft bears {090|zero nine zero} minus 60, which is {030|zero three zero}. So you look 60 degrees right and down for the first, and 60 degrees left for the second.

Now one where the sum wraps past north. On a heading of {240|two four zero}, there is traffic at ten o'clock. Later, on a heading of {350|three five zero}, there is traffic at two o'clock. Pause and try both.
[[pause 6]]
The first: ten o'clock is 60 degrees left. {240|two four zero} minus 60 is {180|one eight zero}. That traffic bears {180|one eight zero}, due south of you.

The second: two o'clock is 60 degrees right. {350|three five zero} plus 60 is 410. But 410 is past 360, so subtract a full circle. 410 minus 360 is {050|zero five zero}. The second traffic is north-east of you. Any time the sum passes 360 or drops below zero, add or remove a full circle.

[[diagram:relative-bearing-wrap]]
Picture that second case on a compass rose. The aeroplane is heading {350|three five zero}, just left of north. Count 60 degrees clockwise from the nose. The first 10 of those degrees take you to north, at {360|three six zero}. The other 50 carry on past it, and you stop at {050|zero five zero}. That is 350 plus 60, which is 410, minus a full circle.

The same arithmetic works for any angle measured from the nose. That angle is called a relative bearing. An object 100 degrees right of the nose is a little behind the right wingtip, between three and four o'clock.

Let's work one. You are heading {300|three zero zero} degrees magnetic, and a dam is 100 degrees right of the nose. What is its magnetic bearing?
[[pause 5]]
Add the relative bearing to the heading. 300 plus 100 is 400. Remove a full circle: 400 minus 360 is {040|zero four zero}. So from where you are, the dam bears {040|zero four zero} degrees magnetic.

Question. In a strong crosswind, your nose points 10 degrees left of your track. ATC calls traffic at twelve o'clock. Where do you look?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: straight over the nose, not along your track. The clock code is fixed to the aeroplane's longitudinal axis, so twelve o'clock is wherever the nose points, whatever your path over the ground.

## Heading

Sit in the cockpit and look along the top of the engine cowling. The direction that line points is the aeroplane's longitudinal axis. Measured clockwise from north, it is the aeroplane's heading. Heading is where the aeroplane is pointing.

A heading can be quoted against true, magnetic or compass north. So a full heading always carries its letter: {095|zero nine five} M for magnetic, or {093|zero nine three} T for true.

Pointing is not the same as going. An aeroplane flies through a mass of air, and the air itself is moving over the ground. That is the wind.

Picture a ferry crossing a river. It points upstream of the jetty on the far side, because the current carries it sideways while it crosses. An aeroplane is the same. The direction it actually moves over the ground is its track. And the angle between the heading and the track is the drift.

Drift is caused entirely by the wind. With no wind, or with the wind straight on the nose or straight on the tail, heading and track are the same.

[[diagram:heading-track-drift]]
Picture the aeroplane from above. Its nose points along the heading. But a wind from the left side carries it sideways, so over the ground it moves along a different line, the track. The angle between the two lines is the drift. Take the wind away, and the two lines become one.

This is why your nav log has two columns. You draw the track on the chart, because that is where you want to go over the ground. Then you work out the heading you must fly to make good that track in today's wind. A wind from the left drifts you right, so you point the nose a little left of the track to compensate. You will do the arithmetic for that with the flight computer, in the navigation unit.

For this subtopic, hold on to the two definitions. Heading is where the nose points. Track is where the aeroplane goes. And only the heading is shown directly by the instruments.

The clock code connects back here. Because 12 o'clock is the nose, the clock is fixed to your heading, not your track.

Question. In still air, what is the drift, and how do heading and track compare?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the drift is zero, and heading and track are the same. Drift is caused by the wind carrying the aeroplane sideways. With no wind, there is nothing to separate where the nose points from where the aeroplane goes.

## Three norths: true, magnetic and compass

So far, north has been a single direction. In fact there are three, and the same physical direction gets a different number against each. Let's take them in turn: true, then magnetic, then compass.

True north is the direction of the geographic North Pole, the point the Earth spins about. The meridians of longitude on every aeronautical chart run true north and south. So when you lay a protractor on the chart and measure a track against a meridian, you get a true direction. True north is the only north that never moves, and it is the one maps are built on.

Now magnetic north. A compass needle does not point at the geographic pole. It lines up with the Earth's magnetic field, which points towards magnetic north. That is a region in the Canadian Arctic, a long way from the geographic pole, and it slowly wanders. A direction measured against the magnetic field is a magnetic direction.

The angle between true north and magnetic north, at the place you are standing, is the variation. It is called east when magnetic north lies east of true north, and west when it lies west.

Variation depends on where you are. Your angle to the magnetic pole changes as you move about the Earth. It also changes slowly over the years, as the field drifts. But it does not depend on what your aeroplane is doing. Charts show it with dashed isogonal lines. Those are lines joining places of equal variation, each labelled with its value.

Around Perth the variation is small and westerly, about 1 to 2 degrees west. Check the isogonal line on the current chart, because the value changes year by year. Across the eastern states the variation is easterly, and in places much larger. That has a practical consequence for a WA pilot. In the west, the true and magnetic numbers are very close. So an error in applying variation here can go unnoticed, until you fly somewhere it is 10 degrees or more.

[[diagram:isogonals-wa-to-east]]
Picture a sketch map of Australia, with dashed isogonal lines running roughly north to south across it. Around Perth, on the left, the variation is small and westerly, about 1 to 2 degrees west. Moving east, the lines pass through zero and the variation becomes easterly, reaching 10 degrees east or more in the eastern states. So fly from Perth to Kalgoorlie and you cross isogonals: the variation changes, but the deviation does not. Variation belongs to the place. Read its value off the current chart.

Now the third north, compass north. Your aeroplane's compass is not alone in the cockpit. The engine, the steel parts of the airframe, the radios and the electrical wiring all make magnetic fields of their own. They pull the compass needle away from magnetic north. The error this causes is the deviation. And the direction the compass actually shows is a compass direction.

Deviation depends on which way the aeroplane is pointing. Turn the aeroplane, and the engine and wiring turn with it. They now sit at a different angle to the Earth's field, so they disturb the needle by a different amount. Every aeroplane has its own deviation. It is measured on the ground in a compass swing, and recorded on the compass deviation card mounted near the compass. The card lists the correction for each heading.

[[diagram:true-magnetic-compass]]
Picture three lines fanning out from one point. The first is true north, along the meridian. The second is magnetic north, offset from it by the variation. The third is compass north, offset again by the deviation. The angles are exaggerated so you can see them. Take a track of 120 degrees true. With a variation of 3 degrees east, it becomes {117|one one seven} magnetic. With a deviation of 2 degrees west, it becomes {119|one one nine} compass. Variation east is subtracted, and deviation west is added.

So, to sum up the three norths. True is measured from the geographic North Pole, the meridians on the chart. Variation takes you from true to magnetic, and it depends on where you are. Magnetic is the direction a freely suspended magnet points. Deviation takes you from magnetic to compass, and it depends on which way the aircraft points. And compass is what the aircraft's own compass actually reads. There is no further correction after compass.

Now, converting between them. You work from the chart towards the cockpit. True, then magnetic, then compass. One memory aid covers both steps. Variation east, magnetic least. Variation west, magnetic best. Or, the same for deviation: deviation east, compass least; deviation west, compass best. In short: east is least, west is best.

Least means subtract. Best means add.

Here is why. If magnetic north lies east of true north, the needle is already turned towards the east. So every direction measured from it comes out smaller. Subtract an easterly variation. A westerly variation adds. Deviation works the same way, between magnetic and compass. And going the other way, from compass back to true, reverse the signs.

Let's work one. A track measured on the chart is 120 degrees true. The local variation is 3 degrees east, and the deviation card shows 2 degrees west on that heading. What do you fly by the compass? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
First, magnetic. East is least, so 120 minus 3 is {117|one one seven} degrees magnetic. Then compass. West is best, so 117 plus 2 is {119|one one nine} degrees compass. To hold that direction you fly {119|one one nine} by the compass.

Now one with westerly variation. A track of {265|two six five} degrees true, variation 2 degrees west, deviation 3 degrees east.
[[pause 6]]
Magnetic first. West is best, so 265 plus 2 is {267|two six seven} degrees magnetic. Then compass. East is least, so 267 minus 3 is {264|two six four} degrees compass. The signs swapped from the first example, and so did the arithmetic. Add the variation, subtract the deviation.

Now try one in the reverse direction. Your compass reads {095|zero nine five} degrees compass. The deviation card shows 1 degree west for that heading, and the variation is 2 degrees west. What is the true direction? This one needs a moment.
[[pause 8]]
Going from compass back to true, reverse the rule. West is now subtracted. Magnetic is {095|zero nine five} minus 1, which is {094|zero nine four} degrees magnetic. True is {094|zero nine four} minus 2, which is {092|zero nine two} degrees true. Check it by going forward. {092|zero nine two} plus 2 is {094|zero nine four} magnetic, because west is best. Then {094|zero nine four} plus 1 is {095|zero nine five} compass. It matches. So the answer is {092|zero nine two} degrees true.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Variation and deviation are easy to swap. Variation belongs to the place: it is on the chart. Deviation belongs to the aeroplane and its heading: it is on the card in the cockpit. So a question that changes the heading but keeps the location changes the deviation only. And the rule works from true towards compass. Going backwards with east is least gives an answer wrong by twice the correction.

Finally, which north is used where. In Australia, the reference changes with who is giving you the number, and the exam tests it. There are three groups.

First, true. Tracks measured on charts are true. So are winds in forecasts, the TAF, the GAF or the area forecast, and winds in METAR and SPECI reports.

Second, magnetic. Runway directions are magnetic. So are headings and tracks given by ATC. And so are surface winds passed by ATC or on an ATIS, the recorded aerodrome information, so that they can be compared directly with the runway.

Third, compass. That is what you actually steer by. In practice, you fly the direction indicator. It is a gyro instrument that you align with the magnetic compass before take-off, and check every 15 minutes or so, because the gyro drifts.

So: chart tracks and forecast winds, true. Runways and the winds ATC gives you, magnetic.

Question. You fly from Perth to Kalgoorlie. Which changes along the way, variation or deviation, and why?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: variation. It depends on where you are, and the isogonal lines change as you move east. Deviation changes only when the heading changes, because it comes from the aeroplane's own magnetic fields turning with it.

## Bringing it together

You are planning Jandakot to Northam. On the chart you rule a line from Jandakot to Northam, and measure it against a meridian. It comes out at about {056|zero five six} degrees true.

The nearest isogonal line says 2 degrees west. West is best, so the magnetic track is {058|zero five eight} degrees magnetic. The deviation card says 1 degree east on headings near {060|zero six zero}. East is least, so in still air you would steer {057|zero five seven} degrees compass.

You depart runway {06L|zero six left}. The runway number says it points about {060|zero six zero} magnetic, so the nose is already close to your track as you lift off.

Climbing out towards the hills, the controller calls traffic ten o'clock, five miles, 2,500 ft. Your heading is {058|zero five eight}. Ten o'clock is 60 degrees left of the nose. {058|zero five eight} minus 60 is {358|three five eight}, almost due north of you. You look out over the left wing towards the front, find it, and report traffic sighted.

Every form of direction in this lesson has turned up in ten minutes of flying.

## Key points

Let's recap.

Directions are angles measured clockwise from north. Navigation directions are three-figure groups: {005|zero zero five}, {090|zero nine zero}, and {360|three six zero} for north.

Runways are two-figure groups. Take the magnetic direction to the nearest 10 degrees and drop the last zero. The two ends differ by 18.

The clock code puts 12 o'clock on the nose, and each hour is 30 degrees. Right adds, left subtracts, and wrap past 360.

Heading is where the nose points. Track is where the aeroplane goes over the ground. Drift is the angle between them, and it is caused by the wind.

True is measured from the meridians, magnetic from the Earth's field, and compass from your own compass. Variation, which belongs to the place, turns true into magnetic. Deviation, which belongs to the aeroplane and its heading, turns magnetic into compass. East is least, west is best, working from true towards compass. And Perth variation is small and westerly, so check the current chart.

A final exam tip. Expect three kinds of question. A clock-code question: multiply the hours by 30, and add or subtract from the heading, watching the wrap past 360. A runway designator question: the ends differ by 18. And a true to magnetic conversion, using east is least, west is best. Read which way the question is converting, from true towards compass, or back again. And remember which reference goes with which source. Chart tracks and forecast winds are true. Runways and ATC or ATIS winds are magnetic. And 12 o'clock is always the nose.

That's the end of BAKC 2.1.
