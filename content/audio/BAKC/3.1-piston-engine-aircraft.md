This is BAKC 3.1, Piston engine aircraft. By the end you will have a picture of what is under the cowling: how the engine turns fuel into rotation, what sets its power, what each part around it is for, what the gauges tell you, and what the aeroplane's four systems run. There are a few questions along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (BAKC 3.1)

The exam wants eight things from you. First, the four-stroke cycle, and what each main engine part does. Second, how altitude and temperature change engine performance, and how throttle and RPM set the power. Third, what each component around the engine is for. Fourth, why you monitor each engine gauge. Fifth, the lubrication system, and its part in cooling. Sixth, what the mixture control is for, and what too rich or too lean a mixture does. Seventh, a simple carburettor compared with fuel injection. And eighth, what the hydraulic, electrical, ignition and vacuum systems provide, and how you detect and deal with a fault in each.

## Why this matters

Your training aeroplane at Jandakot or Serpentine has one engine. Everything you do before flight, from the fuel drain to the magneto check, exists to keep it running for the next hour.

You do not need to be a mechanic. But you need a picture of what is under the cowling, because that picture is what lets you read the gauges and catch a problem early.

The exam asks recall questions, like which stroke makes power. And it asks cause-and-effect ones, like what a blocked fuel vent or a lean mixture leads to. The next three lessons, on fuels and oils, engine handling, and malfunctions, build directly on this one.

## How the engine turns fuel into rotation

In one sentence: a mixture of petrol vapour and air is squeezed into a small space and set alight, and the hot gas pushes a piston, which turns a shaft, which turns the propeller.

The engine in a typical trainer does this as a spark-ignition, four-stroke, air-cooled piston engine. It has four or six cylinders, lying flat in two opposed rows. That layout is called horizontally opposed.

Now the parts, and what each one does. There are seven to know.

First, the cylinder. Each one is a strong metal tube, closed at the top by the cylinder head, and the burning happens inside it. Thin fins on the outside give the passing air a large surface to carry heat away. That is what air-cooled means.

Second, the piston. It slides inside the cylinder, a close-fitting metal plug. It draws the mixture in, squeezes it, is driven down by the burning gas, and pushes the exhaust out. A connecting rod links it to the crankshaft.

Third, the piston rings. The piston cannot fit perfectly, because metal expands as it heats. So the gap is sealed by springy rings. The top rings, the compression rings, stop the high-pressure gas leaking past into the crankcase, so the force of combustion goes into the piston. The bottom ring, the oil control ring, scrapes oil off the cylinder wall. It leaves a thin film, but keeps most of the oil out of the combustion chamber. Worn rings cost both power and oil. They are one cause of the blue exhaust smoke you will meet in the lesson on engine handling.

Fourth, the valves. In the cylinder head, the inlet valve lets the fresh mixture in, and the exhaust valve lets the burnt gas out. Each is held shut by a spring, and pushed open at the right moment.

Fifth, the crankshaft. It turns the piston's up-and-down motion into rotation, the way your legs drive a bicycle's cranks. In most light aeroplanes the propeller bolts straight onto it. So propeller RPM and engine RPM are the same.

Sixth, the camshaft. It times the valves. A lobe for each valve pushes it open, and then lets the spring close it. The camshaft is geared to turn at half the crankshaft speed, and you will see why in a moment.

And seventh, the spark plugs. There are two in every cylinder, and they ignite the compressed mixture.

Now, four strokes, two turns. A stroke is one movement of the piston, up or down. One working cycle takes four strokes.

The first is induction. The inlet valve opens and the piston moves down. The space above it grows, the pressure there falls, and fuel-air mixture flows in to fill it.

The second is compression. Both valves close, and the piston moves up, squeezing the mixture into a fraction of its original volume. Compressed mixture burns faster, and pushes harder.

The third is power. Just before the piston reaches the top, both spark plugs fire. The burning gas heats up, its pressure rises sharply, and it drives the piston down. This is the only stroke that produces power.

And the fourth is exhaust. The exhaust valve opens, and the piston moves up, pushing the burnt gas out through the exhaust pipe.

The memory aid: suck, squeeze, bang, blow.

[[diagram:four-stroke-cycle]]
Picture a single cylinder, cut open, with the piston moving inside it and the two valves at the top, running through those four strokes. Watch the valves: only one is ever open, and both are shut for compression and power. The crank turns twice for one spark.

Each stroke is half a turn of the crankshaft. So a cycle takes two crankshaft revolutions, and each valve opens once every two turns. Hence the half-speed camshaft. The other three strokes are carried through by the crankshaft's momentum, and by the other cylinders' power strokes.

Question. Why does the camshaft turn at half the speed of the crankshaft?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: each valve must open once per four-stroke cycle, and a cycle takes two turns of the crankshaft. Turning the camshaft once for every two crankshaft turns brings each lobe round to its valve exactly once per cycle.

## What sets the power: density, throttle and RPM

Think of the engine as an air pump that burns fuel on the way through. Fuel is easy to supply. The limit on power is how much oxygen each cylinder swallows on its induction stroke. And since the cylinder's volume is fixed, that depends on how dense the air is.

First, altitude and temperature. As you climb, the air thins. The same volume holds less mass, so less fuel is burnt, and there is less power. As a rule of thumb, a normally aspirated engine, one without a supercharger or turbocharger to pack the air in, loses about 3 percent of its sea-level power for every 1,000 ft. By about 7,000 to 8,000 ft, a typical trainer at full throttle can only produce about 75 percent power.

[[diagram:power-loss-with-altitude]]
Picture a line sloping down as height increases: full-throttle power, as a percentage of sea-level power. It falls by roughly 3 percent for every 1,000 ft. So by about 7,000 to 8,000 ft, it is down to about 75 percent.

Temperature works the same way. Warm air is less dense than cool air at the same pressure. So a hot day costs power, just as height does. Humid air costs a little more again, because water vapour displaces some of the oxygen. What the engine responds to is density height: the height in the standard atmosphere that has the same density as the air around you. You will calculate it in the lesson on take-off and landing performance.

Second, the throttle. The throttle moves a flat disc, the throttle butterfly, in the passage to the cylinders. Closed, it almost blocks the passage. Open, it lets the cylinders fill. The pressure after the butterfly is called the manifold pressure. Opening the throttle raises it. More mixture reaches each cylinder, and each power stroke is stronger.

And third, RPM. Power is also how often the engine does its work. At 2,400 RPM, a four-cylinder engine makes 4,800 power strokes a minute. At 1,200 RPM, half as many. More RPM, for the same charge in each cylinder, is more power.

With a fixed-pitch propeller, the kind on most trainers, you cannot set these separately. Opening the throttle makes the engine turn faster, and the tachometer is your power gauge. With a constant-speed propeller, the propeller lever sets the RPM and the throttle sets the manifold pressure, and you need both gauges to know the power.

Question. You take off from Northam on a 40 degree day. Why does the engine give less power at full throttle than it does at Jandakot in winter?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the hot air is less dense. So each cylinder draws in less mass of air, and less oxygen, on every induction stroke, and can burn less fuel. And Northam's higher elevation lowers the density further.

## The components around the engine

Now the parts around the engine, starting with the carburettor.

First, the carburettor and throttle. The engine needs fuel and air in the right proportion, with the fuel broken into a fine mist. In most trainers, the carburettor does both.

Air is forced through a narrowing called the venturi, where it speeds up and its pressure drops. A float chamber beside it holds a steady level of fuel, and a main jet connects it to the throat of the venturi. The lower pressure in the throat draws fuel up and sprays it into the air. The faster the airflow, the more fuel is drawn, so fuel is metered in proportion to the air. A mixture control needle adjusts how much fuel reaches the jet. And the throttle butterfly sits just above the venturi.

[[diagram:carburettor-cross-section]]
Picture the carburettor cut in half. Air comes in at the bottom and rises through the venturi, a waist in the passage, where it speeds up and its pressure drops. To one side is the float chamber, where a float and a needle valve hold the fuel at a constant level. The low pressure in the venturi draws fuel from the float chamber, through the main jet, and out of a nozzle, where it breaks into a mist in the airflow. The mixture control meters the fuel reaching the jet. And above the venturi, the throttle butterfly controls how much mixture goes on to the cylinders.

Hold on to one fact for the lesson on malfunctions: the pressure drop in the venturi and at the butterfly, and the fuel evaporating, both chill the air. They chill it enough to form ice inside the carburettor on a warm, humid day.

Second, the magnetos, and dual ignition. A spark plug needs many thousands of volts. They come from a magneto. That is an engine-driven generator with a spinning permanent magnet, a coil that steps the voltage up, and a distributor that sends each spark to the right cylinder. A magneto is self-contained. It needs no battery and no aircraft electrical system, so a total electrical failure does not stop the engine.

Every aeroplane engine has two independent magnetos. Each one fires one of the two plugs in every cylinder, through its own leads. This dual ignition gives two things. Redundancy: if one magneto, lead or plug fails, every cylinder still has a spark, with a small loss of power. And better combustion: two flame fronts burn the mixture faster and more completely.

[[diagram:dual-ignition-system]]
Picture two magnetos, side by side, both driven by the engine. From each one, a set of leads runs out to the cylinders, one lead to one plug in every cylinder. So every cylinder has a plug from the left magneto and a plug from the right. If one magneto fails, the engine keeps running on the other, with a small loss of power. And the ignition switch stops a magneto by earthing it. A broken earth lead leaves that magneto live.

The ignition switch works backwards from what you might expect. A magneto sparks whenever it turns. To stop it, the switch connects it to earth, through a wire called the P-lead. So OFF means earthed. If a P-lead breaks, that magneto is live with the switch at OFF, and moving the propeller by hand can start the engine. Treat every propeller as live.

Third, the impulse start. At cranking speed, a magneto turns too slowly to make a strong spark. And the normal spark, timed well before the piston reaches the top, can drive the piston backwards and kick the propeller back.

The impulse coupling fixes both. It is a spring-loaded coupling on one magneto. During cranking it holds the magneto back while the spring winds up, then lets it flick round fast. That gives a stronger spark, delivered later. Later is called retarded. So the engine starts easily without kick-back. Once it is running, the magneto runs at normal timing.

Question. The battery is flat, but someone hand-swings the propeller, and the engine starts and runs. How is that possible?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the magnetos generate their own high voltage from the engine's rotation, and need no battery. The battery only powers the starter and the electrical services. And this is also why a propeller must always be treated as live.

Fourth, the electrical system: the alternator, the battery, the bus bar and the circuit protection.

The alternator is a belt-driven generator. It supplies electrical power in flight, for the radios, the transponder, the lights, electric flaps and fuel pump, the turn coordinator and pitot heat. And it recharges the battery. In most light aeroplanes, it needs battery voltage to excite its field. That is one reason a completely flat battery is a problem.

The battery turns the starter motor, and it is the emergency reserve if the alternator fails. While charging, a lead-acid battery gives off hydrogen, which is explosive, and acid fumes, which are corrosive. The battery compartment vent carries them overboard.

Power is collected on a bus bar, a metal strip that each service draws from, like a power board at home. Each circuit is protected by either a circuit breaker or a fuse. A breaker pops out, and can be reset. A fuse melts, and must be replaced. Either one cuts off an overloaded circuit before its wiring overheats and starts a fire. The usual airmanship rule is to reset a popped breaker once only, after it cools. One that trips again is protecting you from a fault.

[[diagram:electrical-system-schematic]]
Picture a long horizontal strip across the middle: the bus bar. Every service hangs off it, each through its own circuit breaker. On one side, the belt-driven alternator feeds the bus in flight and recharges the battery. On the other, the battery connects through the master switch and the ammeter, turns the starter, and stands by as the reserve. And notice what is not on this picture at all: the magnetos.

Fifth, the propeller. It converts engine torque into thrust. Each blade is a small rotating wing, whose lift points forwards. On most trainers it is fixed pitch: a blade angle set at manufacture, as a compromise between take-off and cruise. Constant-speed propellers come later, in the private licence units.

Sixth, the oil cooler. It is a small radiator in the cooling airflow. It sheds the heat the oil has carried out of the engine, keeping the oil within its temperature limits. A thermostatic valve usually bypasses it while the oil is cold.

And seventh, the fuel tank vents. Every litre of fuel that leaves a tank must be replaced by a litre of air. The fuel tank vent, a small tube usually under the wing, lets it in.

Block it, with a mud-wasp nest or with ice, and the tank pressure falls as fuel is drawn off. The flow slows and stops, and the engine starves with fuel still in the tank. The low pressure can even buckle the tank. That is why you check the vents on every pre-flight.

[[diagram:fuel-system-schematic]]
Picture two wing tanks, one on each side, each with a vent at the top and a drain at its lowest point. Pipes from both run down to a fuel selector. From there, fuel passes through the strainer, called the gascolator, which has its own drain at the lowest point of the whole system. Then through an electric booster pump where one is fitted, and the engine-driven pump, to the carburettor. Water is heavier than fuel and settles at the low points, which is why the drains are there and why each is sampled before flight. And remember the vent at the top of each tank: block it, and the engine starves with fuel still in the tank.

## Reading the engine gauges

The engine gauges are how the engine talks to you. The skill is to notice a trend long before a needle reaches the red. What each abnormal reading means is in the lesson on malfunctions. Here is what each gauge is for.

The tachometer shows RPM. With a fixed-pitch propeller, it is the power indicator. Its red line marks the maximum RPM. And it is the gauge you read for the magneto and carburettor heat checks.

The cylinder head temperature gauge, the CHT, shows how hot the engine itself is working, usually at the hottest cylinder. A high CHT warns of poor cooling, a too-lean mixture, or detonation.

The exhaust gas temperature gauge, the EGT, responds quickly to mixture. It rises as you lean towards the chemically correct ratio. Its main use is to lean accurately in cruise.

Then the electrical gauges. A centre-zero ammeter shows current going into the battery, which is charge, or coming out of it, which is discharge. A loadmeter shows the alternator's output, from zero upwards. A voltmeter shows system voltage. That is about 14 volts on a 12 volt system, or 28 on a 24 volt system, while the alternator charges. It sinks towards battery voltage when the alternator stops.

The fuel pressure gauge confirms fuel is reaching the engine. A fall warns of a failing pump, a blocked vent, an empty tank, or vapour in the lines, often before the engine falters.

Oil pressure is the most important gauge on the panel. No pressure means dry bearings, and an engine about to seize. Oil temperature confirms the oil is warm enough to flow, and not so hot that it thins. A slow rise in oil temperature with a slow fall in oil pressure is the classic warning of an engine losing its oil. And it gives you minutes to choose a field while the engine still runs.

## Oil: lubrication and cooling

Metal sliding on metal at engine speeds would weld itself together within minutes. The lubrication system keeps a film of oil between every pair of moving surfaces.

The oil lives in a sump under the crankcase. That is called a wet sump. An engine-driven oil pump pushes it through a filter, and along drilled passages to the bearings, the valve gear and the cylinder walls. A pressure relief valve stops cold, thick oil over-pressurising the system. Then the oil drains back to the sump.

On the way, it does four jobs. It lubricates. It seals the piston rings. It cleans away carbon and metal particles. And it cools.

Cooling is the part the exam emphasises. The air over the cylinder fins removes most of the heat. But it cannot reach the pistons, the underside of the heads, or the bearings. Oil splashed onto those parts carries their heat to the sump and the oil cooler. So oil temperature is as much a cooling gauge as a lubrication gauge. And an engine low on oil runs hot, because less oil is carrying the same heat.

[[diagram:engine-lubrication-circuit]]
Picture the oil's round trip, from the sump at the bottom, through the pump and filter, and up to the bearings, the valve gear and the cylinder walls. A relief valve spills the excess back while the oil is cold and thick, and a thermostatic valve sends cold oil past the cooler and hot oil through it. Oil sprayed under the pistons picks up heat the fins cannot reach, and the cooler sheds it to the airflow.

## The mixture control

The chemically correct mixture is about 15 parts of air to 1 of fuel, by weight. Engines give their best power a little richer than that, and their best economy a little leaner. A rich mixture has more fuel than the air can burn. A lean one has less.

The carburettor meters fuel by the volume of air through it. But what burns is the mass of air. As you climb, each litre of air weighs less, while the fuel delivered stays about the same. So the mixture becomes progressively richer with height. The mixture control reduces the fuel flow to restore the ratio. That gives smoother running, better economy, cleaner plugs and the correct temperatures.

[[diagram:mixture-rich-to-lean]]
Picture a sketch of power as you lean the mixture from full rich, on the left, towards lean, on the right. Power rises to a peak near the correct mixture, and falls away on both sides of it. To the left, too rich: rough running, power loss, high fuel burn, cool running, fouled plugs and black smoke. To the right, too lean: power loss, rough running, high CHT and EGT, and a risk of detonation.

So, too rich first. Unburnt fuel goes out of the exhaust. The engine loses power, may run rough, wastes fuel, and runs cool, because the excess fuel absorbs heat as it evaporates. Lead and carbon foul the spark plugs, and the exhaust may smoke black.

Now too lean. Power falls and the engine runs rough. And above all, it runs hot. CHT and EGT rise. At high power, a lean mixture invites detonation, which can wreck pistons and valves in minutes. You will meet detonation in the lesson on engine handling. Leaned all the way to idle cut-off, the engine stops. That is how you shut it down.

Hence the rule. Full rich for take-off, and for the climb at low levels. The POH typically says below about 5,000 ft. That is because the extra fuel cools the cylinders at high power. In cruise, you lean by the EGT gauge. Or, with a fixed-pitch propeller, you lean until the RPM peaks and starts to fall, then go slightly richer, as the POH directs.

[[diagram:leaning-by-rpm]]
Picture the tachometer as you lean in cruise. The RPM rises from 2,350 to a peak of 2,400. Lean further, and it falls to 2,370, and the engine roughens. So you enrich back to the peak, and then a little richer.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Lean the mixture to cool the engine is backwards. Leaning from full rich towards the correct mixture makes the engine hotter. And a lean mixture at high power is a cause of detonation. It is the rich mixture that runs cool, which is why you take off at full rich.

## Carburettor or fuel injection

In a fuel injection system, a pump delivers fuel under pressure to a metering unit. The metering unit sends a measured flow to an injector nozzle at each cylinder's inlet port, instead of mixing the fuel in a venturi upstream.

A simple carburettor is simple, cheap and easy to maintain, with few parts to fail. It is also tolerant of poor fuel delivery. Its weaknesses come from the venturi, and from the long path to the cylinders. The cooling in the venturi makes it prone to carburettor ice. And the mixture does not reach every cylinder in the same strength. That is called uneven distribution.

Fuel injection removes both. There is no carburettor ice, because there is no venturi, and the fuel evaporates at the hot inlet port. The air intake can still collect impact ice, which is what alternate air is for, in the lesson on malfunctions. And each cylinder gets an even, precise mixture. That means better economy, more power and smoother running.

The price is three things. Fuel injection is more complex and costly. It needs a fuel pump running at all times. And it is hard to start when hot, because fuel in the hot lines boils. That is called vapour lock.

[[diagram:carburettor-vs-injection]]
Picture two induction systems side by side. On the left, the carburettor. Air speeds up through the venturi, its pressure and temperature drop, and fuel evaporating in it cools it further, all upstream of the throttle butterfly. So ice can form in the venturi and at the butterfly. On the right, fuel injection. There is no venturi. A pump and a metering unit send fuel to a nozzle at each cylinder's inlet port, where it evaporates against hot metal. So the induction air is never chilled. Only the air intake, on either one, can still collect impact ice.

Question. Why can't a fuel-injected engine get carburettor ice?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: carburettor ice comes from the cooling in the venturi and at the throttle butterfly, and from fuel evaporating upstream of the cylinders. An injection system has no venturi, and sprays the fuel at the warm inlet port. So neither cooling effect happens in the induction air. The air intake can still ice up, though. That is impact ice.

## The aeroplane's systems and what goes wrong

There are four systems. For each one, ask three questions. What does it run? How would you notice it failing? And what do you do?

First, the hydraulic system. In a trainer, it is usually just the wheel brakes. Each toe pedal pushes fluid from a master cylinder to the brake at the wheel. Larger types add flaps and retractable gear. A leak, or air in the lines, shows as a spongy or sinking pedal on the taxi brake check, or as fluid on the wheel. On the ground, stop and get it fixed. If you find it in flight, pump the pedal, choose a long runway, and keep straight with rudder and the other brake.

Second, the electrical system. It runs the starter, the radios, the transponder, the lights, electric flaps and fuel pump, the turn coordinator and pitot heat. Its usual failure is the alternator stopping. That shows as a discharge, a low voltage, or a warning light. Check the switch and the breaker, shed load, and land before the battery runs flat. The engine keeps running on its magnetos.

Third, the ignition system: two magnetos, their leads, and two plugs per cylinder. It is tested on the run-up by the magneto check, one magneto at a time. A drop beyond the POH limit, or rough running, means a fault on that side. At shutdown, a dead cut check confirms the switch earths both magnetos. You will meet that in the lesson on engine handling.

And fourth, the vacuum system. It drives the attitude indicator and the directional indicator. An engine-driven pump sucks air out of the instrument cases, and the air drawn in spins each gyro. A low reading on the suction gauge, or a flag, means the gyros will slow, drift and topple. Cross-check with the turn coordinator, the compass and the pressure instruments, and stay clear of cloud.

[[diagram:gyro-instruments-panel]]
Picture three gyro instruments in a row. The attitude indicator, normally vacuum driven. The directional indicator, also normally vacuum driven, with no magnetic sense, so you reset it to the compass about every 10 to 15 minutes. And the turn coordinator, showing the rate of turn, with a ball for balance, which is normally electric. So one failure never takes out all three.

Question. The alternator and the vacuum pump are both driven by the engine. Which instruments would you lose if each one failed?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: if the alternator fails, then once the battery is flat you lose the electric instruments, which means the turn coordinator, plus the radios and the other electrical services. If the vacuum pump fails, you lose the attitude and directional indicators. The airspeed indicator, altimeter, VSI and compass need neither. And in both cases, the engine keeps running on its magnetos.

## Key points

Let's recap.

Induction, compression, power, exhaust. Two crankshaft turns per cycle, and only the power stroke does work. The rings seal and scrape oil. The camshaft opens the valves at half crankshaft speed. And two plugs per cylinder ignite the charge.

Power depends on the mass of air the cylinders swallow. Higher altitude and higher temperature reduce it, by roughly 3 percent per 1,000 ft. More throttle and more RPM increase it. And with a fixed-pitch propeller, the tachometer is the power gauge.

The carburettor meters fuel through a venturi. Magnetos are self-contained, and the switch stops them by earthing. The impulse coupling gives a strong, retarded spark for starting. The battery vent removes hydrogen and acid fumes. Breakers and fuses protect the circuits fed from the bus bar. And the tank vents let air in as fuel is used.

The gauges show trends early. RPM for power. CHT and EGT for heat and mixture. Ammeter and voltmeter for charging. Fuel pressure for supply. Oil pressure for lubrication, and oil temperature for cooling.

Oil lubricates, seals, cleans, and carries heat from the parts the air cannot reach. The oil cooler sheds it. Low oil means a hot engine.

The mixture richens as you climb, and the mixture control restores the ratio. Too rich: cool, rough, fouled plugs, black smoke. Too lean: hot, rough, and detonation.

The carburettor is simple and cheap, but it ices and distributes unevenly. Injection has no carburettor ice and an even mixture, with better economy, but it is complex, needs a pump, and is hard to start hot.

And the four systems. Hydraulic brakes: a spongy pedal. Electrical: a discharge, so shed load and land. Ignition: the magneto check and the dead cut. Vacuum: low suction, the gyros topple, so stay visual.

A final exam tip. Know which system drives what. In most trainers, the attitude and directional indicators are vacuum driven. The turn coordinator is electric. The airspeed indicator, altimeter and VSI need no power at all. And the engine's ignition is independent of the electrical system. Expect recall questions on the power stroke, the camshaft's half speed, the impulse coupling's retarded spark, the purpose of the battery vent, and what a blocked fuel vent does.
