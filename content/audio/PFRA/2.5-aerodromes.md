This is PFRA 2.5, Aerodromes, for the private pilot licence. By the end you will be able to read the markings at an aerodrome you have never seen, from the runway and its threshold to the ground signals and helicopter sites, build the circuit and its dead side from the landing direction, and say what a white cross means. There are worked examples and questions along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (PFRA 2.5)

The exam wants three things from you. First, recognising and explaining the markings at aerodromes, landing areas and helicopter landing sites. That means runway, threshold and runway end markings, cone and gable markers, taxiway markings, holding points and bays, the double white cross beside the windsock, the horizontal white dumbbell, the movement area, and the markings of a helicopter landing site. Second, pointing out the legs of a circuit, and its dead side. And third, explaining what a white cross on the movement area means.

## Why this matters

At recreational level, you learned to read your own training aerodrome, where you know every marking before you see it. You'll find that in the recreational lesson on aerodromes. The PPL takes you to aerodromes you have never seen. A sealed country runway at Katanning with a displaced threshold. A grass field near York after a week of winter rain. A station strip marked with old tyres. A hospital helipad beside the Kalgoorlie aerodrome. At each one, the markings and ground signals are information you have to read from overhead. And sometimes they are the only information there is.

Everything in the recreational lesson applies here. The parts of the movement area. The colours of runway and taxiway paint. Cones and gables. The circuit legs and the dead side. And the single and double white cross. This lesson adds what is new at private level. The dumbbell. Landing area and helicopter site markings. A closer look at thresholds. And the skill of putting the ground signals together with the ERSA entry and the NOTAMs. The exam does this with a diagram or a short scenario, and asks what you may do.

## Reading the runway: designator, threshold and end

Picture yourself on a long final to a runway you have never landed on. Three questions matter. Which runway is this? Where may I touch down? And where does the usable surface end? The runway markings answer them in that order.

Runway markings are white. The designator is the runway's magnetic direction to the nearest 10 degrees, with the last zero dropped. So runway {24|two four} means {240|two four zero} degrees magnetic. On parallel runways, it carries L, C or R, for left, centre or right, as seen by the approaching pilot. A dashed centreline runs down the middle, and side stripes mark the paved edges.

On larger runways, the aiming point bars sit about 300 m from the threshold, with touchdown zone bars on longer runways. The aiming point is where a normal approach path meets the runway. On a short country runway, you usually aim nearer the threshold than that. But the bars tell you how the runway was designed to be used.

Picture two runways from above. At the top is a sealed runway. Its white markings run from the threshold piano keys, to the designator {24|two four}, the aiming point bars, a dashed centreline and side stripes. Beside it, a taxiway has a yellow centreline, and a holding position of two solid and two dashed lines. At the bottom is an unsealed runway, with white cones along its edges, gables further out on the strip boundary, and a white cross closing an unserviceable section. White for runways, yellow for taxiways. On the unsealed strip, cones at the runway edge, and gables at the strip boundary.

Threshold markings, the white piano keys, show where the landing portion of the runway starts. Usually that is the physical end of the pavement. Sometimes it is not.

That brings us to the displaced threshold. A threshold is displaced when the landing portion begins some distance along the runway, rather than at its end. The common reasons are an obstacle under the approach, such as a line of trees, a road or power lines. Or a weak or damaged section of pavement at the end. The displaced threshold is marked with arrows along the centreline, pointing to a transverse bar. The bar is the line where landing may begin.

The rule follows from why the threshold moved. If an obstacle sits under the approach, the approach path to the new threshold clears it. An approach aimed at the pavement's end would not. So you land beyond the displaced threshold. But the pavement before it is still sound for an aeroplane already on the ground. So it may be used for the take-off run, and for the roll-out after landing in the other direction. The arrows mean "roll on me", and the bar means "touch down only past here".

The practical effect is on your performance planning. A displaced threshold reduces the landing distance available, without reducing the take-off distance available from that end.

[[diagram:displaced-threshold]]
Picture the runway from the worked example, from above and from the side. It is 1,250 m long, and its northern threshold is displaced 220 m because of power poles under the approach. White arrows along the centreline lead to a white bar, and the threshold markings start at the bar. In the side view, an approach to the bar clears the poles, while an approach aimed at the pavement's end would not. Only the landing distance shrinks: 1,250 m minus 220 m leaves 1,030 m.

Let's work one: landing distance with a displaced threshold. A sealed country runway is 1,250 m long. The NOTAM and ERSA show the threshold of the northern end displaced 220 m, because of a new line of power poles under the approach. Your aeroplane needs 540 m to land over a 50 ft screen today. That is from the POH chart, with your safety margin applied. And it needs 610 m to take off. Is the runway adequate for each case? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
First, landing towards the south, over the displaced threshold. You land beyond the bar. 1,250 m minus 220 m leaves 1,030 m available. You need 540 m, so it is adequate.

Second, taking off towards the south, starting from the northern end. The arrowed section may be used for the take-off run, so the full 1,250 m is available. You need 610 m, so that is adequate too.

Third, landing towards the north. The approach is over the southern end, so you touch down at the normal threshold. And the roll-out may continue onto the arrowed section. So the full length is available for the roll-out.

The answer again: 1,030 m to land towards the south, and the full 1,250 m for everything else. The displacement only costs you landing distance in the direction where the obstacle is under the approach.

Now try one where the unknown is different. Suppose instead the northern threshold is normal, and the southern threshold is displaced 400 m for pavement works. Your aeroplane needs 880 m to land. Can you land towards the north?
[[pause 6]]
Here is the solution. Landing towards the north, over the southern end, you must touch down beyond the displaced threshold. 1,250 m minus 400 m leaves 850 m available. 850 m is less than the 880 m required, so no. Land towards the south if the wind allows, where the full length is available for the roll-out. Or go elsewhere.

Last on the runway, the end. Runway end markings show where the usable surface stops. On a sealed runway, that is the side stripes and a transverse stripe. On an unsealed strip, it is the markers at the ends.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. The arrowed section before a displaced threshold is not closed. It is closed for touchdown only. Treating it as unusable throws away take-off distance. And landing on it may put you into the obstacle the displacement was there to clear.

Question. Why may you take off from the arrowed section before a displaced threshold, but not land on it?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the displacement protects the approach path, not the pavement. An aeroplane landing short of the bar would have come down over the obstacle on too low a path. An aeroplane taking off starts on the ground, and climbs away from the obstacle at the other end.

## Unsealed runways, landing areas and cones and gables

Many WA aerodromes are gravel or grass, where paint will not last. There, the job of the white paint is done by markers.

Cone markers are white cones along the edges of the runway or landing area, on unsealed or unrated strips. The usable surface lies between them. Gable markers are white gables marking the boundary of the runway strip. The strip is the graded clear area around the runway. It must be kept free, so that an aeroplane running off the edge has somewhere reasonable to stop. A gable is not a runway edge. So, from the centre outward: the runway surface, then the cones, then the strip, then the gables.

Now, landing areas. A landing area, an LA, or aeroplane landing area, an ALA, is a place used for take-off and landing that is not a certified or registered aerodrome. A farm strip. A station strip. A mine's dirt runway. Nobody inspects it to an aerodrome standard. So its markings are whatever the operator has put out: cones, drums, tyres or gables, for the boundaries and runway edges.

Whether the LA is suitable for you depends on its length, slope, surface and obstacles. That is decided by the operator's markings, and the guidance in {AC 91-02|A C ninety-one dash zero two}, the Advisory Circular on places suitable for take-off and landing. And in the end, it is decided by you, as pilot in command.

That last point is the difference between an aerodrome and an LA. At a certified or registered aerodrome, someone has measured the runway and published it in ERSA. At an LA, a row of whitewashed tyres tells you where the owner thinks the strip is. You confirm the rest yourself. First from the owner's information beforehand. And then from a low inspection pass before landing.

Question. A station strip is marked with white-painted tyres along both sides of a graded runway. What do the tyres tell you, and what do they not?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: they mark the runway edges the operator has set out, so land between them. They do not tell you the length, the slope, the surface strength, or the obstacles. At an LA, you judge suitability yourself, using the operator's information and {AC 91-02|A C ninety-one dash zero two}.

## Taxiways, holding points and the movement area

The movement area is the manoeuvring area, which means the runways and taxiways, plus the aprons. Aprons are marked with parking positions and lead-in lines, and apron edges with a yellow line. The yellow lead-in line takes you into a parking position clear of other aircraft and buildings. So you can follow it without guessing the clearances.

Taxiway markings are yellow. There is a centreline, there are edge lines, and there is the runway holding position. That is two solid lines towards the taxiway, and two dashed lines towards the runway. Hold on the solid side. Approaching the runway, the solid lines face you and say stop. Vacating the runway, the dashed lines face you and let you cross. And intermediate holding positions, where you may be told to wait on a taxiway away from any runway, are a single dashed line.

The holding point is where you hold before entering or crossing a runway. It is set back, so that an aircraft waiting there is clear of aircraft using the runway. A holding bay is a widened area, where an aircraft can wait or complete its run-up checks without blocking the taxiway. At a country aerodrome with one stub taxiway and no holding bay, your run-up may block the only way onto the runway. So do it on the apron, or look over your shoulder for anyone taxiing behind you.

## The signal area: what the ground is telling you

Beside the windsock at many aerodromes is a signal area. It is a square of ground where white symbols can be laid out. Think of it as the aerodrome's message board, readable from overhead by an aircraft with no radio contact with anyone on the ground. Before every landing at an unfamiliar aerodrome, you look at it.

The symbols you need follow one pattern. Each tells you what you may use, or what activity to expect. There are five to know.

First, the primary wind indicator, the windsock. It shows the wind direction, and roughly its strength. Where night operations are permitted, it is lit at night.

Second, a double white cross beside the primary wind indicator. It means gliding operations are in progress. Expect gliders and tugs in the circuit, and winch cables across the field.

Third, a horizontal white dumbbell. It means use only the sealed, hard surfaces: the runways and taxiways. Landing on the grass areas is prohibited while it is displayed, usually because the unsealed surfaces are waterlogged or soft. The shape is the clue. Two discs joined by a bar, like two sealed areas joined by a sealed strip, with nothing outside them.

There is a variant of the dumbbell, with a black bar across each disc. It means take-off and landing must be on runways only, but taxiing and other ground movement need not be confined to the hard surfaces. That is from Appendix 1 of ICAO Annex 2. Verify it against the current AIP AD 1.1, because older material had this the other way round.

Fourth, a white landing T. It shows the landing direction where no windsock applies. Land parallel to the shaft, towards the crossbar.

And fifth, a right-hand circuit indicator, a right-turning arrow. It shows that right-hand circuits are in force.

[[diagram:ground-signals]]
Picture six tiles laid out side by side. The windsock, showing the wind. A single white cross, meaning that part of the movement area is unserviceable. Two white crosses beside the windsock, for gliding. The white dumbbell, for sealed surfaces only. The landing T, setting the landing direction. And the right-turning arrow, for a right-hand circuit.

Why does the dumbbell matter more at private level? Because the PPL takes you to grass aerodromes where the whole field may normally be used. You'll find that in the private lesson on flight rules, under aerodromes where operations are not restricted to runways. You may have planned a landing into wind across the grass. The dumbbell takes that option away for the day. The ground signal overrides the plan.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. The double white cross and the single white cross mean different things, and the difference is where they are. Two crosses in the signal area, beside the windsock, report an activity: gliding. A single cross on a runway or taxiway reports a closure. Read the position before the shape.

Question. At a grass aerodrome where ERSA says operations are not restricted to runways, you see a white dumbbell in the signal area. Where may you land?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: only on the sealed runway. The dumbbell restricts operations to the sealed surfaces. It overrides the normal freedom to use the whole landing area, usually because the grass is soft or waterlogged.

## Helicopter landing sites

A helicopter landing site, an HLS, is marked with a letter H inside a circle, a square or a triangle, at the touchdown and lift-off area. There is often a lit or painted perimeter.

Aeroplanes keep clear of it. A helicopter arrives and departs steeply, and may come from directions that have nothing to do with the runway in use. And its downwash can overturn a light aeroplane taxiing nearby. Helicopters may also operate from an HLS located away from the runways. A hospital pad, for example, or a pad at a mine site, or on a station. So an H anywhere in your field of view tells you to expect helicopter traffic that is not flying your circuit.

Picture three helicopter landing sites from above: an H inside a circle, an H inside a square, and an H inside a triangle, each with a lit or painted perimeter. Below them is what an H tells an aeroplane pilot. Keep clear of the downwash. Expect steep arrivals and departures from any direction. And remember that pads may be away from the runways.

## The circuit at an aerodrome you do not know

The circuit legs and the dead side are as described in the recreational lesson on aerodromes. Upwind, after take-off. Crosswind, the first turn. Downwind, parallel to the landing direction and opposite to it. Base, the turn towards the runway. And then final. The dead side is opposite the circuit side. It is where overflying aircraft descend to circuit height before joining crosswind.

[[diagram:circuit-pattern]]
Picture a left-hand circuit on runway {24|two four}, seen from above. You climb straight ahead on the upwind leg, and turn left onto crosswind at or above 500 ft. Downwind runs parallel to the runway at 1,000 ft above aerodrome level. Then base, descending, and final onto the runway. The dead side is the right-hand side of the runway, away from the circuit.

What is new at private level is that the circuit direction is not always left. ERSA may show right-hand circuits for a particular runway. That is usually to keep traffic away from a town, high ground, or another aerodrome's circuit. And the right-hand circuit indicator in the signal area may show it. When the circuit is right-hand, every leg moves to the other side of the runway, and so does the dead side. If you learn the dead side as "the right of the runway", you will descend through the circuit at the first aerodrome with right-hand circuits.

[[diagram:right-hand-circuit-dead-side]]
Picture two views of runway {31|three one}, with the landing direction running left to right. In the top view, the default left-hand circuit sits on the south-west side, and the dead side is on the north-east. In the bottom view, ERSA shows right-hand circuits for runway {31|three one}. Now every leg sits on the north-east side, so the dead side moves to the south-west. Rebuild it from the landing direction every time.

So the safe method is to rebuild the circuit from the landing direction, every time, in four steps. First, find the landing direction, from the windsock, the landing T, or the runway in use. Second, find the circuit direction for that runway, from ERSA, the signal area or the NOTAMs. It is left-hand unless shown otherwise. Third, put the downwind leg on the circuit side, parallel and opposite to the landing direction. And fourth, the dead side is the other side.

[[widget:circuit-3d]]
Now imagine flying that circuit in three dimensions, and then swapping the runway in use. When the runway changes, the whole pattern moves, and the dead side moves with it.

Let's work one: a right-hand circuit. You arrive overhead a country aerodrome. The windsock favours runway {31|three one}, and the ERSA entry says right-hand circuits for runway {31|three one}. Where are the legs, and where is the dead side? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
Step one, the landing direction. It is {310|three one zero} degrees, roughly north-west, so final points {310|three one zero}.

Step two, the circuit direction. Right-hand for {31|three one}, from ERSA.

Step three, the circuit side. It is to the right of the runway looking along {310|three one zero}, which is the north-east side. Upwind continues on {310|three one zero}. Crosswind turns right, to about {040|zero four zero}. Downwind runs {130|one three zero}, parallel and opposite to landing. Base turns right, to about {220|two two zero}. And final turns right again, onto {310|three one zero}.

Step four, the dead side. It is the other side of the runway, the south-west. That is to the left of the runway, looking along the landing direction.

So the answer: the circuit is on the north-east side, and the dead side is the south-west.

[[diagram:overhead-join]]
Now the overhead join. Picture a circuit seen from above. The joining aircraft crosses overhead the aerodrome, above circuit height. It descends on the dead side, the side away from the circuit, to circuit height, 1,000 ft above aerodrome level. Then it crosses the upwind end of the runway, joins the crosswind leg, and continues downwind. At our right-hand aerodrome, that means you cross above circuit height, descend on the south-west side, cross the upwind end, and join crosswind heading north-east.

Question. Why do right-hand circuits exist at all, when left-hand is the standard?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: to keep circuit traffic away from something on the standard side. A town, because of noise and the populous-area rules. High ground. Or another aerodrome's circuit. The direction is published in ERSA for that runway, which is why you must read it before arriving.

## The white cross

A white cross on any part of the movement area marks it as unserviceable. Do not use it. Crosses at the ends of a runway close the runway, and a cross on a taxiway closes that taxiway. The cross is large and white, so it can be read from the air, in any direction, against bitumen or grass. And its meaning has no shades. There is no "use with care".

At private level, the cross is rarely a surprise, because a runway closure is normally also published by NOTAM. The cross is what you see. The NOTAM is what you read beforehand. If you read the NOTAMs, the cross confirms what you already know. If you skipped them, the cross is the last line of defence. And if a NOTAM says a runway is closed but no crosses are out, the NOTAM still applies.

Question. A NOTAM closes one of two runways for resealing, and from overhead you see crosses at each end of it. The other runway has a strong crosswind. May you use the closed runway?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: no. The crosses and the NOTAM both say the runway is unserviceable, whatever the wind. Use the open runway if the crosswind is within your limits, or divert.

## Bringing it together

It is a winter Sunday, and you fly from Jandakot to a country aerodrome in the Avon Valley for lunch.

On Saturday, you read ERSA. It shows two runways: {03/21|zero three, two one}, sealed, and {12/30|one two, three zero}, gravel. It shows right-hand circuits for runway {30|three zero}, and operations not restricted to runways. A NOTAM says the northern threshold of {03/21|zero three, two one} is displaced 150 m for works.

On arrival, you fly overhead above circuit height, and read the ground. The windsock favours {30|three zero}. But in the signal area is a white dumbbell. The gravel is soft after the rain, so only sealed surfaces may be used. That rules out {30|three zero}, and the grass.

So you check {21|two one}. Landing towards the south, over the displaced northern threshold, you subtract 150 m from the published length. It still exceeds your landing distance, with margin. Runway {21|two one} has no published right-hand circuit, so it is left-hand, and the dead side is to the right of the runway, looking along {210|two one zero}. You descend on the dead side, join crosswind, and land beyond the bar.

Taxiing in, you follow the yellow lead-in line to a parking position. And you keep clear of the H painted at the apron's edge, for the rescue helicopter. Each marking, read together with ERSA and the NOTAM, changed a decision you had made the evening before.

Question. On that flight, which two pieces of information overrode the plan from ERSA, and where did each come from?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: first, the dumbbell in the signal area, seen on arrival. It closed the gravel runway and the grass, overriding ERSA's "not restricted to runways". And second, the displaced threshold, from the NOTAM, and marked with arrows. It reduced the landing distance on {21|two one}.

## Key points

Let's recap.

Markings. Runway paint is white: the designator, centreline, side stripes, the aiming point about 300 m in, and touchdown zone bars. Piano keys start the landing portion. Arrows to a bar mark a displaced threshold. Before the bar you may take off and roll out, but not land. Side stripes and a transverse stripe, or end markers on an unsealed strip, mark the end. Cones mark the runway edge, and gables the strip boundary. At an LA, cones, drums, tyres or gables mark what the operator has set out, and {AC 91-02|A C ninety-one dash zero two} guides suitability.

Taxiway paint is yellow. Hold on the solid side. A single dashed line is an intermediate holding position. A holding bay lets you wait, or run up, clear of the taxiway. The movement area is the runways, taxiways and aprons. A double white cross by the windsock means gliding. A horizontal white dumbbell means sealed surfaces only. And an H in a circle, square or triangle is a helicopter landing site, so keep clear.

The circuit. Upwind, crosswind, downwind, which is parallel and opposite to landing, then base and final. Left-hand, unless ERSA or the signal area shows right-hand. The dead side is always opposite the circuit side, and moves with it.

The white cross. On the movement area, it means unserviceable, do not use. Crosses at both ends close the runway, usually with a NOTAM as well.

A final exam tip. New at private level: the horizontal white dumbbell means sealed surfaces only. Together with the double white cross, gliding, and the single white cross, unserviceable, it makes up the three ground signals most often examined. Know the displaced threshold rule: on the arrows you may take off and roll out, but there is no touchdown before the bar. Know cones against gables: the edge against the strip boundary. And know the helicopter site markings: an H in a circle, square or triangle. For circuit questions, build the pattern from the landing direction and the published circuit direction, and never place the dead side from memory. And confirm the marking conventions against the current AIP AD 1.1 and the workbook figures, which the exam's diagrams are drawn from.

That's the end of PFRA 2.5.
