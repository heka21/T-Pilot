This is PFRA 2.8, Security. By the end you will be able to say what an air defence identification zone is and what flying in one requires, respond correctly if a military aircraft intercepts you, and state the powers the law gives you as pilot in command. There are a few questions and worked cases along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (PFRA 2.8)

The exam wants two things from you. First, what an ADIZ is, and how to find in the AIP both the general requirements for flying in one, and what the pilot of an intercepted aircraft must do. And second, the powers the law gives a pilot in command.

## Why this matters

Most of your flying in Western Australia will never bring you near a military aircraft or a security incident. That is exactly why this subtopic needs learning in advance. The first time you meet it, there will be no time to look it up.

A private licence can take you into airspace where defence needs to know who you are. An aeroplane that cannot be identified may find a fighter sitting off its left wing, with a pilot who expects you to know what its signals mean.

Closer to home, every time you carry passengers, you are the person the law makes responsible for their safety. And it gives you powers to match: to direct them, to refuse to carry them, and in the worst case to restrain them.

The exam asks you to read the ADIZ and interception text from the AIP, to know the response to each interception signal, and to state what the pilot in command may do.

## What an ADIZ is

Start with a question a defence force has to answer. Of all the aircraft approaching the country, which are friendly?

Normal air traffic services do not need to answer that. A VFR aeroplane in Class G is not controlled by anyone, and may not be talking to anyone, and that is perfectly lawful. For national security, though, some airspace needs every aircraft in it to be known.

An Air Defence Identification Zone, an ADIZ, is that airspace. It is airspace of defined dimensions, within which aircraft must be identified, located and controlled for national security reasons, in addition to normal ATS requirements.

The words "in addition" matter. The ADIZ does not replace the airspace class underneath it. If the ADIZ overlies Class G, the Class G rules still apply, and the ADIZ rules are stacked on top.

So how does defence identify you? By matching what it sees to what it was told. It uses flight plan data and position reports to identify every aircraft in the zone. A radar return that turns up where a flight plan said an aircraft would be, at the time it said, is identified. A return that matches nothing is an unidentified aircraft. And an aircraft that cannot be identified may be intercepted.

The ADIZ boundaries and procedures are published in AIP ENR 2.2. The exam expects you to read and apply that text, so find it in your current AIP, and read it once before the exam.

Question. An ADIZ lies over Class G airspace. Which rules apply to a VFR aeroplane flying through it?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: both. The Class G rules for a VFR flight still apply, and the ADIZ requirements are added on top. An ADIZ exists for national security, in addition to normal ATS requirements, not instead of them.

## Flying in an ADIZ: the general requirements

Every ADIZ requirement follows from one aim. Defence must be able to match your aircraft to a plan, continuously, from the moment you enter. Hear the requirements with that aim in mind, and each one explains itself. There are five of them, and the exact text and numbers are in AIP ENR 2.2.

First, flight notification. You submit a flight plan, a flight notification, to ATS before entering the zone, with the time and position of entry. This is the "what to expect" that defence compares the radar picture against.

Second, two-way radio. You carry a serviceable radio, and keep a listening watch on the appropriate frequency. Then, if something does not match, someone can ask you about it before sending an aircraft to look.

Third, position reports. You report at the entry point, and as required. The AIP sets tolerances on track and time, between the planned entry and the actual one. Outside them, the aircraft may be treated as unidentified. The tolerance is the size of the window inside which "the aircraft we expected" and "the aircraft we see" can be called the same thing.

Fourth, the transponder. You operate it with the assigned code and altitude reporting, so the radar return carries your identity and your level.

And fifth, deviations. You advise ATS of any deviation from the flight plan as soon as possible. A deviation you have not reported looks exactly like an aircraft that is not the one in the plan.

Check the current AIP ENR 2.2 for the exact numbers: the notification lead time, and the track and time tolerances. Do not rely on memory. The numbers can change, but the logic does not.

[[diagram:adiz-entry-tolerance]]
Picture a VFR track, seen from above, crossing the ADIZ boundary at the planned entry point. Around that point is a window, which is the track tolerance. Below it runs a timeline, with a second window around the planned entry time, which is the time tolerance. An aircraft on track and on time falls inside both windows, and is identified. An aircraft off track, or late beyond the time window, without telling ATS, may be treated as unidentified, and may be intercepted. Advising ATS of the deviation before the boundary restores the match.

Let's work one: planning a flight that crosses an ADIZ. Your private VFR flight crosses the boundary of an ADIZ shown in AIP ENR 2.2. Work through the requirements in the order you meet them. Pause if you'd like to try it first.
[[pause 6]]
Step one, before departure. Lodge a flight notification with ATS, giving the position and time at which you will enter the zone. And meet the notification lead time in ENR 2.2.

Step two, the equipment check. The radio is serviceable, and the transponder works, with altitude reporting.

Step three, approaching the boundary. Set the assigned code, and keep the listening watch on the frequency the AIP specifies.

Step four, at entry. Make the position report. You are within the track and time tolerances of your plan, so the radar return is matched to you, and you are identified.

Step five, en route. A cloud bank forces you 10 nm off your planned track. Tell ATS as soon as possible, because an unreported deviation could make you look like an unidentified aircraft.

Every step answers the same question for defence. Is the aircraft we see the aircraft we were told about?

Now try one yourself. On the same flight, a headwind means you will reach the entry point well after the time in your flight notification, outside the time tolerance in the AIP. What is the risk, and what should you do before you reach the boundary?
[[pause 6]]
The answer: outside the tolerance, your aircraft may be treated as unidentified, and an unidentified aircraft may be intercepted. The requirement is to advise ATS of any deviation from the flight plan as soon as possible. So you call ATS on the radio, before you reach the boundary, with your revised entry time. Defence then expects you at the new time, and the match is made.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. An ADIZ is not a prohibited or restricted area, and it is not closed to civil aircraft. You may fly in it. What it demands is identification: a flight notification with entry time and position, a radio, position reports within tolerance, and the transponder. The distractors offer things like a minimum height, or IFR only. Neither is the point of an ADIZ.

## Interception: what the intercepted pilot does

Why does interception happen? An unidentified aircraft in an ADIZ, an aircraft straying towards a prohibited area, or an aircraft that has stopped answering ATC may be intercepted. Another aircraft, usually military, flies up alongside to identify it, to guide it away, or to direct it to land.

Interception of a civil aircraft is always a last resort. And the procedures are designed so that it can be done without a single radio word, because the intercepted pilot may not be on the right frequency. The procedures are in AIP ENR 1.12, and follow ICAO Annex 2, so they are the same anywhere in the world. The signals also appear in the interception part of ERSA EMERG.

An intercepted aircraft should immediately take five actions. First, follow the instructions of the intercepting aircraft, interpreting and responding to its visual signals. Second, notify ATS, if possible. Third, attempt radio contact with the interceptor on {121.5 MHz|one two one decimal five megahertz}, giving your identity and the nature of the flight. Fourth, squawk {7700|seven seven zero zero}, unless ATS instructs otherwise. And fifth, if instructions from ATS conflict with the interceptor's, follow the interceptor, and ask for clarification.

Each step has a reason. Following comes first, because the interceptor is the one physically next to you, and the one with authority over what happens next. Notifying ATS brings in the people who can explain who you are. {121.5|one two one decimal five} is used because it is the one frequency both aircraft are certain to have. {7700|seven seven zero zero} makes your aircraft stand out on every radar screen as an aircraft in an abnormal situation, so the controllers watching can connect you with the interception. And when the instructions conflict, you follow the interceptor, because ATS cannot see you, but the interceptor can.

[[diagram:transponder-emergency-codes]]
Picture three transponder code windows side by side. {7700|seven seven zero zero} is emergency, and it is the code an intercepted aircraft sets, unless ATS says otherwise. {7600|seven six zero zero} is radio failure. And {7500|seven five zero zero} is reserved for unlawful interference.

Question. During an interception, ATS tells you to continue on your track, but the interceptor is signalling you to follow it. What do you do?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: follow the interceptor, and ask ATS for clarification. The interceptor can see you and is acting on its instructions. When the two conflict, the intercepting aircraft's instructions win, until the conflict is resolved.

Now the visual signals. Without radio, the two aircraft talk with their wings, their lights and their landing gear. The interceptor's signals are few, and each one has a single matching response.

[[diagram:interception-signals]]
Picture three panels. In the first, the interceptor is ahead of you and to your left. It rocks its wings, then makes a slow level turn onto the heading it wants. That means "you have been intercepted, follow me". You reply by rocking your wings, and following.

In the second panel, the interceptor makes an abrupt breakaway: a climbing turn of 90 degrees or more, without crossing ahead of you. That means "you may proceed". You reply by rocking your wings.

In the third panel, the interceptor lowers its gear, if it has retractable gear, shows its landing lights, and flies over the runway. That means "land at this aerodrome". You reply by lowering your gear, if fitted, following, and landing if it is safe.

The signals are built so they cannot be mistaken for normal flying. A gentle level turn after wing rocking draws you after it. An abrupt climbing breakaway that deliberately does not cross in front of you is a release. And lowering the gear and flying low over a runway can only mean "here". Your rocking of the wings is the universal "understood". In the land signal, "if safe" matters. The interceptor's instruction does not oblige you to land on a runway too short for your aeroplane.

The intercepted aircraft has signals of its own, for when it cannot simply obey. There are three. Raising the gear and flashing the landing lights while passing over the runway means the aerodrome you have been directed to is inadequate. Switching all lights on and off at regular intervals means you cannot comply. And flashing the lights at irregular intervals means you are in distress.

The difference between regular and irregular carries the meaning, so listen for it in the question. The irregular flashing is the same idea as the general signal you learned at recreational level, where an aircraft switching its lights on and off intermittently is in difficulty. And at night, flashing navigation lights replace wing rocking. The full table is in AIP ENR 1.12.

Let's work one: working through an interception. You are cruising VFR in a Cessna {172|one seventy-two} when a military jet appears. It takes up position ahead and to your left, rocks its wings, and turns slowly onto a new heading. What does it mean, and what do you do? Pause if you'd like to try it first.
[[pause 6]]
Step one, the signal. Wing rocking from ahead and to the left, then a slow level turn, means "you have been intercepted, follow me".

Step two, respond. Rock your wings, and follow.

Step three, the five actions. Notify ATS on your current frequency if you can. Try {121.5 MHz|one two one decimal five megahertz}, with your callsign and the nature of your flight. And squawk {7700|seven seven zero zero}, unless ATS tells you otherwise.

Step four, the next signal. The jet lowers its gear, shows its landing lights, and flies low along the runway of an aerodrome ahead. That means "land at this aerodrome".

Step five, respond. Follow, and land if safe. Your gear is fixed, so the "lower gear" part of the response does not apply. You join and land.

Had the jet instead pulled up in an abrupt climbing turn away from you, without crossing your nose, the meaning would have been "you may proceed". And your response would have been to rock your wings.

Question. You are directed to land at an aerodrome whose runway is far too short for your aeroplane. How do you say so?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: raise the gear, if it is retractable, and flash the landing lights while passing over the runway. That is the signal for "the aerodrome you have designated is inadequate". Landing is only required if it is safe.

## Powers of the pilot in command

Somebody has to be in charge of an aircraft. In a car, a dispute between driver and passenger can be settled by stopping at the kerb. At 5,000 ft there is no kerb. So the law puts the answer beyond argument. Under CASR Part 91, the pilot in command is responsible for the safety of the aircraft, and of everyone on board, and has final authority over the operation of the aircraft.

Responsibility and authority come together. The law would be unfair if it made you answerable for everyone's safety without giving you the means to secure it. So it gives you powers to match. There are five.

First, you may give safety directions to passengers and crew, and they must comply. Part 91 makes failing to comply an offence. That is the legal backing behind your passenger briefing. "Follow the pilot's instructions" is not a request.

[[diagram:passenger-briefing]]
Picture the passenger briefing as six tiles. Seat belts and harnesses, and when they must be worn. Where the exits are, and how they work. The smoking rules. Where the emergency equipment is, such as the fire extinguisher, and life jackets if carried. The brace position. And last, that passengers must follow the pilot's instructions. That last tile is the briefing's link to your legal authority.

Second, you may refuse to carry, or disembark, a person whose behaviour endangers safety. An intoxicated or disruptive passenger, for example.

Third, you may take reasonable measures, including restraint, against a person on board who is committing, or about to commit, an offence that threatens safety. The {Crimes (Aviation) Act 1991|Crimes Aviation Act of nineteen ninety-one} and the Tokyo Convention give legal protection for reasonable action taken in good faith.

Fourth, you may refuse to carry cargo or baggage that is not safe. That includes dangerous goods that are not permitted for carriage.

And fifth, you may depart from a rule, or an ATC clearance, in an emergency, to the extent necessary for safety. And you report it afterwards.

Notice the limits written into each power. Restraint must be reasonable, and the protection covers action in good faith. Departures from the rules in an emergency go only as far as necessary for safety, and you report them afterwards. Final authority is not unlimited authority. It is authority used for safety.

[[diagram:pic-authority]]
Picture it all on one card. It starts with responsibility for the safety of the aircraft and everyone on board, which comes with final authority over its operation. Then come the five powers: directions, refusing or disembarking a person, reasonable restraint, refusing unsafe cargo, and departing from a rule in an emergency. And beside each power, its limit: reasonable, in good faith, only as far as necessary for safety, and reported afterwards.

Let's work one: which power applies? You are flying three friends from Jandakot to Rottnest for lunch. There are three moments. Pause after each if you'd like to try it.

The first. At the aeroplane, one of them is unsteady on his feet, and smells strongly of beer.
[[pause 4]]
A person whose condition endangers safety. You may refuse to carry him, and he stays behind.

The second. On approach to Rottnest, a passenger unfastens her harness to lean over and take photos.
[[pause 4]]
You give a safety direction: fasten the harness now. She must comply, and failing to is an offence.

The third. On the way home, smoke appears from the panel. The quickest way to a runway takes you into controlled airspace before you can get a clearance.
[[pause 4]]
You may depart from the clearance requirement to the extent necessary for safety, and you report it afterwards.

In each case, the power used is the smallest one that restores safety.

Question. Why does the law give the pilot in command such wide powers?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because it makes the pilot in command responsible for the safety of the aircraft and everyone on board, and responsibility without the authority to act would be meaningless. In flight, there is nobody else who can make the decision. So the law gives the pilot final authority.

## Everyday security for private pilots

Security is also the dull, daily habit of not making your aeroplane useful to someone else. There are five habits.

First, secure the aircraft. Lock the doors, fit the control locks, and remove the keys. Follow the aerodrome's security rules. Security-controlled airports have identification card requirements, the ASIC and the VIC, under the Aviation Transport Security Act.

Second, unlawful interference, such as a hijack or a threat on board. Squawk {7500|seven five zero zero}. ATC will confirm discreetly. Do not change it unless they ask. The code exists because the person threatening you may be listening to every word you say on the radio.

Third, weapons and firearms. Their carriage is tightly restricted under the Aviation Transport Security Act and its regulations. Never carry them on board unless the law expressly permits it.

Fourth, dangerous goods. Their carriage is controlled by CASR Part 92. Check before carrying anything doubtful: fuel cans, aerosols, lithium batteries, ammunition.

And fifth, report suspicious activity around aircraft or aerodromes, to the aerodrome operator or the national security hotline.

Question. You have squawked {7500|seven five zero zero}, and ATC asks you to confirm your code. Why might they ask, and what do you do?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: ATC confirms discreetly, to make sure the code was not set by mistake, without saying anything that would alert a hijacker listening on the frequency. You leave {7500|seven five zero zero} set. You change it only if ATC asks you to.

## Bringing it together

You are planning a private cross-country flight that, for one leg, crosses into an ADIZ. Before departure you read AIP ENR 2.2, lodge the flight notification with the entry point and time, and check the radio and transponder.

One passenger shows up with a bag that holds a spare lithium battery pack and a can of insect spray. As pilot in command, you check the dangerous goods rules before agreeing to carry either.

Approaching the zone, you set the assigned code, keep the listening watch, and report at the entry point. A thunderstorm makes you deviate, so you tell ATS at once.

Even so, a military aircraft appears off your left wing, rocks its wings, and turns away slowly. You rock your wings and follow. You call on {121.5 MHz|one two one decimal five megahertz}, and squawk {7700|seven seven zero zero} unless ATS tells you otherwise. Within minutes the interceptor has identified you, and breaks away in a climbing turn. You rock your wings, and carry on.

Every part of the lesson was there. The ADIZ requirements that made you identifiable. The interception responses, when that was not enough. And the authority of the pilot in command over what came aboard.

## Key points

Let's recap.

An ADIZ is airspace in which aircraft must be identified, located and controlled for national security, on top of the normal ATS requirements. It is in AIP ENR 2.2. The requirements: a flight notification with entry time and position, a two-way radio and listening watch, position reports within the track and time tolerances, a transponder with the assigned code and altitude, and deviations advised at once.

Intercepted, under AIP ENR 1.12: follow the interceptor, notify ATS, call on {121.5 MHz|one two one decimal five megahertz}, and squawk {7700|seven seven zero zero} unless ATS says otherwise. If instructions conflict, follow the interceptor.

The interceptor's signals. Wing rocking, then a slow turn: follow me. An abrupt climbing breakaway: proceed. Gear down over the runway: land here. Your replies. Rock your wings to say understood. Regular flashing of all lights: cannot comply. Irregular flashing: in distress.

The pilot in command is responsible for the safety of the aircraft and everyone on board, and has final authority. The powers: safety directions that passengers must obey, refusing carriage or disembarking a person, reasonable restraint in good faith, refusing unsafe cargo and dangerous goods, and departing from rules or clearances in an emergency, only as far as needed, and reporting it. And a hijack is {7500|seven five zero zero}.

A final exam tip. Intercepted: follow the interceptor, call on {121.5|one two one decimal five}, and squawk {7700|seven seven zero zero}, unless ATS says otherwise. Hijack is {7500|seven five zero zero}. The pilot in command has final authority for the safety of the aircraft and its occupants. Expect to match an interceptor signal to its meaning and your response. And expect to pick the ADIZ requirements out of distractors: flight notification with entry details, radio, position reports, and transponder. Check the current AIP ENR 2.2 and ENR 1.12 for the exact tolerances and the full signal table.
