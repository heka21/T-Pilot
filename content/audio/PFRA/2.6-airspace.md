This is PFRA 2.6, Airspace and altimetry, at private pilot level. By the end you will know what each class of airspace gives a VFR flight and asks of it, how to read a boundary off a chart and stay clear of it, and how to set your altimeter so that every vertical limit means what it says. There are a few questions and calculations along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (PFRA 2.6)

The exam wants five things from you. First, to tell the classes of airspace apart, by what each gives a VFR flight and requires of it. Second, the vocabulary of the airspace system, and how to find boundaries and limits on a chart or in ERSA. Third, how to use the tracking tolerances to stay clear of controlled airspace. Fourth, what you do in each class, and at each kind of aerodrome. And fifth, altimetry: the subscale settings, the transition altitude and layer, the error from a wrong setting, and the standard atmosphere behind it all.

## Why this matters

As a recreational pilot, you learned to stay out of controlled airspace. As a private pilot with the controlled airspace endorsement, you will go into it. Out of Jandakot's Class D. Under or through Perth's Class C steps. And on long flights to Kalgoorlie or Geraldton, where the area frequency is your only company.

So you need the controllers' vocabulary, the ability to read a boundary off a chart, a track that does not graze a step, and a correctly set altimeter. Every vertical boundary is only as accurate as your subscale.

The exam tests extraction: read a label, find a limit. It tests definitions: report or broadcast, control zone or control area. And it has one calculation: the error from a wrong subscale setting. Every regulatory figure here is from the AIP, the Part 91 Manual of Standards and the Visual Flight Rules Guide as they were recorded. Airspace rules and boundaries change, so verify each one against the current documents.

## The classes: what each one gives you and asks of you

In controlled airspace, a controller issues clearances and keeps some or all traffic apart. In non-controlled airspace, you separate yourself, by looking out and talking. The exam compares the classes on four questions. What service do you get? Do you need a clearance? Who keeps you apart from whom? And what must you carry?

Think of it as a sliding scale: the more ATC, air traffic control, does for you, the more it needs from you. Now the classes, from the top.

Class A is high-level airspace for jets, all IFR, under the instrument flight rules. See and avoid at closing speeds of 900 knots does not work, so VFR is not available.

Class C, around Perth and in the steps above it, mixes you with fast IFR traffic. So ATC separates you from IFR, and gives you traffic information about other VFR. To do that, it must see you on radar and talk to you. So a clearance is required, and a VHF radio and a transponder are both required.

Class D surrounds towers such as Jandakot, where the traffic is mostly slow VFR in the circuit. A clearance is required. The tower gives you traffic information and sequences you. But it separates only IFR from IFR, plus everyone on the runway. Spacing behind the traffic you were told about is your job. A radio is required, and a transponder only where fitted, because the tower works mainly by eye.

Class E is controlled for IFR only. A VFR flight needs no clearance, and gets no separation. At most it gets flight information, and traffic information where possible. The radio is recommended, so that you hear the IFR traffic. And the transponder makes you visible to the controller separating it. It is required in Class E below the published level, but check the current rule.

Class G is the only non-controlled class. No clearance, no separation: you see and avoid, broadcast and listen, and information is available on request. The radio is required above 5,000 ft, and at certified or registered aerodromes, formerly called {CTAF(R)|see-taff R}. A transponder is recommended.

Two limits apply on top. A speed limit of 250 kt indicated airspeed below 10,000 ft, in all classes, and 200 kt in Class D unless ATC approves otherwise. And the visual weather minima differ between low-level Class G and everywhere else.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. "Controlled" does not mean that ATC separates you from everyone. In Class D, ATC does not separate VFR from VFR in the air. In Class E, a VFR flight gets no separation at all. Yet both are controlled airspace. So match the class to the separation it gives, not to the word "controlled".

Question. You are VFR in Class E at 8,500 ft. What does ATC do for you, and why do you still listen on the frequency?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: no clearance, and no separation. Class E is controlled for IFR only, so at most you get flight and traffic information. You listen because the IFR traffic around you is on that frequency, and hearing it helps you see and avoid it.

## The language of the airspace system

Each term here names a piece of a structure you can picture. Start with the biggest, and work down.

The whole of Australia's airspace, and a great deal of ocean around it, is split into two flight information regions, or FIRs: Melbourne and Brisbane. A flight information region is the volume in which one air traffic services centre provides flight information and alerting. Western Australia is in the Melbourne FIR.

That is too big for one frequency. So each region is divided into flight information areas, FIAs, each with its own area VHF frequency. Their boundaries are drawn on the ERC-L, the En Route Chart Low, and on the VNC, the Visual Navigation Chart. Flying from Perth towards Kalgoorlie in Class G, you change area frequency at each boundary, to stay with the IFR traffic and Flight Information for your area.

OCTA means outside controlled airspace, which is Class G. "OCTA" in a clearance or a report tells the controller that you are, or will be, in Class G.

A control area, a CTA, is controlled airspace above a lower limit. It is a "step", labelled with its class and lower limit, such as {"C LL 4500"|C L L forty-five hundred}, or {"LL FL125"|L L flight level one two five}. A control zone, a CTR, is controlled airspace from the surface, around a controlled aerodrome, so that aircraft taking off and landing there are inside it. Both appear on the VTC, the VNC, the ERC-L and the TAC, with class and limits. ERSA gives the tower's hours.

[[diagram:airspace-cross-section]]
Picture a slice through the sky. At a major airport, a Class C control zone starts at the surface. Around it, Class C steps spread outwards, each starting at a lower limit that rises with distance, with Class G beneath. A regional tower has its own Class D zone from the surface, Class E sits above the steps, and Class A is at high level.

Now reports and broadcasts. A report is a transmission to air traffic services, such as a position report in controlled airspace, or a request. It expects a response. A broadcast goes to all stations on the frequency, with no reply expected: the CTAF calls, or an "all stations" call in Class G. The logic follows the airspace. In Class G nobody controls you, so you broadcast for other pilots. In Class C and D a controller is responsible for you, so you report, and you are answered.

Question. A pilot says: "Traffic Northam, Cherokee {ABC|alpha bravo charlie}, five miles south-west, inbound, three thousand five hundred, joining crosswind runway {21|two one}, Traffic Northam." Report or broadcast, and why?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: a broadcast. It is addressed to all traffic on the CTAF, no reply is expected, and nobody is controlling the Cherokee.

## Finding it on the chart: routes, lanes, areas and aerodromes

The remaining terms are features you find on a chart and look up in ERSA. None of them is visible out of the window.

VFR routes are published tracks on the VTC, the Visual Terminal Chart. They let VFR aircraft transit near or under controlled airspace without a clearance, with altitude limits and reporting points. Around Perth there are coastal and inland routes, and lanes into Jandakot. A lane of entry is a Class G corridor through controlled airspace, with a strict altitude band. Stay inside the lane, at the published altitude, and make the published broadcasts. Controlled airspace lies on both sides, and often above, so wandering off the line is an infringement.

The prohibited, restricted and danger areas, the PRD areas, are identified on the charts, and detailed in the ERSA PRD section and AIP ENR 5.1.

[[diagram:airspace-active-check]]
Picture four steps in a row. First, the chart, for where the area is and its limits. Second, ERSA's PRD section and AIP ENR 5.1, for its hours and its controlling authority. Third, the NOTAMs, for anything different today. And fourth, if you are still unsure, call. Chart for where, ERSA for when and who, NOTAM for today.

Around a non-controlled aerodrome, everyone uses the common traffic advisory frequency, the CTAF, published in ERSA, or {126.7 MHz|one two six decimal seven megahertz} where none is specified. You use it within 10 nm of the aerodrome, at a height where you could conflict with circuit traffic. {CTAF(R)|see-taff R} was the former name for aerodromes where radio was mandatory. The current rule makes radio mandatory at all certified and registered aerodromes. So treat every CTAF with an ERSA entry as radio-required, and verify that against the current Part 91 Manual of Standards.

A controlled aerodrome has a tower, inside a control zone. Taxi, take-off and landing need a clearance. The ATIS gives the runway and conditions, and ERSA gives the tower hours and local procedures.

[[diagram:class-d-tower-hours]]
Picture the same towered aerodrome twice, side by side. On the left, during the tower's hours in ERSA, the zone is Class D and you need a clearance. On the right, outside those hours, the zone reverts to Class G, or Class E above, and the aerodrome becomes a CTAF aerodrome.

On the VTC, a step labelled {"C LL 2500"|C L L twenty-five hundred} means Class C from 2,500 ft up. A control zone from the surface is marked SFC, and ERSA gives the same in words. Two habits prevent most misreadings. A lower limit is the bottom of the controlled airspace: you are in Class G only while you are below it. And a limit written as a flight level, such as {"LL FL125"|L L flight level one two five}, is a pressure level on {1013|ten thirteen}, not an altitude on QNH.

[[diagram:chart-features-panel]]
Picture four chart panels side by side: the WAC, the VNC, the VTC and the ERC-L. The VTC and the VNC show the control zones, the control area steps and the PRD areas, and the VTC adds the VFR routes. The WAC, the World Aeronautical Chart, shows no airspace at all.

Let's work one: extracting limits along a planned track. Your VFR track passes under two control area steps, and beside a restricted area. On the VTC, step one is labelled {"C LL 2500"|C L L twenty-five hundred}. Step two, further out, is {"C LL 3500"|C L L thirty-five hundred}. The restricted area runs from the surface to 3,000 ft. What are your ceilings, and what about the restricted area? Pause if you'd like to try it first.
[[pause 6]]
Step one. The first step begins at 2,500 ft, so below it you are in Class G up to, but not including, 2,500 ft. Your ceiling under step one is below 2,500 ft.

Step two. The second step begins at 3,500 ft, so your ceiling under it is below 3,500 ft. The ceiling rises as you get further from the airport, as the steps do.

Step three. Look up the restricted area in the ERSA PRD section for its hours, then the NOTAMs. If it is active and you have no clearance, you must stay clear laterally. Going above it is not possible under step one, because 3,000 ft would be inside Class C.

Step four. Plan each altitude with a margin below the lower limit, changing level only once clearly past each step boundary. So the answer is a profile, not a single altitude: below 2,500 ft, then below 3,500 ft, and around the restricted area if it is active.

Question. What is the practical difference between a control area labelled {"LL 4500"|L L forty-five hundred} and one labelled {"LL FL125"|L L flight level one two five}?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the first is an altitude, measured on QNH. The second is a flight level, measured with {1013|ten thirteen} set. On a low QNH day, flight level one two five is lower in true altitude than 12,500 ft. So you need the right altimetry to know where the boundary really is.

## Staying out by design: tracking tolerances

Infringements rarely happen because a pilot decided to enter controlled airspace. They happen because the pilot planned along the boundary and drifted, or cruised at the step's lower limit with the altimeter a little out.

AIP ENR 1.1 requires a VFR pilot to navigate so as to remain clear of controlled airspace without a clearance, allowing for navigation tolerances. Every means of navigation has an uncertainty: a visual fix, a navigation aid, or a GNSS position. The tolerance is the band either side of your track in which you could actually be. If that band touches the boundary, so can you.

So near a boundary, you either stay well inside it, or you obtain a clearance before you reach it. The lesson's source gives the buffer as at least a nautical mile or two laterally, and a few hundred feet vertically below a step. Vertically, an altimeter may legally be up to 100 ft out, and a subscale a few hectopascals out adds tens of feet more. That is why pilots plan 500 ft below a step, rather than at it. The specific tolerance figures for each means of navigation are in AIP ENR 1.1. Check the current text, because the source does not record them.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. A step labelled {"C LL 3500"|C L L thirty-five hundred} does not mean you may cruise at 3,500 ft. At 3,500 ft you are at its lower limit, the bottom of the controlled airspace. Plan below it with a margin, typically 500 ft.

Question. Why does the rule talk about "tolerances", rather than just saying "stay out"?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because no navigation is exact. Visual fixes, navigation aids and the altimeter all have errors, so a track or level planned on a boundary will sometimes cross it. The tolerance tells you how far back to plan.

## Operating in each class and at each kind of aerodrome

The procedures follow from who provides the separation. Where ATC takes responsibility, you ask, read back and comply. Where it does not, you look, listen and broadcast.

In Class C and D, get the ATIS first. Then request a clearance before you reach the control area or zone, giving your callsign, type, position, altitude and intentions. Read back the safety-critical parts: altitude, heading, runway, frequency, transponder code and holding instructions. Then comply, report as directed, and request a clearance to leave, or advise leaving. A clearance is a specific contract about where you will be, and the read-back confirms it.

In Class E there is no clearance. Fly the VFR levels, keep VMC, listen on the appropriate frequency, and squawk the VFR code with altitude, so the controller separating IFR can see you.

In Class G, fly the VFR cruising levels above 5,000 ft, keep a listening watch on the area frequency, broadcast when safety needs it, and use the CTAF procedures at aerodromes.

[[diagram:vfr-cruising-levels]]
Picture a compass rose split down the middle. On magnetic tracks from {000|zero zero zero} to {179|one seven nine} degrees, the eastern half, fly odd thousands plus 500 ft: 5,500, 7,500 or 9,500. From {180|one eight zero} to {359|three five nine} degrees, the western half, fly even thousands plus 500 ft: 6,500 or 8,500. Below 5,000 ft, any altitude may be flown, subject to minimum heights.

At a non-controlled aerodrome, you broadcast taxiing, entering and backtracking the runway, and inbound at about 10 nm, with position, altitude, intentions and ETA. You broadcast joining the circuit, turning base and final where traffic requires, and clear of the runway. Join overhead at 1,500 ft above aerodrome level, or on the circuit legs as the AIP allows. Fly the published circuit height and direction, and give way under the rules of the air. At a certified or registered aerodrome, carry and use a serviceable VHF radio. The calls do a controller's job: everyone in the circuit builds the same picture.

[[diagram:overhead-join]]
Picture the overhead join from above. You cross overhead above circuit height, and descend on the dead side, away from the circuit, to circuit height, 1,000 ft above the aerodrome. Then you cross the upwind end of the runway to join crosswind, and carry on downwind.

At a controlled aerodrome you need a clearance for taxi, take-off and landing, and a transponder. You give the ATIS code in your first call, and a full read-back of runway and holding instructions. A misheard "hold short" is how runway incursions start.

Question. You are approaching a certified non-controlled aerodrome with an ERSA entry. Its ERSA page shows no special radio requirement. Must you carry and use a radio?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: yes. The current rule makes radio carriage and use mandatory at all certified and registered aerodromes, replacing the old {CTAF(R)|see-taff R} designation.

## Altimetry: what the altimeter measures and from where

Every vertical limit, cruising level and minimum height depends on one instrument. The altimeter is a barometer. It converts the outside pressure to a height using the standard atmosphere. And it shows the height difference between the reference pressure set in the subscale and the pressure level you are at.

That conversion uses the ICAO standard atmosphere, the ISA. At mean sea level its temperature is 15 degrees Celsius, and its pressure {1013.25 hPa|ten thirteen point two five hectopascals}. The temperature falls about 2 degrees per 1,000 ft, 1.98 to be exact. And in the lower atmosphere the pressure falls about 1 hPa per 30 ft. That is a planning figure. The real rate is about 1 hPa per 27 ft at sea level, and 1 per 36 ft at 10,000 ft, because thinner air spans more height per hectopascal. Thirty feet is the exam figure.

Three words, three datums. Height is vertical distance above a specified datum, usually the ground or an aerodrome. Altitude is vertical distance above mean sea level, which the altimeter shows with QNH set. And a flight level is pressure altitude in hundreds of feet, with {1013.2 hPa|ten thirteen point two hectopascals} set. So flight level one two five is 12,500 ft pressure altitude.

[[diagram:height-altitude-elevation]]
Picture a side view. An aerodrome has an elevation of 300 ft. An aeroplane in its circuit, with QNH set, reads an altitude of 1,300 ft, so its height above the aerodrome is 1,000 ft. At the same altitude over a 900 ft ridge, it is only 400 ft above the ground.

Now the settings. Set area QNH or local QNH, and the altimeter shows altitude. Set QFE, the pressure at the aerodrome, and it shows height above the aerodrome, reading zero on the runway. Australian procedures do not use QFE, but the exam asks what it shows. And set the standard pressure, {1013.2|ten thirteen point two}, sometimes called QNE, and it shows a flight level.

Local QNH is one aerodrome's value now, from the tower, the ATIS or the AWIS. Area QNH is a forecast value for a wider area and period, from the area forecast. Local QNH is more accurate near its aerodrome.

The standard setting ignores the day's pressure. Everyone above the transition altitude sets it, so every altimeter is wrong by the same amount, and vertical separation still works. In the cruise, agreement between aircraft matters more than truth about the ground.

If no QNH is available on the ground, set the altimeter to read the aerodrome elevation. The subscale then shows the local QNH, because that is how QNH is defined. And with a known QNH, the same check tests the instrument: more than 100 ft from the elevation, and it is unserviceable for VFR.

Question. With {1013.2|ten thirteen point two} set, you read 7,000 ft. What is this number, and is it your height above the sea?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: it is pressure altitude, in effect flight level seven zero. It equals your altitude only if the QNH happens to be {1013.2|ten thirteen point two}. On any other day the two differ, by about 30 ft per hectopascal.

## The transition altitude, layer and level

Low down, you need the truth about the terrain, which is QNH. Higher up, you need agreement between aircraft, which is {1013|ten thirteen}. In Australia the transition altitude is 10,000 ft, the transition level is FL110, and the transition layer lies between them.

Climbing, set {1013|ten thirteen} when passing 10,000 ft. Descending, set the area or local QNH when passing FL110. And never cruise in the transition layer. In the layer, one aircraft could be on QNH and another on {1013|ten thirteen}, their altimeters disagreeing by the difference. Keeping it empty means the two systems never meet.

The lowest usable flight level depends on the day's pressure. With QNH {1013|ten thirteen}, FL110 is 11,000 ft altitude. With a lower QNH, a flight level sits lower, by about 30 ft for every hectopascal below {1013|ten thirteen}. At QNH {1003|ten oh three}, that is 10 hectopascals, times 30, which is 300 ft. So FL110 sits at about 10,700 ft, and the gap to an aircraft at 10,000 ft on QNH has shrunk.

So AIP ENR 1.7 gives a table. For QNH {1013|ten thirteen} or above, the lowest usable level is FL110. For lower QNH it is FL115 or FL120, so the flight level stays clear of 10,000 ft. In the exam, find the area QNH in the table's bands, and read off the level. The transition layer is the airspace between 10,000 ft and that level.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. The transition altitude, 10,000 ft on QNH, and the transition level, FL110 on {1013|ten thirteen}, are not the same thing. Climbing, the change to {1013|ten thirteen} happens at 10,000 ft. Descending, the change to QNH happens at FL110.

Now the subscale error. If the subscale is not the real QNH, the altimeter measures from the wrong datum, and each hectopascal is about 30 ft of error. Reason out the direction. A higher number tells the altimeter there is more air below you than there is. So it over-reads, and you are lower than indicated. A lower number makes it under-read. Set {1003|ten oh three} when the QNH is {1013|ten thirteen}, and it reads 300 ft low. Set {1023|ten twenty-three}, and it reads 300 ft high. Flying from high pressure into low without resetting does the same: high to low, look out below.

Let's work one: true altitude with the wrong subscale setting. The altimeter shows 6,500 ft, with {1020 hPa|ten twenty hectopascals} set. The actual area QNH is {1008 hPa|ten oh eight hectopascals}. Use 30 ft per hectopascal. What is your true altitude? Pause if you'd like to try it first.
[[pause 6]]
Step one, the subscale error. {1020|ten twenty} minus {1008|ten oh eight} is 12 hPa, too high. Step two, the height error: 12 times 30 is 360 ft. Step three, the direction: the setting is too high, so the altimeter over-reads. Step four, the true altitude: 6,500 minus 360 is about 6,140 ft.

So you are at about 6,140 ft, 360 ft lower than you think, and over high ground that works against you. Reset to {1008|ten oh eight}, the reading drops to 6,140 ft, and you climb back to 6,500 ft.

Now try one yourself. You are cruising at an indicated 5,500 ft with {1025|ten twenty-five} set, and the area QNH is {1012|ten twelve}. How far off are you, in which direction, and what do you do?
[[pause 6]]
The subscale is {1025|ten twenty-five} minus {1012|ten twelve}, which is 13 hPa too high. 13 times 30 is 390 ft. Too high a setting over-reads, so your true altitude is about 5,110 ft. Reset to {1012|ten twelve}, and climb back to 5,500 ft.

Question. You fly from an area of QNH {1018|ten eighteen} into one of QNH {1009|ten oh nine} without resetting. Are you higher or lower than the altimeter says, and by about how much?
[[pause 6]]
The answer: lower, by about 270 ft. Your subscale still shows {1018|ten eighteen}, 9 hPa too high, so the altimeter over-reads by 9 times 30, which is 270 ft. High to low, look out below.

## Bringing it together

You are flying Kalgoorlie back to Jandakot, with the controlled airspace endorsement. The track is about {250|two five zero} degrees magnetic, so above 5,000 ft you need an even thousand plus 500: 8,500 ft.

You are in Class G in the Melbourne FIR, on each area frequency in turn, with the radio required above 5,000 ft. There is no AWIS, so you set Kalgoorlie's elevation and read the local QNH from the subscale. En route, you reset area QNH at each forecast area boundary, because the pressure falls towards the coast.

Approaching Perth, you read the "C L L" labels on your descent path, and plan 500 ft below each step. Near Jandakot you take the ATIS, and call the tower before the control zone with callsign, type, position, altitude and intentions. You read back runway, altitude and code, and land on a clearance. Had the tower been closed, Jandakot would have been a CTAF aerodrome in Class G, and you would have broadcast instead of reported.

Question. In that flight, which piece of altimetry most directly protects you from an infringement under the Perth steps?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: flying on the current QNH. The steps' lower limits are altitudes, so a stale setting puts you off by about 30 ft per hectopascal. With the 500 ft planning margin, the correct QNH keeps you out.

## Key points

Let's recap.

The classes. Class C: a clearance, separation from IFR, a radio and a transponder. Class D: a clearance, a radio, and traffic information. Class E: no clearance and no separation for VFR. Class G: no clearance, no separation, and a radio above 5,000 ft and at certified or registered aerodromes. Class A is closed to VFR. And 250 kt below 10,000 ft, 200 kt in Class D.

The vocabulary. Two FIRs, Melbourne and Brisbane, with WA in Melbourne. FIAs, each with an area frequency. OCTA is Class G. A control area starts at a lower limit, a control zone at the surface. A report goes to air traffic services and is answered, and a broadcast goes to all stations. In a lane of entry, stay in the lane at the published altitude. The CTAF is used within 10 nm, on {126.7|one two six decimal seven} if none is published. And {"C LL 2500"|C L L twenty-five hundred} means Class C from 2,500 ft up.

Tolerances. Stay well inside a boundary, or get a clearance. Plan 500 ft below a step, never on it.

Procedures. Class C and D: ATIS, request a clearance, read back, comply. Class E: VFR levels, listen, squawk with altitude. Class G: VFR levels, area frequency, broadcast for safety. A CTAF aerodrome: the standard calls, the overhead join at 1,500 ft, and a radio at certified and registered aerodromes. A controlled aerodrome: clearances, transponder, ATIS code, full read-back.

Altimetry. QNH gives altitude, QFE height above the aerodrome, and {1013.2|ten thirteen point two} a flight level. Transition altitude 10,000 ft, transition level FL110. Never cruise in the layer, and a lower QNH raises the lowest usable level. Set the elevation to find local QNH. One hectopascal is about 30 ft, and too high a setting over-reads. The standard atmosphere at sea level is 15 degrees Celsius and {1013.25 hPa|ten thirteen point two five hectopascals}.

A final exam tip. Altitude is QNH, a flight level is {1013|ten thirteen}, and height is above a surface. Set {1013|ten thirteen} climbing through 10,000 ft, and QNH descending through FL110. A setting that is too high, or a low QNH you failed to set, means you are lower than the altimeter says. Only Class C separates VFR from IFR. When given a chart label or an ERSA extract, read the lower limit as the bottom of the controlled airspace. And verify the class requirements, speed limits and transition figures against the current AIP.

That's the end of PFRA 2.6.
