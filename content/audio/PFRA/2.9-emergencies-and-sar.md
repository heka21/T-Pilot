This is PFRA 2.9, Emergencies and SAR, which stands for search and rescue, at private pilot level. By the end you will be able to read the light signals another aircraft makes, tell an accident from an incident and report each one correctly, plan and manage a SARTIME for a remote flight, and know how the search and rescue system will look for you. There are a few questions and worked cases along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (PFRA 2.9)

The exam wants five things from you. First, what an aircraft means when it switches its navigation and landing lights on and off. Second, how to tell an incident from an accident. Third, what must be reported after an incident or accident, and how. Fourth, what a SARTIME is, and how you use one. And fifth, the document that holds the emergency procedures.

## Why this matters

These five elements are word for word the ones you met at recreational level, and the RPL lesson on emergencies and SAR remains the foundation. What changes with a private licence is how far from help you can be.

A recreational pilot flies near home. A private pilot plans Jandakot to Kalbarri, Northam to Esperance, or a weekend out to Kalgoorlie and Laverton. That is country where a forced landing could leave you a day's drive from anyone who knows you are there. In that country, the SARTIME is not a box on a form. It is the thing that brings an aircraft overhead before your water runs out.

So this lesson revisits the definitions and reporting rules briefly, with new cases. Then it spends most of its time on what the private licence adds. Choosing which flights need a SARTIME. Lodging and changing it. How the search and rescue system responds when it expires. And what you carry, so that a search finds you.

## Lights switched on and off: three meanings to tell apart

You are holding short at Geraldton. An aeroplane on a long final starts switching its landing and navigation lights on and off, again and again, without a word on the radio.

An aircraft that uses its navigation and landing lights intermittently is signalling that it is in difficulty, and compelled to land. Most often its radio has failed. It needs everyone else to give way. So your response is to stay clear of the runway, and if you are in the circuit, to extend or leave the pattern to make room.

At private pilot level, you need to tell this signal from two others that also use lights, because the context decides the meaning.

The first is the acknowledgement of a tower light signal at night. A pilot flashes the landing lights in reply to a light signal from the tower. It is a brief reply to a signal, not a repeated pattern on approach.

The second is interception. An intercepted aircraft switching all its lights on and off at regular intervals means it cannot comply. Flashing them at irregular intervals means it is in distress. The security lesson covers interception.

[[diagram:light-signals-compared]]
Picture four strips of light patterns, side by side on a time line. The first strip is a single flash of the landing lights after a tower signal at night: an acknowledgement. The second is navigation and landing lights switched on and off, repeatedly, on approach: in difficulty and compelled to land, so give way. The third is an intercepted aircraft switching all its lights on and off at regular intervals: cannot comply. And the fourth is an intercepted aircraft flashing its lights at irregular intervals: in distress.

So read the situation. A single flash after a green from the tower is an acknowledgement. Irregular, repeated switching on approach is a request for the runway. And regular switching with a military aircraft alongside is a reply to an interceptor.

Question. At night, an aeroplane on final flashes its landing lights once, just after the tower has shown it a steady green. Is it in difficulty?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: probably not. A single flash in reply to a tower light signal is the night acknowledgement. The signal of an aircraft in difficulty and compelled to land is intermittent, repeated switching of the navigation and landing lights.

## Accident or incident

The first question after an occurrence is how serious it was, because that decides the reporting. The definitions, in the transport safety legislation, turn on outcomes.

An accident involves death or serious injury, serious structural damage to the aircraft, or the aircraft being missing. It applies to an occurrence associated with the operation of the aircraft, between the time someone boards with the intention of flight, and the time everyone has disembarked.

A useful way to test any occurrence is to ask three questions. What happened to the people? What happened to the airframe? And what happened to the aircraft's whereabouts? If none of the three answers is serious, it is not an accident.

Some damage is common, local and repairable, and does not by itself make an occurrence an accident. That is damage limited to the engine, the propeller, the wheels, tyres or brakes, the fairings, or small dents and puncture holes in the skin.

An incident is any other occurrence that affects or could affect safety. The definition is wide on purpose, because incidents are where the lessons are cheap. And a serious incident is a near-accident: one where an accident nearly occurred. A near collision, a runway incursion, or fuel exhaustion that ended without an accident.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. A dramatic event is not automatically an accident. An engine failure that ends in a successful landing in a paddock, with nobody hurt and no structural damage, is an incident. Test the outcome against the three accident questions, people, airframe and whereabouts, not against how frightening it was.

Question. In severe turbulence, a passenger who had loosened her belt hits the cabin roof, and suffers a serious neck injury. The aeroplane is undamaged. Accident or incident?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: an accident. A serious injury to a person on board, between boarding for flight and disembarking, meets the accident definition on its own. The aircraft does not need to be damaged.

## Reporting: from category to action

Once the category is settled, the reporting rule follows from it. Reports go to the ATSB, the Australian Transport Safety Bureau, not to CASA. The ATSB investigates to prevent a recurrence, while CASA enforces the rules.

There are three rules. First, accidents and serious incidents are immediately reportable matters. Report them to the ATSB as soon as practicable by phone, and in writing within 72 hours. The ATSB's 24-hour line is {1800 011 034|one eight hundred, zero one one, zero three four}. Second, routine reportable matters go in writing within 72 hours. And third, defects also go in the maintenance release, and, where airworthiness is affected, to CASA.

[[diagram:accident-incident-reporting]]
Picture a decision chart. It starts by asking whether it was an accident: a person killed or seriously injured, serious damage or structural failure, or the aircraft missing or inaccessible. Accidents, serious incidents and the listed serious events branch one way: phone the ATSB as soon as practicable, then a written report within 72 hours. Other safety occurrences branch the other way: a written report within 72 hours.

The full list of the pilot's duties, including who reports when the pilot cannot, and preserving the site, is in the lesson on emergencies, accidents and incidents.

Let's work one: determining what must be reported. There are three occurrences. Classify each, then apply the reporting rule. Pause after each if you'd like to try it.

The first. Over the wheatbelt, the engine stops from fuel exhaustion on final at Corrigin, and you glide onto the runway. Nothing is damaged, and nobody is hurt.
[[pause 4]]
There is no accident outcome. But it is fuel exhaustion short of an accident, so it is a serious incident, and an immediately reportable matter. Phone the ATSB as soon as practicable, then the written report within 72 hours.

The second. Taxiing at Northam, your wing tip touches a hangar door. The tip fairing is cracked, and nothing else is damaged.
[[pause 4]]
Damage limited to a fairing is not an accident. It affected safety, so it is an incident. That means a written report within 72 hours, and the defect in the maintenance release.

The third. Departing Kalgoorlie, a passenger door pops open on climb. You return and land normally.
[[pause 4]]
No damage and no injury: an incident, with a written report within 72 hours.

The method is always the same. Outcome first, category second, reporting rule third.

Question. Why must an immediately reportable matter be phoned through, as well as written up?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: so the ATSB can start an investigation at once, while the wreckage, the weather records and the witnesses' memories are fresh. The written report within 72 hours adds the detail. The phone call buys time.

## SARTIME at PPL level

A SARTIME, a search and rescue time, is a time in UTC that you nominate as the time by which you will report your arrival. You lodge it with Airservices, through NAIPS, by phone, or by radio. From then on a system is waiting for you. And if the time passes without word from you, that system assumes you are in trouble.

The value of a SARTIME lies entirely in what it does when you cannot do anything. After a forced landing you may be injured, the radio may be damaged, and there may be no phone coverage for 100 km. None of that matters to the SARTIME. The clock keeps running, and when it runs out, the search starts.

[[diagram:sartime-timeline]]
Picture a timeline running from left to right. Before flight, you nominate a SARTIME in UTC, with a sensible margin after your planned arrival. If you are delayed, you extend it, by radio or phone, before it expires. On landing, you cancel it at once. But if the time passes without it being cancelled or amended, the alerting phases begin, ending in a physical search.

So which flights should carry a SARTIME? Anything over sparsely settled or inhospitable terrain. Long over-water legs. And flights where nobody else would know you were missing. In Western Australia that means most cross-country flying beyond the wheatbelt, the coast north of Geraldton, any crossing of the Great Southern towards Esperance, and the goldfields. The test is simple. If you went down halfway, who would notice, and when?

Then there is lodging and changing it. Before departure, you lodge it through NAIPS, or by phone to Airservices. In flight, you amend it by radio to the FIS, the Flight Information Service. If you are delayed, you extend it before it expires. And on arrival, you cancel it.

Choose the time with a sensible margin after your planned arrival. It should be enough to absorb a stronger headwind or a short delay on the ground without setting off a search. But not so much that the search starts hours after you went down. Every minute of margin is a minute you would wait for help.

Let's work one: planning and managing a SARTIME for a remote leg. You plan a private flight from Jandakot to Kalbarri, departing at {0800|oh eight hundred} WST, Western Standard Time. The planned flight time is 2 hours 50 minutes, and nobody will be waiting for you at Kalbarri. What SARTIME do you lodge? Pause if you'd like to try it first.
[[pause 8]]
Step one, the planned arrival. {0800|oh eight hundred} plus 2 hours 50 minutes gives {1050|ten fifty} WST.

Step two, add your margin. Here it is 30 minutes, which gives {1120|eleven twenty} WST.

Step three, convert to UTC. WST is UTC plus 8, which is covered in the lesson on time. So take away 8 hours: {1120|eleven twenty} WST is {0320|zero three twenty} UTC.

Step four, lodge a SARTIME of {0320|zero three twenty} UTC through NAIPS before departure.

And step five, on landing at Kalbarri, cancel it straight away by phone.

So the answer: a SARTIME of {0320|zero three twenty} UTC. The 30 minutes is your choice as pilot in command, not a regulatory figure. What the rules require is that you extend before expiry, and cancel on arrival.

Now try one yourself. On the same flight, low cloud closes in north of Geraldton. So you divert, and land at Geraldton at {1010|ten ten} WST. What do you do about the SARTIME, and why does it matter, even though you are safe?
[[pause 6]]
The answer: cancel it, or amend it if you will continue later, as soon as you land at Geraldton. Do it by phone, or by radio before shutdown. If you do not, then at {0320|zero three twenty} UTC, which is {1120|eleven twenty} WST, the system starts the alerting phases. It is looking for an aircraft it believes is somewhere between Geraldton and Kalbarri. Failing to cancel wastes search resources that may be needed by somebody who really is missing.

For a private VFR flight, the alternative is a flight note, left with a responsible person, who contacts SAR if you do not arrive. The choice is yours as pilot in command. But weigh it honestly. A flight note works only if that person understands the plan, remembers the time, and acts promptly. A SARTIME is held by a system that does not forget, and does not hesitate.

Question. Why is a SARTIME usually a better choice than a flight note for a flight over the goldfields?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because a SARTIME is held by Airservices, which starts the alerting phases automatically when it expires. A flight note relies on one person remembering and acting. Over remote country, any delay in starting the search is time without water and shade.

## How the search and rescue system responds

When a SARTIME expires uncancelled, the response is staged, so that the effort grows only as the evidence that you are in trouble grows. The system moves through three phases. The uncertainty phase. The alert phase. And the distress phase. The JRCC, the Joint Rescue Coordination Centre of the Australian Maritime Safety Authority, coordinates.

As the system moves through the phases, the actions escalate, in the order you met at recreational level. First, attempts to contact you: calls on frequency, and a phone call to your destination. Then a communications search, which widens the net to aerodromes and contacts along your route. Then a physical search, by aircraft and ground parties.

Each step is cheaper and quicker than the next. That is why the system tries them in that order. And it is why a pilot who simply forgot to cancel is usually found by the first one, at the cost of a lot of people's time.

[[diagram:sar-alerting-phases]]
Picture a staircase with three steps, each one higher than the last. The bottom step is the uncertainty phase, the middle step is the alert phase, and the top step is the distress phase. Beside them, the actions climb too: calls to you, then a communications search along your route, then a physical search. The effort grows only as the evidence grows, and the search starts from your notified route and time.

The phases explain why the margin and the route matter so much. The search starts from what you told the system: your route, and your time. A route that you flew but did not notify, or a SARTIME set hours too late, makes the search slower, longer, or in the wrong place.

## Being found: equipment and signals

A search is only as good as the chance of seeing you. What you carry turns a speck in the scrub into something a searcher can find.

At private pilot level, the exam expects you to know the kit. An ELT, an emergency locator transmitter. Water. A signalling mirror. And the ground-to-air visual signals in ERSA EMERG. Flights over designated remote areas must carry the survival equipment prescribed in the Part 91 Manual of Standards. You'll find that in the lesson on air service operations.

The standard advice after a forced landing is to stay with the aircraft. It is far easier to see from the air than a person on foot. It gives shade. And the search follows your notified route.

Then work through the priorities in order. First, protection: first aid and shelter. Second, location: help rescuers find you. Activate the ELT or the PLB, the personal locator beacon, try {121.5 MHz|one two one decimal five megahertz}, and lay out signals. Third, water. And only then, food.

[[diagram:survival-priorities]]
Picture the four priorities in a row: protection, location, water, then food. Beside them, the reminder to stay with the aircraft. And the ground-to-air signals, laid out large on the ground.

The ground-to-air signals are large shapes, laid out with whatever contrasts with the ground. A V means you require assistance. An X means you require medical assistance. N is no. Y is yes. And an arrow says you are proceeding in this direction. They work because a searcher overhead cannot hear you, but can read a large, high-contrast shape on the ground.

## Where the emergency procedures are

In flight, the emergency procedures are in the ERSA EMERG section, the pink pages. The full text is in the AIP: GEN 3.6 for search and rescue, and ENR 1.12 for interception. But ERSA is the one to answer with. It is the document you carry, laid out for use with one hand while you fly the aeroplane.

It holds the distress and urgency calls, the transponder codes, the radio failure procedures, interception, light signals, ditching and survival, and the SAR material, including the ground-to-air signals. How to use it in flight is the subject of the lesson on emergencies, accidents and incidents.

[[diagram:transponder-emergency-codes]]
Picture three transponder code windows side by side. {7700|seven seven zero zero} for an emergency. {7600|seven six zero zero} for radio failure. And {7500|seven five zero zero} for unlawful interference. All three are in ERSA EMERG, with the rest of the emergency procedures.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. The AIP contains the full text. But the question "which document contains the emergency procedures?" wants ERSA EMERG, the in-flight reference. Do not pick the AIP GEN section, the VFRG or the VTC legend when ERSA is offered.

## Bringing it together

It is a May weekend: Northam to Leonora and back. Nobody lives at the strip, and nobody is expecting you. So you lodge a SARTIME through NAIPS, in UTC, with a margin after your planned arrival. You put the ERSA, with its pink pages, in the seat pocket, along with water, the ELT and a signalling mirror.

Near Menzies, a stronger headwind than forecast eats into your margin. So you call the FIS, and extend the SARTIME before it expires.

Approaching Leonora, the radio fails. You squawk {7600|seven six zero zero}, from ERSA EMERG. You see another aeroplane on the ground at the holding point. So on final you switch your landing and navigation lights on and off repeatedly, and it holds clear.

On rollout, a tyre bursts. Nobody is hurt, and damage limited to a tyre is not an accident. So it is an incident: a written report to the ATSB within 72 hours, and the defect in the maintenance release.

But before any of that, with the radio dead, you borrow the other pilot's phone and cancel the SARTIME. Until you do, the system is counting down to the uncertainty phase, for an aeroplane that is sitting safely on the apron.

Question. In that flight, which two actions involving the SARTIME were essential, and why?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: extending it before it expired, when the headwind delayed you, and cancelling it on landing. Either omission would have set off the alerting phases, and a search for an aircraft that was not missing.

## Key points

Let's recap.

Lights. Navigation and landing lights used intermittently mean the aircraft is in difficulty and compelled to land, usually after a radio failure. Give way. Tell it apart from a single flash acknowledging a tower signal, and from the interception replies: regular means cannot comply, and irregular means in distress.

Accident or incident. An accident is death or serious injury, serious structural damage, or the aircraft missing, from boarding for flight to disembarking. Damage limited to the engine, propeller, wheels, tyres, brakes, fairings or small dents is not by itself an accident. An incident is anything else affecting safety. And a serious incident is a near-accident.

Reporting goes to the ATSB. Accidents and serious incidents: by phone as soon as practicable, on {1800 011 034|one eight hundred, zero one one, zero three four}, and in writing within 72 hours. Routine reportable matters: in writing within 72 hours. Defects go in the maintenance release, and to CASA where airworthiness is affected.

The SARTIME is a UTC time by which you will report your arrival. Lodge it with Airservices, through NAIPS or by phone before departure, or by radio to the FIS in flight. Extend it before expiry, and cancel it on arrival. Uncancelled, it starts the uncertainty, alert and distress phases, with the JRCC coordinating the search. Use one over remote country, on long over-water legs, and whenever nobody else would miss you. The alternative is a flight note. Carry an ELT, water and a mirror, stay with the aircraft, and know the ground-to-air signals.

And the emergency procedures are in ERSA EMERG, with the full text in AIP GEN 3.6 for search and rescue, and ENR 1.12 for interception.

A final exam tip. The same five facts as the recreational exam. Lights flashing means compelled to land. An accident needs death, serious injury or serious damage. The ATSB is told immediately, and in writing within 72 hours. The SARTIME is cancelled on arrival. And the procedures are in ERSA EMERG. At private pilot level, add the SAR phases, uncertainty, alert and distress, with the JRCC coordinating. Add how to lodge and amend a SARTIME. And add the regular and irregular light signals of an intercepted aircraft. Verify the reporting rules and the ATSB number against the current Transport Safety Investigation Regulations and ERSA.

That's the end of PFRA 2.9.
