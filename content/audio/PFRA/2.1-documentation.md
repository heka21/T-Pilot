This is PFRA 2.1, Documentation, at private pilot licence level. By the end you will know how to get the aviation publications and keep them current, which document answers which question, and how to read an ERSA entry, a NOTAM and an AIP Supplement, with the times converted. There are a few questions and worked examples along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (PFRA 2.1)

The exam wants four things from you. First, how you obtain the aviation publications, and why keeping them amended matters. Second, given a question that matters to an operation, picking the right reference and pulling the current answer out of it. Third, decoding what ERSA, a NOTAM and an AIP Supplement tell you. And fourth, the terms and abbreviations in the general part of the AIP that matter for a VFR flight.

## Why this matters

As an RPL holder, a recreational pilot, you learned which document holds which kind of rule. You'll find that in the recreational lesson on documentation. As a PPL holder, a private pilot, you will plan a flight from Jandakot to Kalbarri, or across the Wheatbelt to Kalgoorlie. You will pass through restricted areas, and past aerodromes you have never seen. And you will fly into places where the only information is an ERSA entry and a set of NOTAMs written in abbreviations. Nobody is standing beside you to translate.

The PPLA exam reflects that. It hands you extracts from the AIP, ERSA and NOTAM, and expects you to find the answer in them quickly and correctly, with times in UTC and abbreviations left undecoded. So this lesson is about using the documents, not just naming them. Getting them. Keeping them current. Finding your way around them. And reading what they say.

## Where the documents come from, and why they go out of date

Runways are resurfaced and shortened. Frequencies change. A control zone boundary moves. A new restricted area appears around a mine site. Every change has to reach every pilot at the same moment, or two pilots will be flying to different versions of the same airspace. The publishing system exists to solve that problem.

There are two publishers, and the split follows the line between law and information that you met at recreational level.

The first publisher handles the law. That is the Civil Aviation Act, the Civil Aviation Regulations, called CAR, the Civil Aviation Safety Regulations, called CASR, the Manuals of Standards, and the Civil Aviation Orders, the CAOs. All of it is published free, on the Federal Register of Legislation and on CASA's website. CASA also publishes the material that explains the law. There are the plain English guides. There are the Advisory Circulars, the ACs. And there are the older Civil Aviation Advisory Publications, the CAAPs, which are being replaced by ACs over time.

The second publisher handles the aeronautical information. That is the AIP Book, ERSA, the DAP, the charts, the AIP Supplements, and the Aeronautical Information Circulars, the AICs. All of it is published by Airservices Australia. You can buy it in print on subscription, with amendments delivered to you on the AIRAC cycle. Or you can download it free from the Airservices AIP website.

Two more sources are for the day of the flight. NOTAMs, the notices to airmen, and your pre-flight briefings come from NAIPS, the National Aeronautical Information Processing System, used online or through the app, and from the Airservices flight briefing services. And the weather comes from the Bureau of Meteorology aviation service, and through NAIPS.

[[diagram:aviation-law-hierarchy]]
Picture the documents as a staircase, stepping down from top to bottom. On the top step is the Civil Aviation Act. Below it come the regulations, CASR and CAR, then the Manuals of Standards made under the CASR Parts, then the remaining Civil Aviation Orders. All of that is law, from the Federal Register and CASA. Lower down sits the operational information from Airservices: the AIP, ERSA, the DAP, the charts and NOTAM. And at the base is the guidance from CASA, the Advisory Circulars and the Visual Flight Rules Guide, which is not law.

Now, why the amendments come on fixed days. If every amendment took effect the day it was written, a pilot could never be sure their charts were current. So the AIP is amended on the international AIRAC cycle. AIRAC stands for aeronautical information regulation and control. It is a fixed schedule of effective dates, every 28 days, with major amendments typically twice a year. The current pattern is set out in the general part of the AIP, so verify it. Because the dates are agreed internationally and published in advance, charts and procedures change on known days worldwide. Every pilot and every controller switches to the new information at the same moment.

So why is an out-of-date document dangerous? Because it is not a slightly worse document. It is a wrong one that looks right. It can give you five kinds of trouble. First, the wrong frequency, so your calls go to nobody. Second, the wrong airspace boundary, so you infringe a control zone while believing you are clear of it. Third, a runway that no longer exists at the length you planned on, or one that is now closed. Fourth, a changed procedure at an aerodrome you are joining. And fifth, in the exam room, lost marks, because the permitted materials must be current for the date of the exam.

Before each flight, then, there are four checks. Check that the AIP and ERSA you are using are current. Read the Supplements and the AICs that apply to your flight. And get the NOTAMs. The NOTAMs come last, because they are the newest layer. They carry what changed after the printed documents were issued.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. A current ERSA is not the end of the check. ERSA is only current to its effective date. Anything that changed since then, or anything temporary, is in a NOTAM or a Supplement. So an option that says "ERSA is current, so no further check is needed" is wrong.

Question. Why are AIP amendments made on fixed AIRAC dates, rather than as soon as each change is ready?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: so that every pilot and controller switches to the new information at the same, known moment. Changes on random dates would leave people working from different versions of the same airspace or procedure.

## Choosing the right reference

At recreational level, the question was: is this law, information or guidance? At private level the exam goes one step further. It names the specific document families: the CAR, the CAO, the AIP Book, and the CAAP, or its replacement, the AC. And it puts them alongside the CASR Parts you already know. The skill is the same. Work out what kind of fact you need, and the document follows.

[[diagram:where-to-find-it]]
Picture a column of pre-flight questions down the left, each with an arrow across to the document that answers it. VMC minima and fuel rules point to the Part 91 Manual of Standards, summarised in the VFRG. Aerodrome data, PRD areas and emergency procedures point to ERSA. Licence privileges point to CASR Part 61, and medicals to Part 67. The aircraft's serviceability points to the maintenance release. Temporary changes point to NOTAM. And airspace boundaries point to the charts: the VTC, the VNC and the ERC. Rules about you, rules about how you fly, facts about places, today's changes, and the aircraft's state, each have their own home.

Now each family in turn. The CASR holds most of the modern rules. Part 61 and the Part 61 Manual of Standards answer questions about your licence: privileges, recency and flight reviews. Part 67 answers questions about your medical. Part 91 and the Part 91 Manual of Standards answer questions about how you fly. That means the rules of the air, VMC, fuel, oxygen, minimum heights and the passenger rules. CASA's plain English guide to Part 91 makes it readable.

Next, the CAR {1988|nineteen eighty-eight}. This is the older set of regulations, still in force where the CASR has not replaced it. For a private pilot, its important corner has two pieces. Part 4A covers the maintenance release and daily inspections. And Schedule 8 lists the maintenance a pilot may carry out on their own aircraft. Verify the current text, because the maintenance rules are part of the move from the CAR to the CASR.

Then the Civil Aviation Orders. These are what remains of the orders made under the CAR. Few of them now touch private flying, so a CAO is rarely the answer to a private operations question. But it is still on the exam's list of documents.

The AIP Book is the operational handbook, and it comes in three parts. Knowing which part to open saves minutes in the exam. GEN, the general part, covers units, abbreviations, services, and how the AIP itself is organised and amended. ENR, en route, covers the rules as they apply in flight: airspace, altimetry, cruising levels, radio procedures, flight planning, navigation, charts, and search and rescue. And AD covers aerodromes: aerodrome information and standards.

ERSA is the book you carry. It has five sections. GEN, general. FAC, the facilities, which are the aerodrome entries. PRD, the prohibited, restricted and danger areas. EMERG, the emergency procedures. And NAV COMM, the navigation aids and communications.

AIP Supplements and NOTAMs carry temporary changes to all of the above.

And last, the guidance. The Advisory Circulars, with the AC 91 series for general operations, the remaining CAAPs, and the VFRG, the Visual Flight Rules Guide. They explain how to comply, but they are not the rule itself.

So, as a pattern. Questions about you go to Parts 61 and 67. Questions about how you fly go to Part 91 and its Manual of Standards. The maintenance release, daily inspections and pilot maintenance go to CAR Part 4A and Schedule 8. Airspace, altimetry, cruising levels, radio, flight planning and search and rescue go to the AIP Book. Aerodromes, PRD areas and emergency procedures go to ERSA. Temporary changes go to Supplements and NOTAMs. And guidance comes from the ACs, the CAAPs and the VFRG.

Let's work one. You are planning a private flight from Murrayfield to Geraldton in your syndicate's aeroplane, and four questions come up. For each, ask what kind of fact it is. Pause if you'd like to try them first.

The first question: what radio calls do I make when I enter and leave the Geraldton CTAF, the common traffic advisory frequency, and how is a flight plan or SARTIME lodged? The second: what is the runway length and surface at Geraldton, and is there fuel? The third: the aeroplane's oil filler cap is cracked, so may I, as the owner-pilot, replace it myself? And the fourth: is there anything happening at Geraldton this week, such as works or a closed taxiway?
[[pause 6]]
Here are the answers. The first is about radio procedures and flight planning, which are en route rules. So it is the AIP Book, the ENR part. The second is a fact about a place, so it is ERSA, the FAC entry. The third is maintenance a pilot may do. That is CAR {1988|nineteen eighty-eight} Schedule 8, read with Part 4A for how the work is recorded on the maintenance release. And the fourth is a temporary change, so it is a NOTAM, or a Supplement if it is a long, planned change. Notice that none of the four answers is the VFRG. It explains several of them, but none is set out there.

Choosing the document is only half the skill. The other half is extracting the current information, and the method is the same every time. There are four steps. First, identify the kind of fact, and so the document and the part of it. AIP ENR, for example, or ERSA FAC, or ERSA PRD. Second, find the entry, using the contents and the index. In ERSA the aerodromes are alphabetical. In the AIP each section is numbered, such as ENR 1.1 or ENR 5.1. Third, read all of it, including the notes and remarks, because the conditions and exceptions live there. And fourth, check for later changes: a Supplement or a NOTAM that alters what you have just read.

[[diagram:flight-documents-flow]]
Picture a restricted area on your route, with arrows to the documents that each give part of the answer. Runway data comes from the ERSA FAC entry. For the restricted area, the VNC, the VTC or the ERC low shows its identifier and its vertical limits. The ERSA PRD section and AIP ENR 5.1 give its hours, its controlling authority and its conditional status. NOTAMs can activate it or change it. And when in doubt, you call the controlling authority. No single document gives the whole answer.

Question. Where would you look for the rule that sets the fuel you must carry? And where for the fuel available at the aerodrome you are flying to?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the rule is in CASR Part 91 and the Part 91 Manual of Standards, because it is a rule about how you fly. The fuel available is a fact about a place, so it is in the aerodrome's ERSA FAC entry, subject to any NOTAM.

## Reading an ERSA entry

Everything you need before landing at an aerodrome you have never seen is in its ERSA FAC entry. The runway length and surface, the frequency, the fuel, and whether you need permission. The entry always runs in the same order. Once you know the order, you can find any item in seconds.

[[diagram:ersa-entry-anatomy]]
Picture a schematic entry for an invented aerodrome, with a label on each block. The header sits at the top, the runway table below it, then the communications and navigation aids, and the remarks and local traffic regulations at the bottom.

In full, the order has nine items. First, the header: the name, identifier, position, elevation, magnetic variation, time zone and operator. Second, the operational information. That includes PPR, prior permission required, which means you must contact the operator before you arrive. Third, the runway table: designator, length and width in metres, surface, slope, lighting and pavement strength. Fourth, the aerodrome diagram. Fifth, obstacles. Sixth, communications and navigation aids, including the CTAF. Seventh, weather services. That is either the AWIS, the automatic weather information service, or the ATIS, the automatic terminal information service. Eighth, local traffic regulations and noise abatement. And ninth, the remarks.

Some abbreviations turn up in nearly every entry. CTAF you know. PAL is pilot activated lighting, which you switch on by keying your microphone on a published frequency. RWS is the runway strip, the cleared area around the runway itself. PCN is the pavement classification number, the runway's published strength. Then AVGAS and AVTUR availability: AVGAS for piston engines, AVTUR for turbines. HJ means sunrise to sunset. And H24 means continuous.

Let's work one. Here is an invented entry for a Wheatbelt aerodrome. It is not a real one. The elevation is 1,050 ft. Runway {05/23|zero five, two three} is 1,250 m long and 18 m wide, sealed, with PAL. Runway {15/33|one five, three three} is 820 m, with a natural surface. There is a CTAF, and AVGAS is available. The remarks say: PPR for aircraft above 5,700 kg, and kangaroos on and in the vicinity of the runway at night. That last one is written H N.

Four questions. Can you land there in a light aeroplane without phoning ahead? What circuit height do you fly? Can you use the runway at night? And when is the kangaroo hazard worst? Pause if you'd like to try them.
[[pause 6]]
First, phoning ahead. PPR applies only above 5,700 kg in this entry, so no call is required for you. A courtesy call is always good practice, though.

Second, the circuit height. The standard is 1,000 ft above aerodrome level. The elevation is 1,050 ft, so 1,050 plus 1,000 gives 2,050 ft on the QNH.

Third, night use. Runway {05/23|zero five, two three} has PAL, so its lights can be activated. Runway {15/33|one five, three three} has none listed.

And fourth, the kangaroos. HN means the hours of night, sunset to sunrise. That is when the hazard is worst.

So the answers again: no call needed below 5,700 kg, a circuit at 2,050 ft, night use on {05/23|zero five, two three} only, and kangaroos at night. Every one came from reading every line, including the remarks. That is where the PPR condition and the wildlife warning were.

Question. An ERSA entry gives a runway as 1,100 m long. Is that in feet or metres? And where else in the entry is height given in feet?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: runway length and width in ERSA are in metres. The elevation in the header, and obstacle heights, are in feet. That is why a quick read can mix them up.

## Decoding a NOTAM

A NOTAM, a notice to airmen, is how a change reaches you between the printed issues. A runway closed for works this week. A navigation aid unserviceable. A restricted area activated for a military exercise. A crane near the approach. There may be dozens on your route, so they are written in a fixed, compressed format, and you have to unpack it.

Each NOTAM carries four things, in order. First, the identifier and series, so it can be referred to and later cancelled. Second, the location, as an ICAO four-letter code. ICAO is the International Civil Aviation Organization, and YPJT, for example, is Jandakot. Third, the validity period, in UTC, Coordinated Universal Time. B is the time it takes effect, and C is the time it ends. Each is written as a ten-figure date-time group: year, month, day, hour and minute. C may say PERM, permanent, meaning until the documents are amended. Or it may carry EST, estimated, meaning the end time may change, and a further NOTAM will confirm it. And fourth, the subject and the text, in abbreviations from AIP GEN 2.2.

The common content is runway or taxiway closures, unserviceable lighting or navigation aids, airspace activations, temporary restricted areas, cranes and obstacles, aerodrome works, and changed frequencies. Head office NOTAMs cover a whole flight information region, rather than one aerodrome. In NAIPS, a location or route briefing lists the NOTAMs relevant to your flight. Read them all, and convert every validity time.

[[diagram:notam-anatomy]]
Picture the NOTAM from the worked example coming up, for Geraldton, with each part labelled. First the identifier and series. Then the location, {YGEL|Y G E L}, the four-letter code for Geraldton. Then the B and C times as ten-figure UTC groups. And then the text, in abbreviations. Under it runs a timeline in local time, with the closure marked as a bar. A planned arrival at {1600|sixteen hundred} WST on the 15th falls inside the bar, so it meets a closed runway.

[[diagram:utc-and-wst-strip]]
Now picture two time strips, one above the other, lined up so that the same instant sits in the same column. The top strip is UTC and the bottom one is WST, Western Standard Time. WST is UTC plus 8 hours, so the WST date changes when UTC reaches {1600|sixteen hundred}. Two examples sit on the strips. {1530|Fifteen thirty} WST is {0730|oh seven thirty} UTC on the same day. And {0545|oh five forty-five} WST on Tuesday 7 October is {2145|twenty-one forty-five} UTC on Monday 6 October. An early-morning WST time is the previous day in UTC.

The conversion is the part that catches people. Every aviation time is UTC unless stated otherwise. Western Australia is UTC plus 8 hours, and does not use daylight saving. So you add 8 hours to a UTC time to get WST. And when the sum passes {2400|twenty-four hundred}, the WST date moves to the next day.

Let's work one. An invented NOTAM for Geraldton reads: {RWY 03/21 CLSD DUE WIP|R W Y zero three two one, C L S D, due, W I P}. Then {B 2611140100|B, two six, one one, one four, zero one zero zero}. And {C 2611150930|C, two six, one one, one five, zero nine three zero}. When does it apply in local time? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
Step one, the text. Runway {03/21|zero three, two one} is closed due to work in progress.

Step two, the B group. Two six is the year {2026|twenty twenty-six}. One one is November. One four is the 14th. And the last four figures are {0100|oh one hundred} UTC.

Step three, convert it. {0100|Oh one hundred} plus 8 hours is {0900|oh nine hundred} WST on 14 November.

Step four, the C group. It means 15 November, at {0930|oh nine thirty} UTC.

Step five, convert it. {0930|Oh nine thirty} plus 8 hours is {1730|seventeen thirty} WST on 15 November.

So the runway is closed from {0900|oh nine hundred} WST on the 14th to {1730|seventeen thirty} WST on the 15th. A planned arrival at {1600|sixteen hundred} WST on the 15th meets a closed runway.

Now try one that crosses midnight. A NOTAM for a danger area reads {B 2612022130|B, two six, one two, zero two, two one three zero}, and {C 2612030500|C, two six, one two, zero three, zero five zero zero}. When is it active in WST?
[[pause 6]]
Here is the solution. B is 2 December, at {2130|twenty-one thirty} UTC. Adding 8 hours gives {2930|twenty-nine thirty}, which is {0530|oh five thirty} on the next day. So it starts at {0530|oh five thirty} WST on 3 December. C is 3 December at {0500|oh five hundred} UTC. Plus 8 hours, that is {1300|thirteen hundred} WST on 3 December. So the area is active from {0530|oh five thirty} to {1300|thirteen hundred} WST on 3 December. A morning activity, even though the B time is printed with the 2nd.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set: reading the NOTAM date as the local date. An early-morning WST start appears in UTC as the evening of the previous day. So convert the whole date-time group, not just the hours.

## AIP Supplements and Circulars

Some changes are too big for a NOTAM. An air show that needs a temporary airspace arrangement and a page of procedures. A runway works program that runs for months, with staged closures. A trial of a new procedure. These go in an AIP Supplement, which pilots shorten to SUP. A Supplement carries a temporary or advance change to the AIP that is too long for a NOTAM. Supplements are numbered by year, for example {H23/26|H twenty-three stroke twenty-six}. The current ones are listed in a checklist, so you can see which are in force. A Supplement is part of the AIP, and it is permitted in the exam with it.

An Aeronautical Information Circular, an AIC, is different in kind. It carries explanatory and advance information that does not change the AIP itself. Background to a coming change, for example, or a safety message.

Here is a simple way to place the three. The AIP and ERSA are the base layer. A Supplement is a long overlay, temporary or in advance. And a NOTAM is a short or urgent overlay, and the newest one you read.

Question. A two-week military exercise needs temporary restricted areas, and several pages of procedures. Would you expect it in a NOTAM or a Supplement?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: a Supplement, because the change is too long and detailed for a NOTAM. A NOTAM may still be issued to activate the areas, or to draw attention to the Supplement.

## AIP GEN terms and abbreviations

AIP GEN 2.2 is the abbreviation list, and GEN 2.1 holds the units and conventions. You do not need all of them. You need the ones a VFR pilot meets in ERSA, NOTAMs and forecasts. They fall into groups, and learning them by group is easier than learning a list. There are six groups.

The first group is heights and altimetry. AGL and AMSL say what a height is measured from: above ground level, or above mean sea level. HGT is height, above a stated datum, often the ground. ALT is altitude, above mean sea level. QNH is the altimeter setting that makes the altimeter read altitude. QFE is the setting that makes it read height above the aerodrome. QNE refers to the standard setting, {1013|ten thirteen}. And FL, a flight level, is pressure altitude in hundreds of feet, with {1013|ten thirteen} set. The altimetry itself is in the recreational lesson on conditions of flight.

The second group is airspace. CTA is a control area. CTR is a control zone, controlled airspace from the surface around an aerodrome. CTAF is the common traffic advisory frequency. And OCTA is outside controlled airspace. FIR, FIA and FIS are the flight information region, area and service. PRD is prohibited, restricted and danger. And RA1, RA2 and RA3 are the conditional status levels of a restricted area. LL and UL are the lower and upper limits of a piece of airspace. MSA is minimum sector altitude, and LSALT is lowest safe altitude.

The third group is flight rules and conditions. VMC and IMC are visual and instrument meteorological conditions. VFR and IFR are the visual and instrument flight rules.

The fourth group is times. UTC is Coordinated Universal Time, and all aviation times are UTC unless stated. ETA and ETD are the estimated times of arrival and departure. EET is estimated elapsed time. And ATD is actual time of departure. HJ is sunrise to sunset. HN is night. H24 is continuous. And HO is hours of operation.

The fifth group is search and rescue. SARTIME is the search and rescue time you nominate. SAR is search and rescue. And JRCC is the Joint Rescue Coordination Centre.

The sixth group is the information products and services. NOTAM, SUP, AIC, PPR, PAL, AWIS and ATIS you have met already in this lesson. The forecast and report products, the TAF, the METAR, the GAF and the rest, are taught in the meteorology lessons.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. HJ and HN are easy to swap. J is for "jour", the French for day, so HJ is sunrise to sunset. HN is the night hours. And an altitude is not a height. An altitude, ALT, is on QNH. A height, HGT, is above ground. So a 1,500 ft obstacle near an aerodrome at 1,050 ft elevation sticks up only 450 ft.

Question. An ERSA remark says a restricted area is RA2, and active H24. What does each part tell you?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: RA2 is the restricted area's conditional status level, which tells you how likely a clearance through it is. H24 means it is active continuously, day and night, unless a NOTAM changes that.

## Bringing it together

It is a Friday evening at home, and you are planning Saturday's flight from Jandakot to Kalbarri.

First, the currency check. Your ERSA's effective date is the latest one on the Airservices site, so it is current. In the AIP ENR you confirm how to lodge your SARTIME. The ERSA FAC entry for Kalbarri gives you the runway in metres, the surface, the CTAF, and whether AVGAS is available. And you read the remarks, for PPR and wildlife.

Your track passes near a restricted area. The VNC shows its limits, and the ERSA PRD section gives its hours and controlling authority.

Then NAIPS. A NOTAM activates that restricted area from {B 2611212200|B, two six, one one, two one, two two zero zero} to {C 2611220400|C, two six, one one, two two, zero four zero zero}. Adding 8 hours puts that at {0600|oh six hundred} to {1200|twelve hundred} WST on Saturday the 22nd. That is right across your planned {0830|oh eight thirty} departure, so you route around it.

A Supplement lists an air show at Geraldton next month. Not your problem this weekend. Five documents, one flight, and each one answers a question the others could not.

Question. In that story, why was the NOTAM the last thing checked, rather than the first?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because it is the newest layer, and it can change anything in the printed documents. Reading the base documents first tells you what normally applies. The NOTAMs then tell you what is different today.

## Key points

Let's recap.

Getting the documents. The law, meaning the Act, the CAR, the CASR, the Manuals of Standards and the CAOs, is published free on the Federal Register of Legislation and CASA's site, along with the ACs and CAAPs. The AIP Book, ERSA, the DAP, the charts, the Supplements and the AICs come from Airservices, in print by subscription or free online. NOTAMs and briefings come from NAIPS, and weather from the Bureau. The AIP is amended on the 28-day AIRAC cycle, so changes take effect on known dates worldwide. Out-of-date documents give wrong frequencies, wrong boundaries and wrong runways, and the permitted exam materials must be current.

Choosing the reference. Licence: Part 61. Medical: Part 67. How you fly: Part 91 and its Manual of Standards. The maintenance release and pilot maintenance: CAR Part 4A and Schedule 8. Airspace, altimetry, radio, flight planning and search and rescue: the AIP Book, in its GEN, ENR and AD parts. Places, PRD areas and emergencies: ERSA. Temporary changes: the Supplement and the NOTAM. Guidance: the AC, the CAAP and the VFRG. Read the whole entry, then check for later changes.

Decoding. An ERSA FAC entry runs header, PPR, runway table in metres, diagram, obstacles, comms and nav aids, weather, local rules, and remarks. A NOTAM gives the identifier, the ICAO location, the B and C times in UTC, which may be PERM or EST, and the text in abbreviations. WST is UTC plus 8. A Supplement is a long temporary or advance change, numbered by year, part of the AIP, and permitted in the exam.

Abbreviations. AIP GEN 2.2 is the abbreviation list. Know the height, airspace, time, search and rescue and service abbreviations. HJ is day, HN is night, H24 is continuous, and all times are UTC unless stated.

A final exam tip. The PPLA exam gives you AIP, ERSA and NOTAM extracts, and expects you to navigate them quickly. Know where each kind of information lives. In the AIP: GEN, ENR and AD. In ERSA: GEN, FAC, PRD, EMERG and NAV COMM. Read NOTAM validity times as UTC, and add 8 hours for WST, watching for the date change. Remember that an AIP Supplement is part of the AIP, and permitted in the exam. And when a question asks where a rule is set out, the answer is the regulation or the Manual of Standards, not the guidance that explains it.

That's the end of PFRA 2.1.
