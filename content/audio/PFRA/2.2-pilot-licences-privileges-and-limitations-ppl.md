This is PFRA 2.2, Pilot licences, privileges and limitations, for the private pilot licence. By the end you will know what the private licence lets you do and where it stops, what keeps you current to use it, how every flight is classified, and what the flight and duty time rules mean for a private pilot. There are worked examples and questions along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (PFRA 2.2)

The exam wants two things from you. First, for the private licence with the aeroplane category rating, three pieces: what the licence lets you do and where it stops, the recent experience you need to use it, and how flights are classified into types of operation. And second, applying the flight and duty time rules as they affect a private pilot.

## Why this matters

The private pilot licence, the PPL, is the licence that lets you go places. Jandakot to Esperance with three friends. A weekend at Monkey Mia. A business trip to Kalgoorlie in your own aeroplane, through the Perth control zones without a second thought. The recreational licence's fences of weight, distance and airspace fall away.

What remains are the fences that matter most, because they are the ones private pilots cross without meaning to. There are three of them. Carrying passengers at night without recent night landings. Flying a type of aeroplane with a feature they have never been trained on. Or accepting money for a flight, which turns it from private into commercial.

The exam asks this subtopic in two ways: as "may this pilot do this?" scenarios, and as recall of a few figures. This lesson builds on the recreational lesson on licence privileges and limitations, and teaches what changes at private level.

## How a licence is built

Before the privileges make sense, you need the structure they sit on. That is because "may I fly this?" is answered by several layers at once. Picture them stacked, from the top down.

At the top is the licence. It says what kind of pilot you are, and in what kind of operation: recreational, private, commercial or airline transport. Each licence carries a category rating, which is the kind of aircraft: aeroplane, helicopter and so on. This lesson is about the {PPL(A)|P P L eigh}, the private licence with the aeroplane category rating.

Under the category sit the class ratings and the type ratings, which say which aeroplanes you may fly. Most light aeroplanes are flown on a class rating. That is either the single-engine aeroplane class rating, or the multi-engine aeroplane class rating. Larger or more complex aeroplanes need their own type rating. The rule of thumb is aircraft over 5,700 kg, or turbojets. That is in CASR Part 61, the licensing part of the regulations, so verify the current text.

Next come the design feature endorsements. There is one for each feature that changes how an aeroplane is flown, and that your basic training did not cover. There are six: tailwheel, retractable undercarriage, manual propeller pitch control, pressurisation, floats, and gas turbine. If the aeroplane has the feature, you need the endorsement.

Then the operational ratings, which extend the conditions you may fly in. A private pilot meets two: the night VFR rating, and the instrument rating. And last, the activity endorsements: aerobatics, formation, spinning and others.

To fly a particular aeroplane in a particular way, every layer has to say yes. The licence permits the operation. The class or type rating covers the aeroplane. Each design feature on it is endorsed. The conditions are within your ratings. And any special activity has its endorsement.

## What the PPL lets you do, and where it stops

Start from what you know. The RPL, the recreational licence, is built for flying close to home in light aeroplanes. Most of its limits are lifted by endorsements on the RPL itself. But a few limits belong to the RPL, and no endorsement removes them. Those are four: single-engine, not more than 1,500 kg, private operations, and day VFR.

[[diagram:rpl-privileges-map]]
Picture the bare recreational licence on the left, with its fences. Single-engine, single-pilot, not more than 1,500 kg maximum take-off weight, day VFR, private or training flights, within 25 nm of the departure aerodrome, non-controlled aerodromes and Class G airspace, below 10,000 ft, and passengers allowed. On the right are the endorsements that lift each limit: recreational navigation, controlled aerodrome, controlled airspace, flight above 10,000 ft, design features and aerobatics. The point for you is that the PPL removes the 1,500 kg, 25 nm and airspace limits, while private operations stay.

So the PPL removes the weight, distance and airspace fences. The holder of a PPL with an aeroplane category rating may fly as pilot in command or co-pilot of an aeroplane in a private operation. They may also fly as pilot in command in flight training of certain kinds. And they do it without the weight, distance or airspace limits of the RPL. Verify the current text of Part 61.

Compared with the RPL, there are seven things to know about a PPL holder.

First, they may carry passengers anywhere in Australia, and overseas with the agreement of the country concerned. That is in any aeroplane within their class or type rating and design feature endorsements.

Second, they may fly in all classes of airspace, at controlled aerodromes, and above 10,000 ft, subject to the oxygen rules. The controlled airspace procedures are part of PPL training, rather than separate endorsements.

Third, they may fly at night only with the night VFR rating, and in IMC, the instrument meteorological conditions, only with an instrument rating.

Fourth, they fly under VFR by day by default. Aerobatics, formation, spinning and other activities need the relevant endorsement.

Fifth, they fly single-engine aeroplanes on the single-engine aeroplane class rating. Multi-engine aeroplanes need a class rating and endorsement. And aircraft over 5,700 kg, or turbojets, need a type rating.

Sixth, they must hold the design feature endorsements where the aeroplane has the feature: tailwheel, retractable undercarriage, manual propeller pitch control, pressurisation, floats or gas turbine.

And seventh, they must not fly for hire or reward, or in any operation that needs a commercial licence. The one exception is the cost sharing the regulations allow.

That last limit is the important one. The PPL is still a private licence. Its whole basis is that nobody is paying you to fly. Why should that matter to safety? A commercial pilot's passengers have bought a service. They assume the pilot, the aeroplane and the operator meet a commercial standard, and they cannot judge the risk for themselves. A private pilot's passengers are friends or family, who know they are flying with a private pilot. The higher training, medical and maintenance standards of commercial flying exist to protect people who are paying, and cannot see the risk.

Now, cost sharing. The line between private and commercial runs through money, so the regulations draw it precisely. A PPL holder may share the direct costs of a private flight equally with the passengers, with no profit. Direct costs means things like fuel, landing fees and aircraft hire. The conditions are in the Part 91 rules, so verify the current cost-sharing conditions. Anything beyond that makes it a commercial operation, and a PPL is not enough. That includes a passenger paying more than an equal share, a payment for your time, or a profit.

The logic is this. An equal share means the pilot is also paying. So the pilot is flying for their own purposes, and simply not paying for everyone else's seat. Once the pilot is paid, the flight exists for the passenger's purposes, and that is a service.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. "No profit" is not the whole test. The share must also be equal, and only direct costs can be shared. A pilot who flies free while the passengers split the whole bill is not paying an equal share, even though nobody made a profit.

Next, the medical behind the licence. A PPL is used with a Class 2 medical certificate, or with a Basic Class 2 and its limits. A RAMPC, the recreational aviation medical practitioner's certificate, supports only RPL-level privileges. In the recreational lesson you met the principle: the easier the medical is to get, the tighter its limits.

The Basic Class 2 is issued by a GP, to the commercial driver standard. It limits you in five ways: private operations, day VFR, below 10,000 ft, piston aircraft up to 8,618 kg, and no more than 5 passengers. Verify the current limits on CASA's Basic Class 2 page. The licence limit and the medical limit stack, so you are bound by whichever is tighter.

[[diagram:rpl-medical-certificates]]
Picture three cards side by side. On the left, the Class 2, issued by a DAME, a designated aviation medical examiner, together with CASA. It is the standard private pilot medical, with no extra operational limits, and it supports the full PPL. In the middle, the Basic Class 2, from any GP to the commercial driver standard: private, day VFR, below 10,000 ft, piston aircraft up to 8,618 kg, and no more than 5 passengers. On the right, the RAMPC, from any GP to the private driver standard: single-engine piston up to 1,500 kg, day VFR, below 10,000 ft, no more than 1 passenger, no aerobatics, private operations, and RPL privileges only.

That stacking matters for a PPL holder in a way it did not for an RPL holder. A PPL holder with a night VFR rating, on a Basic Class 2, cannot use the night rating, because the medical keeps them in day VFR. And a PPL holder planning a flight at 11,500 ft over the Great Victoria Desert cannot do it on a Basic Class 2, which keeps them below 10,000 ft.

Let's work one: may this pilot fly this aeroplane? Mei holds a {PPL(A)|P P L eigh} with the single-engine aeroplane class rating. She holds two design feature endorsements, retractable undercarriage and manual propeller pitch control, and a Class 2 medical. A syndicate member offers her a four-seat single-engine aeroplane with a retractable undercarriage, a constant-speed propeller and a tailwheel. The flight is a day VFR private flight from Jandakot to Albany, through the Perth control zone. May she fly it? Pause if you'd like to try it first.
[[pause 6]]
Take the layers one at a time. First, the licence and the operation: a PPL, on a private flight. Allowed.

Second, the class rating: single-engine aeroplane. Covered.

Third, the design features. The retractable undercarriage is endorsed. The constant-speed propeller, which is manual propeller pitch control, is endorsed. But the tailwheel is not endorsed.

Fourth, the airspace and distance. The PPL has no airspace or distance fences. Allowed.

And fifth, the conditions and medical: day VFR, on a Class 2. Allowed.

So one layer says no, and it is the tailwheel. Mei may not fly this aeroplane as pilot in command until she has the tailwheel endorsement, however experienced she is on the other two features.

Question. A PPL holder with every design feature endorsement wants to fly a 6,500 kg turbojet. What else must they hold?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: a type rating for that aircraft. Design feature endorsements cover features of aeroplanes flown on a class rating. Aircraft over 5,700 kg, or turbojets, are flown on a type rating, which needs its own training and test.

## Recent experience: staying current

A licence does not expire, but skill does. The recent experience rules match each kind of fade to a time window. You met the logic at recreational level. Take-off and landing feel fades over weeks. General handling fades over years. The PPL keeps the same rules, and adds one for the night.

There are five rules to know. First, passengers by day. You need at least 3 take-offs and 3 landings in the previous 90 days, in the same category of aircraft.

Second, passengers at night. You need 3 take-offs and 3 landings at night in the previous 90 days, in addition to the night VFR rating.

Third, the flight review. You need an aeroplane flight review within the previous 24 months. And a flight test for a licence or a rating counts as a flight review for that category.

Fourth, the medical certificate: a Class 2, or a Basic Class 2 with its limits.

And fifth, general competency. You must not fly an aircraft unless you are competent to do so. That covers currency on the type and in the activity, whatever the formal rules say.

[[diagram:rpl-recency-timeline]]
Picture two look-back windows, both ending today on the right. The short one reaches back 90 days. To carry a passenger, you need at least 3 take-offs and 3 landings inside it, in the same category of aircraft. The long one reaches back 24 months. To fly as pilot in command at all, you need an aeroplane flight review inside it, and the review is valid to the end of the 24th month after it was done. The PPL adds a night version of the 90-day window.

Why a separate night rule? A night take-off is flown on instruments within seconds of lift-off, because the horizon disappears. A night landing is judged from runway lights and a sloping row of approach lights, rather than a whole visible landscape. Those skills fade independently of your daylight flying. A pilot who flies every weekend by day, but has not landed at night since autumn, has fresh day skills and stale night ones. And the passenger in the back is relying on the night ones.

The general competency rule sits behind all of this. The exam likes it, because it overrides the arithmetic. Meeting 3 landings in 90 days in a Cessna 172 does not make you competent in a tailwheel aeroplane you last flew three years ago, even though you hold the endorsement. The formal rules set a minimum. Competency is the real test.

Let's work one: is this pilot current to take passengers tonight? Today is 3 October. Ravi holds a PPL with a night VFR rating and a Class 2 medical. His flight review was done in March last year. His logbook shows three entries. On 14 June, three night take-offs and landings at Jandakot. On 30 August, two day take-offs and landings. And on 26 September, two more day take-offs and landings. He wants to fly two friends on a night scenic flight this evening. Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
Step one, the flight review. March last year is within 24 months. Current.

Step two, the window. The 90 days before 3 October reach back to early July. So the 14 June night landings do not count.

Step three, day passengers. 30 August and 26 September give 4 take-offs and 4 landings. So Ravi could take his friends by day.

Step four, night passengers. There are no night take-offs or landings in the window. Not current.

So Ravi may not carry passengers tonight. He can fly 3 night take-offs and landings solo first, and then take his friends. Or he can take them by day. The answer again: current by day, not current at night.

Question. A PPL holder passed the night VFR rating flight test 20 months after their last flight review. When is their next flight review due?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the flight test counts as a flight review for that category. So the 24-month window restarts from the flight test, not from the earlier review.

## Classification of operations

Every flight falls into a class of operation. The class decides which set of rules applies: who may be pilot in command, what licence and medical they need, how the aeroplane must be maintained, and whether an operator's certificate is involved. The classes are graded by how much the people on board rely on someone else for their safety. There are four.

The first is private operations, flown under CASR Part 91 alone. These are flights for your own purposes. Cost-shared flights within the rules. Flying your own or a hired aeroplane for pleasure or for business, without hire or reward. The pilot flies alone, or with people who know they are flying with a private pilot. "For business" is allowed. Flying yourself to a meeting is a private flight, provided nobody pays you to fly. A PPL may be pilot in command, and an RPL for the narrower RPL privileges.

The second is flight training, conducted under Parts 141 and 142, by an organisation that holds the approval. That covers dual training, solo training flights and flight reviews. Instructors fly as pilot in command, and students fly under their supervision.

The third is aerial work, under Part 138. This is flying in which the aeroplane is the tool for a task: survey, photography, agriculture, towing, dropping. When it is done for reward, it needs a commercial licence, the CPL, and the operational rules in Part 138. A PPL may do only the specified non-commercial aerial work the rules allow.

And the fourth is air transport, under Parts 119, 121, 133 and 135. This is carrying passengers or cargo for hire or reward: charter and airline flying. It needs a CPL or an ATPL, the airline transport pilot licence, under an air operator's certificate. Verify the current text of all of these Parts, and CASA's plain English guide on classification of operations.

The practical test is a single question. Is anyone paying for the flight beyond equal cost sharing? If so, it is not a private operation, and a PPL is not enough.

[[diagram:operations-classification]]
Picture the four classes stacked like the floors of a building, graded by how much the people on board rely on someone else for their safety. On the ground floor are private operations, under Part 91 only, where a PPL may be pilot in command. Above that is flight training, under Parts 141 and 142. Then aerial work, under Part 138, with a CPL. And at the top is air transport, under Parts 119, 121, 133 and 135, with a CPL or ATPL under an air operator's certificate. Underneath the stack sits the one-question test. The moment someone pays beyond an equal share of costs, the flight is no longer private, and a PPL is not enough.

Let's work one: classifying four flights. For each, ask who benefits, and whether anyone is paying beyond an equal share of direct costs. Pause if you'd like to try them first.

The first flight: you fly your own aeroplane from Jandakot to a client meeting in Merredin, and your company reimburses nothing. The second: a neighbouring farmer offers you 400 dollars to photograph his paddocks from the air for an insurance claim. The third: a mining company asks you to fly two of its geologists to Leonora, paying you a fee plus fuel. And the fourth: two club members and you fly to Rottnest for lunch, and split the hire, fuel and landing fees three ways.
[[pause 6]]
The first is a private operation. You fly for your own purposes, and nobody pays you to fly. A PPL is enough.

The second is aerial work for reward. The aeroplane is the tool for a task, and you are paid. A PPL is not enough.

The third is air transport. Passengers are carried for hire or reward. It needs a CPL and an air operator's certificate.

And the fourth is a private operation, within the cost-sharing rules. It is equal sharing of direct costs, with no profit.

So flights two and three fail on the same point. Someone is paying the pilot to fly.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. "I'm not making a profit, I'm just covering my hours" is still hire or reward, if the passenger pays more than an equal share, or pays for the pilot's seat. The test is equal sharing of direct costs, not whether the pilot comes out ahead.

Question. A friend offers to pay the whole cost of the aeroplane hire if you fly her to Busselton for a wedding. Is that private or not, and why?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: it is not a private operation within the cost-sharing rules. She would be paying more than an equal share, and you would be flying at no cost, for her purposes. That is carriage for reward, which a PPL does not permit.

## Flight and duty time for a PPL holder

Fatigue kills pilots as surely as weather does. Commercial aviation manages it with prescribed limits on how long a pilot may fly and be on duty. Those limits are in CAO 48.1, the Civil Aviation Order on fatigue management. And they apply to the crew of commercial operations.

For a private operation, there are no prescribed flight and duty time limits. That does not mean you may fly as tired as you like. The PPL holder is still bound by the general rules on fitness for flight. You must not fly while fatigued to the extent that it affects safety. How to recognise and manage fatigue is covered in the human factors lessons.

Why no prescribed limits for private flying? A commercial pilot's hours are set by a roster that someone else writes, under pressure to keep aircraft flying. So the law sets a ceiling. A private pilot chooses when to fly. So the law leaves the judgement with them, backed by the general obligation to be fit.

The picture changes when a PPL holder also flies in a commercial or training operation. For those flights, they are subject to that operator's fatigue rules. And the hours flown privately count towards the cumulative limits in the operator's system. Verify the current text of CAO 48.1. Picture a student working towards a commercial licence, who flies privately on weekends and trains with a school during the week. That student has to declare the private hours, because fatigue does not care which logbook column the hours went in.

So if the exam asks for the rules on flight and duty time limits for private licence holders, here is the answer. CAO 48.1 does not impose limits on private operations. But pilots must still be fit for flight, and must comply with any operator or training organisation limits that apply to them.

Let's work one: may she fly this afternoon? Dana is a PPL holder and an emergency department nurse. She finished a 12-hour night shift at {0730|oh seven thirty}, and has slept three hours. She plans to fly her own aeroplane from Murrayfield to Cervantes at {1400|fourteen hundred}, on a private flight. Pause if you'd like to decide first.
[[pause 6]]
Step one, the prescribed flight and duty limits. None apply to a private operation, so no rule on hours stops her.

Step two, fitness for flight. After a night shift and three hours' sleep, she is very likely fatigued to an extent that affects safety. So the general fitness rule says do not fly.

Step three, a variation. If she were also a student in a commercial licence course, the school's fatigue rules would apply to her training flights, and today's private flight would count in that system.

So the absence of a prescribed limit is not a clearance. The decision rests with Dana, and the safe decision is to go tomorrow.

Question. Does CAO 48.1 limit how many hours a PPL holder may fly on a private weekend trip?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: no. CAO 48.1 sets limits for commercial operations, and private operations have no prescribed limits. But the pilot must still not fly while fatigued to an extent that affects safety. And they must count those hours if they also fly under an operator's fatigue system.

## Bringing it together

Here is Tom. He holds a {PPL(A)|P P L eigh}, with the single-engine class rating, the night VFR rating and the retractable undercarriage endorsement, on a Basic Class 2 medical. His flight review was 18 months ago. He wants to fly three friends from Jandakot to Kalbarri on Friday afternoon, returning after dark, in a retractable single. And the friends are paying for everything, because "it's their trip".

Work through the layers. The licence and airspace: a PPL, so the Perth zone and the distance are no problem. The aeroplane: he has the single-engine class rating and the retractable undercarriage endorsement. If the aeroplane also has a constant-speed propeller, he needs that endorsement too. The flight review: 18 months ago, so current.

Now the night. He holds the rating, but the Basic Class 2 keeps him in day VFR. So the return after dark is out, whatever his night recency. The passengers: three is within the Basic Class 2 limit of 5. And the money: friends paying everything is not equal cost sharing, so the flight as proposed is not a private operation.

The fix is a four-way equal split of the direct costs, and a return before last light. Every layer had to say yes, and two of them said no.

Question. In that story, which single change would let Tom use his night rating, and why?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: a Class 2 medical. The night rating and his recency would allow night flight. But the Basic Class 2 limits him to day VFR, and the tighter of the licence and medical limits applies.

## Key points

Let's recap.

What the licence lets you do. The {PPL(A)|P P L eigh} lets you fly as pilot in command or co-pilot in private operations. That is with passengers, anywhere in Australia, in all airspace, at controlled aerodromes and above 10,000 ft, in any aeroplane your class or type rating and design feature endorsements cover. Night needs the night VFR rating. IMC needs an instrument rating. Aerobatics and formation need their endorsements. Over 5,700 kg, or a turbojet, needs a type rating. No hire or reward, only equal sharing of direct costs. And the medical is a Class 2, or a Basic Class 2, which means day VFR, below 10,000 ft, and up to 5 passengers.

Recent experience. 3 take-offs and 3 landings in 90 days for day passengers. 3 at night in 90 days for night passengers, with the rating. A flight review within 24 months, and a licence or rating flight test counts as one. And always, the general competency rule.

Classification. Private operations under Part 91. Flight training under Parts 141 and 142. Aerial work under Part 138. Air transport under Parts 119, 121, 133 and 135. If anyone pays beyond an equal share of direct costs, it is not private, and a PPL is not enough.

Flight and duty time. CAO 48.1 limits commercial crews, not private operations. A PPL holder must still not fly fatigued. And when flying under an operator, they follow that operator's limits, counting their private hours.

A final exam tip. Know the recency triplet. 3 take-offs and landings in 90 days for day passengers. The same at night for night passengers. And a flight review every 24 months. Know the boundary of private flying: equal cost sharing is allowed, and anything for hire or reward is not. In scenario questions, check each layer in turn: licence, rating, design features, conditions, medical, recency and money. Don't stop at the first one that looks right. And confirm the current Part 61 wording and the cost-sharing conditions before the exam.

That's the end of PFRA 2.2.
