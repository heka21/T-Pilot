This is PFRA 2.4, Air service operations, for the private pilot licence. By the end you will know the planning duties before a long flight, when the forecast forces you to carry an alternate or holding fuel, and how much, what you may carry and how, and the rules for testing the radio and keeping a listening watch. There are worked examples and questions along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (PFRA 2.4)

The exam wants two things from you. First, applying three sets of rules: the pilot's responsibilities before flight, the weather minima at aerodromes, and the carriage of cargo, sick and handicapped persons, parachutists, flotation and survival equipment, animals and dangerous goods. And second, the requirements to test the radio before taxiing, and to keep a listening watch.

## Why this matters

As a recreational pilot, you flew from an aerodrome you knew, in weather you could see, usually back to where you started. In the recreational lesson on air service operations, you learned the pilot in command's duties for that kind of flight, and every one of them still applies.

The PPL changes the kind of flight. Jandakot to Esperance is more than three hours, and at departure you do not know the weather you will meet on arrival. Kalgoorlie to Laverton crosses country where a forced landing means a long wait for rescue. The flight to Rottnest crosses open water. And your passengers may bring a dog, a bag of farm chemicals, or a parachute.

So the private subtopic adds the planning and loading decisions a pilot makes before going somewhere new. The exam asks "is an alternate required?", "how much holding fuel?" and "may this be carried?", with the exact figures. So here are the figures, with the reasoning that makes them stick.

## Before flight: from checking the aeroplane to planning the flight

The recreational list applies in full: fuel and oil physically checked and reserves carried, refuelling precautions, a safe engine start, the passenger briefing, control locks removed, doors and caps secure, controls checked, no frost or ice, instruments checked, harnesses secured, fuel drains checked, and children restrained. Those duties are about the aeroplane in front of you.

At private level, you add duties about the flight ahead of you. On a local flight, the plan is in your head. On a long cross-country, the plan carries the safety. The weather, the airspace, the fuel and the aerodromes are all things you can no longer see from the run-up bay.

So the pilot in command must do six more things. First, obtain a weather and operational briefing: the forecasts for the route and the aerodromes, and the NOTAMs. The weather decides whether the flight can be flown VFR at all. And the NOTAMs tell you what has changed since the charts were printed.

Second, plan the route and cruising levels, so the flight complies with the visual flight rules and the airspace. That includes the VMC minima, the hemispherical cruising levels, and any clearance needed. You'll find those in the recreational lesson on conditions of flight.

Third, calculate the fuel. That includes any holding and alternate requirements, which come in the next section, on top of the fixed reserve.

Fourth, check the weight and balance for this load, because the PPL lets you fill every seat and the baggage area.

Fifth, submit flight notification, or a SARTIME, where required. That way someone knows to look for you if you do not arrive.

And sixth, confirm the aerodromes are suitable. That means the runway length against the performance charts, which is covered in the lesson on take-off and landing performance. It means whether PPR, prior permission required, applies. And it means lighting, if you could arrive near last light.

[[diagram:sartime-timeline]]
Picture a timeline running left to right. Before flight, you nominate a SARTIME in UTC, with a sensible margin after your planned arrival. If you are delayed, you extend it before it expires, and on landing you cancel it at once. If it lapses, the search begins: attempts to contact you, then a communications search, then a physical search by the Joint Rescue Coordination Centre.

The whole list answers one question: will this flight still be safe at the far end?

Question. Why does the private list add "confirm the aerodromes are suitable", when the recreational list did not stress it?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: at recreational level, you mostly operate from aerodromes you know. As a private pilot, you plan into unfamiliar ones, whose runway length, PPR requirements and lighting you can only learn from ERSA and the NOTAMs. If you find a short runway, or a PPR you lack, only on arrival, the plan has already failed.

## Aerodrome meteorological minima: is an alternate needed?

Start with the problem the minima solve. You depart Jandakot for Albany in fine weather. Albany is two and a half hours away. By the time you get there, a band of low cloud may have come in off the Southern Ocean. You cannot see Albany from Jandakot, so you rely on the forecast. If the forecast is wrong, or if it says conditions might deteriorate, you need a way out. Either somewhere else to land, or enough fuel to wait until the weather clears. The alternate minima set the point at which the forecast is bad enough that you must plan that way out before you leave.

A VFR flight may depart only if the forecast for the destination allows a visual arrival. The forecast for the departure aerodrome matters as well, if you may need to return. The Part 91 Manual of Standards and AIP ENR 1.1 set the alternate minima for VFR flights. That is in Chapter 7 of the Manual of Standards, so verify the current text.

Now the trigger figures. An alternate aerodrome is required, or holding fuel where that option applies, when the destination forecast shows any of four things. And it looks at a window, from 30 minutes before to 60 minutes after your ETA, your estimated time of arrival.

First, more than scattered cloud, which means more than 4 oktas, below 1,500 ft above the aerodrome. Second, visibility less than 8 km. Third, a probability of fog, mist, dust, thunderstorms or other phenomena that would take conditions below those figures. Those are the PROB, TEMPO and INTER groups. And fourth, a crosswind or tailwind component beyond the aircraft's limits.

An alternate is also required when no forecast is available. In that case, a flight may still depart if it can return and land within 1 hour. And it must obtain a forecast within 30 minutes of departure, or return. Check the current wording of the Manual of Standards.

Each figure has a reason. More than scattered means broken or overcast: a layer that forms a ceiling you cannot see through to the runway. Below 1,500 ft leaves too little room under it for a circuit at 1,000 ft above the aerodrome, with margin. Visibility below 8 km is getting close to the 5,000 m VMC minimum, with little margin if the forecast is optimistic.

And the time window exists because your ETA is an estimate. A headwind stronger than forecast can make you 30 minutes late, and the weather does not wait for you. The window is longer after ETA than before, because delays are far more common than early arrivals.

The main conditions pass the alternate minima. But a change group bar of showers, with 4,000 m visibility and broken cloud at 1,200 ft, covers the whole window, and fails both tests. Any change group that overlaps the window and goes below the trigger figures counts.

[[diagram:cloud-amounts-in-oktas]]
Now picture the sky cut into eight slices, because cloud amount is measured in oktas, eighths of the sky. FEW is 1 to 2 oktas, scattered 3 to 4, broken 5 to 7, and overcast 8. A broken or overcast layer forms a ceiling, and that is where the minima trigger.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. "Scattered at 1,200 ft" does not trigger the cloud criterion. Scattered is 3 to 4 oktas, and the trigger is more than 4 oktas. "Broken at 1,400 ft" does trigger it. So read the cloud amount before the cloud height.

Now, holding fuel instead of an alternate. Sometimes the forecast says the destination will be fine, except for a temporary deterioration. A shower passing through, or fog lifting slowly. Diverting a hundred miles to an alternate, for a shower that will be gone in half an hour, makes little sense. You can wait for it to pass instead, provided you carry the fuel to wait. That is the holding fuel option.

The two change groups describe how long a deterioration lasts. You'll find how to read them in a TAF in the meteorology lesson on forecasts and reports. TEMPO is for temporary changes, each lasting up to an hour. INTER is for intermittent changes, each lasting less than 30 minutes.

The holding fuel follows the same logic. 60 minutes for a TEMPO deterioration below the minima, and 30 minutes for an INTER. Verify the current figures in the Manual of Standards. PROB conditions are treated in the same way as the group they qualify. You carry enough to outlast the worst spell the forecast describes.

[[diagram:taf-change-groups]]
Picture an example TAF laid out as a timeline, from {0600|oh six hundred} to {1800|eighteen hundred} UTC. Across the first part of it, a TEMPO bar adds spells of showers and broken cloud, each lasting 30 to 60 minutes. Underneath, the other groups are listed: BECMG, a gradual change, INTER, fluctuations of less than 30 minutes, and PROB, a probability. TEMPO spells last up to an hour, INTER fluctuations under 30 minutes, and the holding fuel matches each.

If you plan an alternate instead, it must itself meet the alternate minima for your arrival period there. An alternate under the same band of cloud is no alternate. You must also carry fuel to fly to it, plus the fixed reserve. Holding or alternate fuel is always in addition to the fixed reserve, never instead of it. That is because the fixed reserve is the fuel you plan never to use.

Let's work one: holding fuel for an INTER group. You plan a flight from Jandakot to Geraldton, with an ETA of {0400|oh four hundred} UTC. The Geraldton TAF has main conditions of wind {200|two zero zero} at 15 knots and CAVOK. Then comes {INTER 0300/0700 3,000 m SHRA BKN012|inter, zero three zero zero to zero seven zero zero, three thousand metres, S H R A, broken zero one two}. That means intermittent showers of rain, 3,000 m visibility, and broken cloud at 1,200 ft. Your aeroplane burns 32 L/h. Is holding fuel needed, and how much? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
Step one, the window. 30 minutes before to 60 minutes after ETA is {0330|oh three thirty} to {0500|oh five hundred} UTC.

Step two, the main conditions. CAVOK is above the minima. No trigger.

Step three, the INTER group. It runs from {0300|oh three hundred} to {0700|oh seven hundred}, overlapping the whole window. It forecasts 3,000 m visibility, which is less than 8 km. And it forecasts broken cloud at 1,200 ft, which is more than scattered below 1,500 ft. So it is below the minima, and an alternate or holding fuel is required.

Step four, the holding option. INTER means 30 minutes of holding fuel. 32 litres an hour, times 0.5 hours, is 16 L.

Step five, the total. Trip fuel, plus any variable reserve, plus 16 L of holding, plus the fixed reserve.

So the answer: 16 L of holding fuel. If the showers arrive as you do, you hold clear of them for up to 30 minutes, and still land with your fixed reserve intact.

Now try one that varies what the exam varies: the change group and the trigger. The Esperance TAF for your ETA of {0630|oh six thirty} UTC has main conditions of {9999|nine nine nine nine} and {SCT025|scattered zero two five}. Then {TEMPO 0700/1000 4000 DZ BKN010|tempo, zero seven zero zero to one zero zero zero, four zero zero zero, D Z, broken zero one zero}. Your aeroplane burns 36 L/h. Is holding fuel needed, and if so, how much?
[[pause 6]]
Here is the solution. Step one, the window: {0600|oh six hundred} to {0730|oh seven thirty} UTC.

Step two, the main conditions. {9999|Nine nine nine nine} means visibility 10 km or more, and the cloud is scattered at 2,500 ft, not more than scattered. So no trigger.

Step three, the TEMPO group. It starts at {0700|oh seven hundred}, inside the window. It forecasts 4,000 m in drizzle, and broken cloud at 1,000 ft. Both are below the minima. So an alternate or holding fuel is required.

Step four, the holding option. TEMPO means 60 minutes. 36 litres an hour, times 1 hour, is 36 L.

So the answer: yes, 36 L of holding fuel. The window catches a TEMPO that begins half an hour after your ETA. That is the reason the window runs to 60 minutes after ETA.

Question. Why is the holding fuel for a TEMPO twice that for an INTER?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because the groups describe deteriorations of different length. An INTER spell lasts less than 30 minutes, and a TEMPO spell up to an hour. So the holding fuel is sized to outlast the longest spell each one forecasts: 30 and 60 minutes.

## Carrying people, cargo and animals

Everything you load changes the flight. It adds weight. It moves the centre of gravity. It may be able to move, block an exit, interfere with the controls, or harm someone. Each of the carriage rules applies that idea to one kind of load. As pilot in command, you decide what comes on board, and you are responsible for it once it does. There are seven kinds of load to know.

The first is cargo. Picture a hard landing, or turbulence over the Darling Scarp, with a 20 kg box of engine parts loose on the back seat. The cargo rule stops that box from becoming a projectile. Cargo must meet four conditions. It must be secured, so it cannot shift or injure anyone. It must be placed so it does not block the exits, or the pilot's access to the controls. It must be within the floor loading limits and the aeroplane's loading system. And it must be included in the weight and balance.

Cargo in a passenger seat must be restrained to the seat's limits. A seat belt is designed for a person, and a heavy box may exceed what that seat and belt are certified to hold.

The second is sick and handicapped persons. A sick or handicapped person may be carried, if the pilot in command is satisfied they can be carried safely, and will not endanger the flight. They must not occupy a seat where they could obstruct an exit or the controls. And they should be briefed and assisted. The logic is the exit. In an emergency, every occupant must be able to get out. Someone who cannot move quickly must not be the one between the others and the door.

A person who is medically unfit to fly needs medical advice, and the equipment to cope. That might be someone just after surgery, someone with a contagious disease, or someone needing oxygen. Altitude lowers the oxygen available, which is covered in the human factors lesson on hypoxia. Cabin pressure changes affect recent surgical wounds. And a light aeroplane carries no medical help. A private pilot should think hard before carrying such a passenger.

The third is parachutists. Parachute descents are regulated by CASR Part 105. The pilot must hold the required experience and approvals. The operation must be conducted by an approved parachute organisation, at an approved drop zone. Parachutists are carried under the restraint and briefing rules in Part 105. And the drop is coordinated with ATS, the air traffic services. A private pilot cannot casually drop a parachutist. There is an open door in flight. There are people moving about the cabin near the controls. And there are bodies falling through airspace that other aircraft are using. Each needs its own procedures and training.

The fourth is flotation equipment. The flight to Rottnest is short, but for part of it you cannot glide to land. If the engine stops there, you ditch. So the flotation rule follows the glide.

Life jackets must be carried for each person, and worn or readily accessible, when a single-engine aeroplane flies over water beyond gliding distance of land. Larger aircraft need them on longer over-water sectors. They must be within reach of the seated occupant, because after a ditching there is no time to climb into the back for a locker. And life rafts and signalling equipment are required for extended over-water flights, beyond the distances in Chapter 26 of the Part 91 Manual of Standards. Verify the current distances.

The trigger is gliding distance of land, not a fixed distance from the coast. So it depends on your height. A climb makes your glide longer. A stretch of water you could not cross within gliding distance at 1,500 ft may be within gliding distance at 6,500 ft. The glide itself is covered in the lesson on descents.

[[diagram:overwater-gliding-distance]]
Picture a side view of a crossing, from the coast on the left to an island on the right, with a dashed glide line rising out over the water from each shore. At 1,500 ft, the middle of the crossing lies below both lines, beyond gliding distance of land, so life jackets must be carried. At 6,500 ft, the track stays above the lines all the way. The rule follows the glide, not the coastline.

The fifth is survival equipment. Western Australia's interior is a place where a survivable forced landing can turn fatal for lack of water. So flights over designated remote areas must carry the survival equipment prescribed in the Part 91 Manual of Standards. The remote areas are shown on the remote area map in the AIP and ERSA. The equipment is four things: water, an ELT, the emergency locator transmitter, signalling equipment, and a first aid kit. In Western Australia, most of the interior is designated remote.

The list matches the survival priorities, which are covered in the human factors lesson on first aid and survival. Treat injuries. Help rescuers find you. And stay alive with water until they do.

[[diagram:survival-priorities]]
Picture the survival priorities as four steps, in order: protection, meaning first aid and shelter, then location, then water, and last, food. Beside them is the advice to stay with the aircraft, because it is easier to see than a person, it gives shade, and the search follows your notified route. The remote area equipment covers the first three priorities.

The sixth is animals. Animals may be carried only with the pilot in command's consent. They must be restrained in a container or harness, so they cannot interfere with the controls or the occupants. And their weight must be included in the load. A frightened kelpie in the footwell is one of the more dangerous things you can put in a cockpit. Assistance animals have specific rights under the transport rules.

And the seventh is dangerous goods. CASR Part 92 prohibits the carriage of dangerous goods, except as permitted. These are things that can burn, explode, corrode or poison in an aircraft. Flammable liquids and gases. Explosives. Corrosives. Oxidisers. And lithium batteries in bulk. Passengers may carry limited quantities of specified items: toiletries, medicines, and small lithium batteries in devices. The pilot in command must know what is on board, and refuse anything not permitted. So ask before loading. Passengers rarely think of camping gas or pool chlorine as dangerous.

Let's work one: loading for a station trip. You are flying a family of three from Jandakot to a station strip north-east of Meekatharra, over a designated remote area. They have a 15 kg box of groceries and their working dog. On the apron, they produce five things. Work through each one. Pause if you'd like to try it first.
[[pause 6]]
First, the dog. With your consent, it goes in a crate secured in the baggage area, with its weight in the load sheet. Allowed.

Second, the groceries. They are secured in the baggage area by the cargo net, clear of the exits, inside the baggage area's floor limit, and in the weight and balance. Allowed.

Third, a 4 kg pressurised camping gas canister. That is a flammable gas, and a dangerous good under Part 92. It is not one of the limited items passengers may carry. So you refuse it, and it goes by road.

Fourth, phones, a laptop and the grandmother's medicines. Small lithium batteries in devices, and medicines, are within the limited quantities passengers may carry. Allowed.

And fifth, survival equipment. The route is over a remote area, so you need water, an ELT, signalling equipment and a first aid kit on board. You confirm all four are loaded. Required, and carried.

One more thing. The grandmother uses a walking frame. You seat her where she does not block the door, and brief the family on helping her out in an emergency.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. "The passenger packed it, so it is their responsibility" is wrong. The pilot in command must know what is on board, and refuse what is not permitted. Ask about the bags before you load them, not after a smell of fuel fills the cabin.

Question. Why do life jackets have to be within reach of the seated occupant, rather than stowed together in the baggage area?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: after a ditching, the cabin fills quickly, and the aeroplane may sink within minutes. Nobody will have time to climb into the back for a locker. So each person must be able to reach a life jacket from their seat.

## The radio: test before taxi, listen in flight

The radio is your link to the other aircraft and to ATS. A fault you find in the run-up bay costs you a few minutes. The same fault found downwind on a busy CTAF costs a great deal more. So before taxiing, test the radio, in three steps. Select the correct frequency. Check the squelch and volume. And make a radio check call where appropriate.

How you confirm the radio works depends on the aerodrome. At a controlled aerodrome, the taxi call confirms two-way communication. If the controller answers and you hear them, both directions work. At a non-controlled aerodrome, a radio check on the CTAF, the common traffic advisory frequency, or a check against the AWIS or ATIS broadcast, confirms the receiver. Signal quality is reported on the readability scale, from 1, unreadable, to 5, perfectly readable.

Now, the listening watch. A radio is no use if you are not listening. In flight, an aircraft with a VHF radio must keep a continuous listening watch on the appropriate frequency. That is set out in AIP GEN 3.4. There are three cases. First, the CTAF, when you are in the vicinity of a non-controlled aerodrome, which means within 10 nm, and at the aerodrome. Second, the ATC frequency, when you are in or near controlled airspace. And third, otherwise, the area frequency for the flight information service.

Where you are able, monitor {121.5|one two one decimal five} megahertz as well. That is the emergency frequency, and an aircraft in distress, or an ELT, may be heard on it.

The logic follows who needs to reach you. Near a non-controlled aerodrome, it is the circuit traffic, on the CTAF. In or near controlled airspace, it is ATC. In the cruise, away from both, the flight information service may need to pass you traffic or weather, or relay a SARTIME message.

Carrying a VHF radio is mandatory in four places: in controlled airspace, at certified and military aerodromes, in {CTAF(R)|see-taff R} areas, and for flight above 5,000 ft. It is required for all flights at registered aerodromes, and strongly recommended everywhere else. Verify the current requirements in the Part 91 Manual of Standards and the VFRG.

Let's work one: which frequency, when? A flight from Jandakot to Northam, then on to Kellerberrin. Pause if you'd like to plan the frequencies first.
[[pause 6]]
Stage one, taxiing at Jandakot, which is controlled. You are on the ground frequency. Your taxi call is answered, so two-way communication is confirmed.

Stage two, departing through the control zone. You are on the tower frequency, then the ATC frequency while near controlled airspace.

Stage three, cruising over the Wheatbelt in Class G, away from aerodromes. You are on the area frequency, with {121.5|one two one decimal five} on the second radio, if you have one.

Stage four, within 10 nm of Northam. You are on the Northam CTAF, where the other circuit traffic is listening.

And stage five, departing Northam. The CTAF until you are clear of the vicinity, then the area frequency again until within 10 nm of Kellerberrin, then that CTAF.

At each stage, you listen where whoever may need to reach you is transmitting.

Question. At a non-controlled aerodrome, with nobody else on the CTAF, how can you confirm your radio receives before taxiing?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: listen to the AWIS, or ATIS, broadcast on its frequency. If you hear it clearly, the receiver works. A radio check call on the CTAF confirms transmission only if someone answers.

## Bringing it together

It is Easter Saturday, and you are flying from Jandakot to Esperance with two friends, and one of their dogs.

On Friday you get the area forecast, the TAFs and the NOTAMs. The Esperance TAF has a TEMPO for showers, and broken cloud at 1,200 ft, starting 30 minutes after your ETA. That is inside the window from 30 minutes before to 60 minutes after. So you either plan an alternate that itself meets the minima, or carry 60 minutes of holding fuel. With Esperance's forecast, you choose holding. Your fuel plan becomes trip fuel, plus 60 minutes of holding, plus the fixed reserve.

You run the weight and balance, and trim the bags to fit. Part of the route crosses a remote area, so water, the ELT, signalling equipment and the first aid kit go in.

On Saturday morning you refuse a gas canister from the camping gear. You secure the bags and the crate, and lodge a SARTIME. In the run-up bay, the tower answers your taxi call, so the radio works. Across the Wheatbelt you listen on the area frequency, with {121.5|one two one decimal five} on the second radio. And within 10 nm of Esperance, you change to its CTAF.

Every decision on that list was made before you could see Esperance.

Question. On that flight, which two decisions depended on the timing of the TEMPO group, and why?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: first, whether an alternate or holding fuel was needed at all. The TEMPO fell inside the window from 30 minutes before to 60 minutes after ETA. And second, how much holding fuel to carry: 60 minutes, because each TEMPO spell may last up to an hour.

## Key points

Let's recap.

Before flight. On top of the recreational duties, plan the flight. A weather and NOTAM briefing. A route and levels that comply with VFR and the airspace. Fuel, including holding and alternate requirements. Weight and balance. Flight notification, or a SARTIME. And aerodromes confirmed suitable, for runway length, PPR and lighting.

Weather minima. An alternate, or holding fuel, is required if, from 30 minutes before to 60 minutes after ETA, the destination forecast has more than scattered cloud below 1,500 ft, visibility under 8 km, a probability of fog, mist, dust or thunderstorms below those figures, or an excessive crosswind or tailwind. It is also required if there is no forecast. Holding is 60 minutes for TEMPO, and 30 for INTER. An alternate must itself meet the minima. And all of it is on top of the fixed reserve.

Carriage. Cargo is secured, clear of exits and controls, within floor limits, and in the weight and balance. Sick and handicapped passengers are carried only if safe, not by an exit or the controls, and briefed. Parachuting only under Part 105. Life jackets within reach beyond gliding distance of land, in a single-engine aeroplane. Water, an ELT, signalling equipment and a first aid kit over remote areas. Animals with consent, restrained and weighed. And dangerous goods refused, except the limited passenger items Part 92 permits.

The radio. Test it before taxi: frequency, squelch and volume, and a check call, or the AWIS or ATIS. Keep a listening watch on the CTAF within 10 nm of a non-controlled aerodrome, on ATC in or near controlled airspace, and on the area frequency otherwise. And monitor {121.5|one two one decimal five} where able.

A final exam tip. The alternate minima and the holding fuel are the examinable numbers here, so have them by heart. Read the cloud amount before its height, because scattered never triggers. And check whether a change group overlaps the window, not just the ETA. For carriage questions, ask what the item could do in the cabin: shift, block an exit, distract the pilot, burn or leak. And confirm the figures in the current Part 91 Manual of Standards before the exam.

That's the end of PFRA 2.4.
