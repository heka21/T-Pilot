This is PFRA 2.7, Emergencies, accidents and incidents, at private pilot level. By the end you will be able to find the right procedure in the emergency pages of ERSA and apply it, decide whether a flight may be a mercy flight, list your duties after an accident or incident, and recognise the hazards you must pass on. There are a few questions and worked cases along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (PFRA 2.7)

The exam wants four things from you. First, to find an emergency procedure in ERSA and apply what it says. Second, when a pilot may declare a mercy flight, and when one must not be flown. Third, what a pilot must do to notify an accident or incident, as the AIP sets it out. And fourth, examples of the hazards to navigation a pilot must report.

## Why this matters

As a recreational pilot you learned the shape of this subject. What an accident is. That the ATSB, the Australian Transport Safety Bureau, wants to hear about it. And that the emergency procedures live in the pink pages of ERSA. You'll find that in the RPL lesson on emergencies and SAR.

A private licence takes you further from home, and the questions get harder. Halfway to Kalbarri, the radio dies, and you need the procedure, not the name of the book it is in. A station owner near Wiluna asks you to fly an injured stockman to Meekatharra at dusk, and you have to know whether the law allows it, and whether you should. A flock of corellas settles on the runway at Northam, and you are the only person who has seen it.

So this lesson teaches the four things the private pilot exam adds. Reading ERSA EMERG for the actual procedure. The mercy flight. The pilot's full set of reporting duties. And the hazards you are expected to pass on.

## Using ERSA EMERG in flight

The EMERG section of ERSA, the En Route Supplement Australia, is printed on pink paper, and placed where you can find it by feel. It is organised in the order a pilot in trouble needs things. First, how to tell someone. Then, what to do about the specific problem. Then, what happens afterwards, if you end up on the ground somewhere you did not plan to be. The RPL exam asked you to name the document. The private pilot exam hands you an extract from it, and asks you to use it.

[[diagram:transponder-emergency-codes]]
Start with three transponder code windows, side by side. {7700|seven seven zero zero} is emergency. {7600|seven six zero zero} is radio failure. And {7500|seven five zero zero} is unlawful interference. All three sit in ERSA EMERG, beside the calls and procedures they go with.

The first part of EMERG covers the two levels of emergency call. MAYDAY is the distress call: grave and imminent danger, and you need immediate help. PAN PAN is the urgency call: a problem that needs attention, without immediate danger. The difference matters. A MAYDAY clears the frequency and commits help at once. A PAN PAN warns everyone, and lets the system prepare.

ERSA gives the format of the call, and the order is built around what a rescuer needs first. Your callsign. The nature of the emergency. Your intentions. Your position, altitude and heading. And the persons on board. Think of it as answering, in order: who you are, what is wrong, what you are going to do, where to find you, and how many people to look for. If the message is cut off after the first few items, the most useful ones have already gone out.

It also tells you where to make the call. First on the frequency you are already using, because someone there is already listening to you. Then, if that gets no answer, on {121.5 MHz|one two one decimal five megahertz}, the international emergency frequency.

Each transponder code tells a radar controller something without a word. Setting {7700|seven seven zero zero} makes your return stand out on every screen that can see you. {7600|seven six zero zero} says you cannot hear them, but you are following the radio failure procedure. And {7500|seven five zero zero} says what you may not be able to say aloud.

Next, radio failure. It is the procedure you are most likely to use. ERSA splits it by where you are, because what other people expect of you depends on the airspace.

As a VFR flight in Class G, nobody is controlling you. So the procedure is about staying safe and staying predictable. Continue. Remain in VMC. Land at the nearest suitable aerodrome. Broadcast blind, which means transmitting your calls as normal, in case your transmitter works even though your receiver does not. And squawk {7600|seven six zero zero}.

In controlled airspace, ATC has a plan for you, and has cleared other traffic around it. So the procedure is to follow the last clearance you received, fly the expected procedures, and watch for light signals from the tower. The controller can only keep others clear of you if you do what they were expecting.

The remaining parts are the ones you hope never to open. The interception signals, and the responses required: follow, rock your wings, land. The security lesson covers those in detail. Ditching and forced landing preparation, and survival afterwards. The search and rescue material: the SAR phases, how to operate the ELT, the emergency locator transmitter, and the ground-to-air visual signals. The light signals from a control tower. And the emergency contact numbers for the ATSB accident line, Airservices, and the JRCC, the Joint Rescue Coordination Centre.

[[diagram:ersa-emerg-contents]]
Picture the pink pages sorted into three groups, by purpose. The first group is "tell someone": the MAYDAY and PAN PAN calls and their format, and the transponder codes. The second is "deal with the problem": radio failure for Class G and for controlled airspace, interception, and ditching and forced landing. The third is "if you end up on the ground": the SAR phases, the ELT, and the ground-to-air signals. The light signals and the emergency contact numbers are in there too. This is grouped by purpose, not by page order, so check the order in your current ERSA.

Let's work one: extracting a procedure from ERSA EMERG. You are VFR at 4,500 ft in Class G, between Jandakot and Bunbury, when the radio goes silent. The intercom still works, but no transmissions are heard on any frequency. You open ERSA EMERG. What do you do? Pause if you'd like to try it first.
[[pause 6]]
Step one, find the right part. The problem is the radio, so radio failure. And you are VFR in Class G.

Step two, read the actions: continue, remain in VMC, land at the nearest suitable aerodrome, and broadcast blind.

Step three, read the transponder entry: squawk {7600|seven six zero zero}. Then any radar that sees you knows the problem without a call.

Step four, apply it. You stay visual, choose the nearest suitable aerodrome on your route, and make every normal call blind, because your transmitter may still be working.

The skill the exam tests is matching the situation to the right paragraph before reading the actions. Which problem, which airspace, VFR or IFR.

Now try one yourself. Twenty minutes later, a smell of burning comes from behind the panel, and a little smoke appears. You decide to land at the next aerodrome, but there is no immediate danger to the aeroplane yet. Which call does ERSA give you, and what goes in it?
[[pause 6]]
The answer: a problem that needs attention without immediate danger is an urgency situation, so PAN PAN. The content follows the ERSA format. Your callsign. The nature of the emergency, smoke from behind the panel. Your intentions, landing at the next aerodrome. Then position, altitude, heading and persons on board. Make it on the current frequency, then on {121.5|one two one decimal five} if that gets no answer. And if the smoke becomes a fire, upgrade to MAYDAY, and squawk {7700|seven seven zero zero}.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. In controlled airspace, radio failure does not mean leave and land at the nearest aerodrome. The procedure there is to follow the last clearance and the expected procedures, because ATC has separated other traffic from the path it expects you to fly. "Remain in VMC and land at the nearest suitable aerodrome" is the Class G procedure.

Question. Why does the MAYDAY format put callsign, nature and intentions before position?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: so that the most useful information goes first. A rescuer needs to know who you are and what kind of help you need. The position, altitude, heading and persons on board follow. If the call is cut short, the essentials have already gone out.

## Mercy flights

Picture a cattle station east of Meekatharra. A child has been badly hurt. The road is cut by floodwater, the Flying Doctor is committed elsewhere, and the station has an aeroplane and a private pilot. The flight that would save the child cannot be made inside the normal rules. It has to leave as the sun goes down, and the pilot has no night rating.

That situation is what a mercy flight is for. It is a flight made to relieve a person in grave danger or urgent need, such as carrying a critically ill person out of a remote area, where the flight cannot comply fully with the normal rules. The departure from the rules might be flight at night by a VFR-only pilot. Flight in weather below the VFR minima. Or flight beyond the normal fuel or aircraft limits.

The idea behind it is a weighing of risks. The rules exist because flying outside them raises the chance of an accident. When a life is at stake on the ground, the law accepts that a pilot may sometimes reasonably choose the higher risk in the air. The mercy flight gives that choice a legal footing.

So when may you declare one? The pilot in command, or the operator, may declare a mercy flight when four things are all true. First, the flight is necessary to relieve a person's suffering, or to save life. Second, no other means of transport is available in time. Third, the pilot in command has considered the risks, and decided the flight can be made with an acceptable level of safety. And fourth, CASA or ATS is notified of the declaration, and of the departures from the rules it involves, before the flight where possible. ATS passes the declaration on to CASA.

Hear those four as tests, each one closing off a way the provision could be abused. The first excludes convenience. The second excludes cases where an ambulance, a road trip or the Flying Doctor would do. The third makes it a judgement, not a reflex. And the fourth means the system knows a non-standard flight is under way, so it can give it priority and watch for it.

Now the other side. There are four occasions when a mercy flight must not be undertaken. First, when the risk to the aircraft and its occupants outweighs the benefit. Second, when the medical need could be met by other transport, or by waiting. Third, when the pilot is not competent for the conditions: for instrument conditions, an instrument-rated pilot and aircraft should be used. And fourth, simply to carry a sick person who is not in grave danger.

One sentence sums it up. Declaring a mercy flight does not change the physics or the weather. It changes the legal position only. A VFR pilot who flies into cloud on a mercy flight loses control in the same time as one who flies into cloud on a sightseeing trip. And if the honest risk assessment says the likely outcome is a second accident, there are now two casualties instead of one.

[[diagram:mercy-flight-decision]]
Picture the decision as a flowchart. The first box asks: can the flight be made within the normal rules? If yes, there is nothing to declare, and it is an ordinary flight. If no, the chart runs down the four conditions: grave need, no other transport in time, an acceptable risk with a competent pilot, and CASA or ATS notified. Every one must be a yes. Any single no, and it must not be flown as a mercy flight.

Let's work one: may this be a mercy flight? There are three cases. Test each against the four conditions, then against the must-not list. Pause after each if you'd like to try it.

The first case. A farmer near Southern Cross has a suspected heart attack. The ambulance is 90 minutes away. You could fly him to Merredin in 30 minutes, in daylight and good weather, within all the normal rules.
[[pause 4]]
No rule needs to be broken, so there is nothing to declare. It is an ordinary private flight carrying a passenger, though you may use the urgency procedures if needed.

The second case. A station hand near Wiluna has a severe crush injury. The Flying Doctor cannot reach the station before tomorrow, and the road is cut. The flight to Meekatharra would end 30 minutes after last light. You have no night rating, but the sky is clear, there is a moon, and the destination has lighting.
[[pause 4]]
Grave need. No other transport in time. And a departure from the rules, night flight by a VFR-only pilot, that you judge acceptable after weighing the risks. This can be a mercy flight, declared to ATS before departure.

The third case. The same patient, but a band of low cloud and rain lies across the route, and you hold no instrument rating.
[[pause 4]]
Now the risk outweighs the benefit, and you are not competent for the conditions. It must not be flown. The need is for an instrument-rated crew, or for waiting.

So the three answers: nothing to declare, a mercy flight, and must not be flown.

Question. A friend's relative is unwell, and wants to get to Perth tonight to see a specialist tomorrow. Your flight would end after last light, and you have no night rating. Is it a mercy flight?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: no. The person is not in grave danger or urgent need, and the need can be met by other transport, or by waiting until tomorrow. A mercy flight is for relieving suffering or saving life when nothing else will do in time.

## Notifying accidents and incidents: the pilot's duties

The RPL lesson taught the two categories, and the 72 hours. At private pilot level, the exam asks you to extract the pilot's responsibilities as the AIP and the ATSB rules set them out. That means knowing who must report, by what means, and what else must happen before and after.

[[diagram:accident-incident-reporting]]
Picture a decision chart. At the top: was it an accident? That means a person killed or seriously injured, serious damage or structural failure, or the aircraft missing or inaccessible. One branch leads to the immediately reportable matters: accidents, serious incidents and the listed serious events. Phone the ATSB as soon as practicable, then a written report within 72 hours. The other branch leads to the routine reportable matters: a written report within 72 hours. And across the bottom: reports go to the ATSB, not CASA.

Who reports? The duties fall on the responsible persons. That means the pilot in command, the operator, and others listed in the regulations. Naming several people is deliberate. The pilot is the obvious reporter. But after a serious accident the pilot may be injured or dead, and the report must still reach the ATSB. So if the pilot is unable, the operator must report.

How fast? There are two speeds, set by how serious the occurrence is. Immediately reportable matters are accidents and serious incidents. Report those to the ATSB as soon as practicable, by phone, on the 24-hour number, {1800 011 034|one eight hundred, zero one one, zero three four}. Then a written report within 72 hours. Routine reportable matters are the other safety occurrences. For those, a written report to the ATSB within 72 hours.

The phone call exists because evidence decays. Wreckage is moved, weather passes, and witnesses forget. So an investigation that might be needed must be able to start the same day.

There is more you must do. First, preserve the aircraft and the site until the ATSB releases it, except to rescue people or protect property. A fuel selector position, or a bent control rod, is evidence, and moving it destroys it.

Second, report to the operator, and record the defect in the maintenance release, so that nobody flies the aeroplane before it has been dealt with. The documentation lesson covers the maintenance release.

Third, report airworthiness defects to CASA, through the defect reporting system. The ATSB investigates occurrences. CASA tracks defects across the fleet, so that a fault in one aeroplane can be found in the others.

And fourth, airspace infringements, near misses and ATC-related occurrences are also reported by ATS. But your own report is still required. The controller's report gives ATC's view. Yours gives what happened in the cockpit. The investigator needs both.

Let's work one: what are the pilot's duties after this occurrence? You land a hired aeroplane at a farm strip near Narrogin. On rollout, the left main gear leg collapses into a hidden hole. The wing tip strikes the ground, and the wing spar is buckled. You and your passenger climb out unhurt. Work through the category, who reports, how and when, the site, and the paperwork. Pause if you'd like to try it first.
[[pause 6]]
The category. Structural damage to the wing, affecting its strength, between boarding and disembarking. That is an accident, so an immediately reportable matter.

Who. You are the pilot in command, and uninjured, so you report. Had you been unable, the operator would have to: the flying school that hired you the aeroplane.

How and when. Phone the ATSB on {1800 011 034|one eight hundred, zero one one, zero three four} as soon as practicable. Then the written report within 72 hours.

The site. Leave the aeroplane where it is, unless the ATSB releases it. The only exceptions are to rescue people or protect property.

The paperwork. Tell the operator. The defect goes in the maintenance release, and the operator reports the airworthiness defect to CASA.

So the answer: an accident. You phone the ATSB, write within 72 hours, leave the aeroplane in place, and tell the operator.

Question. After an airspace infringement, the controller tells you they will file a report. Do you still need to report?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: yes. ATS reports airspace infringements, near misses and ATC-related occurrences, but the pilot's own report is still required. The two reports describe the same event from different seats, and the investigator needs both.

## Hazards to navigation

You are often the first, and sometimes the only, person to see a hazard. A controller cannot see a new mast from the tower. A NOTAM office cannot know that the windsock blew away this morning. So the system relies on pilots passing on what they see.

The rule is this. A pilot who observes a hazard to navigation reports it to ATS as soon as possible, by radio. Use the SECURITE prefix where the message is a safety broadcast. And report afterwards in writing, if that is required.

The examples fall into six groups, and the groups are easier to remember than the list.

[[diagram:hazard-to-navigation-groups]]
Picture six tiles. The first tile: things you could hit that the chart does not show. That means an unlit or unmarked obstacle, such as a crane, a mast or a wind turbine that is not on the chart, or an obstacle light that has failed.

The second tile: things on or near the runway. Birds concentrated on or near an aerodrome, and bird strikes, which also go to the ATSB. And animals, vehicles or people on a runway or landing area.

The third tile: aids that are not working. Unserviceable navigation aids, runway lighting, or a wind indicator.

The fourth tile: weather nobody forecast. Thunderstorms, severe turbulence, wind shear, dust storms, volcanic ash, or a fog bank forming.

The fifth tile: other things in the air. Unmanned aircraft, model aircraft, kites or balloons, near an aerodrome or at altitude. And laser illumination of the cockpit.

And the sixth tile: activity nobody told you about. Smoke, bushfire activity, or a fire-fighting operation affecting the airspace. And military activity, parachuting or gliding that has not been promulgated.

So, six groups: things to hit, the runway, failed aids, unforecast weather, things in the air, and unannounced activity. The common thread is that each one could catch out the next pilot, who would not expect it from the chart, the forecast or the NOTAMs. Your report lets ATS warn other aircraft and issue a NOTAM. And your SECURITE broadcast warns the aircraft on the frequency at once.

Let's work one: is it a hazard, and what do you do? You are joining the circuit at Wongan Hills on a summer afternoon. There are three things to judge. Pause after each if you'd like to try it.

The first. A large flock of galahs is feeding on the threshold of the runway in use.
[[pause 4]]
Birds concentrated on an aerodrome: a hazard to navigation. Broadcast on the CTAF straight away, so others on the frequency know, and report to ATS. If you strike one, the bird strike also goes to the ATSB.

The second. Ten miles south, you saw a thick column of smoke from a new bushfire, with a water bomber working it.
[[pause 4]]
Bushfire activity, and a fire-fighting operation affecting the airspace. Report it to ATS, so that it can warn other traffic and, if needed, issue a NOTAM.

The third. The wind is gustier than the TAF suggested, but within your limits.
[[pause 4]]
A difference from the forecast that is not hazardous, with no severe turbulence and no wind shear, is not in the hazard list. You may pass it on as useful information, but it is not a hazard report.

Question. Why report a failed obstacle light to ATS, rather than just avoiding the mast yourself?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because the next pilot, perhaps at night, does not know it has failed, and expects the light to show them the mast. ATS can warn other aircraft and issue a NOTAM. Your own avoidance protects only you.

## Bringing it together

It is a Sunday in autumn. You have flown a friend from Jandakot to a farm strip near Kulin. On the ground, a neighbour arrives with news. A farm worker has collapsed with severe chest pain. The ambulance is two hours away by road, the nearest hospital with surgery is in Perth, and it is three hours to last light.

You weigh it. The flight back to Jandakot is within the normal rules: daylight, good weather, a qualified pilot. So no mercy flight is needed. You simply carry the patient, and you consider a PAN PAN to get priority into Jandakot.

Climbing out, you see a new wind turbine on a ridge west of the strip that is not on your chart. So you tell ATS on the area frequency.

Half an hour later, the radio fails. You open ERSA EMERG at radio failure. You are VFR, still in Class G. So you continue in VMC, squawk {7600|seven six zero zero}, broadcast blind, and plan the nearest suitable aerodrome, rather than entering Jandakot's zone without a clearance. You land at Beverley, where an ambulance that drove out to meet you collects the patient.

Taxiing in, a wheel drops into a soft patch, and the propeller tips strike the ground. Nobody is hurt, and the damage is limited to the propeller. That is not an accident, but it is an incident that affected safety. So a written report to the ATSB within 72 hours, the defect in the maintenance release, and a call to the operator. Every element appeared, and each decision came from a reason you could explain.

Question. In that scenario, why was the flight with the injured worker not a mercy flight?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because it could be flown entirely within the normal rules, in daylight, in good weather, by a qualified pilot. A mercy flight is a declaration that the flight departs from the rules for a person in grave danger. When no departure is needed, there is nothing to declare.

## Key points

Let's recap.

ERSA EMERG, the pink pages. MAYDAY for distress, PAN PAN for urgency. The call goes in the order callsign, nature, intentions, position, altitude, heading and persons on board. Make it on the current frequency, then on {121.5 MHz|one two one decimal five megahertz}. The codes: {7700|seven seven zero zero} emergency, {7600|seven six zero zero} radio failure, {7500|seven five zero zero} unlawful interference. Radio failure, VFR in Class G: continue, remain in VMC, land at the nearest suitable aerodrome, broadcast blind, and squawk {7600|seven six zero zero}. In controlled airspace, follow the last clearance. EMERG also holds interception, ditching, SAR, light signals and the emergency contacts.

Mercy flights. A mercy flight relieves grave danger or urgent need when the rules cannot be fully met. It must be necessary to save life or relieve suffering, with no other transport in time, the risks judged acceptable, and CASA or ATS notified. Never when the risk outweighs the benefit, when other transport or waiting would do, when the pilot is not competent for the conditions, or when the person is not in grave danger. It changes the law, not the weather.

Reporting. The responsible persons are the pilot in command, and the operator if the pilot cannot. Immediately reportable matters: phone the ATSB on {1800 011 034|one eight hundred, zero one one, zero three four} as soon as practicable, then write within 72 hours. Routine matters: write within 72 hours. Preserve the site, tell the operator, and put the defect in the maintenance release and to CASA. Your report is required even if ATS also reports.

Hazards. Report them to ATS at once by radio, with a SECURITE broadcast, and in writing if required. Uncharted or unlit obstacles. Birds and animals on runways. Unserviceable aids and lighting. Unforecast hazardous weather. Drones, kites, balloons and lasers. And bushfire smoke and unpromulgated activity.

A final exam tip. Expect an ERSA EMERG extract with a scenario. Identify the problem and the airspace first, then read the action. Have the three codes by heart: {7700|seven seven zero zero}, {7600|seven six zero zero} and {7500|seven five zero zero}. Know the ATSB rule: phone as soon as practicable, then write within 72 hours, and routine matters in writing within 72 hours. And know the mercy flight conditions: grave danger, no other transport in time, acceptable risk, and notified to ATS or CASA. Mercy flight distractors offer convenience, or a patient who is not urgent. Verify the mercy flight provisions and the ATSB number against the current Part 91, the AIP and the Transport Safety Investigation Regulations.

That's the end of PFRA 2.7.
