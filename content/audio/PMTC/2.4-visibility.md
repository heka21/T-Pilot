This is PMTC 2.4, Visibility. By the end you will know what visibility means, how to read it from a TAF, METAR or GAF, how to estimate it from the cockpit, what weather reduces it, and how each kind of fog forms and clears. There are worked examples and questions along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (PMTC 2.4)

The exam wants two things from you. First, how to work out the visibility, either from what you can see from the cockpit, or from a forecast or report. And second, the weather factors that reduce visibility in flight.

## Why this matters

Visibility is the one VMC figure you cannot check against the altimeter. You judge it with your own eyes, and it can fall below the minimum while the sky above stays blue.

On a still winter morning, the Avon valley around Northam and York can lie under fog, while the hills either side are in sunshine. On a February afternoon, a dust-laden northerly can take Kalgoorlie from 10 km to 2,000 m in minutes. And in autumn, a burn-off south of Perth can spread smoke over the whole coastal plain.

Poor visibility hides terrain and other aircraft. Worse, it can take away the horizon you fly by.

The exam asks you to read visibility from a TAF, METAR or GAF, and to estimate it from what you can see in flight. It asks you to name the things that reduce it, and to say how each kind of fog forms and clears. You met the local picture in the RPL lesson on local weather. This lesson adds the physics and the numbers.

## What "visibility" means (2.4.1)

Look out across the airfield, and pick out a dark hangar against the sky. Walk away from it, and it fades. Its edges blur, and at some distance you can no longer tell what it is. That distance is the visibility.

Formally, meteorological visibility is the greatest horizontal distance at which a dark object of suitable size can be seen and recognised against the sky by day, or at which lights can be seen at night. The word recognised matters. A vague shape you cannot identify does not count.

A pilot meets three versions of visibility.

The first is the reported or forecast visibility. That is the observer's or the sensor's figure at the aerodrome. In a TAF or METAR it is four digits, in metres. So {4000|four zero zero zero} is 4,000 m. And {9999|nine nine nine nine} means 10 km or more. A TAF or METAR gives the prevailing value. The GAF gives the visibility for each subdivision of its area, often split into a general figure and a figure in showers, for example 3,000 m in showers of rain. And CAVOK includes visibility of 10 km or more.

The second is flight visibility. That is the visibility forward from the cockpit in flight, and it is the figure the VMC rules use: 5,000 m below 10,000 ft above mean sea level. That comes from the Part 91 Manual of Standards, so verify it against the current Visual Flight Rules Guide. It is your judgement, made in the air, and it can differ from the aerodrome report in either direction.

[[diagram:vmc-minima-by-airspace]]
Picture a cloud with an invisible box drawn around it, a keep-out zone. In Class C and E, and in Class G up high, the box is 1,500 m horizontally and 1,000 ft vertically. In Class D it is smaller: 600 m, 1,000 ft above and 500 ft below. Low in Class G, you only need to be clear of cloud and in sight of the ground or water. And in every one of those cases, the flight visibility is the same: 5,000 m. Only the distance from cloud changes. Above 10,000 ft, the visibility rises to 8 km. The full table is in the RPL lesson on conditions of flight.

The third is slant visibility. That is what you see looking down at the ground, or along the approach. Under a haze or smoke layer, the horizontal visibility at the surface may be fine. But looking down from above the layer, you look through its whole depth at a shallow angle, and the ground can almost disappear. Looking into the sun is worse again, because the particles scatter sunlight towards you, and the haze glows.

Question. The METAR at your destination shows {9999|nine nine nine nine}. Yet as you descend towards it through a smoke layer, you can barely make out the runway. Why the difference?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the METAR is horizontal visibility at the surface, measured below or within the bottom of the layer. Descending from above, you look down through the whole depth of the smoke at a slant, so far more particles lie along your line of sight. Slant visibility can be much worse than the reported figure, especially into the sun.

## Judging visibility from the cockpit (2.4.1)

You cannot measure visibility in flight. You estimate it from known distances.

The method is simple. Pick a feature ahead whose distance you can measure on the chart: a town, a dam, a silo, or a road junction. Note where you are when it first becomes recognisable. The distance between the two is the flight visibility.

To use it, you need the VMC minimum in the units you navigate in. One nautical mile is 1,852 m. So 5,000 m is about 2.7 nm. If the feature you expect at 3 nm is not yet in sight, the visibility is below VMC.

Let's work one. What does 5,000 m look like in nautical miles, and in time, at a groundspeed of 100 kt? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
First, the distance. 5,000 divided by 1,852 is 2.7 nm. Then the time. 2.7 nm at 100 kt is 2.7 divided by 100, times 60, which is 1.6 minutes. That is about 1 minute 40 seconds.

So at 100 kt, if a feature first becomes recognisable less than about 1 minute 40 seconds before you reach it, the visibility is under 5,000 m. In practice you want much more than that. The minimum is a legal floor, not a comfortable working figure.

Timing works the other way too. Suppose you first recognise the York silos, and start the stopwatch. You pass over them 2 minutes 30 seconds later, at a groundspeed of 105 kt. What was the flight visibility, and is it above VMC?
[[pause 8]]
The distance flown is 105, times 2.5, divided by 60. That is 4.4 nm. In metres, 4.4 times 1,852 is about 8,100 m, so about 8 km. That is above the 5,000 m minimum.

Remember that a stopwatch figure is only as good as the moment you judged the silos recognisable. It gives you a rough check, not a measurement. The answer again: about 8,100 m, above VMC.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Visibility in TAFs and METARs is in metres, while you navigate in nautical miles. Mixing them up is how a pilot reads {4000|four zero zero zero} as comfortably more than 2.7 nm, and decides it is VMC. It is not. 4,000 m is below the 5,000 m minimum, even with no cloud at all.

## Reading visibility from the forecast (2.4.1)

Before flight, the visibility comes from the forecasts and reports. The full decode is taught in the RPL lesson on forecasts and reports. The skill the exam tests is reading every group, not just the first line. The opening conditions of a TAF are only the background. The change groups can put the visibility below VMC for the very hour you plan to arrive.

[[diagram:taf-change-groups]]
Picture a timeline from {0600|oh six hundred} to {1800|eighteen hundred} UTC, running left to right. The main conditions start at the left edge. At {1000|ten hundred} UTC, an FM group cuts in and replaces them for good, with wind {090|zero nine zero} at 8 kt and CAVOK. Over the first part of the line, from {0600|oh six hundred} to {0900|oh nine hundred}, a TEMPO bar lays spells of 4,000 m in showers, with broken cloud at 2,000 ft, over the background. Each spell lasts 30 to 60 minutes. What to notice: the main line sets the background, and TEMPO and INTER groups lay temporary spells over it for the hours they cover.

Let's decode one, a Kalgoorlie TAF. It reads {TAF YPKG 030500Z 0306/0318 25015G25KT 9999 SCT040|taff, Y P K G, zero three zero five zero zero Zulu, zero three zero six, stroke, zero three one eight, two five zero one five, gust two five K T, nine nine nine nine, scattered zero four zero}. Then on the next line, {TEMPO 0308/0312 2000 DS|tempo, zero three zero eight, stroke, zero three one two, two zero zero zero, D S}. What does it mean for the visibility? Pause if you'd like to work it out.
[[pause 8]]
First, the main conditions. {9999|Nine nine nine nine} means a visibility of 10 km or more, from {0600|oh six hundred} to {1800|eighteen hundred} UTC.

Second, the TEMPO group. Between {0800|oh eight hundred} and {1200|twelve hundred} UTC, there are temporary periods of 30 to 60 minutes each.

Third, during those periods, the visibility is 2,000 m in dust storm. D S is the code for a dust storm.

And fourth, compare with VMC. 2,000 m is below 5,000 m.

So during the TEMPO periods the visibility is below VMC. A VFR arrival should be planned outside {0800|oh eight hundred} to {1200|twelve hundred} UTC, which is {1600|sixteen hundred} to {2000|twenty hundred} WST. Or it should carry the holding fuel the TEMPO requires. That rule is in the RPL lesson on forecasts and reports.

The GAF works the same way for the route. A subdivision might show a visibility of 10 km generally, but 3,000 m in showers of rain. If showers are forecast along your route, the 3,000 m figure is the one you will meet when you fly into one. And it is below VMC.

Question. The TAF for your destination is {9999|nine nine nine nine} throughout. But the GAF for the route shows 3,000 m in showers of rain. Which matters for the flight, and why?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: both. The TAF covers the aerodrome, and the GAF covers the route between. En route, any shower you fly into will take the visibility to about 3,000 m, below the 5,000 m minimum. So you plan to fly round the showers, or not go, whatever the destination TAF says.

## Why visibility falls: particles and droplets (2.4.2)

Light travels from an object to your eye. Anything suspended in the air between, a water droplet, a dust grain, a smoke particle, scatters some of that light out of the beam. It also scatters other light into the beam from the side. So the object loses contrast against its background, until you can no longer recognise it. The more particles along the path, and the bigger they are, the shorter the visibility.

Two things follow straight from that picture.

First, visibility depends on what is in the air, and how much of it. That is why the weather codes name the cause.

Second, anything that traps particles near the ground makes visibility worse. A stable layer, especially an inversion, stops the air mixing upwards. So smoke, dust, haze and fog collect underneath it. Unstable air mixes everything through a deep layer, and carries it away. So visibility is generally poor in stable air, and good in unstable air, outside the showers.

Now the factors, one by one. There are eight.

First, fog, coded F G. Fog is cloud at the surface. The air is saturated and full of water droplets, and the visibility is under 1,000 m.

Second, mist, coded B R. Mist is the same process, less advanced: fewer or smaller droplets. The humidity is high, about 95 percent or more, and the visibility is 1,000 to 5,000 m. Mist often comes before fog on a cooling evening, and after it as fog clears. The code comes from the French word brume.

Third, haze, coded H Z. Haze is made of dry particles: fine dust, salt and pollution. They are too small to see one at a time, but numerous enough to take the edge off the horizon. Haze collects under an inversion, and it is at its worst looking into the sun.

Fourth, smoke, coded F U. Smoke from bushfires and burn-offs is the big seasonal problem in the south-west, in late summer and autumn. A large fire can cover hundreds of kilometres. And under a morning inversion, the smoke spreads flat at a few hundred feet.

Fifth, dust and sand. The codes are D U for dust, S A for sand, D S for a dust storm, and S S for a sandstorm. Dust and sand are raised by strong winds over the dry interior and the wheatbelt: ahead of fronts, in hot northerlies, and in the outflow of thunderstorms. A dust storm or sandstorm can drop the visibility to a few hundred metres in minutes.

Sixth, precipitation: drizzle, rain, showers of rain, and thunderstorms with rain. It reduces visibility in proportion to how hard it falls. Drizzle is fine but dense, and it often comes with low stratus. Heavy showers and thunderstorm rain can drop the visibility to a few hundred metres. And water streaming across the windscreen makes it worse still.

Seventh, low cloud. It has no code of its own here, but it does the same job. Cloud on the hills, or a lowering base, forces you to choose between flying into it and flying below it, close to the ground.

And eighth, sea spray. In strong winds over the sea, it reduces the visibility at low level along the coast.

Some things that make visibility worse are not weather at all. Flying into the sun. A dirty, scratched or misted windscreen. Rain on the screen. And darkness. The exam may offer them as distractors, in a question about meteorological factors.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Mist and haze both blur the horizon, but they are different things. Mist is water droplets in very humid air. Haze is dry particles. The humidity tells you which. A small dew point spread means mist. A large one means haze.

## Fog: how each kind forms and clears (2.4.2)

Fog forms when the air at the surface is cooled to its dew point, or has moisture added until it saturates. The kinds of fog differ only in what does the cooling or the moistening. And that also decides what clears them.

Start with radiation fog, the fog of the Avon valley in winter. On a clear night, the ground radiates its heat to space, with no cloud to send it back, and it chills the air resting on it. If the air is humid, with a small dew point spread at sunset, the chilled layer reaches its dew point, and fog forms.

Four conditions have to come together. A clear sky. A light wind, about 2 to 5 kt. Moist air. And long nights, so autumn and winter.

[[diagram:radiation-fog-overnight]]
Picture four panels, from evening to mid-morning. In the evening, the sky is clear, the air calm and humid. Overnight, the ground radiates its heat to the clear sky, and the air touching it cools to its dew point. At dawn, fog lies in the river valleys and along the coast. And by mid-morning, the sun has heated the ground, and the fog clears.

The wind is the subtle part. Radiation fog needs some wind. In a dead calm, only the thinnest layer touching the ground cools, and you get dew or frost rather than fog. A light breeze stirs the cooling through a layer deep enough to make fog. Too much wind, above about 10 kt, mixes the chilled air with warmer air from above, through a deep layer. Then the result is low stratus instead of fog, or nothing.

Cold air is dense, so on sloping ground it drains downhill overnight, and pools in the valleys. That is why the fog lies in the Avon valley around Northam and York, and on river flats, while a strip on the hills nearby stays clear.

The fog forms around dawn, the coldest time of the night, not at sunset. It often thickens just after sunrise. It clears as the sun heats the ground, burning off from the edges, and often lifting into low stratus first. Or it clears when the wind strengthens and mixes it out. The warning signs are there the evening before: a clear sky, a light wind, and a small spread that is closing, such as {14/12|fourteen stroke twelve} at sunset.

Question. Why does fog lie over Northam at dawn, while a farm strip on the hills a few miles away is clear?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: air chilled by the ground overnight is denser than the air around it. So it drains downhill, and pools in the valley. The valley floor collects the coldest, most saturated air, which is where the fog forms. The hilltop air never gets as cold.

Next, advection fog, the fog of the coast. Advection means horizontal movement. Advection fog forms when warm, moist air moves over a colder surface, and is cooled from below to its dew point.

Because the cooling comes from the surface the air is flowing over, not from the night sky, advection fog can form day or night, with moderate winds, and over large areas. It clears only when the wind changes direction, or a different air mass arrives. Sunshine does little to it.

Sea fog is advection fog over the sea: moist air moving over cooler water. It can form offshore, and then drift onto a coastal aerodrome on an onshore wind. The sun cannot warm the sea much in a day, so heating does little to clear it. Only a change of wind direction removes it.

There are four more kinds.

Frontal fog forms ahead of a warm front, or a slow-moving front. Rain falling from the warm air above the frontal surface drops into the colder air below. It evaporates, and adds moisture until that air saturates. It clears with the passage of the front.

Steaming fog, also called sea smoke, forms when very cold air flows over much warmer water. Water evaporates into the cold air and saturates it. So fog rises from rivers, lakes and the sea on cold mornings, usually in a shallow layer. Heating, or the air warming, clears it.

[[diagram:fog-types]]
Picture four panels side by side, one for each kind of fog. In the first, radiation fog pools in the valleys on a clear night with a light wind, and the sun or a stronger wind clears it. In the second, moist air moves over a colder sea, and advection or sea fog drifts onto the coast, day or night, until the wind changes direction. In the third, rain from the warm air above a front falls into the cold air below, and makes frontal fog that clears when the front passes. And in the fourth, very cold air over much warmer water makes steaming fog, which heating clears. What to notice: radiation fog burns off with the sun, but advection fog needs a wind change, frontal fog the front to pass, and steaming fog the air to warm.

The last kind is hill fog, or upslope fog. It is cloud formed by orographic lifting, sitting on high ground. Moist onshore winds forced up the coastal ranges cool to their dew point. It clears with a wind change, or as the air dries out.

[[diagram:low-cloud-and-rising-terrain]]
Picture a side view. A VFR aeroplane flies inland under a cloud base that lowers ahead, while the ground rises towards hills. The gap between cloud and terrain narrows, until there is no room left to stay clear of cloud and above the minimum height. The safe choice is to turn back early, while there is still room. What to notice: hill fog is cloud sitting on the ground, and as the terrain rises into it, the visibility goes to nothing.

So, each fog with what clears it. Radiation fog: the sun after dawn, or the wind strengthening. Advection fog, and sea fog: only a change of wind direction, or of air mass. Frontal fog: the passage of the front. Steaming fog: heating, or the air warming. And hill fog: a wind change, or drying out.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. The exam separates radiation and advection fog by the clues in the question. A clear night, a light wind, an inland valley, cleared by mid-morning sun: that is radiation fog. A moderate wind, over or beside the sea, persisting through a sunny day: that is advection or sea fog. Sunshine clears radiation fog because it heats the ground that made it. It cannot clear fog made by a cold sea.

Question. Why does steaming fog form over a river on a cold morning, when the air above it is far from saturated?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the cold air is being moistened, not cooled. Water from the much warmer river evaporates into it faster than the cold air can hold. So the lowest layer saturates, and the excess condenses as wisps of fog rising off the surface.

## Bringing it together

It is a Sunday in June, and you plan an {0800|oh eight hundred} WST departure from Jandakot to Northam.

On Saturday evening, the Northam observation is {11/09|eleven stroke zero nine}, with a light easterly, and the sky clearing after a front. A 2 degree spread, a clear sky, a light wind, and a long winter night. That is everything radiation fog needs, in the valley where it pools.

The Northam TAF on Sunday morning confirms it, with {0300 FG|zero three zero zero, F G} in the opening conditions, and becoming {9999|nine nine nine nine} by mid-morning. Three hundred metres is far below the 5,000 m VMC minimum. So you plan to arrive late morning, when the sun has heated the ground and burned the fog off.

On the way, climbing over the Scarp, you meet a layer of haze trapped under the morning inversion. The horizontal visibility is fine. But looking east into the low sun, the ground ahead fades.

You check it by timing. The Northam silos first become recognisable about 3 minutes out, at 100 kt. That is roughly 5 nm, about 9 km, comfortably above VMC.

Every part of that flight used this lesson. The fog type, and when it clears. The forecast groups. Slant visibility into the sun. And an estimate made from the cockpit.

Question. Why is the visibility often poor for days under a winter high, but excellent in the showery air behind a cold front?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: air sinking in a high warms, and forms a subsidence inversion. That is a stable lid, which traps haze, smoke and fog beneath it. The cold air behind a front is unstable. So it mixes particles up through a deep layer, and carries them away. Only the showers themselves reduce the visibility.

## Key points

Let's recap.

Visibility is the greatest distance at which a dark object can be seen and recognised, or lights at night. Reports and TAFs give it in metres, with {9999|nine nine nine nine} meaning 10 km or more. The GAF gives it by subdivision, often separately for showers.

Flight visibility for VMC below 10,000 ft is 5,000 m, about 2.7 nm. Verify it against the current rules. Estimate it in flight from a feature at a known distance. Read every TAF change group, not just the first line. And slant visibility through haze or smoke, and into the sun, can be far worse than the reported figure.

Visibility is reduced by fog, under 1,000 m. By mist, 1,000 to 5,000 m, in humid air. By haze, which is dry particles. By smoke. By dust and sand. And by precipitation, low cloud and sea spray. It is poor in stable air under an inversion, and good in unstable air outside the showers.

Radiation fog needs a clear sky, a light wind, humid air and long nights. It forms in valleys, and the sun clears it. Advection and sea fog are moist air over a cold surface. They can form at any time, and only a wind change clears them. Frontal, steaming and hill fog complete the set.

A final exam tip. {9999|Nine nine nine nine} means 10 km or more, not {9,999|nine thousand nine hundred and ninety-nine} m. Visibility in TAFs and METARs is in metres. The VMC minimum is 5,000 m, so {4000|four zero zero zero} is below VMC, even with no cloud at all. Radiation fog: clear, calm with a light wind, humid, over land, at night, and cleared by the sun. Advection fog: moving air over a cold surface. It can persist all day, and a wind change clears it. If the question says the fog persisted through the day despite sunshine, or formed with a 12 kt wind, it is advection.
