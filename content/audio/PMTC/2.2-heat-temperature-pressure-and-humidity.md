This is PMTC 2.2, Heat, temperature, pressure and humidity. By the end you will know how the air is heated and how its temperature is measured, what dew point, relative humidity and an inversion are, what changes air density, and how to work out a pressure height, an ISA temperature and a QFE in your head. There are worked examples and questions along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (PMTC 2.2)

The exam wants five things from you. First, how surface air temperature is measured, and how the air just above the runway can differ from it. Second, what a temperature inversion is, what saturated air, relative humidity and dew point are, and what evaporation, condensation and freezing do. Third, how temperature, pressure and humidity change air density. Fourth, how to calculate an ISA temperature and a pressure height. And fifth, the meanings of height, elevation, altitude, QNH and QFE.

## Why this matters

This is the lesson that turns weather into numbers the aeroplane cares about.

On a 38 degree afternoon at an inland strip, the METAR temperature, the QNH and the elevation together decide whether you clear the trees at the end of the runway. On a still winter evening at Northam, the temperature and the dew point decide whether there will be fog in the Avon valley at dawn. And every altimeter setting you dial in, QNH in the circuit and {1013|ten thirteen} on the flight levels, rests on the definitions at the end of this lesson.

The exam asks for definitions: dew point, inversion, QFE. It asks for the direction of an effect, such as whether humidity raises or lowers density. And it asks for two calculations: pressure height and ISA temperature.

## How the air is heated

The air gets its heat in four ways, and each one appears later in this unit.

First, radiation. The sun's energy arrives as radiation, passes mostly straight through clear air, and is absorbed by the ground. The ground also radiates its own heat away to the sky, day and night. By day it gains more than it loses, and warms. On a clear night it only loses, and cools.

Second, conduction. Heat passes by contact, from a warmer thing to a cooler one. The ground warms or chills only the thin layer of air actually touching it. Air is a poor conductor, so conduction alone affects only the bottom few feet.

Third, convection. Air warmed at the ground becomes lighter than the air around it, and rises in bubbles called thermals, carrying the heat upwards. Cooler air sinks to replace it. This vertical stirring is what spreads the day's heating through thousands of feet. Moving air sideways, so that warm air flows over cold ground or the reverse, is called advection.

And fourth, latent heat. Water absorbs heat when it evaporates, and gives the same heat back when it condenses. So moist air carries a hidden store of energy with it.

## Measuring surface temperature (2.2.1)

The official air temperature in a METAR is measured in the shade, about 1.2 m above short grass. The thermometer sits in a ventilated, white, louvred instrument shelter called a Stevenson screen, or in the radiation shield of an automatic weather station.

Each detail has a reason. Shade stops sunlight heating the thermometer. White paint reflects sunlight. The louvres let air flow through, so it reads the air rather than the box. And grass gives every station the same surface.

The air you take off through is often different. There are two cases.

On a sunny afternoon, the dark bitumen of the runway absorbs far more sunlight than grass, and heats the air touching it by conduction. The layer just above the runway can be several degrees hotter than the reported temperature. Hotter air is less dense, so take-off performance is worse than the METAR temperature suggests.

On a clear, calm night, the opposite happens. The ground radiates its heat to the open sky, and chills the air in contact with it. The air at runway level can be colder than the screen reading. Sometimes that is enough for frost or fog to form, while the reported temperature is still a few degrees above the dew point.

So use the reported temperature for performance charts, which were built for it. But treat it as optimistic on hot days.

Question. Why might fog form on a runway when the METAR shows a 2 degree dew point spread?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the temperature is measured 1.2 m above grass. On a clear, calm night the ground chills the air touching it below the screen temperature. So the air at runway level can reach its dew point while the screen still reads 2 degrees above it.

## Water in the air (2.2.2 b)

Leave a glass of iced water on the table on a humid Perth summer day, and droplets form on the outside. The air touching the glass has been cooled until it cannot keep all its water as vapour. Every cloud and every fog forms the same way.

Air at any temperature can hold only so much water vapour. When it holds that maximum, it is saturated. Any more vapour, or any further cooling, makes some of it condense into droplets. The maximum depends very strongly on temperature. Warm air can hold far more vapour than cold air, roughly doubling for every 10 degrees or so of warming.

Relative humidity is the amount of vapour the air actually holds, as a percentage of the most it could hold at its present temperature. The word relative is the key. Relative humidity depends on the temperature, as well as on the water. Cool a parcel of air without adding or removing any water, and its relative humidity rises. The maximum it can hold has fallen, while the water in it has not changed. At 100 percent the air is saturated.

The dew point is the temperature to which air must be cooled, at constant pressure and with no change in its water content, to become saturated. Unlike relative humidity, the dew point measures the water itself. Moist air has a high dew point, and dry air a low one. And the dew point stays the same as the air warms and cools through the day.

The gap between the air temperature and the dew point is the dew point spread. It is the quick measure of how close the air is to making cloud or fog. A METAR group of {12/11|twelve stroke eleven} is a 1 degree spread, and a strong hint of fog or low cloud. {35/05|Thirty-five stroke zero five} is a 30 degree spread, and very dry air. You first met the dew point spread in the RPL lesson on local weather. In the lesson on clouds and precipitation, you use it to estimate the base of cumulus.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. A rising relative humidity does not mean water has been added. Overnight, the relative humidity climbs towards 100 percent simply because the air is cooling. The dew point is the measure of the water content. The spread is the measure of how close the air is to saturation.

Question. During the evening the temperature falls from 20 to 14 degrees, and the dew point stays at 12. What happens to the relative humidity, and to the spread?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the relative humidity rises, because cooler air can hold less vapour, while the water in it is unchanged. The spread closes from 8 to 2 degrees. So the air is getting close to saturation, and fog or low cloud becomes possible if it keeps cooling.

## Evaporation, condensation and freezing (2.2.2 c)

Water changes between three states: vapour, liquid and ice. Each change either takes heat in or gives heat out. That heat changes the state without changing the temperature, and it is called latent heat, which means hidden heat.

Evaporation, liquid to vapour, absorbs heat from the surroundings, so it cools them. In the atmosphere, rain falling into dry air below a cloud evaporates and chills that air. The chilled air is heavy, and it is driven downwards. Those are the cold, gusty downdraughts under showers and thunderstorms.

Condensation, vapour to liquid, releases the same heat back into the air. That is the hidden fuel of the atmosphere. Each cloud droplet that forms warms the air around it a little. That is why saturated air cools more slowly as it rises, which comes up in the next section. And it is why a big thunderstorm keeps growing. The more vapour condenses, the more heat is released, and the more buoyant the rising air becomes.

Freezing, liquid to ice, also releases heat, and melting absorbs it. Going straight from vapour to ice, or back, is called deposition and sublimation, and it moves the most heat of all.

So the pattern is simple. Evaporation, melting and sublimation absorb heat, and cool the surroundings. Condensation and freezing release heat, and warm the air.

Water in cloud often stays liquid well below 0 degrees, because tiny droplets need something to freeze onto. These supercooled droplets freeze the moment they strike a wing or a propeller. They are the source of airframe icing, which is covered in the lesson on flight considerations.

Question. Why is the air under a shower from a high-based cloud often colder and gustier than the air around it?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the rain evaporates as it falls through the dry air below the cloud. Evaporation absorbs latent heat, cooling that air. The cooled air is denser, so it sinks, and spreads out at the ground as a cold, gusty downdraught.

## Lapse rates and stability

To understand inversions, and every cloud in the next lesson, you need one more idea. What happens to a bubble of air, called a parcel, when it moves up or down?

When a parcel rises, it moves into lower pressure and expands. Expanding takes energy, which comes from the parcel's own heat, so it cools. When it sinks, it is compressed, and warms. If no heat passes between the parcel and the air around it, the process is called adiabatic. That is close to true for a large parcel moving quickly.

An unsaturated parcel cools at the dry adiabatic lapse rate, the DALR: 3 degrees per 1,000 ft. Once it is saturated, every further degree of cooling condenses some vapour and releases latent heat, which offsets part of the cooling. So a saturated parcel cools at the smaller saturated adiabatic lapse rate, the SALR: about 1.5 degrees per 1,000 ft. The SALR is smaller in warm air, and closer to the DALR in very cold air.

Neither of these is the temperature of the air around the parcel. The real temperature profile on the day is the environmental lapse rate, the ELR. It is measured by a balloon-borne radiosonde, and it can be anything. And the ISA's 1.98 degrees per 1,000 ft is simply the reference average ELR.

Stability compares the parcel with the air around it. Give a parcel a nudge upwards. If it ends up colder than the air around it at its new level, it is denser, and sinks back. The air is stable. If it ends up warmer, it is lighter, and keeps rising on its own. The air is unstable.

So stability depends on whether the environment cools faster or slower with height than the parcel does. There are three cases.

First, if the ELR is less than the parcel's lapse rate, the surrounding air stays relatively warm aloft. The lifted parcel ends up colder than it, and the air is stable.

Second, if the ELR is greater than the DALR, the surrounding air gets cold so quickly with height that even a dry parcel, cooling at 3 degrees per 1,000 ft, stays warmer than it, and keeps rising. That air is unstable.

And third, in between. With the ELR between the SALR and the DALR, the air is conditionally unstable. It is stable while the rising air is dry, and unstable once it saturates.

[[widget:lapse-rate-stability]]
Picture two lines on a graph of temperature against height: the parcel's line and the environment's line. Where the parcel is warmer, it keeps rising. And once it saturates, it cools more slowly, and can break free.

Let's work one. The surface temperature is 26 degrees. The ELR is 2.5 degrees per 1,000 ft. An unsaturated parcel is lifted 2,000 ft. Is it stable? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
The parcel cools at the DALR: 26 minus 2 times 3 is 20 degrees at 2,000 ft. The environment cools at the ELR: 26 minus 2 times 2.5 is 21 degrees. So the parcel is 1 degree colder than the air around it, and it sinks back. The air is stable for dry parcels.

But 2.5 degrees per 1,000 ft is between the SALR and the DALR. If the parcel were saturated, it would cool at only about 1.5 degrees per 1,000 ft. It would reach 2,000 ft at about 23 degrees. That is 2 degrees warmer than its surroundings, so it would keep going. That is conditional instability. It is the classic setting for cumulus that grows once the thermals reach their condensation level.

The exam often describes a day and asks which kind of air it is. Stable air gives layered, stratiform cloud: stratus, stratocumulus, altostratus and nimbostratus. It gives continuous rain or drizzle, poor visibility in haze, smoke and fog, and smooth air, except near terrain. Unstable air gives heaped, cumuliform cloud: cumulus, towering cumulus and cumulonimbus. It gives showers, thunderstorms and hail, good visibility outside the showers, and bumpy air, with thermals and a gusty surface wind.

## Temperature inversions (2.2.2 a)

Most of the time, temperature falls as you climb. A temperature inversion is a layer where it does the opposite, and increases with height. It is the most stable arrangement there is. Any parcel lifted into it cools, while the air around it gets warmer, so it sinks straight back. An inversion acts as a lid on everything beneath it.

Four processes make inversions, and each has a setting you can picture.

First, the radiation inversion. On a clear, calm night, the ground radiates its heat away and chills the air touching it. So the coldest air is at the bottom, with warmer air above. This is the inversion of a winter dawn in the Avon valley, and the one that makes radiation fog.

Second, the subsidence inversion. In a high pressure system, the air slowly sinks over a wide area, and warms by compression as it descends. That leaves a warm layer sitting on cooler surface air. The long summer highs over Western Australia make this one common.

Third, the frontal inversion. At a front, warm air rides over a wedge of cold air. So climbing through the frontal surface, you pass from cold into warm.

And fourth, the turbulence inversion. Strong wind over rough ground mixes the lowest layer thoroughly, giving it the DALR. At the top of the mixed layer, the temperature can jump back up into the undisturbed air above.

[[diagram:radiation-fog-overnight]]
Picture four panels, left to right, from evening to mid-morning. In the evening, the sky is clear, the air is calm and humid. Overnight, the ground radiates its heat to the clear sky, and the air touching it cools to its dew point. At dawn, fog lies in the river valleys and along the coast. By mid-morning, the sun has heated the ground, and the fog clears. What to notice: the coldest air lies at the bottom, under warmer air, and if it reaches its dew point, it becomes fog.

Under an inversion, smoke, haze, dust and fog are trapped, and cannot mix upwards, so visibility is poor. The air below is smooth. At the top of the inversion there can be a sharp change of wind, because the light surface wind is cut off from the stronger wind above. That is wind shear, and it can catch you on climb-out or approach. Climbing through the top of a morning inversion, you will often see the haze end in a sharp line, and the visibility open up.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. An inversion is defined by temperature rising with height, not just falling more slowly. An isothermal layer, where the temperature stays constant, is also very stable. But it is not an inversion.

Question. Why does smoke from a morning burn-off spread out flat at a few hundred feet, instead of rising?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: it has reached the top of a radiation inversion. The rising smoke cools as it climbs, but the air in the inversion gets warmer with height. So the smoke becomes colder and denser than its surroundings, stops rising, and spreads sideways under the lid.

## Air density (2.2.3)

The aeroplane cares about density, the mass of air in each cubic metre. Lift, drag, engine power and propeller thrust all depend on it. Three things set it.

First, temperature. Heat a gas, and its molecules move faster and spread apart. At a given pressure, warmer air is less dense.

Second, pressure. Squeeze a gas, and the same molecules occupy less space. At a given temperature, higher pressure means denser air. Climbing reduces pressure, which is why density falls with altitude.

Third, humidity. This one surprises people, because humid air feels heavy. A water vapour molecule is lighter than the nitrogen and oxygen molecules it replaces. Its molecular weight is about 18, against about 29 for dry air. Each litre of air holds the same number of molecules at the same temperature and pressure. So swapping heavy molecules for light ones makes it lighter. Humid air is less dense than dry air. The effect is small next to temperature, but it always points the same way.

High, hot and humid is the worst case. A summer afternoon at an inland Western Australian strip, or a muggy wet-season day in the Kimberley. Cold, high pressure, dry air is the best.

Question. Two days have the same temperature and QNH. One has a dew point of 22, and the other a dew point of 2. Which day gives the better take-off performance?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the dry day, with the dew point of 2. The humid day has more water vapour, whose molecules are lighter than the nitrogen and oxygen they replace. So its air is slightly less dense.

## Pressure height and ISA temperature (2.2.4)

Performance charts do not know about QNH. They are drawn for the ISA, so they want to know how high the aerodrome is in the standard atmosphere. That is the pressure height: the height in the ISA at which today's actual pressure would be found. It is also what the altimeter reads with {1013|ten thirteen} set on the subscale.

If today's QNH is exactly {1013|ten thirteen}, the standard atmosphere and the real one agree at sea level, and the pressure height equals the elevation. If the QNH is lower, say {1003|ten oh three}, then the real pressure everywhere is about 10 hPa lower than standard. In the ISA, you would have to climb about 10 times 30, which is 300 ft, to find that pressure. So the aerodrome feels 300 ft higher.

That gives the rule, using the 30 ft per hectopascal from the last lesson. Pressure height equals the elevation, plus {1013|ten thirteen} minus the QNH, times 30. When the QNH is below {1013|ten thirteen}, the pressure height is above the elevation. When the QNH is above {1013|ten thirteen}, it is below.

The ISA temperature at that pressure height comes from the last lesson: 15, minus 2 times the pressure height in thousands of feet. Then there is the density height, the height in the ISA with the same density as today's air. Each degree of ISA deviation, actual minus ISA, moves the density height by about 120 ft. So the density height is about the pressure height, plus 120 times the actual temperature minus the ISA temperature.

Picture the two corrections side by side. Heat is the big one. Every degree above ISA adds about 120 ft of density height, while each hectopascal of QNH is worth only 30 ft.

Let's work one, a hot inland afternoon. The elevation is 1,200 ft, the QNH is {1008 hPa|ten oh eight hectopascals}, and the temperature is 38 degrees. Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 8]]
Step one, the pressure height. {1013|Ten thirteen} minus {1008|ten oh eight}, times 30, is 150. 1,200 plus 150 is 1,350 ft.

Step two, the ISA temperature at 1,350 ft. 1.35 times 2 is 2.7, and 15 minus 2.7 is 12.3 degrees.

Step three, the deviation. 38 minus 12.3 is 25.7, so about ISA plus 26.

Step four, the density height. 120 times 25.7 is 3,084. Add the 1,350, and the density height is about 4,400 ft. So the aeroplane performs as if the strip were at 4,400 ft. The charts in the lesson on take-off and landing performance do this correction for you, once you enter the pressure height and temperature.

Now a cold, high pressure morning. The elevation is 800 ft, the QNH is {1021 hPa|ten twenty-one hectopascals}, and the temperature is 12 degrees.
[[pause 8]]
The pressure height: {1013|ten thirteen} minus {1021|ten twenty-one} is minus 8. Minus 8 times 30 is minus 240. 800 minus 240 is 560 ft. The ISA temperature at 560 ft: 0.56 times 2 is 1.1, and 15 minus 1.1 is 13.9 degrees. The deviation: 12 minus 13.9 is minus 1.9, about ISA minus 2. The density height: 120 times minus 1.9 is minus 228. 560 minus 228 is about 330 ft. High pressure and cool air: the aeroplane performs as if the strip were at about 330 ft, better than its 800 ft surveyed elevation suggests.

Here is one for you to try. A wheatbelt strip at 650 ft elevation, with a QNH of {1006 hPa|ten oh six hectopascals}, and a temperature of 35 degrees. Find the pressure height, the ISA temperature and the density height.
[[pause 10]]
The pressure height: {1013|ten thirteen} minus {1006|ten oh six}, times 30, is 210. 650 plus 210 is 860 ft. The ISA temperature at 860 ft is 15 minus 1.7, which is 13.3 degrees. The deviation is 35 minus 13.3, which is plus 21.7. 120 times 21.7 is 2,604. Add 860, and the density height is about 3,460 ft. So a strip of 650 ft is behaving like one at nearly 3,500 ft, almost all of it because of the heat. The answer again: about 3,460 ft.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Get the sign of the QNH term right by asking what the altimeter would do. With low pressure, the altimeter set to {1013|ten thirteen} reads higher than the elevation. So a QNH below {1013|ten thirteen} adds to the elevation. The common mistake is to work out the QNH minus {1013|ten thirteen}, and get the sign backwards.

## Height, elevation, altitude, QNH and QFE (2.2.5)

Three words describe vertical distance. The difference between them is simply where you measure from.

Height is the vertical distance from a specified datum, usually the ground or the aerodrome. It is written AGL, above ground level. Your 1,000 ft circuit is a height.

Elevation is the vertical distance of a point on the surface, such as a runway, a hilltop or the base of a mast, above mean sea level. Jandakot's runway has an elevation. An aeroplane never does.

Altitude is the vertical distance of an aircraft, or a level, above mean sea level, written AMSL. Your cruise at 4,500 ft is an altitude.

In the circuit the three are tied together. Altitude equals the aerodrome's elevation, plus your height above it.

[[diagram:height-altitude-elevation]]
Picture a side view, with the sea on the left, and an aerodrome sitting 300 ft above it. That is its elevation. An aeroplane in the circuit, with QNH set, reads an altitude of 1,300 ft, so its height above the aerodrome is 1,000 ft. Fly the same altitude over a 900 ft ridge, and it is only 400 ft above the ground. What to notice: the altimeter on QNH tells you altitude, not height.

The altimeter is a barometer. It measures the pressure outside, and converts it to feet using the ISA, counting from whatever pressure you set on the subscale. Choose the subscale setting, and you choose the datum.

QNH is the setting that makes the altimeter read altitude. On the ground, it reads the aerodrome elevation. It is the pressure the forecaster works out for mean sea level at that place. It is used in Australia below the transition altitude of 10,000 ft. You get it from the ATIS, the AWIS, the METAR, or, for the area, the forecast. Because charts give terrain and obstacle elevations above sea level, QNH lets you compare your altimeter directly with the chart.

QFE is the setting that makes the altimeter read zero on the aerodrome. So in flight, it shows height above the aerodrome. It is the actual pressure at aerodrome level. Since pressure falls about 1 hPa per 30 ft, the QFE is lower than the QNH by the elevation divided by 30. So the QFE is about the QNH, minus the elevation divided by 30.

With {1013.2|ten thirteen point two} set, the altimeter reads pressure height. That is how flight levels above the transition level are flown.

You may also see QFF on weather maps. It is the station pressure reduced to mean sea level using the actual temperature, and it is what the isobars on a synoptic chart show. It is a forecaster's quantity, not an altimeter setting.

Let's work two. First, a coastal aerodrome with an elevation of 300 ft, and a QNH of {1015 hPa|ten fifteen hectopascals}. What is the QFE?
[[pause 5]]
300 divided by 30 is 10 hPa. {1015|Ten fifteen} minus 10 is {1005 hPa|ten oh five hectopascals}. With {1005|ten oh five} set, the altimeter reads zero on the runway, and 1,000 ft at circuit height.

Second, an inland aerodrome with an elevation of 1,200 ft, and a QNH of {1010 hPa|ten ten hectopascals}.
[[pause 5]]
1,200 divided by 30 is 40 hPa. {1010|Ten ten} minus 40 is 970 hPa. The higher the aerodrome, the further the QFE sits below the QNH.

Setting the wrong QNH, or flying into lower pressure without updating it, gives an error of about 30 ft for every hectopascal. Fly from high pressure towards low pressure, with the old, higher QNH still set, and the altimeter over-reads. You are lower than it says.

Question. You are on the ground at an aerodrome with an elevation of 500 ft, on a day when the QNH is {1003|ten oh three}. What does the altimeter read with QNH set, with QFE set, and with {1013|ten thirteen} set?
[[pause 6]]
The answer: with QNH, 500 ft, the elevation. With QFE, zero. And with {1013|ten thirteen}, the pressure height. {1013|Ten thirteen} minus {1003|ten oh three} is 10, times 30 is 300, and 500 plus 300 is 800 ft. It is higher than the elevation, because the pressure is below standard.

## Bringing it together

It is a winter evening at Northam, after a day of showers. The {1800|eighteen hundred} observation reads {09/07|temperature nine, dew point seven}. The wind is calm, the skies are clearing, and the QNH is {1022|ten twenty-two}.

The 2 degree spread and the clear sky tell you the ground will radiate freely overnight. The chilled air at runway level will drop below the screen temperature and reach its dew point. And a radiation inversion will trap the result in the Avon valley, as fog at dawn.

Your {0700|oh seven hundred} departure is unlikely. You plan for mid-morning, when the sun has heated the ground, the inversion has broken, and the fog has cleared.

When you do go, the day is kind for performance. With an elevation of about 500 ft and a QNH of {1022|ten twenty-two}, the pressure height is about 230 ft. A 12 degree morning is close to ISA, so the density height is a few hundred feet at most. And climbing out on QNH, your altitude is what you compare with the Darling Scarp's elevations on the chart.

## Key points

Let's recap.

Surface temperature is measured in the shade, 1.2 m above grass, in a ventilated screen. Above sunlit bitumen the air is hotter, so performance is worse than the METAR suggests. On clear, calm nights the runway air is colder, so frost and fog can form before the screen shows saturation.

An inversion is a layer where temperature rises with height. It is very stable, traps haze, smoke and fog, is smooth beneath, and has shear at its top. There are radiation, subsidence, frontal and turbulence inversions. Stability compares the ELR with the DALR, 3 degrees, and the SALR, about 1.5 degrees per 1,000 ft.

Saturated air holds all the vapour it can. Relative humidity is the vapour present, as a percentage of that maximum, and it rises as air cools. The dew point is the temperature at which the air would saturate, and the spread measures how close it is.

Evaporation absorbs latent heat, and cools. Condensation and freezing release it. Supercooled droplets cause airframe icing.

Density falls with higher temperature, lower pressure and higher humidity. High, hot and humid is the worst case.

Pressure height equals the elevation, plus {1013|ten thirteen} minus the QNH, times 30. The ISA temperature is 15, minus 2 times the pressure height in thousands. And density height adds about 120 ft per degree above ISA.

Height is above a datum, AGL. Elevation is a surface point above mean sea level. Altitude is an aircraft above mean sea level. QNH reads altitude, and the elevation on the ground. QFE reads zero on the aerodrome, and is about the QNH minus the elevation divided by 30. {1013|Ten thirteen} reads pressure height.

A final exam tip. Have these by heart: 30 ft per hectopascal, 2 degrees per 1,000 ft, 3 degrees for the DALR, about 1.5 degrees for the SALR, and 120 ft per degree. And remember "high to low, look out below". Flying from high to low pressure without resetting the QNH, the altimeter over-reads, and you are lower than indicated, by about 30 ft per hectopascal.

That's the end of PMTC 2.2.
