This is PMTC 2.7, Flight considerations. By the end you will know, for each weather hazard the syllabus names, what makes it and what ends it, how to recognise it, what it does to the aeroplane, and what you do about it. You will also know when a forecast is compulsory, how to test a TAF against your arrival time, and when you must make a special AIREP. There are worked examples and questions along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (PMTC 2.7)

The exam wants four things from you. First, for six phenomena, what makes them form and die away, how to recognise them, what they do to the aeroplane, and what the pilot does about it. The six are turbulence, wind shear, mountain waves, land and sea breezes, thunderstorms, and down-draughts from terrain and cloud. Second, when the rules make it compulsory to obtain a forecast. Third, how to decode the forecasts and reports into plain language, decide whether a forecast is valid for your flight, and use it to plan and fly. Those are the GAF, which the rules still call the ARFOR, the TAF, the TTF, the METAR and SPECI, and the AIRMET and SIGMET. And fourth, the conditions that oblige you to make a special AIREP.

## Why this matters

The RPL lessons taught you to recognise the hazards close to home, and to decode a TAF group by group. As a private pilot you take that further. A flight to Busselton as a front comes through. A crossing of the Stirling Range in a strong northerly. A February afternoon with storms building over the wheatbelt.

Now the question is not "what does this group mean?" It is "can I go, when, and what will I do if the forecast is wrong?"

The exam tests the causes and signs of each hazard, the legal duty to get a forecast, the decoding of real messages against an arrival time, and the special AIREP list. This lesson takes each in turn, and ends with a go or no-go decision.

## Turbulence and down-draughts

Turbulence is irregular motion of the air. Each kind has its own cause, so each has its own conditions, signs and cure. Here they are as the exam's three questions.

First, what makes it, and what ends it. Mechanical turbulence comes from strong wind over rough ground, hills and buildings, and it eases as the wind drops. Thermal turbulence comes from strong surface heating of unstable air, and it dies away in the evening as the heating stops. Frontal and wind shear turbulence come where two airstreams meet, and pass with the front or the shear.

Second, the signs. Turbulence groups in the GAF. Gusts in the TAF and METAR. Cumulus, where taller and more ragged means stronger. Dust devils. And ragged, fast-moving low cloud.

Third, the effect and the cure. Turbulence gives height, airspeed and attitude excursions. Every gust changes the angle of attack, and so the load factor. Flying fast, a gust can overstress the airframe. Flying slow, it can stall the wing.

So slow to the turbulence speed, at or below VA. That is the speed at which the wing stalls before it can be overstressed. Hold attitude, not altitude. Chasing height with the elevator adds your loads to the gust's. Keep the belts tight, and avoid the lee of hills in strong wind.

A down-draught is the sinking half of the same motions. Two kinds can overpower a light aeroplane.

The first is in the lee of hills and ridges in a strong wind, where air pours down the far side. The second is beneath and beside cumulonimbus and large cumulus, and under virga and rain shafts, where rain-cooled air sinks.

The signs are the lee of a ridge, lenticular cloud, dark rain shafts, virga, and a falling airspeed and climb rate at full power. The sink can exceed the aeroplane's climb. So cross ridges with height in hand, keep away from the lee in strong wind, and stay clear of storm cells and virga.

[[diagram:rotor-in-the-lee-of-the-scarp]]
Picture a west to east cross-section near Perth, in a strong easterly. The wind flows across the plateau on the right, over the edge of the Darling Scarp, and down onto the coastal plain on the left. There it rolls into a rotor of tumbling, turbulent air on the lee, western, side of the Scarp. The same happens on a smaller scale downwind of a hangar, buildings and trees. What to notice: the down-draught and rotor are on the lee side, so approach a ridge in strong wind with height in hand, from upwind.

## Wind shear

Wind shear is a sudden change in wind speed or direction over a short distance. Its danger rests on one idea from the RPL lessons. The aeroplane's inertia holds its groundspeed for a moment. So a change in wind becomes a change in airspeed, and with it lift. A loss of headwind costs airspeed, and the aeroplane sinks. A gain of headwind adds airspeed, and it floats.

What makes it, and what ends it? There are five sources. Thunderstorms and microbursts. Fronts. Low-level inversions, with a nocturnal jet above a calm surface. Sea breeze fronts. And terrain. Wind shear disperses with its cause. The inversion burns off by mid-morning, the front passes, the storm dies.

The signs are cumulonimbus and virga, a frontal wind change, and a strong wind above a calm surface. For example, the GPWT shows 25 kt at 2,000 ft while the windsock hangs limp. And reports of shear on the ATIS or from other pilots.

The effect is a sudden change of airspeed and lift near the ground, where there is no height to recover. The cure: add a speed margin, be ready to go around at full power, and never land through a storm outflow.

[[diagram:wind-shear-on-approach]]
Picture a side view of an approach into a strong headwind that decreases near the ground. As the headwind falls away, inertia keeps the groundspeed, so the indicated airspeed drops. The aeroplane sinks below the approach path, and undershoots, with no change in attitude or power. So in strong winds, add a speed margin, and be ready with power.

## Mountain waves

When a strong wind blows across a ridge in stable air, the air pushed up and over the crest is denser than its new surroundings. It sinks back down the lee side, overshoots, rises again, and sinks again. That is a standing wave downwind of the ridge, like water flowing over a rock in a stream. The waves stay put, while the air rushes through them.

What makes it? Four things. Stable air. A wind of about 20 kt or more. A wind within about 30 degrees of perpendicular to the ridge. And a wind that increases with height, and is steady in direction. The stable air supplies the restoring force, and the strong cross-ridge wind supplies the push. When the wind drops, or turns along the ridge, or the air becomes unstable and mixes, the waves die away.

The signs. Smooth, lens-shaped lenticular cloud, sitting still at the wave crests. A cap cloud draped on the ridge. Ragged rotor, or roll, cloud in the lee, at about ridge height. And dust lifted downwind. In dry air, there may be no cloud at all.

The effect. In the lee, the down-draughts can exceed the climb rate of a light aeroplane. Beneath the crests, the rotor gives severe turbulence. And over the crest, the strong wind is accelerated and its pressure lowered. So the altimeter may over-read. You are lower than it shows, just when you need the margin.

The pilot action. Cross ridges well above them, 2,000 ft or more. Cross at 45 degrees to the ridge, so that you can turn away downhill quickly. And stay upwind of the rotor. In Western Australia, waves and rotor form over the Stirling Range, and in the lee of the Darling Scarp in strong easterlies.

[[diagram:mountain-wave-lenticular-rotor]]
Picture a strong wind blowing from left to right across a ridge. A cap cloud sits on the crest. Downwind, the airflow sinks steeply down the lee slope, then rises and sinks again in a standing wave. Smooth lenticular clouds sit still at the wave crests. Under each crest, at about ridge height, a rotor of severe turbulence turns, with ragged roll cloud. What to notice: lift on the upwind side of each wave crest, sink on the downwind side and down the lee slope, and rotor under the crests.

Question. Why do mountain waves need stable air, when thunderstorms need unstable air?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: in stable air, a parcel pushed up the ridge is colder than its surroundings. So it sinks back, overshoots, and rises again, setting up the wave. In unstable air, the lifted parcel keeps rising on its own. So the energy goes into convection and storms, instead of a standing wave.

## Land and sea breezes

You know the mechanism from the lesson on winds. By day, the land heats faster than the sea, and cool sea air flows in at the surface. At night, the land cools below the sea, and a weaker land breeze blows offshore.

What makes them, and what ends them? A sunny day, a weak pressure gradient, and a land and sea temperature contrast. The sea breeze fades at sunset, and the land breeze takes over at night. A strong gradient wind suppresses both. It holds the sea breeze offshore, which is why the Fremantle Doctor is late on the hottest easterly days.

The signs. A line of cumulus inland, along the sea breeze front. Smoke and windsocks reversing. Ripples on the water. And a sudden drop in temperature.

The effect and the cure. A wind change at coastal aerodromes, the Fremantle Doctor at Jandakot and Rottnest, so a crosswind or tailwind on the old runway. Shear and turbulence at the front. And haze trapped under the marine inversion. Expect a runway change, and check the ATIS or AWIS before joining.

[[diagram:sea-breeze-front]]
Picture a cross-section of the west coast, with the sea on the left. Cool sea air pushes inland under the warm land air, as a shallow wedge, and returns seaward aloft. What to notice: the wind shift, the shear, and any cumulus line are at its leading edge.

## Thunderstorms

A thunderstorm needs three things: moisture, instability, and a trigger. The trigger can be heating, a front, a trough, terrain, or convergence. It lives through three stages. And it dissipates when its own down-draughts cut off the warm inflow that feeds the updraught.

[[diagram:thunderstorm-life-cycle]]
Picture three side views of the same storm cell, left to right, over time. First, the cumulus stage, about 15 minutes. A growing towering cumulus, with updraughts throughout, and no rain at the surface yet. Second, the mature stage, about 15 to 30 minutes, the most dangerous. It begins when rain reaches the ground. Updraughts and down-draughts sit side by side, in a cumulonimbus with an anvil, heavy rain, hail, lightning, and a gust front spreading out ahead. And third, the dissipating stage. Down-draughts throughout, the rain easing, and the anvil left behind.

The signs. Towering cumulus and cumulonimbus. An anvil. Lightning. TS and CB in the TAF, METAR and GAF. SIGMETs. And crackling static on the ADF.

The hazards. Severe turbulence, and structural overstress. Hail, including in clear air under the anvil. Lightning. Severe icing above the freezing level. Heavy rain, and near-zero visibility. Squalls, and the gust front, which can run 15 to 20 nm ahead of the storm. And microbursts.

[[diagram:thunderstorm-hazards]]
Picture a mature cell from the side. Inside and near it, severe turbulence, hail, lightning, icing and heavy rain. Beneath it, a microburst hits the ground and spreads out as a gust front, causing wind shear on take-off and landing. What to notice: the danger reaches well outside the cloud. Keep at least 10 nm from a mature cell, and never fly under the anvil.

A microburst is a concentrated down-draught, less than about 4 km across, that hits the ground and spreads out. It lasts only about 5 to 15 minutes, with outflow winds that can exceed 50 kt. Look for virga, a rain shaft with a curling foot, or a ring of blowing dust.

[[diagram:microburst-encounter]]
Picture an aeroplane on approach, flying from left to right through a microburst beneath a storm. First it meets the outflow as a headwind. The airspeed and lift go up, and it floats above the path. Then it meets the down-draught, and it sinks. Then it meets the outflow as a tailwind. The airspeed and lift go down, with the ground close. What to notice: headwind, then down-draught, then tailwind. The first airspeed gain is the warning, not a gift.

The pilot action. Stay 10 nm or more from a mature cell. Never fly under the anvil, or through a line of storms. And do not take off or land under or near a storm. If you meet the first airspeed gain near a storm, go around immediately with full power. Reducing power to correct the float is the instinctive response. And it leaves you slow and low when the tailwind arrives.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. The mature stage starts when rain reaches the ground, not when the anvil appears. A cell that has just started raining is at its most dangerous, even if it looks less dramatic than the old anvil drifting off a dying storm.

Question. You are 5 nm from a storm, in clear air under its anvil. Why is that not a safe place to be?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: hail can fall from the anvil into clear air, well away from the rain shaft. And the down-draughts and gust front spread out beyond the visible cloud. That is why the stand-off is 10 nm from the cell, and never under the anvil.

## Icing

Icing is not one of the six phenomena. But it is a flight consideration in every forecast you read, because the GAF gives the freezing level, and the icing in cloud.

Structural icing needs visible moisture, cloud or rain, at temperatures at or below 0 degrees on the airframe. Cloud droplets can stay liquid well below freezing. They are supercooled, and they freeze the moment they strike the aeroplane. What builds depends on the drop size. There are three types.

First, clear ice. It forms from large supercooled drops, at 0 to minus 10 degrees in cumulus and cumulonimbus, or in freezing rain. Large drops freeze slowly, so the water runs back before it sets into a hard, heavy, clear sheet over the wing. It is the most dangerous.

Second, rime ice. It forms from small drops, at about minus 10 to minus 20 degrees, in stratiform cloud. Small drops freeze instantly, trapping air. So rime is white and brittle, and builds forward on the leading edges into a rough shape.

And third, hoar frost. It forms in clear air, on an aeroplane parked overnight, or one descending from cold air into warm, moist air. It is feathery. On a parked aeroplane, it must be removed before flight.

Either way, ice adds weight and drag, destroys lift, and raises the stall speed. That is covered in the lesson on stalling, spinning and spiral dives. A VFR pilot in a light aeroplane keeps out of cloud, and notes the freezing level in the GAF.

[[diagram:airframe-icing-types]]
Picture two wing sections side by side, with the airflow coming from the left. On the left, clear ice: a smooth, heavy sheet that has run back over the top of the wing. On the right, rime ice: a white, rough shape built forward on the leading edge. What to notice: large drops run back before they freeze into hard, heavy clear ice, while small drops freeze on impact into white rime.

Carburettor icing is a different problem. It forms inside the engine, in clear air, on a warm, humid day. The fuel evaporating, and the pressure drop at the throttle, cool the air well below the outside temperature. It is possible over roughly minus 10 to plus 30 degrees outside air temperature. It is worst with high humidity, and most likely at low power. The full story is in the lesson on engine handling. Use carburettor heat as your flight manual directs.

[[diagram:carburettor-ice-temperature-ranges]]
Picture a temperature scale running left to right, with a bar for each kind of engine ice. Throttle ice spans roughly minus 5 to plus 20 degrees, at low power. Fuel evaporation ice spans roughly minus 10 to plus 30, at any power. And impact ice needs visible moisture, at or below freezing. What to notice: carburettor ice needs humidity, not freezing air. It forms from well below zero to about 30 degrees.

Question. It is a clear, humid autumn morning, with an outside air temperature of 18 degrees, and you face a long descent at low power into Busselton. Which icing should you be thinking about, and why?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: carburettor icing. There is no visible moisture, and the air is above freezing, so structural icing is impossible. But evaporating fuel, and the pressure drop at the nearly closed throttle, can cool humid air below freezing inside the carburettor. And low power makes it most likely.

## When a forecast is mandatory

Under chapter 7 of the Part 91 Manual of Standards, the pilot in command must, before every flight, study authorised weather forecasts and reports. They must cover the route, the departure aerodrome, the destination, and any alternate.

If you studied them more than 1 hour before departure, you must obtain and review an update. And the destination forecast must cover at least 30 minutes before, to 60 minutes after, your ETA.

There is one exception. If no authorised forecast is available, you may depart, if you reasonably consider that the weather at the departure aerodrome will allow you to return and land there within 1 hour of take-off. You must then obtain a forecast for the destination within 30 minutes of take-off, or return. Verify these figures against the current Part 91 Manual of Standards before the exam.

The logic of each figure is protection against stale or missing information. Weather changes, so an hour-old briefing is updated. An ETA is an estimate, so the forecast must cover a window around it.

Question. You study the forecasts at {0700|oh seven hundred}, and depart at {0830|oh eight thirty}. What must you do before you go?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: obtain and review an update. More than 1 hour has passed since you studied the forecasts. The rules require a fresh look, so that you are not flying on stale information.

## Using the products

Every product has a job, a datum and a validity. And a forecast is only useful if it covers your flight. There are five to know.

First, the GAF. It replaced the ARFOR in {2017|twenty seventeen}, although the Manual of Standards still says ARFOR. It is the area forecast for en route weather. Its cloud heights are above mean sea level, and it is valid for 6 hours.

Second, the TAF, the aerodrome forecast. Its cloud heights are above aerodrome level. Check that its validity period covers your ETA window.

Third, the TTF, the trend forecast. It is attached to the METAR or SPECI at major aerodromes. It is valid for 3 hours from the observation, and it supersedes the TAF for that period.

Fourth, the METAR and SPECI. These are observations, not forecasts. They tell you whether the forecast is coming true.

And fifth, the SIGMET and AIRMET. A SIGMET covers significant en route hazards: thunderstorms, severe turbulence, severe icing, severe mountain waves, volcanic ash, and tropical cyclones. It is valid for up to 4 hours. An AIRMET covers hazards to low-level flights below 10,000 ft that are not already in the area forecast.

The decoding itself is in the RPL lesson on forecasts and reports. Two definitions decide most go or no-go questions. TEMPO spells last 30 to 60 minutes each. INTER spells last less than 30 minutes. In both, the worse conditions can be there at any moment in the period. You cannot choose to arrive in a gap.

[[diagram:taf-change-groups]]
Picture a timeline running left to right. The main conditions start at the left edge. Partway along, an FM group replaces them for good. Over the first part of the line, a TEMPO bar lays spells of showers over the background. What to notice: FM is permanent, TEMPO spells last 30 to 60 minutes, and INTER spells less than 30. Plan for the worst group that touches your window.

Applying a TAF takes three steps. First, validity. Does the TAF cover the ETA window? Second, conditions. For each group that touches the window, is it VMC for your class of airspace? And third, hazards. Is there a thunderstorm, a CB, a gust, or a wind change that matters, even if it is VMC?

## Special AIREPs

Routine AIREPs are not normally required of VFR pilots. But you are often the only observer of the weather where you are. A special AIREP must be made as soon as practicable when you meet any of six things.

First, severe turbulence. Second, severe icing. Third, severe mountain wave. Fourth, thunderstorms, with or without hail, that are obscured, embedded, widespread, or in squall lines. Fifth, a heavy dust storm or sand storm. And sixth, a volcanic ash cloud, or pre-eruption volcanic activity.

Each is a hazard that could bring down the next aircraft, and most are things the forecasters cannot see from the ground.

You should also report any other weather that may affect other aircraft. For example, moderate turbulence or icing, wind shear, or unforecast low cloud or poor visibility. But those are a should, not a must.

The report goes to ATC or FLIGHTWATCH. It gives the aircraft identification, position, time, level, the phenomenon, and, if possible, the outside air temperature and wind.

Question. Why does the special AIREP list include embedded thunderstorms, but not an isolated cumulonimbus you can see from 30 nm?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: an isolated, visible cumulonimbus is already seen by every pilot nearby, and it is usually in the forecast. Embedded or obscured storms are hidden in cloud. So a pilot's report may be the only warning the next aircraft, and the forecasters, get.

## Bringing it together

It is a Saturday in May: Jandakot to Busselton, departing {0830|oh eight thirty} WST, which is {0030|zero zero thirty} UTC. The GAF shows a cold front reaching the coast in the afternoon, with cumulonimbus and moderate turbulence along it.

You study the forecasts at {0715|oh seven fifteen} WST. You check them again just before departure, because more than an hour has passed.

The Busselton TAF covers your arrival window, with a strengthening north-westerly and broken cloud at 3,000 ft. That is VMC. Its TEMPO for thunderstorms starts in the afternoon, so you plan to return by early afternoon.

En route, the north-westerly over the Darling Range is bumpy. You slow to the turbulence speed, and hold attitude.

On the way home, the line of cumulonimbus is visible to the west. You keep well over 10 nm from it. At Jandakot, the ATIS shows a gusty wind, so you add half the gust factor. And had you met severe turbulence, you would have made a special AIREP.

## Key points

Let's recap.

Turbulence is mechanical, from strong wind over rough ground, thermal, from heating, or frontal. It eases as the wind drops or the heating stops. Slow to VA or below, hold attitude, and avoid the lee of hills.

Wind shear comes from storms, fronts, inversions, sea breeze fronts and terrain. Inertia turns a wind change into an airspeed change. Add a margin, and be ready to go around at full power.

Mountain waves need stable air, and a wind of 20 kt or more, within about 30 degrees of perpendicular to the ridge, increasing with height. Look for lenticular, cap and rotor cloud. Expect sink beyond the climb rate, rotor turbulence, and an altimeter that over-reads. Cross 2,000 ft or more above, at 45 degrees, upwind of the rotor.

Land and sea breezes need a sunny day and a weak gradient. A strong gradient suppresses them. Look for a cumulus line, and reversing smoke and windsocks. Expect a runway change, shear at the front, and trapped haze.

Thunderstorms need moisture, instability and a trigger. The mature stage, with rain at the ground, is the worst. The gust front runs 15 to 20 nm ahead. A microburst is under 4 km across, lasts 5 to 15 minutes, and can exceed 50 kt. Stay 10 nm away, never under the anvil. And at the first airspeed gain, go around at full power.

Down-draughts come in the lee of ridges, and beneath cumulonimbus, cumulus, virga and rain shafts. They can exceed the climb rate.

Structural icing needs visible moisture at or below 0 degrees. Clear ice from 0 to minus 10, rime from minus 10 to minus 20, and hoar frost in clear air. Carburettor ice forms from about minus 10 to plus 30 degrees, in humid air.

Study authorised forecasts and reports before every flight. Update them if they are more than 1 hour old. The destination forecast must cover 30 minutes before to 60 minutes after the ETA. And remember the exception: the 1-hour return, and the 30-minute forecast.

The GAF is above mean sea level, and valid for 6 hours. The TAF is above the aerodrome. The TTF lasts 3 hours, and supersedes the TAF. The SIGMET lasts up to 4 hours. The AIRMET covers below 10,000 ft. Test validity, then conditions, then hazards, for every group touching the window.

And the special AIREP: severe turbulence, severe icing, severe mountain wave, obscured, embedded, widespread or squall-line thunderstorms, a heavy dust or sand storm, and volcanic ash.

A final exam tip. Mountain waves need stable air and a strong wind across the ridge. Thunderstorms need unstable, moist air and a trigger. The worst thunderstorm stage is the mature stage, which starts when rain reaches the ground. For TAF questions, work out the ETA window, 30 minutes before to 60 minutes after, and test every TEMPO and INTER group that touches it. And the special AIREP list is about severe conditions. Moderate turbulence alone is a should, not a must.

That's the end of PMTC 2.7.
