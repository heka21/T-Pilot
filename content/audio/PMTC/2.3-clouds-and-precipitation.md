This is PMTC 2.3, Clouds and precipitation. By the end you will know how cloud forms, the five ways air is lifted, the ten cloud genera with their abbreviations, how cloud amount and height are reported, and what weather each cloud brings. You will also be able to estimate the base of cumulus from the temperature and dew point in your head. There are worked examples and questions along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (PMTC 2.3)

The exam wants three things from you. First, how to name and classify clouds, by the height of their base and by the ten genera. Second, the standard abbreviation for each cloud type, and how cloud amount is reported. And third, the weather that goes with each type of cloud.

## Why this matters

The sky is a weather report you can read without a briefing. A morning of turreted altocumulus over the wheatbelt warns of storms by afternoon. A halo round the sun over Rottnest says a front is on its way. And a flat sheet of stratus clinging to the Darling Scarp says the route to York is closed for now.

Each cloud is the visible end of a process: air rising and cooling in a particular way. Once you know the process, you know the weather that comes with it. The bumps, the rain, the icing and the visibility.

The exam asks you to classify clouds by height and genus, to decode cloud groups, to match each cloud with its weather, and to estimate a cumulus base from the temperature and dew point.

## How cloud forms: cooling air to its dew point

You met the essentials in the last lesson. Air can hold only so much water vapour. The dew point is the temperature at which it would become saturated. And cooling it below that point makes some of the vapour condense.

A cloud is simply a mass of tiny water droplets or ice crystals, each formed this way. Each one is so small that the slightest upward movement of air keeps it aloft.

The droplets need something to form on. The air is full of microscopic particles of sea salt, dust and smoke. Vapour condenses readily onto these condensation nuclei, so cloud forms as soon as the air saturates.

So the question "why is there a cloud here?" always becomes "what cooled this air to its dew point?" Near the ground, the answer can be contact with cold ground, which gives fog. That is in the lesson on visibility. In the free air, the answer is almost always lifting.

Rising air moves into lower pressure, expands, and cools adiabatically. While it is unsaturated, it cools at the dry adiabatic lapse rate, the DALR, of 3 degrees per 1,000 ft. Once cloud has formed, it cools at the saturated rate, the SALR, of about 1.5 degrees per 1,000 ft, because condensation releases latent heat. The level at which a rising parcel first reaches its dew point is the condensation level. And that is the cloud base.

## Five ways to lift air

Air rises for one of five reasons, and each leaves a signature in the shape of its cloud.

The first is convection. On a hot afternoon the sun heats the ground, and the ground heats the air touching it. Bubbles of warm, light air break away and rise as thermals. Each thermal cools as it rises, and where it reaches its dew point, a cumulus cloud forms on top of it.

The thermals over one area start with much the same temperature and moisture. So they reach their dew point at much the same height. That is why a field of cumulus has flat bases at one level, with blue sky between the clouds where the air is sinking. This is the cloud of a summer afternoon inland, from fair-weather cumulus to towering cumulus and cumulonimbus.

[[diagram:visible-signs-of-thermals]]
Picture a hot afternoon over bare ground. Bubbles of warm air rise as thermals, and build cumulus clouds with flat bases and growing tops. Between the thermals, the air sinks. Lower down, a dust devil over the bare ground is a visible thermal: a narrow, strong updraught with sharp wind changes around it. Smoke rising steadily then breaking up, and birds soaring in circles, also mark thermals. What to notice: every cumulus sits on top of a thermal, and the flat bases mark the level where the rising air reaches its dew point.

The second is orographic lifting. When wind meets a slope or a range, the air has to go up and over. A moist westerly behind a winter front meets the Darling Scarp, and is forced up a thousand feet or so. If it reaches its dew point on the way, it caps the hills in a layer of stratus or stratocumulus, while the coastal plain stays clear. The cloud sits over the high ground on the windward side. That is exactly where the terrain is closest to you.

In the lee of a range in a strong wind, the air can oscillate in standing waves. Smooth, lens-shaped lenticular clouds form in the crests. The RPL lesson on the significance of observations explains the turbulence they mark.

[[diagram:low-cloud-and-rising-terrain]]
Picture a side view. A VFR aeroplane flies inland, under a cloud base that lowers ahead, while the ground rises towards hills. The gap between cloud and terrain narrows, until there is no room to stay clear of cloud and above the minimum height. The safe choice is to turn back early, while there is still room. Pressing on under the cloud into rising ground, which is called scud running, is a leading cause of fatal VFR accidents. What to notice: orographic cloud forms where the ground rises, so the gap closes just where you need it.

The third is frontal lifting. Where warm and cold air masses meet, the lighter warm air ends up on top. At a warm front, it slides gently up over a wedge of cold air, over hundreds of kilometres, and gives widespread layer cloud. At a cold front, the advancing cold air drives under the warm air like a snowplough, and lifts it steeply. That gives a narrow band of cumulus and cumulonimbus. The details belong to the lesson on air masses and fronts.

The fourth is convergence. When air flows in from different directions, it cannot pile up at the surface, so it goes up. This happens in lows and troughs. And it happens on a small scale along the sea breeze front, where cool sea air pushes inland under the warm land air, and lifts it. A line of cumulus a few miles inland from the coast on a summer afternoon marks the sea breeze front.

[[diagram:sea-breeze-front]]
Picture a cross-section of the west coast on a warm afternoon, with the sea on the left. The land heats, and warm air rises over it. Cool, moist sea air flows onshore underneath as the sea breeze, returns seaward aloft, and sinks over the sea. Where the cool sea air meets the warm inland air, it pushes in as a shallow wedge. That is the sea breeze front, marked by cumulus and by wind shear. What to notice: the line of cumulus marks where the two airs meet.

And the fifth is turbulence, or mechanical mixing. A strong wind over rough ground stirs the lowest few thousand feet thoroughly. Moist air from near the surface is carried up, and cools at the DALR. If the mixing reaches the condensation level, a layer of cloud forms at the top of the mixed layer. With an inversion above acting as a lid, the result is a flat, often lumpy sheet of stratus or stratocumulus.

So the lifting process starts the air moving. Stability decides what it does next. In the last lesson you saw that a parcel in stable air, nudged upwards, ends up colder than its surroundings, and wants to sink back.

Lift stable air, and it rises only as far as it is pushed. So the cloud that forms spreads sideways in a flat sheet. That is stratiform cloud. Lift unstable air, and once it is warmer than its surroundings, it keeps rising on its own. The cloud grows upwards in heaps and towers. That is cumuliform cloud.

That one distinction carries through the rest of the lesson. Layer cloud means stable air: smooth flying, steady precipitation, and often poor visibility. Heap cloud means unstable air: bumps, showers, and good visibility between them. The same orographic lifting over the Scarp gives a flat cap of stratus on a stable winter day, and towering cumulus on an unstable one.

Question. Why does a field of fair-weather cumulus have flat bases, all at one height?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: each cloud sits on a thermal, and the thermals over the same ground start with roughly the same temperature and dew point. Rising and cooling at the same rate, they all reach their dew point at about the same height. That condensation level is the flat base.

## Estimating the cumulus base from the dew point spread

The METAR gives you two numbers that together predict the base of the afternoon's cumulus: the temperature and the dew point.

A thermal leaves the ground at the surface temperature, carrying air with a certain dew point. As it rises unsaturated, it cools at the DALR, 3 degrees per 1,000 ft. Its dew point also falls, but only slowly, about 0.5 degrees per 1,000 ft, because the expanding air spreads its vapour through a larger volume.

So the temperature is catching up on the dew point at about 3 minus 0.5, which is 2.5 degrees per 1,000 ft. When the gap reaches zero, the air is saturated, and the cloud base is there. Every degree of spread at the surface takes 1,000 divided by 2.5, which is 400 ft of climb, to close.

That gives the rule of thumb. The cloud base, in feet above the ground, is about the temperature minus the dew point, times 400.

[[diagram:dew-point-and-cloud-base]]
Picture a chart of height against temperature, for a thermal leaving the ground at 24 degrees with a dew point of 16. Two lines slope up and to the left. The temperature line falls steeply, 3 degrees per 1,000 ft. The dew point line falls gently, only 0.5 degrees per 1,000 ft. So the gap closes at 2.5 degrees per 1,000 ft, which is 400 ft per degree. The two lines meet at about 3,200 ft and 14.4 degrees, where the air saturates. That is the cumulus base.

Let's work it as an example, a spring morning. The METAR gives {24/16|twenty-four stroke sixteen}: a temperature of 24 and a dew point of 16. Where will the cumulus base be? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 5]]
The spread is 24 minus 16, which is 8 degrees. It closes at 2.5 degrees per 1,000 ft, so 400 ft per degree. 8 times 400 is 3,200 ft above the ground. So when the thermals get going, expect cumulus bases about 3,200 ft above the aerodrome. Cruising at 4,500 ft would put you in or among the cloud. Plan to cruise below it, or above it with the VMC cloud clearances in mind.

Now a dry afternoon on the coast. Jandakot, early afternoon, reports {29/13|twenty-nine stroke thirteen}.
[[pause 5]]
The spread is 29 minus 13, which is 16 degrees. 16 times 400 is 6,400 ft above the ground. A high base, and plenty of room under it. The drier the air, the higher the base.

Now try one in the Goldfields. On a summer afternoon, Kalgoorlie reports {36/06|thirty-six stroke zero six}. Estimate the base of any convective cloud, and say what that base suggests about the storms that might form.
[[pause 8]]
The spread is 36 minus 6, which is 30 degrees. 30 times 400 gives a base of about 12,000 ft above the ground. Storms with bases that high are typical of the dry interior. Their rain falls through thousands of feet of very dry air, and evaporates before it reaches the ground. That is called virga. And the evaporation chills the air, and drives strong, dry downdraughts that raise dust at the surface. The answer again: a base of about 12,000 ft, with dry, gusty downdraughts.

Some texts simplify the rule. They divide the spread by 3, and multiply by 1,000, which is about 330 ft per degree. That version uses the DALR alone, and ignores the fall in dew point. So it gives a slightly lower base. In the Jandakot example, 16 divided by 3, times 1,000, is about 5,300 ft, instead of 6,400 ft. Use the method the question states. If it states none, 400 ft per degree is the physically sound figure.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. The rule works only for convective cloud: cumulus formed by thermals rising from the surface where the temperature and dew point were measured. It tells you nothing about the base of frontal or orographic cloud. And the answer is height above the ground at that station, not above sea level. A small spread, 2 or 3 degrees, means a low base, and possible fog.

Question. Why does each degree of spread give 400 ft of cloud base, rather than about 330 ft?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the rising air cools at 3 degrees per 1,000 ft, but its dew point also falls, by about 0.5 degrees per 1,000 ft. So the spread closes at only about 2.5 degrees per 1,000 ft, which is 400 ft per degree.

## The ten cloud genera (2.3.1, 2.3.2)

Cloud names rest on four Latin words. Cumulus means a heap: cloud built upwards by rising air. Stratus means a layer: cloud spread sideways. Cirrus means a curl, or a wisp of hair: the fibrous look of ice crystals. And nimbus means rain-bearing. To these, the prefix alto marks a cloud of the middle layer. It literally means high, but it is used for the middle level.

Combine the form with the height of the base, and you get the ten genera, each with a two-letter abbreviation. The height bands are approximate for Australian latitudes. They are higher in the tropics, where the troposphere is deeper.

[[diagram:cloud-families-by-height]]
Picture three bands of sky stacked one above the other, with two tall clouds standing on the right. The top band is high cloud, above about 20,000 ft. The middle band runs from about 6,500 to 20,000 ft. The bottom band is low cloud, below about 6,500 ft. And on the right, cumulus and cumulonimbus start low and grow tall, the cumulonimbus reaching the tropopause and spreading into an anvil. What to notice: three bands by base height, plus the two clouds that cross them all.

Start at the top, with high cloud, above about 20,000 ft. At these heights the air is far below freezing, so high cloud is made of ice crystals. It is thin, white and silky, and you can usually see the sun through it. There are three high clouds.

Cirrus, abbreviated {Ci|C I}: white wisps, streaks and hooks, sometimes called mares' tails. Cirrocumulus, {Cc|C C}: small white ripples or grains, with no shading. And cirrostratus, {Cs|C S}: a thin white veil. Its ice crystals bend sunlight into a halo round the sun or moon.

Next, middle cloud, about 6,500 to 20,000 ft. Middle cloud is mostly water droplets, often supercooled below 0 degrees. Again there are three.

Altocumulus, {Ac|eigh C}: grey-white rolls or patches, larger than cirrocumulus, and shaded. With small turrets rising from a common base, it is altocumulus castellanus.

Altostratus, {As|eigh S}: a grey or bluish sheet. The sun shows through it dimly, "as through ground glass", with no halo, because the cloud is made of droplets, not ice.

Nimbostratus, {Ns|N S}: a thick, dark, featureless layer, with continuous rain. It is classed as middle cloud, but its base often lowers to near the ground.

Then low cloud, below about 6,500 ft. There are two. Stratocumulus, {Sc|S C}: a grey layer of lumps, rolls or patches, often with blue sky between them. And stratus, {St|S T}: a uniform grey layer with a very low base. Where it touches high ground, the result is hill fog.

Last, the two clouds with a low base and great vertical extent. Cumulus, {Cu|C U}: separate heaps with flat bases and cauliflower tops. When the tops grow tall and bubbling, it is towering cumulus, reported as TCU. And cumulonimbus, {Cb|C B}: the thunderstorm cloud. A huge tower that can reach the tropopause, where it spreads into a flat anvil top.

[[diagram:thunderstorm-hazards]]
Picture a side view of a mature thunderstorm cell, with its anvil spreading at the top. Inside and near the cell there is severe turbulence, hail, lightning, icing in the cloud, and heavy rain. Beneath it, a microburst downdraught hits the ground, and spreads out as a gust front ahead of the storm, causing wind shear on take-off and landing. A light aeroplane stays at least 10 nm from a mature cell, and never flies under the anvil. What to notice: the cumulonimbus is the one cloud you never fly near.

So, the ten again, by level. High: cirrus, cirrocumulus and cirrostratus. Middle: altocumulus, altostratus and nimbostratus. Low: stratocumulus and stratus. And with a low base and great vertical extent: cumulus and cumulonimbus.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set: telling cirrostratus from altostratus. Both are featureless sheets with the sun showing through. The halo decides it. Cirrostratus is ice, and makes a halo. Altostratus is mainly water droplets, and does not, so the sun looks blurred, as if through frosted glass.

Question. Why is high cloud white and thin enough to see the sun through, while nimbostratus is dark?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: high cloud is made of sparse ice crystals, because the air up there is far below freezing. So light passes through, and is scattered white. Nimbostratus is a deep, dense mass of water droplets, which absorbs and blocks most of the light from above.

## Reporting cloud amount (2.3.2)

Two things are reported for each cloud layer: how much of the sky it covers, and how high its base is.

The amount is measured in oktas, eighths of the sky. Imagine the dome of the sky divided into eight equal slices. Count how many slices the cloud would fill if you pushed it all together.

[[diagram:cloud-amounts-in-oktas]]
Picture four sky domes in a row, each more filled with cloud than the last. The oktas are grouped into four codes. FEW is few: 1 to 2 oktas. SCT is scattered: 3 to 4 oktas. BKN is broken: 5 to 7 oktas. And OVC is overcast: 8 oktas. What to notice: only broken and overcast make a ceiling.

The base follows the amount, as three digits in hundreds of feet. So {SCT035|S C T zero three five} is scattered cloud, 3 to 4 oktas, based at 3,500 ft. A layer of broken or overcast is a ceiling. At that amount the sky is more cloud than gap, and you cannot count on finding a hole to climb or descend through.

The datum depends on the product, and this is where pilots slip. In a TAF or METAR, the base is above aerodrome level, because the people using it are about to land or take off there. In the GAF, the graphical area forecast, it is above mean sea level. The GAF covers a wide area of varying terrain, and your altimeter on QNH reads altitude.

[[diagram:cloud-height-datum]]
Picture one cloud base, measured two ways. On one side, the GAF measures it up from mean sea level. On the other, the TAF and METAR measure it up from the aerodrome. At an aerodrome above sea level, the TAF figure is smaller than the GAF figure for the same base, by the aerodrome elevation.

Only two cloud types are named in a TAF or METAR, because only two are a direct hazard: CB and TCU. They are added after the height. So {SCT050CB|S C T zero five zero, C B} is scattered cumulonimbus at 5,000 ft. Every other cloud is just amount and height. When there is no cloud that matters, the report says NSC, nil significant cloud. And an automatic station that sees nothing reports NCD, no cloud detected. Decoding a whole TAF or METAR is practised in the RPL lesson on forecasts and reports.

Let's work one. A METAR for Northam, elevation about 500 ft, has the cloud groups {FEW020 BKN045|few zero two zero, broken zero four five}. Is there a ceiling, and where is it above sea level?
[[pause 6]]
First, few zero two zero: 1 to 2 oktas, based at 2,000 ft above the aerodrome. Not a ceiling. Second, broken zero four five: 5 to 7 oktas, based at 4,500 ft above the aerodrome. This is the ceiling. Above sea level, the ceiling is 4,500 plus 500, which is 5,000 ft. So to compare it with a GAF, or a cruising altitude on QNH, add the elevation first. The answer again: a ceiling at 5,000 ft above sea level.

Here's another trap the exam likes to set. A TAF and a GAF giving the same base number do not describe the same cloud height, unless the aerodrome is at sea level. At an inland aerodrome, the TAF figure is smaller than the GAF figure for the same base, by the aerodrome elevation.

## Weather from each cloud (2.3.3)

Every cloud's weather follows from two things you now know. Whether it formed in stable or unstable air. And whether it is made of ice or water. Take the families in turn.

The cirrus family is thin ice cloud. So it gives no precipitation that reaches the ground, and no icing, because ice crystals do not stick to an airframe. Its turbulence is nil, although cirrocumulus may give light bumps. Its value is as a warning. Cirrus thickening into cirrostratus over a few hours is often the first sign of a warm front, or a jet stream.

Middle cloud is water, often supercooled, so it can ice an airframe. Altocumulus is usually dry, with light icing and light bumps. But altocumulus castellanus shows that the middle layer is unstable. Seen in the morning, it warns of thunderstorms, once surface heating adds its push from below.

Altostratus gives light continuous rain, light to moderate icing, and little turbulence. When it thickens and lowers through the day, a front is approaching. It becomes nimbostratus, the deep rain cloud of stable air. Nimbostratus gives hours of continuous moderate to heavy rain, poor visibility, and moderate to severe icing in the deep supercooled cloud. And ragged stratus fractus forms in the moist air beneath.

Low layer cloud is stable and shallow. Stratus gives drizzle, light icing and smooth air. But its threat is its base, low on the hills, with poor visibility. Stratocumulus gives light drizzle or light showers, and light icing. It is usually fair, with a bumpy ride below.

Heap cloud is unstable. Small, fair-weather cumulus gives no precipitation, only bumps. Large cumulus gives showers, moderate icing, and moderate turbulence below and in it. The visibility is good between the showers, because unstable air carries haze away.

And cumulonimbus brings the lot. Heavy showers, hail and thunderstorms. Severe icing and severe turbulence. Lightning, microbursts, squalls and gust fronts. Those are covered in the lesson on flight considerations.

Now the precipitation itself. The type of precipitation is a fingerprint of the cloud, and so of the air. There are five to know.

First, drizzle. It is very fine droplets falling close together. Small drops can only grow in shallow cloud with weak lift, so drizzle comes only from stratus and stratocumulus. It is a sign of a stable, moist, low-based layer, and often of poor visibility.

Second, continuous rain. It falls steadily for hours from nimbostratus and thick altostratus. Deep layer cloud, lifted gently over a wide area by a front, keeps producing it. It means stable air, and a large-scale system.

Third, showers. They start and stop suddenly, are often heavy while they last, and alternate with clear spells. They fall from cumulus and cumulonimbus, each shower being one cell. Showers mean unstable air.

Fourth, hail. Hail is ice that has been carried up and down through a cloud by strong updraughts, adding a layer each trip, until it is too heavy to hold. Only a cumulonimbus has updraughts strong enough to do that. So hail means cumulonimbus.

And fifth, virga. Virga is precipitation that evaporates before it reaches the ground. You see it as grey streaks hanging below a cloud base. It means dry air beneath the cloud. And because evaporation absorbs latent heat, it marks chilled air sinking: a downdraught.

Question. It has rained steadily all morning. But after lunch the rain turns into heavy bursts, with sunny spells between. What has changed about the air?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: steady rain comes from layer cloud in stable air, typically ahead of or at a front. Showers come from heap cloud in unstable air. So the change suggests the front has passed, and you are now in the colder, unstable air behind it.

## Bringing it together

It is a winter Saturday, and you plan to fly from Jandakot to Albany on Sunday.

In the afternoon, wisps of cirrus spread from the west. By evening they have merged into a milky veil, with a halo round the moon. That is cirrostratus, ice well above 20,000 ft, telling you a front is coming.

By Sunday morning the halo has gone, and the sun is a blur behind a grey sheet of altostratus. By mid-morning the base is low and dark. It is raining steadily, and stratus fractus is forming underneath. That is nimbostratus, with poor visibility, the hills in cloud, and icing above the freezing level. The flight is off.

By Monday the front has passed. There is cumulus with sunny gaps, and sharp showers, with excellent visibility outside them. Jandakot reports {17/09|seventeen stroke zero nine}. The 8 degree spread puts the cumulus base at about 3,200 ft above the ground. So you plan to cruise below it, where the terrain allows, and steer round the showers, provided no CB appears in the TAF.

Every cloud in that sequence is a lifting process, and a stability state, that you can now name.

Question. A TAF contains {SCT040CB|S C T zero four zero, C B}. What kind of air does that indicate, and what three hazards would you expect near the cloud?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: unstable air, because cumulonimbus is cumuliform cloud, growing upwards on its own buoyancy. Expect severe turbulence, hail and heavy showers, along with lightning, severe icing and microburst wind shear. That is the reason only CB and TCU are named in a TAF.

## Key points

Let's recap.

Cloud forms when lifting cools air to its dew point. The five lifting processes are convection, orographic, frontal, convergence and turbulence. Stable air gives layer cloud, and unstable air heap cloud.

The ten genera, by base. High, above about 20,000 ft: cirrus, cirrocumulus and cirrostratus. Middle, about 6,500 to 20,000 ft: altocumulus, altostratus and nimbostratus. Low, below about 6,500 ft: stratocumulus and stratus. And with a low base and great vertical extent: cumulus and cumulonimbus.

The abbreviations are {Ci|C I}, {Cc|C C}, {Cs|C S}, {Ac|eigh C}, {As|eigh S}, {Ns|N S}, {Sc|S C}, {St|S T}, {Cu|C U}, with TCU for towering cumulus, and {Cb|C B}.

Cloud amount is in oktas. FEW is 1 to 2, scattered 3 to 4, broken 5 to 7, and overcast 8. The base is in hundreds of feet. Broken and overcast are a ceiling. TAF and METAR bases are above the aerodrome, and GAF bases are above sea level. Only CB and TCU are named. NSC means nil significant cloud, and NCD means no cloud detected. And the convective cloud base is roughly the temperature minus the dew point, times 400 ft above the ground, as a rule of thumb.

The weather. Cirrus and cirrostratus herald fronts. Altocumulus castellanus warns of storms. Nimbostratus gives continuous rain and icing. Stratus gives drizzle and low bases. Cumulus gives showers and bumps. And cumulonimbus gives heavy showers, hail, thunderstorms, severe icing and turbulence. Drizzle comes from stratus and stratocumulus. Continuous rain from nimbostratus and altostratus. Showers from cumulus and cumulonimbus. Hail only from cumulonimbus. And virga means dry air and downdraughts.

A final exam tip. Showers come from heap cloud, continuous rain from layer cloud, and drizzle from stratus. Hail in a question means cumulonimbus, every time. A halo means cirrostratus. For a cloud base question, multiply the spread by 400 unless the question gives another method. And remember the answer is above the ground, for convective cloud only. In a TAF or METAR the base is above the aerodrome. In the GAF it is above sea level.

That's the end of PMTC 2.3.
