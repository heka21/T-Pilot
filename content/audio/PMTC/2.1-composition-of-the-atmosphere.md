This is PMTC 2.1, Composition of the atmosphere. By the end you will know what the air is made of, the figures of the International Standard Atmosphere by heart, how temperature and pressure fall as you climb, how the atmosphere is divided into layers, and why nearly all the weather lives in the lowest one. You will also be able to work out an ISA temperature and an ISA deviation in your head. There are a few calculations and questions along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (PMTC 2.1)

The exam wants four things from you. First, the sea level temperature and pressure of the International Standard Atmosphere, the ISA. Second, the rates at which the ISA temperature and pressure fall with height in the troposphere. Third, how the atmosphere is divided vertically into the troposphere, the tropopause and the stratosphere. And fourth, why nearly all weather happens below the stratosphere.

## Why this matters

Every number on your aeroplane's performance charts, every altimeter reading, and every density height you calculate, is measured against one imaginary day. That day is the International Standard Atmosphere.

When the ATIS at Jandakot gives 34 degrees on a summer afternoon, your take-off roll is long because the real day is far warmer than that standard day. And you can only say how much warmer if you know the standard by heart.

The same lesson explains two things you will see from the cockpit. Why a February thunderstorm over the wheatbelt grows to a flat anvil top and stops. And why the airliners cruising above it sit in smooth, clear air.

The exam asks for the ISA figures exactly. It asks for an ISA temperature, or a deviation, at a given height. And it asks for the reasons the weather lives in the lowest layer. You met the outline of all this in the RPL lessons on local weather. Here you learn the reference figures, and the physics behind them.

## What the air is made of

Take a breath at Rottnest, and another at 10,000 ft over the Stirling Range. The air in both is the same mixture.

Dry air is about 78 percent nitrogen, 21 percent oxygen, and 1 percent other gases. Those other gases are mostly argon, with a small amount of carbon dioxide. Winds and turbulence stir the lower atmosphere so thoroughly that these proportions stay the same up to well above any height a light aeroplane can reach. So the figures to remember are 78 percent nitrogen and 21 percent oxygen.

What changes with height is the pressure: the total weight of air pressing down from above. At altitude, fewer molecules are packed into each litre of air. So each breath holds fewer oxygen molecules, even though oxygen is still 21 percent of the mix. That is the whole hypoxia story, which you will find in the human factors lessons on the atmosphere and on hypoxia.

[[diagram:atmosphere-pressure-oxygen-vs-altitude]]
Picture a graph with altitude running along the bottom and pressure up the side. Two lines curve down from left to right as you climb. The upper one is the total pressure: {1013 hPa|ten thirteen hectopascals} at sea level, about 700 at 10,000 ft, and about 500, half, at 18,000 ft. The lower one is the oxygen's share, always 0.21 of the total: about 213 hPa at sea level, 147 at 10,000 ft, and 105 at 18,000 ft. What to notice: oxygen stays about 21 percent of the air at every height, but the total pressure, and so the oxygen in each breath, halves by about 18,000 ft.

There is one gas whose share does change, and that is water vapour. That means water in its invisible gas form, not droplets. Over the dry interior there is almost none. In hot, humid tropical air, it can make up about 4 percent of the air by volume.

Nearly all the water vapour sits in the lowest few kilometres, for two reasons. First, it comes from evaporation at the surface: from the ocean, lakes, wet ground and plants. Second, as air carrying it is lifted, it cools. The vapour condenses out as cloud, and falls back as rain, long before it can reach great heights.

Water vapour matters out of all proportion to its amount. It is the raw material of every cloud, fog, shower and thunderstorm. And when it condenses, it releases the heat it absorbed when it evaporated. That heat is the fuel that drives thunderstorms and tropical cyclones. The mechanism is in the lesson on heat, temperature, pressure and humidity.

The air also carries solid particles. Dust from the wheatbelt and the interior. Salt from sea spray along the coast. Smoke from bushfires and burn-offs. And pollen.

These particles do two jobs. First, they give water vapour something to condense onto. So they are the condensation nuclei at the heart of every cloud droplet. Second, when there are enough of them, they scatter light and reduce visibility. That is the haze and smoke story of the lesson on visibility.

Question. At 10,000 ft, is oxygen a smaller percentage of the air than at sea level?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: no. Oxygen is still about 21 percent of the air. What has fallen is the total pressure, so each breath contains fewer molecules of everything, including oxygen.

## Why we need a standard atmosphere

The real atmosphere is different every day, and every hour. On a winter morning at Albany, the air at the surface might be 8 degrees, and the pressure {1028 hPa|ten twenty-eight hectopascals}. On a summer afternoon at Meekatharra, it might be 42 degrees and {1004 hPa|ten oh four hectopascals}.

Now think about what has to be built on top of that. An altimeter measures pressure and displays height, so it has to be built to some rule that turns pressure into feet. A performance chart has to give take-off distances for some baseline condition. And two pilots comparing their aeroplanes' climb rates need to know what day they are comparing.

So the aviation world agreed on one imaginary, average atmosphere, and calibrated everything to it. It is the ICAO International Standard Atmosphere, or ISA. It is a mathematical model of how temperature, pressure and density change with height, on an average day in the mid-latitudes.

The ISA is a reference, not a forecast. No real day matches it exactly, and that is the point. Once you know the standard, you can describe any real day by how far it differs from it.

That difference is the ISA deviation. A day that is 10 degrees warmer than the standard at a given height is called ISA plus 10. One that is 5 degrees colder is ISA minus 5. You will use ISA deviation for density height and true airspeed, in the next lesson and in the aircraft knowledge and navigation units. And performance charts often have a separate line for each ISA deviation.

## The ISA at sea level (2.1.1)

The ISA starts at mean sea level with three values, and two of them you must know exactly.

The pressure is {1013.25 hPa|ten thirteen point two five hectopascals}, which is 29.92 inches of mercury. The temperature is plus 15 degrees Celsius. And the density, the third value, is 1.225 kilograms per cubic metre.

Take the pressure first. {1013.25 hPa|Ten thirteen point two five hectopascals} is the weight of the whole column of air above each square metre of the sea. It is why the altimeter subscale is set to {1013|ten thirteen}, or more exactly {1013.2|ten thirteen point two}, when you fly on flight levels. Everyone is then measuring against the same standard pressure, whatever the real sea level pressure is. The QNH you hear on an ATIS is a real day's sea level pressure, scattered either side of that standard. In the exam, quote the figure exactly: {1013.25 hPa|ten thirteen point two five hectopascals}, and plus 15 degrees.

The temperature, plus 15 degrees, is a sensible average of mid-latitude surface temperatures. A mild 15 degree day at Jandakot, which is close to sea level, is close to an ISA day. A 40 degree February afternoon there is around ISA plus 25.

The density, 1.225 kilograms per cubic metre, follows from the pressure and the temperature. You will not be asked to calculate with it. But it is the number the aeroplane actually cares about. Lift, drag, engine power and propeller thrust all depend on how many kilograms of air pass over and through the aeroplane each second.

So the sea level figures are {1013.25 hPa|ten thirteen point two five hectopascals} and plus 15 degrees.

## How temperature falls with height (2.1.2)

Climb out of Jandakot on any day, and the air gets colder as you go up. That seems backwards at first, because you are getting closer to the sun.

The reason is that the sun barely heats the air directly. Sunlight passes through clear air and is absorbed by the ground. The ground then heats the air touching it. The troposphere is a pot heated from the bottom, so the warmest air is at the bottom.

A second effect adds to it. When warm air near the ground rises, it moves into lower pressure, expands, and cools as it expands. So air at height is further from the heat source, and it has also been cooled by expansion on the way up. The rate at which temperature falls with height is called a lapse rate.

The ISA uses a single average lapse rate through the whole troposphere. It is 1.98 degrees per 1,000 ft, which is 6.5 degrees per kilometre. For mental arithmetic, and in nearly every exam question, use 2 degrees per 1,000 ft.

So here is the rule for the ISA temperature at any height in the troposphere. Start with 15 degrees. Then take away 2 degrees for every 1,000 ft of height. The ISA temperature equals 15, minus 2 times the height in thousands of feet.

Let's work one. What is the ISA temperature at 8,500 ft? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 5]]
The temperature fall is 8.5 times 2, which is 17 degrees. 15 minus 17 is minus 2. So the ISA temperature at 8,500 ft is minus 2 degrees. On a standard day you would cruise at 8,500 ft just below freezing. And any real day is described by how far it differs from that minus 2.

Now a deviation. You are at 6,000 ft, and the outside air temperature is plus 9 degrees. What is the ISA deviation?
[[pause 6]]
First, the ISA temperature at 6,000 ft. 6 times 2 is 12, and 15 minus 12 is plus 3 degrees. Then the deviation: the actual 9, minus the standard 3, is plus 6. So the day is ISA plus 6.

Warmer than ISA means lower density than the charts' standard day. So expect longer take-off rolls, and a poorer climb, than the ISA figures promise.

Now try one where the day is colder than standard. You are cruising at 11,500 ft over the Goldfields on a winter morning, and the outside air temperature gauge reads minus 12 degrees. What is the ISA deviation?
[[pause 6]]
The ISA temperature at 11,500 ft is 15, minus 11.5 times 2. 11.5 times 2 is 23, and 15 minus 23 is minus 8. The deviation is the actual minus 12, minus the standard minus 8. That comes to minus 4. So the day is ISA minus 4.

The method is always the same. Subtract the ISA value from the actual value, and keep the sign. An actual temperature below ISA gives a negative deviation. And colder than standard air is denser, so the aeroplane performs slightly better than the book. The answer again: ISA minus 4.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. It often offers 3 degrees per 1,000 ft as the ISA lapse rate. That is the dry adiabatic lapse rate: the rate at which a rising parcel of unsaturated air cools. You will meet it in the next lesson. The ISA lapse rate is 1.98, about 2 degrees per 1,000 ft. Two different numbers, for two different things. One describes the average atmosphere. The other describes a bubble of air moving through it.

## How pressure falls with height (2.1.2)

Pressure at any level is simply the weight of all the air above it. Climb 1,000 ft, and you leave 1,000 ft of air below you, so there is less air above pressing down. Pressure always falls with height, on every day, everywhere.

Near the surface, the fall is about 1 hPa for every 30 ft. More exactly, at sea level 1 hPa is about 27 ft, and the number of feet per hectopascal grows as you go higher. The 30 ft per hectopascal figure is the one you use for every altimetry and pressure height calculation in the exam. You will meet it again and again in the next lesson.

Why does the figure change with height? Because air is compressible. The air near the bottom of the column is squashed by the weight of everything above it, so it is dense. A thin slice of it holds a lot of mass. Higher up, the air is less squashed and less dense. So you must climb through a thicker slice to leave the same weight of air below you. The pressure fall per foot gets smaller as you climb, and the feet per hectopascal get larger.

The result is that the pressure fall is not linear. Pressure roughly halves every 18,000 ft. It is about 500 hPa at 18,000 ft, and about 300 hPa at 30,000 ft. Half the mass of the atmosphere lies below 18,000 ft.

Let's work one, to see where the 30 ft rule stops working. Start at the ISA sea level pressure, {1013|ten thirteen} hectopascals, and use 30 ft per hectopascal.

First, climb to 1,200 ft. By the rule, the pressure falls by 1,200 divided by 30, which is 40 hPa. That gives about 973 hPa, which is close to the true ISA value.

Now climb to 18,000 ft. By the rule, the fall is 18,000 divided by 30, which is 600 hPa. {1013|Ten thirteen} minus 600 would give 413 hPa. But the true ISA pressure at 18,000 ft is about 500 hPa.

So the linear rule is fine for the few thousand feet that matter for altimeter settings and aerodrome pressure heights. And it is badly wrong at high altitude. Use it near the ground, where the exam uses it.

Question. Why does the pressure fall more slowly per 1,000 ft at 15,000 ft than near sea level?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because air is compressible. Near the surface it is squashed by the weight above, and it is dense, so a thin layer contains a lot of air. Higher up the air is thinner, so you must climb further to leave the same weight of air below you.

## The layers of the atmosphere (2.1.3)

Imagine you could take a thermometer straight up from Perth. You would see the temperature fall steadily for about 36,000 ft. Then it would stop falling, and stay steady for a while. And then it would begin to rise. Those changes in the way temperature behaves with height are what divide the atmosphere into layers. The layers are defined by temperature, not by height. There are three to know: the troposphere, the tropopause and the stratosphere.

First, the troposphere. It is the lowest layer, from the surface up to the tropopause, and it is where you will do all your flying. Temperature generally decreases with height through it, for the reason you have just heard: it is heated from below. It holds about three-quarters of the mass of the whole atmosphere, and nearly all its water vapour.

Its depth is not fixed. It is deepest where the surface is hottest, because strong heating drives powerful convection that lifts the top of the layer. It is about 8 km, or 26,000 ft, over the poles. About 11 km, or 36,000 ft, in mid-latitudes such as Perth. And up to 16 to 18 km, which is 52,000 to 59,000 ft, over the equator. For the same reason, it is higher in summer than in winter. So a thunderstorm over the Kimberley in the wet season can grow far taller than a winter storm off Albany.

Second, the tropopause. It is the boundary at the top of the troposphere, where the temperature stops falling. In the ISA it sits at 36,090 ft, which is 11 km, with a temperature of minus 56.5 degrees.

You can check that figure from the lapse rate. 36.09 thousand feet, at 1.98 degrees per thousand, is a fall of about 71.5 degrees. And 15 minus 71.5 is minus 56.5.

The tropopause behaves like a lid. Air rising through the troposphere arrives at a layer where the surrounding air is no longer getting colder, and it loses its buoyancy. A growing thunderstorm cell hits that lid and spreads out sideways. That is why a mature cumulonimbus has a flat anvil top that can stretch tens of kilometres downwind. Only the strongest updraughts punch a short way above it, as an overshooting top.

The tropopause is not a smooth, continuous surface. It steps down from the high tropical tropopause to the lower polar one. And at the breaks between them are found the jet streams, narrow ribbons of very strong wind, and clear air turbulence. Those are for high-flying aircraft, but the exam may ask where they live.

Third, the stratosphere. It lies above the tropopause, to about 50 km. In its lower part the temperature is constant. The ISA assumes minus 56.5 degrees all the way from the tropopause to about 65,600 ft, which is 20 km. Higher up, temperature increases with height, because the ozone layer absorbs ultraviolet radiation from the sun, and is heated directly.

The stratosphere is very dry, because the water vapour froze or rained out long before reaching it. And it is very stable. A layer in which temperature stays constant, or rises with height, resists any vertical movement. Above the stratosphere are the mesosphere and the thermosphere, which are not in the PPL syllabus.

So, in short. The troposphere: temperature falls about 2 degrees per 1,000 ft, it holds nearly all the water, and it has the convection, cloud and weather. The tropopause: the temperature stops falling, there is very little water, and it is the lid, with the anvils and jet streams. The stratosphere: temperature constant then rising, almost no water, stable, dry and clear.

Question. Why is the tropopause higher over Darwin than over Hobart?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because the surface near the equator is heated far more strongly. The vigorous convection that results carries warm air higher, and lifts the top of the troposphere. It reaches 16 to 18 km over the equator, against about 11 km in mid-latitudes, and 8 km over the poles.

## Why the weather lives below the stratosphere (2.1.4)

Weather is mostly the story of air going up and coming down. Rising, cooling, making cloud and rain, and sinking again. Three facts about the troposphere make that possible, and the exam may ask for any of them.

The first fact: the water is there. Almost all the atmosphere's water vapour is in the troposphere, close to its source at the surface. With no moisture there can be no cloud and no precipitation, however much the air moves. The stratosphere is a desert.

The second fact: the troposphere is heated from below. Warm air underneath cooler air is top-heavy. A bubble of warm air near the ground is lighter than the air around it, and it rises, the way a hot-air balloon does. Cooler air sinks to take its place. That overturning is called convection. It keeps the troposphere constantly mixing, lifting moist air to where it cools and condenses. The name says it: tropos is Greek for turning.

The third fact: the stratosphere is stable. A layer in which temperature does not fall with height, or rises, is called an inversion. And an inversion is the most stable arrangement the air can have.

Push a parcel of air up into an inversion, and the parcel cools as it rises. But the air around it does not get colder. So the parcel ends up colder and heavier than its surroundings, and it sinks back to where it started. No vertical motion means no cloud and no weather. Rising air stops at the tropopause, so cloud tops stop there too, except for the strongest storm overshoots.

You will meet the same idea near the ground, in the next lesson. Stability is decided by how the air around a rising parcel changes with height. A morning inversion over the Avon valley traps fog and smoke under its lid, in exactly the way the tropopause caps a thunderstorm.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. "The stratosphere has no weather because it is too cold." That is the distractor. The stratosphere is no colder than the top of the troposphere. The real reasons are that it is dry and stable. There is no water to make cloud, and the temperature structure stops air rising.

Question. A parcel of air is pushed up into a layer where temperature is constant with height. What happens to it, and why?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: it sinks back. Rising, the parcel expands and cools. But the surrounding air is no colder higher up. So the parcel becomes colder and denser than its surroundings, and returns to its original level. That is a stable layer, and it is why the stratosphere has almost no weather.

## Bringing it together

It is a February afternoon, and you are planning a flight from Jandakot to Kalgoorlie at 8,500 ft. The forecast gives plus 12 degrees at 8,500 ft.

The ISA temperature there is 15 minus 17, so minus 2 degrees. The actual is plus 12. So the day is ISA plus 14. The air is thinner than on the standard day, so you expect less power, and a slower climb, than the standard day figures for that height.

Ahead, over the eastern wheatbelt, a cumulonimbus is building. Its top has spread into a wide anvil, well above your height. It has reached the summer tropopause, and it cannot go higher, because everything above that lid is dry, stable stratosphere. The cloud itself, the rain shaft, and the gust front beneath it are all in the troposphere. That is where the water vapour is, and where heating from the ground drives the air upwards.

Every part of that picture is this lesson. The ISA to describe the day. The lapse rate to work out the deviation. And the layers to explain why the storm looks the way it does.

Question. The outside air temperature at 4,000 ft is 22 degrees. Is the air there more or less dense than the ISA standard, and roughly how many degrees is it off standard?
[[pause 6]]
The answer: less dense. The ISA temperature at 4,000 ft is 15 minus 8, so 7 degrees. The actual 22 degrees is ISA plus 15. Warmer air at the same pressure is less dense, so the aeroplane performs worse than its ISA figures.

## Key points

Let's recap.

The ISA at mean sea level is {1013.25 hPa|ten thirteen point two five hectopascals}, which is 29.92 inches of mercury, and plus 15 degrees. Its density is 1.225 kilograms per cubic metre. The ISA is an agreed average, used to calibrate altimeters and performance charts. It is not a forecast. A real day is described by its ISA deviation.

The ISA temperature falls 1.98 degrees per 1,000 ft. Use 2 degrees for arithmetic. It falls to minus 56.5 degrees at the tropopause. The ISA temperature equals 15, minus 2 times the height in thousands of feet. Pressure falls about 1 hPa per 30 ft near the surface, or 27 ft at sea level. It falls more slowly higher up, roughly halving by 18,000 ft.

The troposphere: temperature falls with height, and it holds nearly all the water. It is about 8 km deep at the poles, 11 km in mid-latitudes, and 16 to 18 km at the equator, and higher in summer. The tropopause is where the fall stops. In the ISA it is at 36,090 ft, at minus 56.5 degrees, and it is the lid that flattens anvils. The stratosphere: temperature constant, then rising. Dry, and stable.

Weather lives in the troposphere because the water vapour is there, and heating from below drives convection. The stratosphere is a dry inversion that stops air rising.

A final exam tip. Quote the ISA exactly. {1013.25 hPa|Ten thirteen point two five hectopascals}. Plus 15 degrees. 1.98 degrees per 1,000 ft, or 2 degrees for arithmetic. And the tropopause at 36,090 ft, at minus 56.5 degrees. The usual distractor is 3 degrees per 1,000 ft, which is the dry adiabatic lapse rate. For ISA temperature questions, work out 15 minus 2 times the height in thousands, then subtract it from the actual temperature, keeping the sign. And for "why is there no weather in the stratosphere", choose the answer that mentions the lack of water vapour, stability, or an inversion.

That's the end of PMTC 2.1.
