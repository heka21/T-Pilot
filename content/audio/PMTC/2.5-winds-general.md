This is PMTC 2.5, Winds, general. By the end you will know why the wind blows along the isobars, which way it goes round highs and lows in the Southern Hemisphere, how to find the low from the wind you are flying in, the difference between a gust and a squall and between backing and veering, how the surface wind differs from the wind aloft, and how it changes through the day. There are worked examples and questions along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (PMTC 2.5)

The exam wants four things from you. First, how pressure differences drive the wind, and how to use Buys Ballot's law to work out roughly where the high and the low lie from the wind you are flying in. Second, the difference between a squall and a gust, and between backing and veering. Third, how the surface wind compares with the gradient wind, in direction and in strength. And fourth, what controls the daily change in the wind, and how the surface wind typically changes over 24 hours.

## Why this matters

As a private pilot, the wind stops being a number on the windsock. It becomes a weather map you fly through.

Tracking south to Albany, you will hold a big drift correction for an hour. You need to know what it says about the pressure ahead, and about your altimeter. Departing Jandakot at dawn, you will lift off in a calm, and climb into a 25 kt easterly a few hundred feet up. By mid-afternoon the same day, the surface wind will be gusty, and the Fremantle Doctor will have turned the runway round.

All of that follows from two forces and one surface effect, which the RPL lessons used without deriving. The exam asks for the Southern Hemisphere answers. And every distractor is the Northern Hemisphere answer from an overseas textbook. So understanding the mechanism is the surest way not to be caught.

## Why air moves: pressure and the isobars

Air pressure at a place is the weight of the column of air above it. Where the column is heavier, the pressure is higher.

On a synoptic chart, the Bureau's mean sea level pressure chart, lines called isobars join places of equal pressure, usually every 2 or 4 hPa. They work exactly as contour lines on a topographic map join places of equal height.

The patterns on that chart have names. There are four to know.

A high, or anticyclone, is a region of higher pressure surrounded by lower pressure. Air in a high is slowly sinking, and sinking air warms and dries. So a high usually brings fine, stable weather, with light winds near its centre. But the sinking can trap haze, smoke and moisture under a subsidence inversion. So a high can also give fog, low stratus and poor visibility, especially in winter.

A low, also called a depression, or a cyclone in the general meteorological sense, is the opposite. Air converges into it near the surface and rises, and rising air cools, and makes cloud and rain.

A trough is an elongated extension of low pressure, often with a wind change and cloud along it. And a ridge is an elongated extension of high pressure, with weather like a high's.

Now picture air sitting between a high and a low. The air on the high side is pushed harder than the air on the low side, so there is a net force towards the low. That force is the pressure gradient force. It always acts at right angles to the isobars, from high towards low. And its size depends on how quickly the pressure changes with distance.

Close isobars mean a big change in a short distance: a steep gradient, a large force, and a strong wind. Widely spaced isobars mean a gentle gradient, and a light wind. The topographic picture holds here too. Close contours mean a steep slope.

If that were the whole story, the wind would blow straight across the isobars, and the low would fill within hours. It does not, because the Earth rotates.

## Coriolis: why the wind blows along the isobars

The Earth turns under the moving air. To someone standing on the ground, air that sets off in a straight line appears to curve away. That apparent deflection is the Coriolis effect. It is usually treated as a force, so that the sums work on a map. Three facts about it matter.

First, in the Southern Hemisphere it deflects moving air to the left of its direction of motion. In the Northern Hemisphere, it deflects to the right. This is where every overseas answer goes wrong for you.

Second, it is proportional to the wind speed. Still air feels no Coriolis force. Fast air feels a lot.

And third, it is zero at the equator and greatest at the poles, because it depends on how much the local vertical is tilted by the Earth's spin. Perth, at about 32 degrees south, has plenty of it.

Now follow a parcel of air between a high to its north and a low to its south. The pressure gradient force starts it moving south, towards the low. As soon as it moves, Coriolis pushes it to its left. For air moving south, that is towards the east. As it speeds up, Coriolis grows, and turns it further left.

The turning continues until the air is moving at right angles to the pressure gradient, straight along the isobars towards the east. At that point the pressure gradient force pulls it south, towards the low. And Coriolis, acting to the left of an eastward-moving parcel, pushes it north, towards the high. The two balance, and the air flows steadily along the isobars. With the high to the north and the low to the south, you have a westerly wind: the westerlies of the Southern Ocean.

That balanced wind along straight isobars is the geostrophic wind. Around curved isobars, the slightly modified balance gives the gradient wind. For the exam they are treated together. Above the friction layer, the wind blows parallel to the isobars, at a speed set by their spacing.

Apply the same reasoning to a whole system. Around a low, the pressure gradient force points inwards, so Coriolis must point outwards. In the Southern Hemisphere, that means the air moves with the low on its right. Air moving with the centre always on its right goes round clockwise. Around a high, the forces swap sides. The high is on the air's left, and the flow is anticlockwise.

[[diagram:highs-and-lows-southern-hemisphere]]
Picture a weather map with north at the top. On one side is a high, with the wind circling it anticlockwise along the isobars. The air in it sinks, giving fine, stable weather. On the other side is a low, with the wind circling it clockwise. Air converges and rises, giving cloud and rain. Underneath, the reason: between a high to the north and a low to the south, the pressure gradient force pulls the air south, Coriolis deflects it to its left, and the two balance when the wind blows east along the isobars. That is a westerly, with the low on its right.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. "Anticlockwise round a low" is the Northern Hemisphere answer, and it will be one of the options. In Australia, it is clockwise round a low, and anticlockwise round a high. A quick check: the westerlies over southern Australia blow between the highs to their north and the big Southern Ocean lows to their south. Only clockwise flow round those lows gives a westerly on their northern side.

Question. The isobars over the Wheatbelt are twice as close together today as yesterday. What has happened to the gradient wind, and why?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: it is stronger. Closer isobars mean the pressure changes faster with distance, so the pressure gradient force is larger. And the wind must blow faster for Coriolis, which grows with speed, to balance it.

## Buys Ballot's law: reading the map from the cockpit

The balance you have just built gives a rule you can use in flight. Above the friction layer, the wind blows along the isobars with the low on its right. So if you stand with your back to the wind, the wind is blowing the way you face. The low pressure is on your right, and the high on your left. That is Buys Ballot's law for the Southern Hemisphere. A Dutch meteorologist stated it for the Northern Hemisphere, where the low is on the left.

In the air, you find the wind from your drift. Put your back to it, and the low is on your right. Back to the wind, low on the right.

Let's work one. You are flying from Perth to Albany, tracking roughly south. You need a large correction to hold track, because the aeroplane is drifting to the left. Where is the low, and what will your altimeter do? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
Step one. Drift to the left means the wind is pushing you from the right. On a southerly track, your right is the west. So the wind is westerly.

Step two. Put your back to a westerly, and you face east.

Step three. Facing east, your right hand points south. So the low is to the south, and the high to the north.

Step four. You are flying towards the low, so the pressure is falling along your track. With the old QNH still set, the altimeter will over-read. You will be lower than it shows. So update the QNH as you go, from the area forecast or the nearest station.

Now try one yourself. You are tracking east from Perth to Kalgoorlie. To hold track, you must point the nose well to the left of track, towards the north. Where is the low, and will the pressure rise or fall along your track?
[[pause 8]]
Pointing the nose north to hold an easterly track means the wind is coming from the north. It is a northerly. With your back to a northerly, you face south, and your right hand points west. So the low is to the west, behind you, and the high is to the east, ahead.

Flying away from the low, towards the high, the pressure rises along your track. So an old QNH makes the altimeter under-read. You are higher than it shows, which is the safe direction. And a northerly over the south-west, with a low to the west, is the classic set-up ahead of a cold front. That is in the lesson on air masses and fronts. The answer again: the low is to the west, and the pressure rises along your track.

The law is exact for the gradient wind above the friction layer. At the surface, as the next sections show, friction turns the wind partly across the isobars towards the low. So with your back to the surface wind, the low is on your right, and a little ahead of you.

Question. In Perth, with your back to the wind, which hand points to the high pressure, and why?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the left. In the Southern Hemisphere, air moves along the isobars with the low on its right, because Coriolis, acting to the left, balances the pressure gradient force pulling it towards the low. So the high is on the left.

## Gusts and squalls

The wind you feel on the ground never blows at a steady speed. Look at the windsock on a hot afternoon at Northam. It fills, sags, swings, and fills again.

Those short bursts are gusts. They come from the same thing that makes the ride bumpy: turbulence. It can be mechanical turbulence, with air tumbling over rough ground, trees and buildings. Or thermal turbulence, with rising bubbles of heated air mixing faster air down from above.

A gust is a brief rise in wind speed, lasting only a few seconds, above the mean wind. In a TAF or METAR, gusts are reported when they exceed the mean speed by 10 kt or more. Verify that figure against the current AIP. The code adds a G and the gust speed to the wind group. So {25015G27KT|two five zero one five, gust two seven K T} is a mean wind from {250|two five zero} degrees true at 15 kt, with gusts to 27 kt.

Gusts are the everyday case. They make the approach rough. They briefly change your airspeed, and so your margin over the stall. And they are why you add half the gust factor to the approach speed, as the RPL lesson on the significance of observations explains.

A squall is a different animal, and the difference is duration and size. A squall is a sudden increase in wind speed of at least 16 kt, rising to 22 kt or more, that lasts at least a minute. It often comes with a sharp change of direction, a drop in temperature, and heavy rain.

So a gust is a few seconds of turbulence in an otherwise steady wind. A squall is a new wind that arrives, and stays for minutes. Squalls come with organised weather. The passage of a cold front. A line of thunderstorms, called a squall line. And the gust front spreading out from beneath a storm. The weather code for a squall is SQ.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. A squall is not just a big gust. The exam's distinguishing feature is duration. A gust lasts seconds. A squall lasts at least a minute. A 15 kt wind gusting 30 kt is a gusty wind, not a squall.

## Backing and veering

Wind direction is always named for where the wind blows from, in degrees.

[[diagram:wind-velocity-from]]
Picture an arrow coming in from the left side of a compass, the west, and pointing towards the east. That is a wind of {270|two seven zero} degrees at 15 kt, written {270/15|two seven zero stroke one five}. It blows from the west, towards the east. It is a headwind for an aeroplane flying west, and a tailwind for one flying east.

When the direction changes, there are two words for which way it changes. They describe the movement round the compass, not the weather that causes it.

Backing means the wind direction changes anticlockwise, to a smaller number of degrees. A wind going from {180|one eight zero} to {150|one five zero} degrees, from south to south-south-east, has backed.

Veering means the wind direction changes clockwise, to a larger number of degrees. A wind going from {320|three two zero} degrees to {010|zero one zero} degrees has veered, through north.

Crossing north is where people slip. So read it as movement round the dial. From {320|three two zero}, the wind moves clockwise, past {360|three six zero}, to {010|zero one zero}. That is a veer of 50 degrees, even though the number got smaller.

The definitions are the same in both hemispheres. What differs is which one happens. At a Southern Hemisphere cold front, the wind backs, from north-west to south-west. At a Northern Hemisphere one, it veers. So learn the words as compass directions, and work out the situation from the physics.

Question. The Jandakot ATIS wind changes from {090|zero nine zero} degrees to {040|zero four zero} degrees over an hour. Has it backed or veered?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: backed. From {090|zero nine zero} to {040|zero four zero}, the direction has moved anticlockwise round the compass, from east towards north-east, by 50 degrees.

## Surface wind and gradient wind

The balance between the pressure gradient force and Coriolis holds in the free air, high enough that the ground does not interfere. Close to the ground, the air drags over paddocks, trees, buildings and hills, and that friction slows it.

The layer in which friction matters is the friction layer. Over land, it is roughly 2,000 to 3,000 ft deep. Above it blows the gradient wind, parallel to the isobars. At the bottom of it, at the 10 m height of the windsock and the anemometer, blows the surface wind.

[[diagram:wind-gradient-on-approach]]
Picture two panels. On the left, the wind gradient: arrows that are short near the ground and grow longer with height, because friction slows the wind close to the ground. The headwind is strongest a few hundred feet up, and weakest at the surface. On the right, an aeroplane on final into a strong headwind. As it descends, the headwind decreases, the airspeed decays, and the rate of descent increases. It sinks below the intended approach path, and undershoots, unless the pilot adds power.

Friction does more than slow the wind. It changes its direction too. Reason it from first principles, in four steps.

First, friction reduces the wind speed near the ground.

Second, Coriolis is proportional to speed, so it weakens too. But the pressure gradient force does not depend on the wind speed at all, so it is unchanged.

Third, the pressure gradient force now wins the tug of war. So the wind turns partly across the isobars, towards the low.

And fourth, in the Southern Hemisphere, with your back to the wind, the low is on your right. Turning towards the low is turning right, which is clockwise.

So in the Southern Hemisphere, the surface wind is veered from the gradient wind, and lighter than it. Turn it round, and you have what happens as you climb. Leaving the surface and climbing out of the friction layer, the wind backs and increases with height. The Bureau of Meteorology's gradient level wind guidance gives the same result.

How much depends on the surface. Rough land slows the air far more than open water, so the speed loss and the turn are both bigger over land. Over land, the surface wind is about half to two-thirds of the gradient wind, and veered about 30 degrees. Over the sea, it is about two-thirds to three-quarters, and veered only about 10 to 15 degrees. These are rules of thumb. The next section shows how much they change between day and night.

Let's work one. The forecast 2,000 ft wind over the Wheatbelt is {320|three two zero} degrees at 24 kt. Estimate the surface wind at Cunderdin.
[[pause 6]]
First, the direction. Over land, it is veered about 30 degrees. {320|Three two zero} plus 30 is {350|three five zero} degrees.

Second, the speed. Half to two-thirds of the gradient. Half of 24 is 12 kt, and two-thirds of 24 is 16 kt. So 12 to 16 kt.

So expect the windsock at Cunderdin to show about {350|three five zero} degrees at 12 to 16 kt, nearly northerly. That gives a different crosswind on each runway from the one the 2,000 ft wind suggests.

Now try one over water. The 2,000 ft wind off the coast near Rottnest is {200|two zero zero} degrees at 20 kt. What surface wind would you expect over the water?
[[pause 6]]
Over the sea, the veer is about 10 to 15 degrees. {200|Two zero zero} plus 10 to 15 gives {210|two one zero} to {215|two one five} degrees. The speed is about two-thirds to three-quarters. Two-thirds of 20 is about 13 kt, and three-quarters of 20 is 15 kt. So 13 to 15 kt. Over smooth water, the surface wind stays much closer to the gradient wind than over land.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Northern Hemisphere textbooks say the surface wind is backed from the gradient wind, and that answer will be among the options. Here, the low is on the right, and friction turns the wind towards the low. So the surface wind is veered, and lighter. Climbing, it backs and strengthens.

Question. You are climbing out of Jandakot in a 12 kt northerly. What do you expect the wind to do by 2,500 ft, and why?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: back towards the north-west, and strengthen, to something like double the surface speed. Above the friction layer, Coriolis is no longer weakened by drag. So the wind turns back anticlockwise, to blow along the isobars at the full gradient speed.

## How the wind changes through the day

The friction layer is not a fixed thing. How well its air is stirred together with the faster air above depends on the temperature profile. And that changes every day with the sun. That is the diurnal variation of the wind.

Start with the day. The sun heats the ground, the ground heats the air touching it, and the lowest layer becomes unstable. Warm bubbles rise as thermals, and cooler air sinks to replace them. The sinking air comes down from higher up, where the wind is faster, and closer to the gradient direction. So the thermals act as a mixer, bringing gradient-level momentum down to the surface.

So through the morning, the surface wind increases, and backs towards the gradient wind. It becomes gusty, because the mixing is done by turbulent eddies. It is strongest and gustiest in the early to mid afternoon, when heating peaks.

Now the night. The ground cools by radiating heat to a clear sky, the air touching it is chilled, and a radiation inversion forms: a layer of cold, dense air under warmer air. Cold air under warm air is stable. The mixing stops, and the surface air is cut off from the wind above.

With no supply of momentum from above, and friction still acting, the surface wind decreases, and veers, often to calm by dawn. It is lightest around dawn. Meanwhile, the wind just above the inversion is no longer dragged on by the surface. It can blow faster than it does by day. That is the nocturnal low-level jet, the source of the dawn wind shear in the RPL lesson on the significance of observations.

The size of the daily swing depends on how strongly the day heats and the night cools, and on how well the air mixes anyway. There are four factors.

First, heating and cooling. Clear skies give strong sunshine by day and strong radiation at night, and so a large variation. An overcast sky blankets both, and damps it.

Second, the surface. Over land the swing is large. Over the sea it is very small, because the sea's temperature hardly changes in a day.

Third, the strength of the gradient. A strong gradient wind stirs the lowest layers mechanically, day and night. So no inversion can form, and the surface wind stays close to its average.

And fourth, terrain and local winds. Slopes, coastlines and valleys add their own daily winds on top: sea and land breezes, and anabatic and katabatic winds.

Question. Which night gives the calmest dawn at Northam: a clear night with a weak gradient, or an overcast night with a strong gradient? Why?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: clear, with a weak gradient. Clear skies let the ground cool fast, and form a strong inversion. A weak gradient cannot stir it away. So the surface air is cut off from the wind above, and goes calm. Cloud, and a strong gradient, both keep the layer mixed.

## Local winds

The last of those factors deserves its own section. In Western Australia, the local winds often matter more than the gradient wind.

The sea breeze is driven by the difference in heating between land and sea. By day, the land heats much faster than the sea. The air over the land warms, and expands upwards. The pressure pattern that results pushes cool sea air in at the surface, with a weak return flow out to sea a few thousand feet up.

A typical sea breeze is 10 to 20 kt, a few thousand feet deep, and reaches 20 to 50 km or more inland by late afternoon. Its leading edge, the sea breeze front, is a line of convergence, where the cool air undercuts the warm. It is often marked by a line of cumulus, with wind shear and turbulence along it. The full mechanism is in the RPL lesson on local weather.

[[diagram:sea-breeze-front]]
Picture a cross-section of the west coast on a warm afternoon, with the sea on the left. The land heats, and warm air rises over it. Cool, moist sea air flows onshore underneath as the sea breeze, returns seaward aloft, and sinks over the sea. Where it meets the warm inland air, the cool air pushes in as a shallow wedge. What to notice: the wind shift, the shear, and any cumulus line are all at that leading edge.

The land breeze is the reverse, at night. The land cools below the sea, and a weaker offshore breeze of about 5 to 10 kt develops, strongest around dawn.

The Fremantle Doctor is Perth's summer sea breeze. It is a south-westerly that typically arrives on the coast in the late morning to mid-afternoon, and it drops the temperature by several degrees.

On the hottest days, the west coast heat trough lies near the coast, with hot easterlies. There is more on that in the lesson on air masses and fronts. The gradient easterly holds the sea breeze offshore, and the Doctor arrives late, or not at all.

When it does arrive, the change is abrupt. Jandakot and Rottnest often swing from a light easterly to a 15 to 20 kt south-westerly within minutes. So expect a runway change and a new crosswind in the afternoon. And expect the wind on final to differ from the wind you planned for.

The slopes have their own daily breezes. By day, a slope heated by the sun warms the air in contact with it. That air is lighter than the air at the same level away from the slope, so it flows up the slope. That is an anabatic wind, and it is usually gentle.

At night, the slope cools the air in contact with it. That air becomes dense, and drains down the slope into the valleys. That is a katabatic wind. Katabatic air pools in valley floors as cold air, frost and fog. At the foot of a long, steep slope it can blow strongly. And the Avon valley's winter fogs owe something to air draining off the hills on either side.

Question. Why does a strong gradient easterly delay the Fremantle Doctor?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the sea breeze is a weak, shallow flow, driven by the land and sea temperature difference. A strong opposing easterly holds it offshore, until the afternoon heating builds the temperature difference enough to push it inland. And on the hottest days, it may not make it at all.

## Bringing it together

It is a January day, with a high in the Bight. With your back to the easterly, the low is on your right, to the north, and the high is to the south, just as the chart shows.

At {0600|oh six hundred}, the Jandakot windsock hangs limp, under a radiation inversion. But climbing out, you meet a 25 kt easterly at about 800 ft. That is the low-level jet, and you feel a sharp bump as you pass through the top of the inversion.

By {1000|ten hundred}, the inversion has burned off. Thermals mix the easterly down, and the surface wind is {090|zero nine zero} at 15 kt, gusting 25. It has backed towards the gradient direction, and it is gusty.

At {1330|thirteen thirty}, a line of small cumulus near the coast marks the sea breeze front. At {1400|fourteen hundred}, the wind at Jandakot swings to a 15 kt south-westerly in a few minutes, and runway {24|two four} comes into use.

At dusk, the Doctor fades, the ground cools, and the surface wind dies to calm again. One weather map, four surface winds, each one predictable from the mechanism.

## Key points

Let's recap.

The pressure gradient force pushes air from high to low across the isobars, harder where they are close. Coriolis deflects it to the left in the Southern Hemisphere, until the wind blows along the isobars. The wind goes clockwise round a low, and anticlockwise round a high. Back to the wind, low on the right.

A gust lasts seconds, and comes from turbulence. It is reported at 10 kt or more above the mean. A squall is an increase of at least 16 kt, to 22 kt or more, lasting at least a minute. It comes from fronts, squall lines, or storm outflow.

Backing is an anticlockwise change of direction. Veering is clockwise. The definitions are the same in both hemispheres.

Friction slows the surface wind, Coriolis weakens, and the wind turns towards the low. So in the Southern Hemisphere, the surface wind is veered and lighter. Over land, by about 30 degrees, and at half to two-thirds of the gradient wind. Over the sea, by about 10 to 15 degrees, and at two-thirds to three-quarters. Climbing, the wind backs and increases.

By day, thermal mixing makes the surface wind stronger, backed and gusty, strongest in the early to mid afternoon. At night, an inversion makes it lighter and veered, lightest around dawn. The swing is largest with clear skies, over land, and with a weak gradient. And local winds add their own daily cycle: sea and land breezes, the Fremantle Doctor, and anabatic and katabatic flows.

A final exam tip. Every wind question has a Northern Hemisphere distractor, so have the Australian answers by heart. Clockwise round a low, anticlockwise round a high. Back to the wind, low on the right. The surface wind is veered and lighter than the gradient wind, about 30 degrees, and half to two-thirds, over land. And the wind backs at a cold front. Backing and veering are compass directions only: anticlockwise and clockwise. And for the daily cycle: strongest and gustiest in the early to mid afternoon, lightest around dawn.
