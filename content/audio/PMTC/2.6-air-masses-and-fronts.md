This is PMTC 2.6, Air masses and fronts. By the end you will be able to describe the flying weather of a cold front, a warm front, a wave depression, an occlusion, a tropical cyclone and the equatorial trough, and the west coast trough that runs Perth's summer, each under the same four headings. There are worked examples and questions along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (PMTC 2.6)

The exam wants two things from you. First, the typical flying weather that comes with six systems: a cold front, a warm front, a wave depression, an occluded front, a tropical cyclone, and the equatorial trough. And second, how to describe that weather under four headings. Temperature, warmer or colder. The wind change, backing or veering, stronger or weaker. Stability and turbulence. And the type and rough amount of cloud, with the precipitation.

## Why this matters

Every few days through a Perth winter, a cold front sweeps in from the Indian Ocean. First a freshening north-westerly and lowering cloud. Then squalls and showers. Then a cold, gusty afternoon, with the Darling Scarp in and out of cloud. In summer, the weather turns on the west coast trough instead. And if you fly north to the Pilbara, you meet the monsoon trough and the cyclone season.

As a private pilot planning a flight to Albany or Geraldton, you will read these systems off the synoptic chart, and you need to know what each one will do to your day. The exam asks you to describe their flying weather under four headings. And every wind question has a Northern Hemisphere distractor waiting for you.

This lesson builds each system from the air masses it separates. So you can work out the weather, rather than memorise it.

## Air masses: where the weather comes from

Air that sits for days over a large, uniform surface takes on that surface's temperature and moisture. A desert. A warm sea. The cold Southern Ocean. The result is an air mass: a body of air hundreds to thousands of kilometres across, with roughly uniform temperature and humidity. The place where it acquires those properties is its source region.

Air masses carry two labels. Maritime, formed over the sea and moist, or continental, formed over land and dry. And tropical, which is warm, or polar, which is cold. Three of them shape Australian weather.

First, continental tropical air, from the inland in summer. It is hot, dry and dusty, with high-based cumulus and dry storms, and a big daily temperature range.

Second, maritime tropical air, from the Coral Sea, the Timor Sea and the Indian Ocean. It is warm and humid: the wet season. It gives low cloud and drizzle when it is cooled, and storms when it is heated.

And third, maritime polar air, from the Southern Ocean. It is cool and moist. It arrives behind cold fronts, mostly from May to October in southern Western Australia.

An air mass does not keep its properties for ever. As it moves, it is changed by the surface beneath it, and the key change is to its stability. Recall from the lesson on heat, temperature, pressure and humidity what stability means. Air is unstable when a parcel pushed upwards stays warmer than its surroundings, and keeps rising on its own. It is stable when a lifted parcel ends up colder than its surroundings, and sinks back.

Now the two cases that matter.

Cold air moving over a warmer surface is heated from below. The bottom of the column warms while the top stays cold. So the temperature falls quickly with height, and the air becomes unstable. Thermals rise freely and build cumulus, which grows into showers. The vertical mixing gives gusts and thermal turbulence. And because the mixing carries dust and haze away upwards, the visibility is good between showers. Southern Ocean air crossing the warmer sea to reach Albany or Perth in winter is the classic unstable, showery stream.

Warm air moving over a colder surface is cooled from below. The bottom of the column becomes colder than the air above it. That is an inversion, and the air becomes stable. Nothing rises, so the cloud forms in flat layers: stratus and drizzle where the cooling reaches the dew point, and fog at the surface. Smoke, haze and moisture are trapped in the cool layer. So the visibility is poor, and the air is smooth.

Hold onto that pair. Most of the rest of this lesson is that pair, applied to different situations.

Question. Warm, humid tropical air drifts south over the cool winter sea off the Gascoyne coast. What weather do you expect in it, and why?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: stable weather. Low stratus, drizzle, and possibly sea fog, with poor visibility and smooth air. The air is cooled from below by the colder sea, which forms an inversion at its base. So nothing rises, and the moisture condenses in flat layers near the surface.

## A front, and the four questions to ask of it

Where two air masses meet, they do not mix easily, because they differ in density. Cold air is heavier than warm. The boundary between them is a front. It slopes, with the cold air lying under the warm air as a wedge. Along the front, the warm air is forced upwards, and lifted air cools, condenses, and makes cloud and rain. That is why fronts are where the weather is.

Fronts lie in troughs, elongated extensions of low pressure, so the isobars kink sharply at the front. That kink is what makes the wind change as a front passes. And you can work out which way it changes from the circulation in the lesson on winds.

In the Southern Hemisphere, air flows clockwise round a low, with the low pressure on its right. On the forward side of a trough, the isobars run roughly north-south, and the wind blows along them from the north or north-west. On the rear side, they turn, and the wind blows from the south-west. As the trough passes over you, the wind swings from north-west to south-west. That is anticlockwise, which is backing. So in the Southern Hemisphere, the wind backs as a front or trough passes. Northern Hemisphere textbooks say it veers, because there the flow round a low is anticlockwise.

The syllabus asks you to describe each system's flying weather under four headings. Those four headings are the questions to ask of any front.

First, temperature. Which air mass replaces which? If colder air arrives behind it, it gets colder. If warmer, warmer.

Second, wind. Does it back or veer as the system passes, and does it strengthen or weaken? A tight kink in the isobars means a sharp, strong change.

Third, stability and turbulence. Is the lifted air stable or unstable? And is the air behind it heated or cooled from below?

And fourth, cloud and precipitation. How fast is the warm air lifted? Fast, steep lifting makes cumuliform cloud, cumulus, towering cumulus and cumulonimbus, with showers: heavy and short. Slow, gentle lifting over a large area makes layered, stratiform cloud, from cirrus and cirrostratus down to altostratus, nimbostratus and stratus, with continuous rain or drizzle: lighter, and long-lasting.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. "The wind veers as a cold front passes" is the Northern Hemisphere answer, and it will be among the options. In Australia, the front lies in a trough, the flow round it is clockwise, and the wind backs. North-west to south-west at a cold front.

## Cold fronts

A cold front is the leading edge of cold air advancing into warmer air. The cold air is dense and moves along the ground. So it pushes under the warm air like a snowplough, and lifts it steeply.

The frontal surface slopes at about {1 in 50|one in fifty} to {1 in 100|one in a hundred}. For every 50 to 100 units you go horizontally from the surface front, back into the cold air, the boundary rises by one. In southern Western Australia, cold fronts sweep in from the west, mostly from autumn to spring.

[[diagram:cold-front-cross-section]]
Picture it in cross-section, moving from left to right, west to east. On the left, cold maritime polar air drives under the warm air as a steep wedge. At the front, the warm air is lifted fast into a narrow band of cumulus, towering cumulus and cumulonimbus, with heavy showers, thunderstorms and hail. Ahead, on the right, there is altostratus and altocumulus, with a strengthening north-westerly. Behind, on the left, there are scattered showery cumulus in the colder air, with good visibility, and the wind has backed to the south-west.

Let's work one. A cold front has a slope of {1 in 50|one in fifty}, and 1 nm is about 6,000 ft. How high is the frontal surface 50 nm behind the surface front? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
50 nm, divided by 50, is a rise of 1 nm. 1 nm is about 6,000 ft. So within 50 nm, the boundary climbs from the ground to about 6,000 ft. That is why the cloud and weather of a cold front are packed into a narrow band.

Now work through the four questions, in the order you meet them.

Ahead of the front, the pressure falls and the isobars tighten. The wind is north-westerly and strengthening, often hot and dusty in summer and autumn. Lifted warm air spreads ahead as altostratus and altocumulus, with some cumulus. And a pre-frontal trough can trigger storms of its own.

At the front, the steep lifting of warm, moist air gives a narrow band of cumulus, towering cumulus and cumulonimbus. That means heavy showers, thunderstorms, and sometimes hail. The wind changes suddenly, often in a squall, with moderate to severe turbulence, and wind shear where the two airstreams meet. The temperature drops sharply.

Behind the front, the air is now maritime polar, colder than what it replaced. The wind has backed to the south-west. That cold air is crossing a warmer sea and then warmer land. So it is heated from below, and it is unstable. Scattered cumulus and showers, gusts and thermal turbulence, and good visibility between the showers. The pressure rises.

So, under the four headings. Temperature: warm ahead, a sharp drop at the front, and colder behind. Wind: north-westerly and strengthening ahead, squalls at the front, and backed to the south-westerly behind. Stability: storms possible ahead, severe turbulence and shear at the front, and unstable, gusty air behind. And cloud: altostratus and altocumulus ahead, the band of cumulonimbus at the front, and scattered showery cumulus behind.

The band of bad weather is narrow, often 50 nm or less, but it is intense, and fast-moving. The thunderstorms in it carry all the hazards of any storm. The thunderstorm flying itself is covered in the lesson on flight considerations.

[[diagram:thunderstorm-hazards]]
Picture a mature thunderstorm cell from the side, with its anvil on top. Inside and near it there is severe turbulence, hail, lightning, icing in the cloud, and heavy rain. Beneath it, a microburst hits the ground and spreads out as a gust front ahead of the storm. A light aeroplane stays at least 10 nm from a mature cell, and never flies under the anvil. And the storms in a cold front may be hidden in the frontal cloud.

For a VFR pilot, the worst of it is low cloud. The moist westerly behind the front rides up the Darling Scarp and the hills of the south-west. It cools further, and the cloud bases lower on the high ground, exactly where the terrain rises to meet them.

[[diagram:low-cloud-and-rising-terrain]]
Picture a side view. An aeroplane flies inland under a cloud base that lowers ahead, while the ground rises towards hills. The gap narrows until there is no room to stay clear of cloud and above the minimum height. So a VFR pilot should not try to cross a front at low level, and should not try to sneak under the post-frontal cloud over the hills. Turn back while there is room.

Question. Why is the weather behind a winter cold front showery and gusty, but with good visibility?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the cold Southern Ocean air behind the front is heated from below by the warmer sea and land, which makes it unstable. Thermals build cumulus that grow into showers, and mix gusts down to the surface. The same mixing carries haze and dust away upwards, so the air between the showers is clear.

## Warm fronts

A warm front is the leading edge of warm air advancing into retreating cold air. Warm air is lighter, so it cannot push the cold air out of the way. Instead, it rides up over the cold wedge, on a very shallow slope, about {1 in 150|one in a hundred and fifty}. The lifting is slow, and spread over a huge area. So the cloud is layered, and the weather extends hundreds of nautical miles ahead of the surface front.

Let's work one. A warm front has a slope of {1 in 150|one in a hundred and fifty}, and 1 nm is about 6,000 ft. How far ahead of the surface front is the frontal surface at 12,000 ft?
[[pause 6]]
First, 12,000 ft in nautical miles. 12,000 divided by 6,000 is 2 nm. Then, the horizontal distance for a rise of 2 nm. 2 times 150 is 300 nm. So cloud forming on the frontal surface at 12,000 ft lies about 300 nm ahead of the surface front. The first high cloud of a warm front can appear a day before it arrives.

That shallow slope gives a warm front its signature: a sequence of cloud that lowers and thickens as the front approaches. There are four steps. First comes cirrus, wisps of ice cloud high on the frontal surface. Then cirrostratus, a thin veil that gives a halo round the sun or moon. Then altostratus, a grey sheet through which the sun looks as if through frosted glass. And then nimbostratus, thick, dark and raining, with ragged stratus fractus beneath it. The base lowers steadily the whole time.

[[diagram:warm-front-cross-section]]
Picture a cross-section moving left to right. Warm air on the left rides up over a wedge of retreating cold air, on a very shallow slope. Cloud forms along the sloping surface, and lowers towards the surface front. Cirrus far ahead on the right, then cirrostratus, altostratus, and nimbostratus with continuous rain. Rain falling into the cold air saturates it, and frontal fog forms ahead of the front. What to notice: the frontal surface is still about 12,000 ft up some 300 nm ahead of the surface front.

Now the four questions.

Cloud and precipitation. The sequence of cirrus, cirrostratus, altostratus and nimbostratus, with long periods of continuous rain from the altostratus and nimbostratus. The rain falling into the cold air beneath saturates it. So the visibility is poor, and frontal fog can form ahead of the front.

Stability and turbulence. The warm air is usually stable as it is lifted, so the ride is mostly smooth. There is moderate icing in the altostratus and nimbostratus above the freezing level. But if the warm air is unstable, cumulonimbus can be embedded in the layer cloud, hidden from view in the grey. That is the one ugly surprise a warm front can spring.

Temperature. Warmer after the front passes, because warm air has replaced cold.

And wind. Ahead of the front, northerly. After it passes, north-westerly. North to north-west is anticlockwise, so the wind backs. And it is often lighter after passage.

True warm fronts are uncommon over the Australian mainland, but common over the Southern Ocean. In Western Australia you are more likely to meet the warm front sequence ahead of a wave, or an occlusion.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. A warm front is not mild weather. Its rain and fog can hold the visibility below VMC for hours, and it can hide cumulonimbus. It is often a longer no-go than a cold front, which passes in an hour or two.

Question. Why does warm front cloud spread hundreds of miles ahead of the surface front, while cold front cloud is a narrow band?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the warm front's slope is very shallow, about {1 in 150|one in a hundred and fifty}. So the frontal surface is still at cloud level hundreds of miles ahead, and the lifting is slow and widespread, making layer cloud. The cold front's slope is steep, {1 in 50|one in fifty} to {1 in 100|one in a hundred}. So all the lifting happens in a narrow zone, and makes cumuliform cloud.

## Wave depressions and occlusions

Picture a long, slow-moving front, with cold polar air on its poleward side, and warm tropical air on its equator side, the two flowing past each other. A small disturbance puts a kink in the front: a wave. At the tip of the kink, the pressure falls, and a low forms. The air begins to circulate round it. The circulation pushes the warm air poleward on the forward side, and the cold air towards the equator on the rear side.

The front is now in two parts. A warm front ahead of the low, and a cold front behind it, with a tongue of warm air between them, called the warm sector. That system is a wave depression.

As it passes, a place on the warm sector side of the low gets the whole sequence of flying weather, in order. First, the warm front's weather: lowering layer cloud, and continuous rain. Second, the warm sector: warm, humid, stable maritime air, often cooled from below by the sea. So low stratus, drizzle and poor visibility, with light turbulence. Third, the cold front's weather: the band of cumulus and cumulonimbus, squalls and showers. And fourth, the clearing, showery cold air behind.

The wind backs at each front. And the system deepens as it moves east. The pressure at its centre falls, the isobars tighten, and the winds strengthen.

Now the occluded front. Cold fronts move faster than warm fronts, because the dense cold air drives along the ground, while the warm air has to climb. As the wave matures, the cold front catches up with the warm front, starting near the low. The warm sector between them is lifted off the ground. Where that has happened, the front is an occluded front, or occlusion. It is the sign of a mature or decaying low.

[[diagram:wave-depression-life-cycle]]
Picture four small weather maps in a row, north up, the systems moving east. In the first, a stationary front, with warm tropical air to the north and cold polar air to the south, flowing past each other. In the second, a kink forms, and a low develops at its poleward tip. In the third, a wave depression, with air circulating clockwise round the low, a warm front ahead, a cold front behind, and the warm sector between them. And in the fourth, the faster cold front catches the warm front near the low, and lifts the warm sector off the ground: an occluded front.

An occlusion combines the weather of both fronts. It has the widespread layer cloud and continuous rain of a warm front. It has embedded cumulonimbus and turbulence from the lifted warm sector, like a cold front. And it has low cloud, poor visibility and icing.

Because the warm air is now aloft, two cold air masses meet at the surface. So the temperature change at the surface is smaller than at either front alone. And the wind backs as it passes.

The defining hazard is the thunderstorm you cannot see: embedded cumulonimbus, hidden in a wide area of layer cloud and rain. Occlusions are a poor choice for VFR flight.

## Tropical cyclones

A tropical cyclone is an intense low that forms over warm tropical seas. It runs on the heat released when water vapour, evaporated from the warm sea, condenses in towering cloud. The heat warms the column, the surface pressure falls further, and more moist air converges.

To get going, it needs three things. First, a sea surface of about 26.5 degrees or warmer, to supply the moisture and heat. Second, a position at least about 5 degrees from the equator, because the rotation needs the Coriolis effect, which is zero at the equator. And third, a disturbance to start it, usually in the monsoon trough.

The Western Australian season is November to April. And the Pilbara coast is the most cyclone-prone coast in Australia.

Its flying weather, under the four headings.

Wind. It rotates clockwise in the Southern Hemisphere, like any low here. Sustained winds are gale force, 34 kt, or more, and over 100 kt in a severe cyclone. At the centre, the eye, 10 to 50 km across, is nearly calm, with little cloud. Around it, the eyewall of cumulonimbus carries the strongest winds.

Cloud and precipitation. Spiral rain bands of cumulonimbus, torrential rain, and visibility near zero.

Stability and turbulence. Extremely unstable, with severe turbulence and wind shear.

And temperature. Warm, saturated tropical air.

[[diagram:tropical-cyclone-structure]]
Picture the cyclone from above. Spiral rain bands of cumulonimbus wind in towards the centre, and the air circulates clockwise. At the centre is the eye, 10 to 50 km across, nearly calm, ringed by the eyewall with the strongest winds. Now picture it in cross-section. Warm, moist air spirals in over a sea of about 26.5 degrees or warmer, rises in the eyewall, and spreads out at the top, while air sinks gently in the eye.

A cyclone weakens over land or cooler water, because it loses its supply of warm, moist air. SIGMETs and Bureau warnings cover it. The only sensible plan for a light aeroplane is to be on the ground and tied down, or flown well away, before it arrives.

Question. A cyclone off Port Hedland crosses the coast and moves inland. Why does it weaken?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: it loses its fuel. Over land there is no warm sea to evaporate moisture into the converging air. So less vapour condenses, less heat is released, and the low fills. The rain and flooding can still last for days.

## The equatorial trough

Between the hemispheres, the trade winds converge in a band of low pressure. That is the equatorial trough, also called the intertropical convergence zone, or ITCZ. In the southern summer it moves south over northern Australia, where it is called the monsoon trough. North of it blow north-westerly monsoon winds, moist from the warm seas. South of it blow the south-east trades.

Converging air must rise. And this air is warm, moist and unstable, so the trough is a belt of convection. Under the four headings:

Cloud and precipitation. Lines and clusters of cumulus and cumulonimbus, with heavy showers and thunderstorms. They come often at night over the sea, and in the afternoon over land, with stratus and nimbostratus in the heaviest areas.

Stability and turbulence. Unstable, with turbulence and wind shear near the storms, but light winds otherwise.

Wind. Light and variable near the trough axis, with squalls near the storms. Close to the equator, Coriolis is too weak to organise the air into strong winds round the pressure pattern.

And temperature. Hot and humid, with a high freezing level, about 15,000 to 16,000 ft.

## The west coast trough

The trough that matters most to a Perth pilot in summer is not a front at all. In summer, the land heats intensely, and a trough of low pressure forms down the west of the continent. That is the west coast trough, and its position each day decides Perth's weather.

When the trough lies off or near the coast, Perth is on its eastern side, where the flow is from the interior. That brings hot, dry easterly to north-easterly winds of continental tropical air. The gradient easterly holds the sea breeze offshore, and temperatures climb past 40 degrees.

When the trough moves inland, Perth is on its western side. The wind swings south to south-westerly behind it. The Fremantle Doctor arrives, and the temperature drops. Inland, the convergence along the trough line can lift the hot air enough to set off high-based storms, and dust. So treat the trough's passage as you would a weak front: a wind change, turbulence and shear along it, and storms to the east.

On the right, the trough has moved inland. Perth is on its western side, with the south-westerly sea breeze, and storms and dust inland.

Question. The west coast trough moves inland during the morning. What happens to the wind and temperature at Jandakot, and what should you expect inland?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: Jandakot is now west of the trough. So the hot easterly gives way to a south-westerly, the sea breeze comes in, and the temperature drops, with shear and turbulence as the change passes. Inland, convergence along the trough line can trigger high-based storms, with dust and gusty outflow.

## Bringing it together

It is a June morning. The chart shows a deep low south of Cape Leeuwin, with a cold front 300 nm west of Perth, moving east at about 30 kt. You plan Jandakot to Albany and back. Ask the four questions.

Ahead of the front, the wind is a strengthening north-westerly. So expect a crosswind, and some mechanical turbulence. But the cloud is still high altostratus and altocumulus.

The front will reach the coast in roughly 10 hours. So the cumulonimbus, the squalls, and the backing to the south-west arrive in the evening, and at Albany a little later. Behind it come gusty south-westerlies, showers, and lowering cloud on the Stirling Range and the Scarp.

So you go early, and are home well before the front. If the low were a wave, with a warm front ahead, the layer cloud and rain would arrive hours before the cold front, and could close the route sooner.

Question. During that flight, the wind at Albany backs from north-west to south-west, the temperature drops 6 degrees, and a squall line passes. What has happened, and what weather follows?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the cold front has passed. Behind it comes colder, unstable maritime polar air. Gusty south-westerlies, scattered cumulus and showers with good visibility between them, and cloud lowering on the high ground.

## Key points

Let's recap.

Air masses are maritime or continental, and tropical or polar. Heated from below, air is unstable: cumulus, showers, gusts, and good visibility. Cooled from below, it is stable: stratus, drizzle, fog, and poor visibility.

A cold front is steep, {1 in 50|one in fifty} to {1 in 100|one in a hundred}. A strengthening north-westerly ahead. A narrow band, often 50 nm or less, of cumulonimbus, squalls, turbulence and shear. Then colder, unstable, showery south-westerlies behind. The wind backs.

A warm front is shallow, about {1 in 150|one in a hundred and fifty}. Cirrus, cirrostratus, altostratus and nimbostratus, with lowering bases, continuous rain and frontal fog. Mostly smooth, but possibly with embedded cumulonimbus. Warmer after. North to north-west: it backs, and is often lighter.

A wave depression is a low on a kink in a front. Warm front, then the warm sector with low stratus, drizzle and poor visibility, then the cold front, then clearing showers. It backs at each front, and deepens moving east.

An occlusion is where the cold front lifts the warm sector off the ground. Layer cloud and rain, with embedded cumulonimbus, icing and low cloud. A small surface temperature change. And it backs.

A tropical cyclone needs a sea of 26.5 degrees or warmer, at 5 degrees or more from the equator. The season is November to April, and the Pilbara is most prone. It turns clockwise, with 34 kt or more, and over 100 kt when severe. A calm eye 10 to 50 km across, an eyewall, and spiral bands of cumulonimbus. It weakens over land or cool water.

The equatorial trough is converging, moist, unstable air. Cumulonimbus and storms, at night over the sea and in the afternoon over land. Light, variable winds with squalls. And a freezing level of about 15,000 to 16,000 ft.

The four headings. Temperature: which air replaces which. Wind: it backs here, and is stronger with tighter isobars. Stability: heated or cooled from below. Cloud: fast lifting gives cumulus and showers, and slow lifting gives layers and continuous rain.

And the west coast trough. Offshore, hot easterlies and no sea breeze. Inland, a south-westerly change at Perth, and storms inland.

A final exam tip. In the Southern Hemisphere, the wind backs as a front passes: north-west to south-west at a cold front. A warm front means layer cloud, continuous rain, and getting warmer. A cold front means cumulonimbus, showers, squalls, getting colder, and clearing behind. Expect a cloud sequence question, where cirrus, cirrostratus, altostratus and nimbostratus mean a warm front. Expect an occlusion question, with embedded cumulonimbus. Expect the warm sector, with low stratus and drizzle. And expect the cyclone recipe: 26.5 degrees, 5 degrees from the equator, clockwise, and November to April.
