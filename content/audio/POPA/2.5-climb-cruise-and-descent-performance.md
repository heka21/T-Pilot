This is POPA 2.5, Climb, cruise and descent performance. By the end you will be able to take the time, fuel and distance for a climb out of a flight manual table, correct it for temperature and wind, get the speed and fuel flow for the cruise, plan the descent, and say which speeds give the maximum range and the maximum endurance. There are worked examples to try in your head, so pause whenever you want a moment to think.

## What the exam expects (POPA 2.5)

The exam wants four things from you, for the climb, the cruise and the descent, taken from typical charts or tables. First, the time, speed, distance, and fuel flow or fuel quantity. Second, the engine settings to use. Third, the rates of climb and descent. And fourth, the conditions under which the aeroplane achieves its maximum range and its maximum endurance.

## Why this matters

Every fuel plan you write as a private pilot rests on three numbers you take from section 5 of the flight manual. How long the climb takes, and how much fuel. What the aeroplane burns in the cruise. And what happens on the way down.

On a flight from Jandakot to Kalbarri, the climb is a small part of the distance, but a large part of the fuel per minute. And on a hot day it takes longer than the book's standard-day figure.

The exam gives you a table, usually a time, fuel and distance to climb table, or a cruise table. It asks you to subtract, interpolate, and correct for temperature and wind. Then it asks why the aeroplane flies a different speed for maximum range than for maximum endurance. The fuel plan that uses these numbers has a lesson of its own, and so do the aerodynamics of the climb and the descent.

## The climb table: time, fuel and distance

A light aeroplane's flight manual gives climb performance as a time, fuel and distance to climb table. Each row is a pressure altitude. Against it are three cumulative figures: the minutes, the litres, and the nautical miles taken to climb from sea level to that altitude. Some tables use pounds or US gallons instead of litres.

Above the table, in small print, are the conditions it assumes: a stated weight, usually the maximum, a climb speed, the power, usually full throttle, the mixture setting, standard temperature, and still air. Those are the engine settings the syllabus asks for. You climb at the power, mixture and speed the table states, or its figures do not apply.

Now the first rule: to climb between two altitudes, subtract. Because the figures are cumulative from sea level, the climb from one altitude to another is the difference between the two rows. That is the single most important rule. And the most common exam slip is to read off the higher row alone.

Let's work one. The table gives these figures. To 3,000 ft: 5 minutes, 3 L, and 6 nm. To 7,000 ft: 13 minutes, 7 L, and 17 nm. The temperature is standard, and the air is still. What does the climb from 3,000 to 7,000 ft take? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
The time is 13 minus 5, which is 8 minutes. The fuel is 7 minus 3, which is 4 L. And the still-air distance is 17 minus 6, which is 11 nm.

Those are the figures for the 4,000 ft climb itself. The first 3,000 ft belonged to an aerodrome at sea level, which yours is not. So the answer: 8 minutes, 4 L and 11 nm.

[[diagram:climb-table-subtract]]
Picture that as a climb profile from sea level, rising to the right. The part you actually fly is only the top piece, from 3,000 to 7,000 ft: the difference between the two rows.

The second rule: when your altitude falls between rows, interpolate. The table may jump in steps of 1,000 or 2,000 ft, and your aerodrome or cruising level will rarely sit on a row. So you assume the figures change evenly between the two rows on either side, and take the same fraction of the way between them.

Let's work one. The table gives sea level as zero for everything. To 2,000 ft: 3 minutes, 2 L, 4 nm. To 6,000 ft: 11 minutes, 6 L, 15 nm. The aerodrome pressure height is 1,000 ft, and you will cruise at 6,000 ft. Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 8]]
Step one. 1,000 ft is halfway from sea level to 2,000 ft. So take half the 2,000 ft figures: 1.5 minutes, 1 L, and 2 nm.

Step two, the time from 1,000 to 6,000 ft. 11 minus 1.5 is 9.5 minutes.

Step three, the fuel. 6 minus 1 is 5 L.

Step four, the still-air distance. 15 minus 2 is 13 nm.

Interpolating is slightly generous, because the aeroplane climbs faster near sea level than higher up. But the error over a 2,000 ft step is a fraction of a minute.

The rows are pressure altitudes. So enter the table with the pressure height of the aerodrome and of the cruising level, not their elevations. On most days the difference is small. On a low-QNH day, it is a few hundred feet.

The third rule: when it is hotter than standard, correct. The table assumes ISA, the standard atmosphere. On a warmer day the density height is higher than the pressure height. The engine makes less power, and the climb is slower, so every figure grows. The flight manual gives a correction in a note under the table. A typical one, and the one this lesson uses, is to add 10 percent for every 10 degrees above ISA, to the time, the fuel and the distance. Your own flight manual's note governs.

Let's work one, the same climb on a warm day with a tailwind. The climb from 3,000 to 7,000 ft has ISA figures of 8 minutes, 4 L and 11 nm. Today is ISA plus 10. There is a 20 kt tailwind, and the climb true airspeed is 75 kt. Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 8]]
Step one, the temperature correction, 10 percent for 10 degrees. The time is 8 times 1.1, which is 8.8 minutes. The fuel is 4 times 1.1, which is 4.4 L. The still-air distance is 11 times 1.1, which is 12.1 nm.

Step two, the groundspeed in the climb. 75 plus 20 is 95 kt.

Step three, the ground distance. 8.8 minutes is 8.8 sixtieths of an hour. Times 95 kt, that is 13.9 nm.

So the top of climb is about 14 nm along track, not 11. The wind changes where you reach your level. It does not change how long it takes, or how much fuel you burn. 

Now the wind, more generally. The table's distance is through the air. To find where along your track you reach the top of climb, you need the ground distance. The reliable way is the one in that example. Multiply the climb time by the climb groundspeed: the true airspeed minus the headwind component, or plus the tailwind. That is the same top of climb calculation you'll find in the navigation lessons.

An alternative is to scale the still-air distance by the ratio of groundspeed to true airspeed. The two agree when the true airspeed you are given matches the table's own average climb speed. They differ a little when it does not. So use the time and groundspeed when you can.

[[diagram:groundspeed-headwind-tailwind]]
Picture two bars, drawn to scale. Both start from a true airspeed of 105 kt. Into a 20 kt headwind, the groundspeed bar shrinks to 85 kt. With a 20 kt tailwind, it stretches to 125 kt. The same true airspeed covers more ground with a tailwind, and less with a headwind. The climb time and the fuel do not change.

Here is one for you to try. The table gives, to 2,000 ft, 3 minutes, 2 L, 4 nm. To 6,000 ft, 11 minutes, 6 L, 15 nm. You climb from 2,000 to 6,000 ft, on a day 20 degrees above ISA, into a 15 kt headwind, at a climb true airspeed of 80 kt. Find the time, the fuel, and the ground distance.
[[pause 12]]
First the ISA figures. 11 minus 3 is 8 minutes. 6 minus 2 is 4 L. 15 minus 4 is 11 nm.

Twenty degrees above ISA is 20 percent. So that is 9.6 minutes, 4.8 L, and 13 nm in still air.

The groundspeed is 80 minus 15, which is 65 kt. By the time method, 9.6 minutes at 65 kt is 10.4 nm. By the ratio method, 13 times 65 over 80 is 10.6 nm. So the answer: about 9.6 minutes, 4.8 L, and 10 to 11 nm over the ground.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Reading the higher row alone gives the climb from sea level, not from your aerodrome. And the temperature correction applies to all three figures. But the wind applies only to the distance over the ground. A headwind does not make the climb take longer, or burn more fuel.

Question. Why does a climb table need a temperature correction, but not a wind correction, to the time and fuel?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: temperature changes the air density. So it changes the engine's power and the rate of climb, and therefore the time and fuel to reach the level. Wind moves the whole air mass. The aeroplane climbs through it at the same rate, so only the distance over the ground changes.

## Rates of climb and descent

The rate of climb is the height gained per minute, in feet per minute. It comes from the surplus of power available over power required. And both ends of that surplus move with height. A normally aspirated engine loses roughly 3 percent of its full-throttle power per 1,000 ft of density height, while the power required to fly at the climb speed rises. So the rate of climb falls with altitude. And on a hot day it falls faster. Typical trainer figures are 700 ft per minute at sea level, at a climb speed of 75 kt indicated, falling to 400 ft per minute at 7,000 ft on a warm day.

[[diagram:power-loss-with-altitude]]
Picture a line sloping down as the height increases: full-throttle power, as a percentage of sea-level power. By about 7,000 to 8,000 ft, a typical trainer can only make about 75 percent. And the surplus that drives the climb shrinks faster than the power itself.

The flight manual gives the rate of climb either as a rate of climb chart, with pressure altitude and temperature in and feet per minute out, or as a column in the climb table. You can also work out the average rate of climb from the climb table: the height gained, divided by the time. For the 3,000 to 7,000 ft climb, 4,000 divided by 8 is 500 ft per minute at ISA. And 4,000 divided by 8.8 is about 455 ft per minute at ISA plus 10.

The climb speed matters too. The flight manual's climb figures assume a stated speed, usually the best rate of climb speed, Vy. That is the speed that gives the most height per minute. The best angle speed, Vx, is slower, and gives the most height per mile, for clearing an obstacle. Climb faster or slower than Vy, and the rate falls.

[[diagram:climb-rate-vs-airspeed]]
Picture a hump-shaped curve: rate of climb against airspeed, for an example trainer. The top of the hump is Vy, best rate, at about 75 kt. Now draw the steepest straight line you can from the origin that still touches the curve. It touches at Vx, best angle, at about 60 kt. Vx is always below Vy.

The rate of descent is the same idea in reverse. And it is usually something you choose, not something the flight manual dictates. Most light-aeroplane flight manuals give little descent data beyond a recommended power setting and speed. So you plan the descent with the rate of descent and the groundspeed. The time is the height to lose, divided by the rate of descent. And the distance is the time, multiplied by the groundspeed.

Let's work one. You are cruising at 6,500 ft, and you will join the circuit at 1,500 ft. Your planned rate of descent is 500 ft per minute, and the descent groundspeed is 110 kt. Use a cruise fuel flow of 30 L per hour, an illustrative figure. Where do you start down, and how much fuel does the descent take? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 8]]
Step one, the height to lose. 6,500 minus 1,500 is 5,000 ft.

Step two, the time. 5,000 divided by 500 is 10 minutes.

Step three, the distance. 10 minutes is a sixth of an hour. A sixth of 110 is 18.3 nm.

Step four, the fuel, at cruise flow. A sixth of 30 L is 5 L.

So start down about 18 nm before the joining point. That is the top of descent. Fuel in the descent is typically at or a little below the cruise flow, if power is kept on to avoid shock cooling the engine. So planning it at cruise flow is the conservative choice. The answer, once more: 10 minutes, about 18 nm, and 5 L.

[[diagram:descent-planning-topd]]
Picture the descent from the side: a level cruise line at 6,500 ft, then a straight slope down to the circuit at 1,500 ft. The top of descent sits about 18 nm back along the track from the joining point.

Some flight manuals do give a descent table, in the same cumulative form as the climb table. Then you subtract rows, exactly as for the climb.

[[diagram:powered-descent-vs-glide]]
Picture three descents from the same point, all at the same indicated airspeed. The middle one is the glide, with power off. Add power, and the path is shallower, the rate of descent lower, and the nose higher. Lower flap, and the path is steeper, the rate of descent higher, and the nose noticeably lower. The rate of descent is the pilot's choice.

Question. The average rate of climb from a table comes out lower on a hot day. Which way does that move the top of climb along your track, and why?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: further along track. The climb takes longer at the same groundspeed, so the aeroplane covers more ground before it reaches the level.

## The cruise table: power, speed and fuel flow

The cruise performance table is entered with the pressure altitude and the RPM. For a constant-speed propeller, it is the manifold pressure and the RPM. For each combination, it gives the percent power, the true airspeed and the fuel flow. Usually there are three columns: a temperature below standard, standard, and above standard.

These are the cruise engine settings the syllabus asks for. You choose a power, set the RPM the table gives for it at your altitude and temperature, and lean the mixture as the table assumes.

The method has four steps. First, find the pressure altitude row, interpolating if you cruise between rows. Second, choose the power setting. 65 to 75 percent is usual. Third, read across to the temperature column for today. And fourth, read off the RPM that gives that power, the true airspeed for navigation, and the fuel flow for the fuel plan.

Two patterns are worth understanding, because the exam asks about them. At a fixed RPM, the percent power falls as the altitude or temperature rises, because the engine breathes thinner air. But for a given percent power, the true airspeed rises with altitude. The same power drives the aeroplane through thinner air, with less drag at the same true speed. That is why a normally aspirated aeroplane often cruises fastest for its fuel at a middle altitude, where it can still make 65 to 75 percent power.

The table's fuel flow is only true for the mixture it assumes. That is often leaned to peak EGT, the exhaust gas temperature, or best economy. Cruise at full rich, and the fuel flow will be noticeably higher than planned. And the reserve will shrink without any warning on the fuel gauge.

[[diagram:mixture-rich-to-lean]]
Picture a curve of engine power as you lean the mixture from full rich. Power peaks near the correct mixture, and falls away on both sides. Too rich wastes fuel. Too lean risks overheating and detonation.

Let's work one, a cruise leg. From the cruise table at your level and power, you read a true airspeed of 105 kt and a fuel flow of 30 L per hour. Those are illustrative figures. The leg is 140 nm, with a 15 kt headwind component. How long does it take, and how much fuel? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 8]]
Step one, the groundspeed. 105 minus 15 is 90 kt.

Step two, the time. 140 divided by 90 is 1.56 hours. That is 93 minutes.

Step three, the fuel. 1.56 times 30 is 47 L.

The wind does not change the fuel flow. But it changes the time, and fuel is flow times time. The same leg with a 15 kt tailwind would take 70 minutes, and burn 35 L. So the answer: 93 minutes and 47 L into the headwind.

Question. You cruise at the RPM the table gives for 65 percent power, but you leave the mixture rich. What happens to your fuel plan?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the fuel flow is higher than the table's figure, which assumed a leaned mixture. So you burn more fuel than planned, on every leg. The power and true airspeed barely change. So nothing in the cockpit tells you, until the gauges and the time-based fuel log disagree.

## Maximum range and maximum endurance

These two ideas answer different questions, so they need different speeds.

Maximum endurance is the longest time in the air, on the fuel you have. Think of holding while fog clears at Albany, searching, or waiting for a runway. Fuel burned per hour depends on the engine power. So endurance is greatest at the speed that needs the least power to stay level: the minimum-power speed. It is slow. It is slower than the minimum-drag speed, and close to the best angle of climb speed. Fly it low in a normally aspirated aeroplane, and leaned. Height gains nothing when distance does not matter, and the power required to fly at a given indicated speed is lowest in dense air.

Maximum range is the greatest distance on the fuel you have. Now the measure is fuel per nautical mile: the fuel flow divided by the groundspeed. In still air, that is best at the speed with the best ratio of true airspeed to fuel flow. That is the minimum-drag speed, the speed of the best lift to drag ratio, at the bottom of the total drag curve. It is faster than the endurance speed. Range improves by flying higher in a normally aspirated aeroplane, which gives a higher true airspeed for the same power. It also improves by leaning, and by being lighter.

[[diagram:drag-curves-vs-airspeed]]
Picture two drag curves against airspeed. Parasite drag climbs steeply as speed rises, with the square of the speed. Induced drag does the opposite, falling as speed rises. Add them, and the total drag is a U shape. Its lowest point is where the two are equal. That is the best lift to drag speed, the still-air range speed. The endurance speed is slower than this.

Now wind. Wind changes the range speed, but not the endurance speed. Range is fuel per ground mile. Into a headwind, every minute in the air is a minute the wind pushes you back. So you fly faster than the still-air range speed, accepting more fuel per hour to spend fewer hours exposed to the wind. With a tailwind, the wind is doing some of the work. So you fly slower, and let it carry you longer. Endurance is measured in time, and the wind does not change time in the air. So the endurance speed stays the same, in any wind.

[[diagram:power-required-range-endurance]]
Picture another U-shaped curve: the power required to fly level, against true airspeed. Fuel flow follows power. The lowest point of the U is the minimum-power speed, the endurance speed: least fuel per hour. Now draw a straight line from the origin that just touches the curve. It touches at a faster speed, the minimum-drag speed, because power divided by speed is drag. That is the still-air range speed. With a headwind, start the line from the wind speed along the speed axis instead. It touches the curve at a higher speed still. So, into a headwind, fly faster for range.

The flight manual's range and endurance charts give the range or endurance at each altitude and power setting, for a full fuel load, with and without the reserve. They show the same pattern: more range with height and lower power, more endurance at low power and low level.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Endurance and range are not the same speed, and the exam's distractor swaps them. Endurance, which is time, is the slower one, at minimum power. Range, which is distance, is faster, at minimum drag. And wind only affects range. Fly faster into a headwind, and slower with a tailwind.

Question. Why does the maximum range speed go up in a headwind, while the maximum endurance speed does not?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: range is measured in ground distance. A headwind costs ground distance for every minute in the air, so flying faster reduces the minutes exposed to it. Endurance is measured in time, which the wind cannot change. So the speed for least fuel per hour stays the same.

## Bringing it together

You plan Jandakot to Albany at 6,500 ft, on a day 10 degrees above ISA.

The climb table gives the climb from the aerodrome's pressure height to 6,500 ft, once you subtract and interpolate. You add 10 percent to the time, fuel and distance for the temperature. And you multiply the climb time by the climb groundspeed, to place the top of climb.

The cruise table at 6,500 ft, at your chosen 65 percent power and today's temperature column, gives the RPM to set, the true airspeed for the navigation log, and the fuel flow for the fuel plan. That holds provided you lean as the table says.

Ten minutes and about 18 nm out, you start a 500 ft per minute descent, and plan its fuel at cruise flow.

Then the forecast changes. There will be a 25 kt headwind on the way home. So for the return, you fly a little faster than the still-air range speed, to cut the time in the headwind. And if Albany's fog is slow to lift on arrival, you slow to the endurance speed, low and leaned, to make the fuel last longest while you wait.

## Key points

Let's recap.

Climb tables are cumulative from sea level. Subtract the lower altitude's row from the higher. Interpolate between rows. Correct for temperature, typically 10 percent per 10 degrees above ISA, though the flight manual governs. Then use the climb time times the groundspeed for the ground distance. Cruise tables give the true airspeed and the fuel flow, and the fuel for a leg is the flow times the time.

The engine settings. Climb at the power, mixture and speed the table states. Cruise at the RPM the table gives for the chosen power, leaned as the table assumes.

The rates. The rate of climb falls with altitude and temperature, and the average rate is the height gained divided by the time. For the descent, the time is the height divided by the rate of descent, the distance is the time times the groundspeed, and the fuel is at cruise flow, to be conservative.

And range and endurance. Maximum endurance is the minimum-power speed: slow, low and leaned. Maximum range is the minimum-drag speed, the best lift to drag, in still air: higher, leaned and lighter. Faster into a headwind, and slower with a tailwind.

A final exam tip. For a climb table, subtract, then correct for temperature, then for wind, in that order. The wind changes only the ground distance. Cruise questions want the pressure altitude row, the temperature column, and the RPM for the chosen power. And the endurance speed is slower than the range speed.

That's the end of POPA 2.5.
