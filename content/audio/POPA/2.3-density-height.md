This is POPA 2.3, Density height. By the end you will be able to get the two inputs, pressure height and temperature, straight from the aeroplane, and work out the density height by any of the four methods the exam names, getting the same answer each way. There are worked examples to try in your head, so pause whenever you want a moment to think.

## What the exam expects (POPA 2.3)

The exam wants two things from you. First, work out density height from the outside air temperature and the pressure height, and also from the aeroplane itself, using the temperature gauge with {1013.2|ten thirteen point two} hectopascals set on the altimeter. And second, do it by any of four methods: a density altitude chart, the manual navigation computer, the flight manual charts, and mathematics.

## Why this matters

Density height is the single number that tells you how the aeroplane will perform today. At Kalgoorlie on a summer afternoon it can be 3,500 ft above the aerodrome's elevation. At Albany on a frosty winter morning it can be below sea level.

The RPL lesson on take-off and landing performance taught the arithmetic. The PPLA goes further. It expects you to get the inputs from the aeroplane itself, to use whichever tool the question names, a chart, the navigation computer, the POH or the formula, and to get the same answer each way.

CASA lists density height and pressure height among the most common PPLA errors. They are almost always sign slips, and the wrong ISA temperature. So this lesson spends as long on checking an answer as on finding it.

## What density height measures, and where the 120 ft comes from

Density height is the height in the International Standard Atmosphere, ISA, at which the air would have the same density as the air around you now. The ISA is the reference atmosphere that every performance chart and altimeter is built on. At sea level it has {1013.25 hPa|ten thirteen point two five hectopascals} and 15 degrees, and the temperature falls 2 degrees per 1,000 ft.

If the air today is exactly ISA for its pressure, density height equals pressure height. If it is warmer, the air is thinner than ISA at that pressure, and the density height is higher.

Where does the size of the correction come from? From two facts you can check. First, air density depends on absolute temperature. At 15 degrees, which is 288 kelvin, warming the air by 1 degree reduces its density by 1 part in 288. That is about 0.35 percent. Second, in the lower atmosphere, ISA density falls by about 2.9 percent per 1,000 ft. So one degree of warming thins the air as much as climbing 0.35 divided by 2.9, times 1,000 ft. That is about 120 ft.

That is the 120 ft per degree rule. It is a rule of thumb, good to within about 100 ft at the heights and temperatures the exam uses. And it works in both directions. Each degree colder than ISA lowers the density height by about 120 ft.

Pressure height is the other input. It is the ISA height with today's pressure. That is what the altimeter reads with {1013.2|ten thirteen point two} hectopascals set. Or it is the elevation, corrected by 30 ft for every hectopascal the QNH is below {1013|ten thirteen}. The lesson before this one sets out the difference between the two heights, and the altimeter procedure.

Question. Why must the ISA temperature be worked out at the pressure height, and not at the elevation?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because density height is the correction of pressure height for non-standard temperature. The question is how warm the air is compared with ISA at the same pressure. And the ISA height with today's pressure is the pressure height. Using the elevation gives the wrong standard temperature whenever the QNH is not {1013|ten thirteen}.

## Getting the two inputs

The exam gives you the inputs in one of three ways, and the syllabus names the first two.

The first way: the outside air temperature and pressure height are given. That is the simplest. The question states, for example, pressure height 2,500 ft, OAT 30 degrees. You go straight to the method.

The second way: from the aeroplane, with {1013.2|ten thirteen point two} set. Sitting in the aeroplane, set {1013.2|ten thirteen point two} hectopascals on the altimeter subscale. Whatever the needles now show, on the ground or in flight, is the pressure height. Then read the temperature from the aeroplane's outside air temperature gauge.

On the ground, give that gauge time. A gauge in a parked aeroplane on a sunny apron reads the heat soaked into the airframe, not the air. Take the reading once it has settled, with the aeroplane in the shade, or with air moving over the probe. Never take it from the sun-heated cabin, which can be far hotter than the air outside.

The third way: the elevation and QNH are given. Work out the pressure height first. It is the elevation, plus {1013|ten thirteen} minus the QNH, times 30. Then carry on as for the first way.

Picture the altimeter with {1013|ten thirteen} wound into the subscale window. Whatever the real QNH is, the needles now show the pressure height. That is the second way of getting the input.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. The cabin is not the air. A cockpit thermometer in a closed aeroplane on a 35 degree day can read 50. Use that, and you add nearly 2,000 ft of density height that does not exist. The reverse error is worse. A temperature taken from an air-conditioned building, or from an early-morning forecast, understates the afternoon's density height.

## Method 1: mathematics

The mathematics is three lines, in order. Do this method even when the question names another, as a check.

Line one, the pressure height. It equals the elevation, plus {1013|ten thirteen} minus the QNH, times 30.

Line two, the ISA temperature. It equals 15, minus 2 for every 1,000 ft of pressure height.

Line three, the density height. It equals the pressure height, plus 120 times the difference between the OAT and the ISA temperature.

Work each line to the nearest 10 ft, and keep one decimal place in the temperatures. Round only the final answer. A numeric exam question allows for the small differences that rounding produces. But it does not allow for a sign error, which typically moves the answer by hundreds or thousands of feet.

Let's work one. The pressure height is 3,000 ft, and the OAT is 30 degrees. Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 8]]
Step one, the ISA temperature at 3,000 ft. 15 minus 2 times 3 is 9 degrees.

Step two, the deviation from ISA. 30 minus 9 is plus 21 degrees.

Step three, the correction. 120 times 21 is 2,520 ft.

Step four, the density height. 3,000 plus 2,520 is 5,520 ft.

So the aeroplane will perform as it would at 5,500 ft on a standard day. That is roughly 16 percent less power than at sea level, and a markedly longer take-off run. The answer, once more: 5,520 ft.

Now one below ISA, taken from the cockpit in flight. You are cruising over the Stirling Range in winter. You set {1013.2|ten thirteen point two}, and the altimeter reads 6,500 ft. The OAT gauge shows minus 4 degrees. What is the density height?
[[pause 8]]
Step one, the pressure height. It is the reading with {1013.2|ten thirteen point two} set: 6,500 ft.

Step two, the ISA temperature at 6,500 ft. 2 times 6.5 is 13. 15 minus 13 is 2 degrees.

Step three, the deviation. Minus 4, minus 2, is minus 6 degrees. Colder than standard.

Step four, the density height. 120 times minus 6 is minus 720 ft. 6,500 minus 720 is 5,780 ft.

The cold air is denser than ISA, so the aeroplane performs as if it were 720 ft lower than its pressure height. The sign of the deviation carries straight through. So the density height is 5,780 ft.

Here is one for you to try, the kind of number you meet in the Goldfields. Kalgoorlie: elevation 1,200 ft, QNH {1008|ten oh eight}, OAT 40 degrees.
[[pause 10]]
The pressure height first. {1013|Ten thirteen} minus {1008|ten oh eight} is 5. 5 times 30 is 150. 1,200 plus 150 is 1,350 ft.

The ISA temperature at 1,350 ft is 15 minus 2.7, which is 12.3 degrees. The deviation is 40 minus 12.3, which is 27.7 degrees. 120 times 27.7 is 3,324 ft. Add the 1,350, and the density height is 4,674 ft. Call it about 4,700 ft.

The aeroplane performs as if it were at nearly 5,000 ft, on a strip only 1,200 ft above the sea.

[[diagram:density-height-kalgoorlie]]
Picture that Kalgoorlie sum as three columns, side by side, drawn to scale. The first is the elevation, 1,200 ft. The second, the pressure height, is only a little taller: the QNH adds just 150 ft. The third, the density height, towers over both, because the heat adds over 3,300 ft. The temperature correction is more than twenty times the pressure correction.

Question. With {1013.2|ten thirteen point two} set, the altimeter reads 2,000 ft, and the OAT is exactly 11 degrees. What is the density height, and why?
[[pause 5]]
The answer: 2,000 ft. ISA at 2,000 ft is 15 minus 4, which is 11 degrees. So the deviation is zero, and the density height equals the pressure height.

## Method 2: the navigation computer

The computer side of a manual navigation computer, the {E6B|E six B} or the CRP type, has a window for converting calibrated airspeed to true airspeed. That conversion needs exactly the same information as density height: pressure height and temperature. That is why most computers carry a density altitude window beside the airspeed window.

The method has three steps. First, in the airspeed window, or the density altitude window, set the pressure height against the OAT. The pressure altitude scale and the temperature scale sit side by side in the window. Turn the disc until the two values line up. Second, without moving the disc, read the density altitude in the density altitude window, against its index mark. And third, check the answer against the mathematics.

Take the case of 2,500 ft and 30 degrees. The computer gives about 4,900 ft. That is the same as the arithmetic. ISA at 2,500 ft is 10 degrees, so the deviation is 20 degrees. 20 times 120 is 2,400, and 2,500 plus 2,400 is 4,900 ft.

The computer is quick. But its scales are crowded at the hot end. And the commonest error is reading the wrong window, or setting the elevation instead of the pressure height. Each make lays out its scales slightly differently, so practise with the computer you will take into the exam.

The link with true airspeed is worth seeing, because it explains why density height matters in the cruise as well as on the runway. The airspeed indicator measures dynamic pressure, which depends on density as well as speed. In thin air, the needle shows less than the speed through the air. So the true airspeed is higher than the indicated, by roughly 2 percent per 1,000 ft of density height, as a rule of thumb.

The same disc setting that gives you the density altitude also lines up the calibrated airspeed scale against the true airspeed scale. So one setting answers both questions. A high density height means a faster true airspeed for the same indicated speed. On the runway, that means a faster groundspeed at lift-off and touchdown.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. The computer's density altitude scale expects pressure altitude. Setting the aerodrome elevation against the temperature skips the QNH correction. On a low-pressure day, that understates the density height by 30 ft for every hectopascal below {1013|ten thirteen}.

## Method 3: a density altitude chart

A density altitude chart is a graph that does the arithmetic for you. Temperature runs along the horizontal axis, and density altitude up the vertical axis. A family of sloping lines crosses the grid, one for each pressure altitude. Some versions use aerodrome elevation lines instead, with a separate QNH correction scale.

To use one, there are three steps. First, find the temperature on the horizontal axis. Second, go straight up to the line for your pressure altitude, interpolating if it falls between two lines. And third, go straight across to the vertical axis, and read the density altitude.

The lines slope because density altitude rises with temperature at a fixed pressure altitude. And the slope is the 120 ft per degree you met in the mathematics. So a chart answer should agree with the formula, to within the width of a line. The CASA workbook does not include a density altitude chart. But the POH may, and the exam may provide one in the question.

[[diagram:density-altitude-chart]]
Picture the chart. Temperature runs along the bottom, density altitude up the side, and one sloping line for each 1,000 ft of pressure altitude. A dashed line joins the points where each pressure line meets its own ISA temperature. Along that dashed line, density altitude equals pressure altitude. Now the worked example: go up from 30 degrees to the 3,000 ft line, then across, and read 5,520 ft. That is the same answer as the arithmetic. In the exam, use the chart you are given.

Question. On a density altitude chart, your pressure altitude falls halfway between the 2,000 ft and 3,000 ft lines. What do you do?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: interpolate. Go up from the temperature to a point halfway between the two lines, then across. The lines are evenly spaced for equal steps of pressure altitude, so halfway between them is 2,500 ft.

## Method 4: the flight manual charts

Most POH take-off and landing charts, and every chart in the CASA workbook, take pressure altitude and temperature as separate inputs. They do the density correction inside the chart. You never see the density height as a number. You enter the pressure height on one scale and the temperature on another, and the chart's guide lines carry the combination through to the result.

So for these charts, the right procedure is to stop after the pressure height, and read the temperature on the chart's own scale. If you work out the density height first, and enter that as a pressure height, you count the temperature twice.

Some POH tables are indexed by density altitude instead. They give take-off distance or rate of climb at sea level, 2,000 ft, 4,000 ft and so on. Then you work out the density height first, by any of the other three methods, and enter the table with it, interpolating between rows. Read the column headings. Pressure altitude and density altitude are different inputs, and the wrong one can be thousands of feet out on a hot day.

[[diagram:power-loss-with-altitude]]
And here is what a higher density height costs the engine. Picture a line sloping down as the height increases: full-throttle power, as a percentage of sea-level power. It loses roughly 3 percent for every 1,000 ft of density height. So the 5,520 ft example is down about 16 percent.

Question. A POH take-off chart has a pressure altitude scale and a temperature scale. Should you enter it with the density height?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: no. The chart applies the temperature correction itself, so you enter the pressure height and the actual temperature separately. Entering the density height as the pressure height would apply the temperature correction twice.

## Checks that catch most errors

Every method should land on the same number. So the best check is to do the mathematics alongside whatever method the question names. Beyond that, four habits catch most mistakes.

First, the ISA temperature falls with height. At 6,000 ft, ISA is 3 degrees. So a mild 20 degree day up there is already 17 degrees above standard. That is about 2,000 ft of correction.

Second, a negative pressure height is normal. Take a high QNH at a coastal aerodrome, say {1025|ten twenty-five}, at an elevation of 20 ft. The pressure height is 20 minus 360, which is minus 340 ft. Carry the sign, and work the ISA temperature at minus 340 ft: 15.7 degrees.

Third, sanity-check the size. On a hot Western Australian afternoon inland, the density height is typically 3,000 to 4,000 ft above the elevation. On the coast in winter, it can be below sea level. An answer far outside that range, for the conditions given, is probably a slip.

And fourth, humidity. It adds a little to the density height, because water vapour is lighter than dry air. But the exam methods ignore it.

## Bringing it together

You are about to leave a station strip in the Murchison at midday. With {1013.2|ten thirteen point two} set, the altimeter reads 1,850 ft. The OAT gauge, after five minutes of taxiing with the vents open, settles on 34 degrees. That is your input taken from the aeroplane: pressure height 1,850 ft, OAT 34.

You work the mathematics. ISA at 1,850 ft is 15 minus 3.7, which is 11.3 degrees. The deviation is 34 minus 11.3, which is 22.7 degrees. The correction is 120 times 22.7, which is 2,724 ft. So the density height is about 4,570 ft.

You check it on the navigation computer. Pressure altitude 1,850 against 34 degrees, read the density altitude window, and it agrees, to within the scale's resolution.

Your POH take-off chart wants pressure altitude and temperature. So you enter it at 1,850 ft and 34 degrees, not at 4,570 ft. The climb table, though, is laid out by density altitude. So there you use 4,570 ft, and interpolate. Three tools, one atmosphere, and the arithmetic is the referee.

## Key points

Let's recap.

You need two inputs: pressure height and temperature. Either they are given directly. Or you take them from the aeroplane: {1013.2|ten thirteen point two} on the subscale gives the pressure height, and the OAT gauge, settled and shaded, gives the temperature. Never the hot cabin. Or you calculate the pressure height from the elevation and QNH.

Then the four methods. Mathematics: the ISA temperature is 15, minus 2 per 1,000 ft of pressure height, and the density height is the pressure height plus 120 times the difference between the OAT and ISA. The navigation computer: pressure height against OAT in the airspeed window, then read the density altitude window. The density altitude chart: up from the temperature to the pressure altitude line, then across to the density altitude. And the flight manual charts: usually you enter pressure height and temperature separately, and let the chart correct. Tables indexed by density altitude need it worked out first.

A final exam tip. Whatever method the question names, the answer must agree with the arithmetic, to within the reading accuracy of the chart or computer. So do the mathematics as a cross-check. The usual errors are three. Working the ISA temperature at the elevation instead of the pressure height. Losing the sign of a below-ISA deviation, or of a negative pressure height. And feeding a density height into a chart that wants pressure height. Know these by heart: 15 degrees, 2 degrees per 1,000 ft, 30 ft per hectopascal, and 120 ft per degree.

That's the end of POPA 2.3.
