This is POPA 2.4, Using the take-off and landing charts. By the end you will know where the structural weight limits live, the order in which to prepare and enter the inputs for every chart, how to work the weight charts and the distance charts in the CASA workbook, and which conditions the chart figures assume. There are worked examples and a few questions along the way, so pause whenever you want a moment to think. And if you have the workbook handy, you can follow each chart later with a ruler.

## What the exam expects (POPA 2.4)

The exam wants two things from you. First, find the maximum structural take-off and landing weights in the flight manual. And second, given a flight scenario, work the performance charts. That means finding the maximum take-off and landing weights, the take-off and landing distances required, and the climb weight limit. It means reading off the take-off parameters, which are the power, the flap setting and the take-off safety speed, and the landing parameters, which are the flap and the threshold speed. And it means saying what conditions those parameters assume.

## Why this matters

Correct use of the take-off and landing charts is first on CASA's list of PPLA weak areas. The charts are not hard, but they are unforgiving. Enter one input on the wrong scale, use a wind speed where the chart wants a component, or apply a slope the wrong way, and the answer is a few hundred metres or fifty kilograms out. And nothing warns you.

In real flying, the same skill decides whether you can take four people out of a 900 m grass strip at York on a February afternoon, or whether two of them drive home. The workbook charts are handed to you in the exam. This lesson teaches the order of work, and the checks that make the answer reliable.

## Where the structural limits come from

Before any chart, write down the limits that no chart can raise. The maximum structural take-off weight, MTOW, and the maximum structural landing weight, MLW, are set by the strength of the airframe and the undercarriage. You met them in the lesson on take-off and landing performance. They live in section 2 of the flight manual, the limitations section.

Section 2 may also give a maximum ramp weight. That is the heaviest weight for taxiing, a little above the MTOW, to allow for the fuel burned before take-off. And for some types it gives a maximum zero fuel weight. For a light trainer, the MTOW and MLW are often the same figure.

In the exam, the flight manual is either a limitations extract printed in the question, or the loading system page in the workbook, which prints the MTOW on the loading envelope. The workbook figures, as this lesson has them, are these. ALPHA has an MTOW of 1,633 kg. BRAVO, 1,000 kg, in the normal category. CHARLIE, 1,115 kg, also normal category. And ECHO, the odd one out, has two limits: an MTOW of 2,950 kg, and an MLW of 2,725 kg.

Read the limit, write it down, and treat it as a ceiling over every chart result. A performance chart can tell you that the runway would allow 1,700 kg. But if the structure allows 1,633 kg, the answer is 1,633 kg.

ECHO is the one with different take-off and landing limits. And that difference creates a question type of its own. A take-off at the MTOW is not legal if the aeroplane will still be above the MLW when it lands.

Let's work one. Aircraft ECHO: MTOW 2,950 kg, MLW 2,725 kg. You plan to take off at 2,950 kg, and to burn 250 L of avgas to the destination, at 0.72 kg per litre. Is that legal, and what is the maximum take-off weight for this trip? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 8]]
Step one, the fuel burned. 250 times 0.72 is 180 kg.

Step two, the landing weight. 2,950 minus 180 is 2,770 kg.

Step three, compare it with the MLW. 2,770 minus 2,725 is 45 kg over.

Step four, the maximum take-off weight for this trip. That is the MLW plus the fuel burned: 2,725 plus 180, which is 2,905 kg.

So the structure would allow 2,950 kg at take-off. But the landing limit brings the take-off weight down to 2,905 kg for this trip. And the landing weight chart for the destination, figure 13, may lower it again.

[[diagram:landing-weight-caps-takeoff]]
Picture two columns side by side. On the left, the aeroplane takes off at the MTOW of 2,950 kg, burns 180 kg, and lands at 2,770 kg, sticking up 45 kg above the MLW line. On the right, it takes off at 2,905 kg, and lands exactly on the MLW. The trip limit is the MLW plus the fuel burned: 2,905 kg.

Question. A performance chart gives a runway-limited take-off weight above the structural MTOW. What is your maximum take-off weight?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the structural MTOW, or a lower climb or landing limit, if one applies. A chart can only tell you what the runway and the air allow. It cannot make the airframe stronger.

## The charts in the workbook

The workbook contains two kinds of chart, and the syllabus labels them.

The weight charts answer the question: what is the heaviest I can be, for this runway, today? In the syllabus wording these are chart A, for the maximum take-off weight and the maximum landing weight.

The distance charts answer the question: how much runway does my weight need today? That is chart B, for the take-off distance required, TODR, and the landing distance required, LDR. The lesson on aerodromes and landing areas defines those distances, and the rule that required must not exceed available. In brief, and verify this against your chart's own definitions, the TODR runs from brake release to 50 ft at the take-off safety speed. And the LDR runs from 50 ft over the threshold to a stop.

There are six charts to know. Three are take-off weight charts: figure 3, figure 5, and figure 12, which is for aircraft ECHO. Then three landing charts: figure 4 is a landing distance chart, figure 6 a landing chart, and figure 13 the landing weight chart for ECHO.

Each chart is drawn for one aeroplane in one configuration, and the configuration is printed on it. Version 3 of the workbook also prints a crosswind limit on the take-off and landing charts, and a question can hinge on it.

Every chart has the same anatomy. It is a chain of panels, joined by guide lines. The first panel turns the air into a single reference value. Each later panel applies one correction, with sloping guide lines that show how much that factor changes the result. The last panel gives the answer, on a scale in kilograms or metres.

You enter on the left, follow the arrows, and never skip a panel. A factor that does not apply today, such as zero wind or a level runway, is still a panel you cross. You go straight through to the reference line, without moving up or down.

## Entering the chart: the order of work

Whatever the chart, the inputs come in the same order. Prepare all of them before you put the ruler down, so the chart work is mechanical. There are seven.

First, the pressure height. Work it from the elevation and QNH: the elevation, plus {1013|ten thirteen} minus the QNH, times 30. Not the density height. The chart applies the temperature itself, as the lesson on density height explains.

Second, the temperature. The actual shade temperature, in degrees Celsius, entered on the chart's own temperature scale.

Third, the weight or the distance. For a weight chart, it is the distance available: the TODA for take-off, the LDA for landing. For a distance chart, it is the weight.

Fourth, the wind component. The headwind or tailwind component along the runway, not the reported wind speed. Note which side of the reference line the chart puts tailwind on.

Fifth, the slope. In percent, and whether it is up or down in the direction you will be rolling.

Sixth, the surface. Sealed, gravel, short dry grass, or long or wet grass, as the chart provides.

And seventh, obstacles. They are not a chart panel, but they are the last check. The chart gets you to 50 ft at the end of the TODR. Whatever is beyond that is a climb gradient question, which you'll find in the lesson on climb, cruise and descent performance, and in the obstacle surfaces of the lesson on landing areas.

To get the component, picture the angle between the wind and the runway, and split the wind into two parts. The chart takes the headwind or tailwind part. The crosswind part goes against the crosswind limit printed on the chart.

[[diagram:runway-slope-take-off-and-landing]]
Now picture two side views of a sloping runway. Taking off uphill, part of the thrust climbs the slope, so the run is longer. Landing uphill, the slope helps you stop. So upslope is a penalty for the take-off, and a help for the landing. Downslope is the reverse.

Let's work one, preparing the take-off inputs at Northam. The elevation is 1,120 ft. The QNH is {1006|ten oh six}, and it is 33 degrees. You are using runway {32|three two}, with a TODA of 1,100 m, 1 percent up, on grass. The wind is {290|two nine zero} degrees at 15 kt. Pause if you'd like to work out the pressure height and the wind components.
[[pause 10]]
Step one, the pressure height. {1013|Ten thirteen} minus {1006|ten oh six} is 7. 7 times 30 is 210. 1,120 plus 210 is 1,330 ft.

Step two, the temperature: 33 degrees, entered as it is.

Step three, the distance: the TODA of 1,100 m.

Step four, the wind. The runway points 320 degrees, and the wind is from 290, so the angle is 30 degrees. The headwind component is 15 times the cosine of 30 degrees. That is 15 times 0.87, which is 13 kt. The crosswind is 15 times the sine of 30 degrees, which is 7.5 kt, from the left. You check that against the chart's crosswind limit.

Step five, the slope: 1 percent up, which is a take-off penalty.

And step six, the surface: grass.

Now you would enter figure 3 at 1,330 ft and 33 degrees. You go through the TODA of 1,100 m, the headwind of 13 kt, the 1 percent upslope and the grass, and read the runway-limited take-off weight. Then you read the climb weight limit for 1,330 ft and 33 degrees. The planned take-off weight from the loading sheet must be no more than the lowest of those two and the structural MTOW. The exact numbers depend on the chart, which is why the workbook is provided in the exam. So the inputs, once more: 1,330 ft, 33 degrees, 1,100 m, a 13 kt headwind, 1 percent up, and grass.

Now try preparing the landing inputs yourself. Later the same day you return to Northam, to land on runway {14|one four}. That is the reverse direction of the same runway, so it slopes 1 percent down. The QNH is now {1010|ten ten}, the temperature 24 degrees, and the wind {180|one eight zero} degrees at 12 kt.
[[pause 10]]
The pressure height: {1013|ten thirteen} minus {1010|ten ten} is 3. 3 times 30 is 90. 1,120 plus 90 is 1,210 ft. The temperature is 24 degrees.

The wind: the runway points 140, and the wind is from 180, so the angle is 40 degrees. The headwind is 12 times the cosine of 40 degrees, 12 times 0.77, which is 9 kt. The crosswind is 12 times the sine of 40 degrees, 12 times 0.64, which is 7.7 kt, from the right.

The slope is 1 percent down. That is a landing penalty, because gravity now opposes the brakes. The surface is grass. Then you enter the landing chart in that order, with the weight to find the LDR, or with the LDA to find the maximum landing weight.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Three input errors cause most wrong chart answers. Entering the full wind speed instead of the component. Entering the density height where the chart wants pressure height. And getting the slope backwards. A runway that is 1 percent up in one direction is 1 percent down in the other. And upslope is a penalty for the take-off, but a help for the landing.

## Working a take-off weight chart (chart A)

Start at the pressure height and temperature grid. Go up from the temperature to the pressure height line, interpolating between lines, then across to the first reference line. From there the chart takes you through the corrections, in the order its arrows show. Typically that is the take-off distance available, entered in metres, then the wind component, the runway slope and the surface, to the take-off weight scale. The result is the maximum take-off weight the runway allows today.

[[diagram:takeoff-weight-chart-walkthrough]]
Picture a stylised take-off weight chart, worked with the Northam inputs. You go up from 33 degrees to a line between the sea-level and 2,000 ft lines, then across to the first reference line. In each correction panel, you follow the sloping guide lines from the reference line to the input value, then carry straight across to the next panel. A headwind and more runway raise the weight. Upslope and grass lower it. The answer comes out on the weight scale at the right. This chart shows only the method, so use the workbook or your flight manual for numbers.

The same chart, or a separate line on it, gives the climb weight limit. That is the weight at which the aeroplane can still achieve the required climb gradient, at that pressure height and temperature. It is read without the runway corrections.

That makes sense once you see what each limit protects. The runway-limited weight depends on how much runway there is, and what the wind and surface do to the run. The climb gradient after take-off depends only on the air the aeroplane is climbing through. And the wind does not change the rate of climb through the air.

Now the real thing.

You run left to the TODA, up through surface and slope, then right into the wind panel, where you read the take-off weight. The same vertical line gives the take-off safety speed for that weight, on the scale above it. This chart uses a distance factor of 1.22, and a 20 kt crosswind limit.

Your permitted take-off weight is the lowest of three numbers. The chart's runway-limited weight. The climb weight limit. And the structural MTOW. And, as the ECHO example showed, any take-off weight forced down by the landing limit.

Question. Why is the climb weight limit read without the runway length, wind, slope and surface corrections?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because it protects the climb after take-off. That depends on the aeroplane's power and drag in today's air, which are set by pressure height and temperature. The runway factors change how far the aeroplane rolls, not how well it climbs through the air once it is airborne.

## Working a distance chart (chart B)

The take-off distance chart runs the other way. Enter the same pressure height and temperature. Then the weight. Then wind, slope and surface. And read the take-off distance required, in metres, to the 50 ft screen height. Compare it with the TODA. The TODR must not exceed it.

Temperature and pressure height come first, then surface, slope and wind, then the take-off weight lines, and the take-off distance required at the top. The climb limit weight is a separate small panel, at the bottom left.

## Working the landing charts

The landing charts follow the same pattern. Enter the pressure height and temperature. Then the weight, or the landing distance available. Then wind, slope and surface. Read either the landing distance required, from 50 ft to a stop, as in figure 4. Or read the maximum landing weight for the LDA, as in figure 13, for aircraft ECHO. Then compare the LDR with the LDA, and the landing weight with the structural MLW.

Each panel of these charts applies one factor from a familiar table of arrows. Only a headwind shortens both distances. A tailwind lengthens both markedly. Lower density, from heat, low QNH or high elevation, lengthens both. Uphill lengthens the take-off, and downhill lengthens the landing. And grass, gravel, wet surfaces and more weight lengthen both.

## The parameters and the conditions printed on the charts

Every chart states the technique and configuration it assumes. Its figures are only true if you fly that way. That is why the exam asks you to read them off, and state them.

Start with the take-off parameters. The conditions box gives the power: full throttle, maximum power. It gives the flap setting, for example 10 degrees of take-off flap, or flaps up. And it gives the take-off safety speed, to be reached by 50 ft.

The TOSS is either a single indicated airspeed, or a small table or scale on which it varies with weight. Figure 3 gives 60 KIAS, with 10 degrees of flap and full throttle. Figure 12 gives a scale. A heavier aeroplane stalls at a higher speed, so it needs a higher TOSS.

The box also states the surface type the basic chart is drawn for, and that the distances are factored. The CASA charts include a safety factor of 1.15 on the measured distance for take-off, and the landing charts a larger factor, measured to or from the 50 ft screen. Verify that on the chart in front of you.

Now the landing parameters. The flap setting, usually full flap. And the threshold speed, the indicated airspeed at 50 ft over the threshold. Figure 6 prints 58 KIAS. Figure 13 gives a scale that varies with weight. The landing distances also assume power off from the threshold, maximum braking, a dry surface unless corrected, and the factored distance.

And for both. The pressure height and temperature are ISA-based inputs. And each correction is from a baseline: still air before the wind correction, a level runway before the slope correction, and a sealed surface before the surface correction.

So the pattern is this. Take-off: full throttle, the printed flap, the TOSS by 50 ft, and distances factored by 1.15 on the CASA charts. Landing: power off from the threshold, usually full flap, the threshold speed at 50 ft, maximum braking, and a larger factor. For both, the speed is either one fixed indicated airspeed, or a scale by weight.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. The chart's distance is only valid for the chart's technique. Take off with less flap than the chart assumes, rotate late, or cross the threshold 10 kt fast, and the real distance is longer than the chart says, however carefully you read it. And when a question asks for the conditions on which the take-off figures are based, it wants the power, flap, TOSS and factoring from the conditions box. Not the day's weather.

Question. Why does the take-off safety speed on the ECHO chart vary with weight?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because the stall speed rises with weight, and the TOSS must keep the same margin above the stall. A heavier aeroplane needs a higher speed at 50 ft to have the same protection against a gust or a turn.

## Reading accurately

Most marks lost on charts are lost in the reading, not the understanding. Seven habits fix that.

First, use a ruler and a sharp pencil. Draw every line. Do not sight across the chart by eye.

Second, follow the guide lines exactly parallel. In each correction panel, run your line parallel to the nearest guide lines, not straight across. A line drawn flat through a sloping panel is the commonest single error.

Third, read the scales to the nearest division, and interpolate between the printed lines.

Fourth, take the wind component from the table or the trigonometry, never the full wind speed. And check whether the chart wants it as a head or a tail component.

Fifth, confirm the slope direction. Up, for take-off, is a penalty. Up, for landing, is a benefit.

Sixth, do not round intermediate readings. Carry each value through, and round the answer.

And seventh, write the answer with its units, kilograms or metres. Then check it against the structural limit, and the distance available.

Then ask whether the answer makes sense. A hot, high, tailwind, grass, upslope take-off should give a lower weight or a longer distance than a cool, sealed, into-wind one. If your answer moved the other way, a line went the wrong way in one of the panels.

Question. In a wind correction panel, the guide lines slope down to the right. Your line arrives at the reference line, and the headwind is 13 kt. Do you draw straight across?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: no. You follow the slope of the guide lines from the reference line until you reach the 13 kt headwind position. Then you continue to the next panel's reference line. Drawing straight across ignores the wind correction entirely.

## Bringing it together

You plan to take three friends from Northam to Rottnest for lunch, on a hot day. The loading sheet puts you at a take-off weight 10 kg below the structural MTOW from the limitations page.

You use the Northam inputs you worked earlier. You enter figure 3 at 1,330 ft and 33 degrees. You cross the TODA, the 13 kt headwind, the 1 percent upslope and the grass panels, and read the runway-limited weight. It comes out above your planned weight, so the runway is long enough.

Then you read the climb weight limit for 1,330 ft and 33 degrees, with no runway corrections. Suppose it is below your planned weight. The runway would let you get airborne, and the structure would carry you. But the aeroplane could not then make the required climb gradient. The climb weight limit governs, and you remove fuel or baggage until you are under it.

Finally you check the conditions box. The figures assume full throttle, the stated flap, and the TOSS by 50 ft. So that is how you fly the take-off. On arrival, you would repeat the process on the landing chart, for Rottnest's pressure height, temperature, wind and runway.

## Key points

Let's recap.

The structural MTOW and MLW are in section 2 of the flight manual, the limitations, or on the loading system page in the workbook. ALPHA, 1,633 kg. BRAVO, 1,000 kg, normal category. CHARLIE, 1,115 kg, normal category. And ECHO, 2,950 kg, with an MLW of 2,725 kg. They cap every chart answer. And the MLW can cap the take-off weight, through the fuel burn.

Enter every chart in the same order. Pressure height and temperature. Then the distance available for a weight chart, chart A, or the weight for a distance chart, chart B. Then the wind component, slope and surface.

The weight chart gives the runway-limited weight. The climb weight limit is read from pressure height and temperature alone. Take the lowest of those, with the MTOW. The distance charts give the TODR to 50 ft, and the LDR from 50 ft, to compare with the TODA and the LDA.

The conditions box gives the take-off power, flap and TOSS, and the landing flap and threshold speed. And it gives the assumptions: factored distances, power off from the threshold, maximum braking, and still air, level, sealed and dry baselines.

A final exam tip. Practise the workbook charts until the entry order is automatic: pressure height and temperature, then distance or weight, then wind, slope and surface. Use the wind component, not the wind speed. Always compare the chart weight with the structural limit and the climb weight limit. And always compare the required distance with the distance available. Know where the TOSS and threshold speed are printed: figure 3, 60 KIAS. Figure 6, 58 KIAS. And ECHO, by weight. And know that the CASA distances are already factored.

That's the end of POPA 2.4.
