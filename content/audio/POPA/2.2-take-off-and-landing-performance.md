This is POPA 2.2, Take-off and landing performance, the private pilot version. By the end you will know what every factor does to the take-off, the landing and the climb, which weight limit governs today, and what happens if you exceed it. There are worked examples and a few questions along the way, so pause whenever you want a moment to think.

## What the exam expects (POPA 2.2)

The exam wants five things from you. First, whether each factor in the syllabus increases or decreases the take-off distance, the landing distance and the take-off climb. That means wind, density, slope and surface, ground effect, wind shear and frost. Second, the difference between pressure height and density height. Third, how the altimeter on the ground gives you the local QNH, the pressure height and the elevation. Fourth, the maximum structural take-off and landing weights, and the climb weight limit. And fifth, what is likely to happen if you exceed the weight limits.

## Why this matters

As a private pilot you will fill four seats and the tanks, and set off for Kalbarri in January. The aeroplane will be heavier, slower to accelerate and slower to climb than the trainer you soloed in.

The RPL lessons taught each factor on its own. This lesson puts them together the way a private pilot meets them: several at once, on the same afternoon. And it adds what actually happens when you load past the limits.

The PPLA exam asks the same increase-or-decrease questions as the RPLA, but in an operational frame. Given today's conditions, which limit governs, and does the planned flight comply?

## Two heights the aeroplane might care about

Elevation is the surveyed height of the aerodrome above mean sea level, printed in ERSA. It never changes, and the aeroplane is indifferent to it.

Next, pressure height. It is the height in the International Standard Atmosphere, ISA, at which the pressure equals today's pressure at the aerodrome. ISA is the agreed model atmosphere. At sea level it has {1013.25 hPa|ten thirteen point two five hectopascals} and 15 degrees. The temperature falls 2 degrees per 1,000 ft, and near the ground the pressure falls about 1 hectopascal per 30 ft.

If today's QNH is {1013|ten thirteen}, the pressure height equals the elevation. If a trough has dropped the QNH to {1003|ten oh three}, the pressure at the aerodrome is what ISA has 300 ft higher. So the pressure height is 300 ft above the elevation.

Then density height. It is the height in ISA at which the air density equals today's density at the aerodrome. Density depends on pressure and on temperature. So density height is pressure height corrected for temperature: about 120 ft higher for every degree the air is warmer than ISA at that pressure height, and the same amount lower for every degree colder.

[[diagram:height-altitude-elevation]]
Picture a side view, with the sea on the left and an aerodrome 300 ft above it. That 300 ft is the elevation, fixed by survey. An aeroplane in the circuit with the QNH set reads 1,300 ft, so it is 1,000 ft above the aerodrome. What the altimeter shows depends on the subscale setting. And none of these is the density height.

Each height serves a different customer. Pressure height serves the altimeter and the airspace system, and it is one of the two inputs to every CASA take-off and landing chart. Density height serves the aeroplane. The wing, the propeller and the engine all respond to the number of air molecules they meet, not to the pressure alone.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Pressure height and density height are equal only when the temperature is exactly ISA for that pressure height. An option that says density height is the altimeter reading with {1013|ten thirteen} set is describing pressure height. Another favourite distractor says a high QNH raises the density height. It lowers it, because high pressure packs more air into each cubic metre.

Question. Two aerodromes have the same pressure height today. One is at 12 degrees, the other at 36 degrees. Which has the higher density height? And does the altimeter on the ground tell you?
[[pause 5]]
The answer: the 36 degree aerodrome, by roughly 24 times 120, which is 2,880 ft. The altimeter cannot tell you. With {1013|ten thirteen} set, both read the same pressure height. Only the temperature correction reveals the density height.

## What the altimeter tells you on the ground

An altimeter is a barometer graduated in feet. Whatever pressure is in the subscale window, the needles show height above the level where that pressure exists, assuming ISA. A higher setting gives a higher reading. So sitting on the ground at a known elevation, one instrument gives you three readings.

First, the local QNH. Wind the subscale until the needles show the aerodrome elevation. The number in the window is now the local QNH: the pressure that, set on the subscale, makes the altimeter read true height above mean sea level at this spot.

Second, the pressure height. Set {1013|ten thirteen}. The needles now show height above the standard pressure level, which is the definition of pressure height.

Third, the elevation. Set the aerodrome QNH, from the ATIS, the AWIS or the tower. The needles should show the aerodrome elevation. This is also the pre-flight accuracy check. An error within 100 ft is acceptable for VFR. Check that figure against the current AIP. Larger than that, and the altimeter is unserviceable for the flight.

[[widget:altimeter-subscale]]
Picture winding the subscale knob: each hectopascal of difference from the real QNH moves the reading about 30 ft.

Let's work one, at a farm strip. You know the strip's elevation is 960 ft. With the needles set to 960 ft, the subscale shows {1004 hPa|ten oh four hectopascals}. Later you hear an area QNH of {1005|ten oh five}. What are the local QNH and the pressure height, and what does the altimeter read with the area QNH set? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 8]]
Step one, the local QNH. It is the subscale reading with the needles at the elevation: {1004|ten oh four}.

Step two, the pressure height. Setting {1013|ten thirteen} is a change of 9 hectopascals upward. So the reading rises by 9 times 30, which is 270 ft. 960 plus 270 is 1,230 ft.

Step three, the area QNH. {1005|Ten oh five} is 1 hectopascal above the local value. So the altimeter reads 960 plus 30, which is 990 ft. That is an error of 30 ft.

The 30 ft error is well inside the 100 ft VFR tolerance. And the pressure height of 1,230 ft is the number you take to the take-off chart.

Question. You set {1013|ten thirteen} on the subscale at an aerodrome of elevation 400 ft, and the altimeter reads 160 ft. Is the QNH above or below {1013|ten thirteen}, and roughly what is it?
[[pause 6]]
The answer: above. The pressure height is 240 ft below the elevation. So the sea-level pressure is higher than standard by 240 divided by 30, which is 8 hectopascals. The QNH is about {1021|ten twenty-one}.

## Density: temperature, QNH, elevation and density height

The syllabus lists temperature, QNH, density height and elevation separately. But they are one factor, seen four ways. Each of the first three lowers the density, and density height combines them.

Thin air hurts three parts of the aeroplane at once.

First, the engine. A normally aspirated piston engine draws in a fixed volume of air each stroke. Thinner air means less oxygen per stroke, so less fuel can be burned. Full-throttle power falls by roughly 3 percent per 1,000 ft of density height.

Second, the propeller. It is a rotating wing, and in thinner air each blade makes less thrust at the same RPM.

Third, the wing. Lift depends on the dynamic pressure, which is half the air density times the true airspeed squared. With a lower density, the wing needs a higher true airspeed to make the same lift. The airspeed indicator responds to the same dynamic pressure. So you still lift off at the same indicated airspeed. But the true airspeed, and so the groundspeed the aeroplane must reach, is higher.

[[diagram:power-loss-with-altitude]]
Picture a line sloping down as the height increases: full-throttle power, as a percentage of sea-level power. A 5,000 ft density height on a hot afternoon leaves about 85 percent.

Put those together. For the take-off, there is less thrust, accelerating the aeroplane to a higher groundspeed. So the take-off distance increases. For the landing, the same indicated approach speed is a higher true airspeed and groundspeed, so there is more kinetic energy for the brakes to absorb. The landing distance increases. For the climb, the rate of climb depends on the surplus of power available over power required. Thin air shrinks the power available, while raising the power required. So the take-off climb performance decreases, and it suffers most of the three.

So higher temperature, lower QNH, higher elevation and higher density height all increase both distances, and reduce the climb. The reverse of each improves performance. A cold, high-pressure morning at a coastal aerodrome can have a density height below sea level.

Question. Why does a high density height lengthen the landing distance, when you fly the same indicated approach speed as always?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because the same indicated airspeed is a higher true airspeed in thin air. So the aeroplane touches down at a higher groundspeed, with more kinetic energy for the brakes to absorb. The indicated airspeed keeps the wing safe. It does not keep the energy the same.

## Wind: what the component does

Only the part of the wind along the runway counts for performance: the headwind or tailwind component. The crosswind component does nothing for performance, and everything to your workload. The workbook charts take the along-runway component as their wind input, not the reported wind speed.

[[widget:crosswind-component]]
To split a wind into its two parts, picture the angle between the wind and the runway. The crosswind part grows fast at first. Thirty degrees off the runway already puts half the wind across it. But the headwind component is still nearly nine-tenths of the wind.

The aeroplane lifts off at a fixed airspeed. A headwind supplies part of that airspeed before the aeroplane moves, so lift-off happens at a lower groundspeed. A tailwind means a higher groundspeed at lift-off. The runway used depends on the energy built up, and kinetic energy goes with the square of the groundspeed.

[[diagram:kinetic-energy-speed-squared]]
Picture a curve of energy against speed, bending upward more and more steeply. Go 10 percent faster, and you carry 21 percent more energy. Double the speed, and you carry four times the energy.

Let's work one. The aeroplane lifts off at 55 kt true airspeed. Compare an 8 kt headwind component with an 8 kt tailwind component, for the ground roll only. Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 8]]
With the headwind, the groundspeed at lift-off is 55 minus 8, which is 47 kt. 47 over 55, squared, is 0.73. So the roll is about 27 percent shorter.

With the tailwind, the groundspeed at lift-off is 55 plus 8, which is 63 kt. 63 over 55, squared, is 1.31. So the roll is about 31 percent longer.

The same 8 kt costs more as a tailwind than it saves as a headwind. That is why the CASA charts penalise tailwind more steeply than they credit headwind. And it is why the rule of thumb says a tailwind of 10 percent of the lift-off speed adds roughly 20 percent to the take-off distance.

The landing roll obeys the same square law. A headwind shortens it, and a tailwind lengthens it markedly.

For the take-off climb, wind changes the climb gradient over the ground, but not the rate of climb. The aeroplane gains the same feet per minute in the moving air. Into a headwind, it covers less ground in that minute, so it is higher over any given tree.

[[diagram:climb-gradient-wind]]
Picture the same aeroplane climbing at 600 ft per minute, three times over. In still air it gains 450 ft for every nautical mile. With a 20 kt headwind the path is steeper, 600 ft per nautical mile. With a 20 kt tailwind it is flatter, only 360.

## Slope and surface, including wet and slush

Now the ground itself. Performance charts are drawn for a level, dry, sealed runway. Every departure from that has a direction and a reason.

Start with slope. On an upslope, a component of the aeroplane's weight acts down the runway, roughly the weight times the slope as a fraction. It opposes the thrust during the take-off, and helps the brakes during the landing. So an upslope increases the take-off distance and decreases the landing distance. A downslope does the reverse. As a rule of thumb, a 2 percent upslope adds around 10 percent to the take-off distance. The climb through the air is unchanged. But rising ground beyond an upslope strip reduces the clearance.

[[diagram:runway-slope-take-off-and-landing]]
Picture two side views of a sloping runway. Taking off uphill, part of the thrust is spent climbing the slope. Landing downhill, gravity fights the brakes. So with light wind, land uphill and take off downhill.

Now the surface, in four cases.

The first is grass and gravel. They have a higher rolling resistance than bitumen, so the take-off is longer. On landing, the extra drag helps a little. But braking on grass is poorer, so the landing distance is also longer on most charts.

The second is a wet sealed runway. Water barely changes the rolling resistance, so the take-off changes little. But it lubricates the tyre contact, so braking falls away, and the landing distance increases, often by 15 percent or more.

The third is wet grass. Both distances increase. The soft surface drags at the wheels on take-off, and the slippery grass robs the brakes on landing.

The fourth, and the worst case, is slush and standing water. The wheels have to push the slush aside, and the spray strikes the airframe. Both drags grow with speed. So the net accelerating force shrinks just as you approach lift-off speed. The take-off distance increases greatly, and the aeroplane may never reach lift-off speed at all. On landing, the tyres can aquaplane, losing braking and steering together. The landing distance increases greatly. Most light-aeroplane flight manuals give no slush figures at all, and that absence is the answer.

[[diagram:runway-slope-and-surface-effects]]
Picture those effects as a small table, slope in the top rows and surface below. The pattern to notice: wet sealed hurts the landing only, wet grass hurts both, and slush hurts both greatly.

On a contaminated surface, the climb through the air is unchanged. But the aeroplane leaves the ground further down the strip, closer to whatever is beyond the end. The exam calls that climb unchanged, but starting later.

Question. A sealed runway is wet after a shower. Which distance does that affect most, and why?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the landing distance. Water hardly adds rolling resistance, so the take-off is little changed. But it lubricates the tyre contact, and the brakes can no longer stop the aeroplane as quickly.

## Ground effect and wind shear

First, ground effect. Within about one wingspan of the ground, the ground blocks the downwash behind the wing and squashes the wingtip vortices. The wing's induced drag, the drag that comes with making lift, falls sharply. Two consequences follow.

On take-off, the aeroplane can become airborne at a speed and angle of attack at which it cannot fly once clear of the ground. Pulled off early, a heavily loaded aeroplane on a hot day lifts to 10 or 15 ft, and leaves ground effect. It meets its full induced drag, and either sinks back, or hangs there, unable to accelerate or climb. So ground effect shortens the ground run, but it can destroy the take-off climb. The protection is to hold the aeroplane down until the flight manual rotation speed.

On landing, an aeroplane arriving a little fast floats in ground effect, using up runway before it settles. So the landing distance increases.

Second, wind shear, a change in wind speed or direction over a short distance. The aeroplane's inertia keeps its groundspeed for a moment, so a sudden loss of headwind, or a gain in tailwind, drops the indicated airspeed and the lift, with the attitude unchanged. On the take-off climb, that means a loss of airspeed and climb rate, just when height is scarce. On the approach, it means sinking below the path and undershooting. In Western Australia the classic case is the sea-breeze front arriving at Jandakot on a summer afternoon.

[[diagram:wind-shear-on-approach]]
Picture an approach from the side, into a strong headwind that dies away near the ground. The groundspeed holds for a moment, so the indicated airspeed drops, and the aeroplane sinks below the path with no change in attitude or power.

The defence is speed in hand, a margin on the approach speed in gusty or sheared conditions, and power ready. For the exam, wind shear on take-off reduces the take-off climb performance and can be dangerous. On the landing its effect is variable, usually an undershoot.

Question. Why can an aeroplane lift off in ground effect, but then fail to climb?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: in ground effect the induced drag is much lower, so the wing can fly at a speed too low to support it in free air. Above about a wingspan, the induced drag returns in full, and if the speed is still low, the aeroplane cannot accelerate or climb.

## Frost on the aeroplane

A thin frost on a winter morning at Narrogin looks harmless. It is not. Frost roughens the upper surface of the wing, especially near the leading edge, and the flow separates at a lower angle of attack. The maximum lift coefficient falls, and drag rises. So the stall speed goes up, sometimes by a large margin, while the thrust is unchanged.

So the take-off distance increases, because the aeroplane must reach a higher speed before it can lift off. It may not lift off at the normal rotation speed at all. Pulled off at the usual speed, it can stall in ground effect, or just after leaving it. And the take-off climb performance decreases, because the wing is giving less lift for more drag.

Frost forms on a parked aeroplane, so the exam treats it as a take-off problem. The rule is simple. Remove all frost before flight, every time.

## The factors side by side

The exam is built from this comparison, so let's sort the factors by their pattern. Test yourself as we go.

First, the density group: higher temperature, lower QNH, higher density height and higher elevation. All four increase both distances, and decrease the take-off climb.

Next, wind. A headwind component decreases both distances, and steepens the climb over the ground. A tailwind component increases both distances markedly, and flattens the climb over the ground.

Then slope, where the two distances go opposite ways. An upslope increases the take-off and decreases the landing, and the climb is reduced relative to the rising ground. A downslope decreases the take-off and increases the landing, and the climb is unchanged, with the ground falling away.

Then surface. A wet sealed runway: little change to the take-off, a longer landing, and the climb unchanged. Wet grass: both distances increase, and the climb is unchanged but starts later. Slush: both distances increase greatly, and again the climb is unchanged but starts later.

And the last three. Ground effect makes lift-off possible before climb is, makes you float on landing, and the climb sags as you leave it. Wind shear that takes away the headwind makes the take-off longer, has a variable effect on the landing, and causes a dangerous loss of airspeed and climb. Frost increases the take-off distance, may prevent lift-off, and decreases the climb.

[[diagram:takeoff-landing-factors]]
Picture all of that as a single table of arrows. Only one factor shortens both distances: a headwind. Everything that lowers density, adds weight, adds a tailwind or roughens the surface lengthens them.

The factors also stack. Each one multiplies the distance. So a moderate tailwind, a hot day and a grass strip together can double the book figure, even though no single factor looks alarming. That is why the charts take each correction in turn.

## Structural weights and the climb weight limit

There are two quite different reasons an aeroplane can be too heavy. The exam wants you to keep them apart.

The maximum structural take-off weight, MTOW, is the heaviest weight at which the aeroplane may begin a take-off. It is set by the strength of the airframe, certified to carry a stated load at a stated load factor. The maximum structural landing weight, MLW, is the heaviest weight at which it may land, where the undercarriage has to absorb the vertical speed of touchdown. For most light trainers the two are equal. Both are printed in section 2 of the flight manual, and they never change with the weather.

The climb weight limit is a performance limit, not a structural one. It is the heaviest weight at which the aeroplane can still achieve the required climb gradient after take-off, at today's pressure height and temperature. It is sometimes called the WAT limit: weight, altitude, temperature.

On a cool morning at a coastal aerodrome, it is usually above the structural MTOW, and it does not bite. On a 40 degree afternoon inland, with the density height thousands of feet above the elevation, it can fall below the MTOW. Then it is the governing limit, however strong the airframe. The workbook take-off weight chart gives it as a separate reading, alongside the weight the runway allows.

Your permitted take-off weight is the lowest of four numbers. First, the structural MTOW. Second, today's climb weight limit. Third, the runway-limited weight from the take-off chart. And fourth, the take-off weight that will burn down to no more than the MLW, and the landing-chart weight for the destination, by the time you land.

Let's work one. The structural MTOW is 1,150 kg, and so is the MLW. Today's take-off chart gives a runway-limited weight of 1,185 kg, and a climb weight limit of 1,095 kg. The loading sheet gives a planned weight of 1,120 kg. Which limit governs, and by how much are you over? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 8]]
The candidates are 1,150 kg for the structure, 1,095 kg for the climb, and 1,185 kg for the runway. The lowest is 1,095 kg, the climb weight limit. And 1,120 minus 1,095 is 25 kg over.

The runway is long enough, and the structure is strong enough. But the aeroplane cannot meet the required climb gradient at 1,120 kg today. You must offload 25 kg. That is about 35 L of avgas at 0.72 kg per litre, or a bag. Or wait for cooler air.

[[diagram:climb-weight-limit-governs]]
Picture those numbers on one weight scale, with the climb weight limit at the bottom, below your planned weight. So the answer: the climb weight limit governs, and you are 25 kg over it.

Question. Why can the climb weight limit change during the day, when the structural MTOW never does?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the climb weight limit depends on how well the aeroplane can climb in today's air. As the day warms, the density height rises and climb performance falls, so the heaviest weight that still meets the required gradient falls with it. The structure does not care about the air.

## What happens when you exceed the limits

This element has a clear physical answer, under four headings.

The first is performance. More weight needs more lift, which needs more speed. The stall speed rises with the square root of the weight. So every speed the flight manual gives, rotation, take-off safety speed, threshold speed, is too low for an overweight aeroplane.

Accelerating more mass to a higher speed takes much longer. As a rough rule, the take-off distance grows with about the square of the weight. So 10 percent overweight costs about 20 percent more runway, because 1.1 squared is 1.21. The landing distance grows too, with more kinetic energy to absorb. And the climb shrinks, because the extra weight raises the power required and adds a component of weight along the climb path. That is exactly the margin the climb weight limit exists to protect.

Let's work one. The stall speed is 48 kt at the MTOW of 1,150 kg. The aeroplane has been loaded to 1,265 kg, 10 percent over. What is the new stall speed? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 8]]
Step one, the weight ratio. 1,265 divided by 1,150 is 1.10.

Step two. The stall speed scales with the square root of the weight. The square root of 1.10 is 1.049. 48 times 1.049 is 50.3 kt.

So every published speed now carries less margin. A take-off safety speed meant to be 1.2 times the stall speed is now only about 1.14 times it. The answer, once more: the stall speed rises from 48 to 50.3 kt.

The second heading is structure. The airframe is certified to a limit load factor at the MTOW. In the normal category, that is plus 3.8 g. What that really means is that the structure can carry 3.8 times the maximum weight. Load it 10 percent heavier, and the same structure reaches its limit at 3.8 divided by 1.1, about 3.45 g.

So a gust, a firm pull-out, or a heavy landing that the aeroplane would shrug off at normal weight can now bend something permanently. The undercarriage, tyres and brakes are certified for the MLW. Landing heavier overloads them, and a hard landing overweight is where most of the damage happens.

[[diagram:category-load-limits]]
Picture three bars, for the normal, utility and aerobatic categories. Their limit load factors are plus 3.8, 4.4 and 6 g. Beyond the limit, the structure may bend permanently. Those limits are set at maximum weight. Overload the aeroplane, and the same structure reaches its limit at a lower g.

The third heading is handling. Weight usually comes with a centre of gravity problem. The extra bags go in the back, and the extra passenger in the rear seat.

[[diagram:cg-position-effects]]
Picture the centre of gravity sliding along the aeroplane. Forward of the limit, it is nose heavy, with a higher stall speed, and there may not be enough elevator to flare. Aft of the limit, the pitch control is light and over-sensitive, and stall and spin recovery is poor.

The fourth heading is the law. Flying above a structural or performance limit is a breach of the aeroplane's operating limitations, and so of the regulations. The certification no longer describes the aeroplane you are flying.

[[diagram:overweight-ten-percent]]
Picture three before-and-after bars for that 10 percent overload: about 5 percent on the stall speed, about 20 percent on the runway, and a lower g limit. One overload hits speed, distance and strength at once.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. The aeroplane will simply use a bit more runway. That is the answer to reject. Exceeding the weight limits affects everything at once: distances, climb, speeds, range and manoeuvrability, the structure, tyres and brakes, and the law.

## Bringing it together

It is the first week of January, and you plan to fly three friends from Northam to Kalbarri for a long weekend. Before you leave home, the AWIS gives Northam 37 degrees and a QNH of {1005|ten oh five}.

In the aeroplane, you set the subscale to {1013|ten thirteen}, and the altimeter shows about 240 ft above the elevation. The air is nearly 25 degrees above ISA, so the density height is about 3,000 ft above that. The wind is a light easterly, a tailwind component of 4 kt on the runway you would otherwise use. And the far end of the runway is a little higher than the near end.

Every factor so far lengthens the take-off and weakens the climb. Four people and full tanks put you 15 kg below the structural MTOW. But the workbook-style chart for today gives a climb weight limit 40 kg below your planned weight.

You have three options. Take off from the other end, downslope and out of the tailwind. That helps the distance, but not the climb weight limit. Offload about 55 L of fuel, and plan a stop at Geraldton. Or leave at dawn tomorrow, at 22 degrees.

What you may not do is go at the planned weight and hope. The aeroplane would be over a limit that exists precisely for the hot afternoon you are about to fly into.

## Key points

Let's recap.

The factors. Only a headwind shortens both distances. Heat, low QNH, high elevation and high density height all mean lower density: both distances longer, and the climb poorer. An upslope lengthens the take-off and shortens the landing. A wet sealed runway lengthens the landing, wet grass both, and slush both greatly. Ground effect lets you lift off before you can climb. Wind shear drops the indicated airspeed. Frost raises the stall speed.

The heights. Pressure height is the ISA height with today's pressure, the altimeter reading with {1013|ten thirteen} set. Density height is the ISA height with today's density: the pressure height corrected by about 120 ft per degree from ISA. Performance follows density height.

On the ground, needles at the elevation give the local QNH. {1013|Ten thirteen} gives the pressure height. The aerodrome QNH gives the elevation, within 100 ft for VFR.

The weights. Structural MTOW and MLW are airframe limits that never change. The climb weight limit is the heaviest weight that still meets the required climb gradient today. The permitted take-off weight is the lowest of all the limits.

And over the limits: longer distances, a poorer climb, higher stall and approach speeds, structure and undercarriage beyond certification, centre of gravity and control problems, and a breach of the regulations.

A final exam tip. Expect increase-or-decrease questions, usually with one factor that cuts the other way to catch you. An upslope shortens the landing. A wet sealed runway barely changes the take-off. For weight questions, the answer is always the lowest limit, and the climb weight limit is the one that falls on a hot day. Have these by heart: 30 ft per hectopascal. 120 ft per degree from ISA. ISA 15 degrees, falling 2 per 1,000 ft. And the 100 ft altimeter tolerance.

That's the end of POPA 2.2.
