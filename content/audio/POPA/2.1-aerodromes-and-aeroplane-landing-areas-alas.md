This is POPA 2.1, Aerodromes and aeroplane landing areas, the strips pilots call ALAs. By the end you will know the five distances and speeds that decide whether a runway is long enough, where each one starts and ends, and how to judge whether a farm strip is fit to use. There are worked examples and a few questions along the way, so pause whenever you want a moment to think.

## What the exam expects (POPA 2.1)

The exam wants two things from you. First, the terms: take-off safety speed, take-off distance available, take-off distance required, landing distance available and landing distance required. You must explain each one, and use them to decide whether a runway is long enough. Second, whether a given aeroplane landing area is suitable for a safe take-off and landing, using CASA's guidelines for these strips.

## Why this matters

Much of Western Australian private flying ends on a strip with no ERSA entry. A farm runway near York for a family lunch. A station strip in the Murchison. A mate's paddock outside Esperance. There is no aerodrome operator, no published distances, and no one to tell you the grass is long or the far end is soft.

The pilot in command decides whether the place is suitable. That decision is made with five defined distances and speeds, and a short list of guidelines from CASA. The exam asks both halves: what each term means, and where it is measured from and to; and whether a described strip passes. This lesson builds the terms from a picture of an actual take-off and landing. Then it works through the ALA guidelines one at a time, with the reason behind each.

## The take-off, from brake release to 50 ft

Picture a take-off from the threshold of a 700 m grass strip. You release the brakes at full power, and the aeroplane accelerates along the ground until it reaches lift-off speed. That part is the ground run. Then it lifts off and climbs, still accelerating, until it is 50 ft above the runway surface. That second part is the airborne segment. Together, they are the take-off distance.

Why stop counting at 50 ft? Because the aeroplane is not safe from the ground until it is clear of the things that sit on the ground. Fences, stock, a parked tractor, the trees at the end of the paddock. So performance data is measured to an imaginary screen, 50 ft high. Every take-off chart in the CASA workbook, and every take-off table in the flight manual, stops there. Above 50 ft the problem becomes the climb gradient, which is a separate question. You'll find that in the lesson on climb, cruise and descent performance.

At the 50 ft screen, the aeroplane must be flying at a speed that gives it a safe margin above the stall. That speed is the take-off safety speed, or TOSS. For light aeroplanes it is at least 1.2 times the stall speed in the take-off configuration. And the performance charts are based on reaching it at the screen height.

The margin is there because the first few hundred feet after take-off are where the aeroplane is most exposed. A gust that drops the airspeed, a wind shear as you climb out of the shelter of the trees, or a turn to avoid a bird, all raise the speed at which the wing stalls. Twenty percent above the stall speed is enough to absorb an ordinary gust, or to bank to about 45 degrees in a level turn, before the wing reaches its critical angle.

That figure follows from the turn formula. A stall speed 1.2 times higher means a load factor of 1.2 squared, which is 1.44. And one over the cosine of the bank angle equals 1.44 at a bank of about 46 degrees.

The CASA charts print the TOSS in their conditions box. Figure 3 in the workbook, for example, gives 60 KIAS, with 10 degrees of flap and full throttle. The ECHO chart, figure 12, gives a scale on which the TOSS varies with weight. A heavier aeroplane stalls at a higher speed, so it needs a higher TOSS.

Let's work one. The flight manual gives a stall speed of 47 kt indicated, with take-off flap, at maximum weight. What is the minimum take-off safety speed? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
1.2 times 47 is 56.4 kt. Round up, never down. So the minimum TOSS is 57 kt, at the very least.

If the flight manual or the chart gives a higher figure, fly that. The 1.2 factor is a floor, not a target, and the manufacturer's number already includes it. So the answer: 1.2 times 47 gives 56.4, which rounds up to 57 kt.

Question. Why is the take-off distance measured to 50 ft, and not to the point of lift-off?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because lifting off is not the same as being safe. The aeroplane is clear of fences, stock and vehicles only once it has climbed above them. So performance is measured to a 50 ft screen, at the take-off safety speed.

## Required against available

Every distance comparison in this subtopic has the same shape. One number comes from the aeroplane: how much it needs today. The other comes from the runway: how much it has. The first must never exceed the second.

Start with the take-off distance required, TODR. It is the distance from the start of the take-off run to the point where the aeroplane reaches 50 ft at the take-off safety speed. You get it from the performance chart, for the day's weight, pressure height, temperature, wind, slope and surface. The lesson on using the take-off and landing charts shows how to work them. And the TODR includes the safety factor built into the chart.

That last phrase matters. The distance a test pilot achieves in a new aeroplane on a perfect day is the measured, or unfactored, distance. You are not a test pilot. Your aeroplane is not new, and your strip is not a test runway. So the measured figure is multiplied by a factor, to give a distance a line pilot can rely on.

The CASA charts use a factored distance. Each one prints its own factor in the conditions box, 1.15 on figure 3, for instance, and the chart output already includes it. The flight manual figures are typically unfactored, and the advisory circular {AC 91-02|eigh C ninety-one oh two} applies a factor to them. Read the chart or table before you use it, so you know which kind of number you have.

Next, the take-off distance available, TODA. It is the length of runway, called the take-off run available, plus any clearway. A clearway is an area beyond the end of the runway, free of obstacles, over which the aeroplane may complete its climb to 50 ft. It is not for rolling on. It is airspace over flat, clear ground.

[[diagram:ersa-entry-anatomy]]
At a licensed aerodrome, the TODA is published in ERSA, in the runway distances supplement. Picture an ERSA entry as a stack of blocks. At the top, a header with the elevation, position and operator. Under it, the runway table, giving each runway's length and width in metres, its surface and its slope. Then the communications and navigation aids, and the remarks. At an ALA there is no publication, so the pilot measures these things.

The rule is simple. TODR must not exceed TODA. If it does, the aeroplane will not be at 50 ft and the safety speed by the end of the available distance. And whatever is beyond the end of the strip is in the way.

Let's work one. The take-off chart gives a TODR of 640 m for today's conditions. The strip is 700 m long, with no clearway. Is it long enough? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 5]]
The TODA is 700 plus nothing, so 700 m. The margin is 700 minus 640, which is 60 m. TODR is less than TODA, so the take-off is legal, with 60 m to spare.

Now change one thing. If the strip were only 600 m, the TODR of 640 m would exceed the TODA. The weight would have to come down until the chart gave a TODR of 600 m or less.

Now try one yourself. The chart gives a TODR of 580 m, and the strip is 650 m, with no clearway. Is the take-off legal, and with what margin?
[[pause 6]]
The TODA is 650 m. The margin is 650 minus 580, which is 70 m. TODR is less than TODA, so the take-off is legal, with 70 m to spare. But a margin of about 10 percent leaves little room for a soft patch, a late rotation, or a gust that dies at lift-off. It is legal, not generous.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. TODR is measured to 50 ft, not to lift-off. An answer that compares the ground run with the runway length, or says the take-off distance ends when the wheels leave the ground, is wrong. The other trap is counting a clearway as runway. A clearway lengthens the TODA, but you cannot roll on it. So it does nothing for a ground run, or for a rejected take-off.

Now the landing. The landing is the take-off run backwards. The aeroplane crosses the threshold at 50 ft, at the threshold speed, the approach speed the chart is based on. Figure 6 prints 58 KIAS. Figure 13 gives a scale that varies with weight. Then the aeroplane descends to touch down, and is braked to a stop.

The landing distance required, LDR, is the distance from 50 ft over the threshold to a full stop. It comes from the landing chart, for the weight, pressure height, temperature, wind, slope and surface. And it is factored, like the take-off distance.

The landing distance available, LDA, is the length of runway from the threshold that is available for the landing. Usually that is the whole runway. But sometimes the threshold is displaced, moved along the runway to give a clearer approach over an obstacle, or because the first part of the surface is unfit. Then the LDA starts at the displaced threshold, and it is shorter than the runway. You can often still use the full length for take-off. So a runway can have a TODA and an LDA that differ.

The rule mirrors the take-off. LDR must not exceed LDA. Because the aeroplane crosses the threshold at 50 ft, the airborne part of the landing is already inside the LDA.

Let's work one. The landing chart gives an LDR of 520 m. The runway is 700 m, with the threshold at the end. Is it long enough? And what if a displaced threshold cuts the LDA to 500 m? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 5]]
First, the LDA is 700 m. The margin is 700 minus 520, which is 180 m. LDR is less than LDA, so the landing is fine.

Now the displaced threshold reduces the LDA to 500 m. 520 is more than 500, so the LDR exceeds the LDA by 20 m. With the displaced threshold, you may not plan to land on that runway at that weight in those conditions, even though the bitumen is still 700 m long. So the answers: 180 m to spare on the full runway, and 20 m short with the displaced threshold.

The farm strip at the end of this lesson makes the same point the other way. An LDR of 520 m against an LDA of 650 m is legal, with 130 m to spare.

[[diagram:takeoff-landing-distances-ala]]
Now picture that 700 m strip three times, side on: the take-off on top, the landing in the middle, and the landing with a displaced threshold at the bottom. Notice that both required distances include the 50 ft segment. And the displaced threshold shortens the LDA, while the strip stays 700 m long.

[[diagram:takeoff-landing-factors]]
What makes these distances longer or shorter? Picture a table of arrows. Only a headwind shortens both distances. A tailwind lengthens both, markedly. Higher temperature, lower QNH and higher elevation lengthen both, through lower density. An uphill slope lengthens the take-off, and a downhill slope lengthens the landing. Grass, gravel, wet surfaces and more weight lengthen both. And frost on the wing lengthens the take-off, and may prevent it.

Question. A runway's threshold is displaced. Does that change the TODA, the LDA, or both?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: normally only the LDA. The landing must begin at the displaced threshold, so the length available for landing is shorter. The pavement before the threshold can usually still be used for the take-off run. Check the published distances, which give each one separately.

## What an ALA is, and whose decision it is

An aeroplane landing area, an ALA, is any place that is not a certified or registered aerodrome, and is used for taking off and landing. A farm strip. A station strip. A beach or a salt-lake surface used regularly. Nobody certifies it, and nobody maintains it to a standard. The pilot in command is responsible for deciding that it is suitable for the aeroplane, the conditions and the operation.

CASA's guidance for that decision is {AC 91-02|eigh C ninety-one oh two}. It is the advisory circular that replaced {CAAP 92-1|C eigh eigh P ninety-two dash one}, and the syllabus still uses the old name. It applies to aeroplanes with a maximum take-off weight up to 5,700 kg, and it sets out what a suitable place looks like.

It is guidance, not a rule with a pass mark. But if you ignore it and something goes wrong, you will be asked why. The figures that follow are as this lesson has them from the advisory circular. Verify them against the current version before relying on them.

Think of the guidelines as answering four questions in turn. Is there enough room? Is the way in and out clear? Is the ground fit to roll on? And can you identify and use it safely?

## Enough room: length and width

First, length. The strip must provide at least the TODR and the LDR for your aeroplane, in the expected conditions. That is the whole of the previous section, applied to a field. Take the chart distances for the weight, pressure height, temperature, wind, slope and surface, and compare them with the measured strip.

Second, width. The advisory circular recommends minimum runway widths: about 15 m for light single-engine aeroplanes, and more for larger or faster types. Verify that figure. Width is your margin for a swing on take-off, a crosswind gust at touchdown, or a tyre that grabs on wet grass. A 15 m strip leaves a high-wing trainer, with a wingspan of about 11 m, only 2 m of grass beyond each wingtip, if it is on the centreline.

Third, the runway strip. Beyond the edges and ends of the runway itself, the advisory circular calls for a cleared runway strip. That is an area free of obstacles that could strike the aeroplane if it runs off the side or the end. Think of it as the space a mistake needs. A fence post 3 m off the edge of a runway is fine, until the day the aeroplane swings 4 m.

[[diagram:ala-plan-view]]
Picture a farm strip from above. Down the middle runs the runway, about 15 m wide for a light single. Around its edges and ends is the cleared runway strip. At each end, an obstacle-free approach and take-off area fans out and slopes upward at about 5 percent, 1 in 20, with transitional surfaces along the sides. Add a firm, even, short-grass surface, a slope of not more than about 2 percent, a windsock and boundary markers. Notice that the runway, the cleared strip around it, and the obstacle-free surfaces at each end are separate tests.

Question. Why does an ALA need a cleared strip either side of the runway, as well as a runway wide enough for the wheels?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because the wings are much wider than the wheel track, and the aeroplane does not always stay on the centreline. The cleared strip gives room for a swing, a crosswind drift, or a run-off, without the wing or the wheels hitting anything.

## The way in and out: approach and take-off areas

Length tells you the aeroplane can reach 50 ft on the strip. It says nothing about what is beyond the end. So the advisory circular calls for obstacle-free approach and take-off areas at each end. These are imaginary surfaces, sloping up and out from the end of the strip, with nothing poking through them. No trees, poles, power lines or sheds.

For day operations, it gives a gradient of about 5 percent, which is {1 in 20|one in twenty}. Verify that figure too. It also calls for lateral transitional surfaces, clear of trees, poles and fences, along the sides.

A {1 in 20|one in twenty} slope rises 1 unit for every 20 out. So a 50 ft tree must be at least 50 times 20, which is 1,000 ft, about 300 m, beyond the start of the surface to stay below it. A line of tall gums 150 m past the end of a farm strip breaks the surface, however long the strip.

[[diagram:ala-obstacle-surface]]
Picture the view from the side, beyond the end of the strip. The take-off surface rises gently from the strip's end, 1 ft for every 20 ft out. A 50 ft tree has to stand at least 300 m out to stay under it. Now put a line of tall gums 150 m past the end. There the surface is only about 25 ft up, so the gums break it, and the strip fails, however long it is.

Why 5 percent? Because the aeroplane has to climb faster than the surface rises, with some to spare. Take a light trainer climbing at 500 ft per minute, at a groundspeed of 70 kt. It covers 70 nm in 60 minutes, which is 1.17 nm per minute. So it gains about 429 ft for every nautical mile. A nautical mile is about 6,080 ft, so 429 ft per nautical mile is a gradient of about 7 percent. That clears a 5 percent surface, but not by much. And a tailwind or a hot day narrows the gap.

[[diagram:climb-gradient-and-wind]]
Picture three climb paths, height against distance over the ground, all at 500 ft per minute and 70 kt true airspeed. The vertical speed indicator reads 500 ft per minute in all three. In still air, the path gains 429 ft per nautical mile. With a 15 kt headwind, the groundspeed is 55 kt, and the path steepens to 545. With a 15 kt tailwind, the groundspeed is 85 kt, and the path flattens to 353, down towards the obstacle surface.

Let's work that last one properly. The rate of climb is 500 ft per minute, the climb true airspeed is 70 kt, and there is a 15 kt tailwind on the take-off. Does the climb clear a 5 percent take-off surface? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 8]]
Step one, the groundspeed. 70 plus 15 is 85 kt.

Step two, the ground covered each minute. 85 divided by 60 is 1.42 nautical miles.

Step three, the height gained per nautical mile. 500 divided by 1.42 is 353 ft.

Step four, the gradient. 353 divided by 6,080 is 5.8 percent.

So the aeroplane still out-climbs a {1 in 20|one in twenty} surface, but by less than 1 percent, and only if it really makes 500 ft per minute. On a hot afternoon, at a lower rate of climb, the same tailwind take-off may not. Take off into wind, and check the climb rate the flight manual gives for today's density height. The answer, once more: a gradient of 5.8 percent, against a surface of 5.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. A strip long enough for the TODR is not therefore suitable. The approach and take-off areas are a separate test. A strip with trees or power lines inside the obstacle surface fails it, even when the length is generous. Power lines are the classic killer. They are thin, hard to see, and often strung across the end of a paddock.

## Ground fit to roll on: surface and slope

Start with the surface. The advisory circular calls for a surface that is firm, even, and free of holes, ruts, loose stones and long grass. It wants drainage, so the strip is still usable after rain, and grass kept short.

Each item maps to a force you have met before. Long grass and soft ground add rolling resistance, which lengthens the take-off. Wet grass reduces braking, which lengthens the landing. Holes and ruts can collapse a nosewheel. And loose stones damage the propeller.

Check the surface on foot if you possibly can. If you are arriving for the first time, make a low pass and inspect the strip from the air before committing to land. Look for stock, washouts, soft patches, and the windsock.

Now slope. The longitudinal slope, along the strip, should be within the chart limits. The advisory circular suggests not more than about 2 percent for most light aeroplanes. Verify that one too. The transverse slope, across the strip, should be small, so the aeroplane does not drift towards the low side, and rainwater drains off without cutting gullies.

[[diagram:runway-slope-take-off-and-landing]]
Picture two side views of a sloping runway. Taking off uphill, part of the thrust is spent climbing the slope, so the run is longer. A 2 percent upslope can add around 10 percent to the take-off distance. Landing uphill, the slope helps you slow down, so the roll is shorter. Landing downhill, gravity fights the brakes. So on a sloping strip with light wind, you usually land uphill and take off downhill.

The advisory circular also reminds you what the book figures assume. Flight manual distances are for a sealed, level, dry runway, flown by a test pilot. A grass ALA, soft ground and an average pilot all add distance, and the advisory circular gives the factors to apply for each.

If the chart or those factors show that the ALA cannot provide the TODR or LDR with the required margin, the ALA is not suitable at that weight. The fix is usually less weight, meaning fuel or passengers, a cooler time of day, or a different strip.

Question. An ALA has a 2.5 percent longitudinal slope, and is otherwise ideal. What does the guideline suggest, and why does slope matter?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: it exceeds the suggested maximum of about 2 percent, so it should be treated as unsuitable, unless the flight manual and charts allow that slope. Slope adds a component of weight along the strip. Uphill, it slows the take-off. Downhill, it fights the brakes on landing.

## Finding it and using it safely

First, wind indication. The advisory circular calls for a windsock or other reliable wind indicator. That way you know the wind component on the strip itself, rather than at an aerodrome 40 nm away. Plan for the prevailing wind, and know the crosswind your aeroplane can handle.

[[widget:crosswind-component]]
A strip has only one runway direction, so the crosswind matters. Picture the angle between the wind and the strip. The crosswind part grows fast at first. Just thirty degrees off the strip already puts half the wind across it.

Second, markings. The usable area should be marked, with cones, gables, drums or tyres, so you can identify the strip and its boundaries from the air. And you must know what the markings mean. A grass strip in a grass paddock is surprisingly hard to find, and harder to line up on accurately.

[[diagram:runway-markings-and-markers]]
Picture two runways from above. The top one is sealed, with white painted markings: piano keys at the threshold, the runway number, a dashed centreline. The bottom one is unsealed, and that is the one that matters for an ALA. White cones mark the runway edges. Gable markers, further out, mark the boundary of the runway strip. And a white cross closes off a section that is unserviceable.

Third, the other matters. The list ends with the things that make an ALA an ALA, rather than an aerodrome. There are five. Stock and wildlife: kangaroos at dawn and dusk, and sheep that wander onto the strip after the low pass. Power lines, on or near the approach. The owner's permission. The location relative to populous areas, because the minimum height rules still apply on approach and departure. And communications and search and rescue. There is no CTAF or AWIS, so plan how you will tell someone where you are, and when you are due.

## Bringing it together

A friend invites you to land at the family farm near Wongan Hills. On the phone you learn the strip runs north-south. It is 650 m of short, dry grass, and it has a windsock. Satellite imagery shows a fence across the southern end, and a line of trees about 300 m beyond the northern end. A power line runs along the road to the west.

Your chart for the expected weight, pressure height and temperature, corrected for short dry grass and the forecast 10 kt southerly, gives a TODR of 580 m and an LDR of 520 m. Against 650 m, that is 70 m spare for take-off, and 130 m for landing. Both legal.

You ask about width. About 20 m is mown, with a cleared strip each side. You ask about slope. The farmer thinks the north end is a bit higher. You note it. In calm air you would land uphill, to the north. But a 10 kt headwind outweighs a gentle slope.

You check the trees against a {1 in 20|one in twenty} surface. At 300 m out, they must be below about 50 ft. Tall gums would not be, so you plan to depart to the south, over the fence, into the southerly.

On arrival you make a low pass, to check the surface and the stock, and confirm the windsock. Then you land to the south, into wind, crossing the northern trees with height in hand.

Then the decision changes. You arrive after rain, and the grass is wet and long. Long wet grass is the worst surface on the CASA charts. The TODR rises well above 580 m, and the 70 m margin is gone. The answer is the one the guidelines lead you to. Wait for the strip to dry, or reduce weight and rework the chart before you plan a take-off.

## Key points

Let's recap.

The take-off safety speed is at least 1.2 times the stall speed in the take-off configuration, reached by 50 ft.

The TODR runs from brake release to 50 ft at the take-off safety speed. The TODA is the runway plus any clearway. TODR must not exceed TODA.

The LDR runs from 50 ft over the threshold to a stop. The LDA is the runway from the threshold, shortened by a displaced threshold. LDR must not exceed LDA. And know whether your distances are factored.

The pilot in command decides whether an ALA is suitable, using {AC 91-02|eigh C ninety-one oh two}, which used to be {CAAP 92-1|C eigh eigh P ninety-two dash one}. Check the length for the TODR and the LDR. A width of about 15 m, plus a cleared strip. Obstacle-free approach and take-off areas at about {1 in 20|one in twenty}. A firm, short-grass surface. A longitudinal slope of not more than about 2 percent. A windsock and markings. And the stock, power lines, permission, and search and rescue. Verify those figures against the current advisory circular.

A final exam tip. TODR and LDR are measured from or to 50 ft, and must not exceed TODA and LDA. For an ALA question, check the length against the chart distances, then the width, slope, surface, approach gradient, windsock and markings, in that order. And say no if any one of them fails.

That's the end of POPA 2.1.
