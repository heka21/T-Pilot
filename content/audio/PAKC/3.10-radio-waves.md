This is PAKC 3.10, Radio waves. By the end you will be able to work out a wavelength and a VHF range in your head, match each radio band to its job and to the path its waves take, and say why HF works at night on one frequency and not on another. There are worked examples and a few questions along the way, so pause whenever you want a moment to think.

## What the exam expects (PAKC 3.10)

The exam wants two things from you. First, how radio waves behave, and how they are sent and received. That covers five parts: the MF, HF, VHF and UHF frequency bands. What a radio wave is, and how far a transmission reaches. The ground-wave and sky-wave paths. What affects propagation and reception: terrain, the ionosphere, sunspots, electrical interference, thunderstorms, and the loss of signal power. And how antennas are built and used. Second, what limits VHF and HF communication, and what affects the quality and range of the signal.

## Why this matters

At 2,500 ft over the Swan Coastal Plain, you can talk to Perth all day. Fly east past the scarp and descend into a valley near Toodyay, and the same radio, on the same frequency, can fall silent, although nothing in the aeroplane has changed. Climb a few hundred feet, and the controller comes back.

That is VHF behaving exactly as physics says it should. Once you understand why, you stop thinking of the radio as unreliable, and start flying to keep the signal.

Out over the Nullarbor or the Kimberley, beyond any VHF station, aircraft use HF. It reaches thousands of kilometres, but it hisses, fades, and depends on the time of day.

The exam asks you to calculate a wavelength and a VHF range, to match each band to its use and its propagation path, and to say why HF works at night on one frequency and not another. This lesson builds all of that from what a radio wave is.

## What a radio wave is

Throw a stone into a pond, and ripples spread out. The water does not travel. The disturbance does. A radio wave is a similar travelling disturbance, but in electric and magnetic fields rather than in water. It is an electromagnetic wave, the same kind of wave as light, only with a much longer wavelength. It needs no medium, so it crosses empty space. And it travels at the speed of light, about 300,000,000 metres per second.

Two numbers describe any wave. The first is the frequency: how many complete waves pass a point each second. It is measured in hertz, and one hertz is one cycle per second. Radio frequencies are large, so they are given in kilohertz, which is thousands, and megahertz, which is millions. So {126.7 MHz|one hundred and twenty-six point seven megahertz} is 126,700 kHz. The second number is the wavelength: the distance from one wave crest to the next, in metres.

Because every radio wave travels at the same speed, frequency and wavelength are locked together. If more waves pass each second, each one must be shorter. Speed equals frequency times wavelength. So the wavelength equals the speed of light, 300,000,000 metres per second, divided by the frequency in hertz.

Now measure the frequency in megahertz instead, and the millions cancel. That leaves the working rule. The wavelength in metres equals 300, divided by the frequency in megahertz.

Let's work one: the wavelength of a VHF COM signal, and the length of its antenna. The COM frequency is 118 MHz. Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
The wavelength is 300 divided by 118, which is 2.54 m. A quarter-wave antenna is 2.54 divided by 4, which is 0.64 m, about 64 cm. A VHF COM antenna is a quarter-wave whip or blade, so a little over half a metre is all it needs. That is why the COM antennas on a light aeroplane are short. So: 2.54 m for the wave, and about 64 cm for the antenna.

Now try one yourself. What is the wavelength of an NDB signal at 300 kHz?
[[pause 6]]
Convert to megahertz first: 300 kHz is 0.3 MHz. Then 300 divided by 0.3 is 1,000 m. A wave a kilometre long cannot be matched by a short whip. That is why low-frequency transmitters need large antennas, and the aircraft receiver uses loop and sense antennas instead. So the answer is 1,000 m.

One more check: at 125 MHz, the wavelength is 300 divided by 125, which is 2.4 m. And a quarter-wave antenna is about 0.6 m.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. The rule needs the frequency in megahertz. Put 300 kHz in as 300, and you get a wavelength of 1 m, instead of 1,000 m. Convert kilohertz to megahertz, by dividing by 1,000, before you divide into 300.

## The frequency bands (3.10.1 a)

The radio spectrum is divided into bands, each ten times higher in frequency than the one below. Aviation uses four of them. Each behaves differently enough that it has its own job.

[[diagram:frequency-band-ladder]]
Picture a ladder with four rungs, the highest frequency at the top. On the bottom rung is MF, medium frequency: 300 kHz to 3 MHz, with wavelengths from 1 km down to 100 m. It carries the NDBs, and travels as a ground wave. Together with the LF band just below it, the NDBs use roughly 190 to 1,750 kHz.

The next rung up is HF, high frequency: 3 to 30 MHz, wavelengths 100 m down to 10 m. It carries long-range voice, in oceanic and remote areas, and travels as a sky wave. Aviation HF uses roughly 2.8 to 22 MHz.

Then VHF, very high frequency: 30 to 300 MHz, wavelengths 10 m down to 1 m. VHF COM sits at 118 to 137 MHz, and the VOR and ILS at 108 to 118 MHz. It travels by line of sight, which is called the space wave.

And at the top, UHF, ultra high frequency: 300 MHz to 3 gigahertz, wavelengths 1 m down to 10 cm. It carries the DME, the transponder at 1,030 and 1,090 MHz, GNSS, and the ILS glideslope. It also travels by line of sight. Each rung up is ten times the frequency, and a tenth of the wavelength.

The pattern in those paths is the key to the whole lesson. As frequency rises, waves behave more like light. Low-frequency waves bend around the curve of the earth, and around obstacles, the way long ripples spread around a post in a pond. As the frequency rises, the ground wave dies out faster. The ionosphere stops bending the waves back. And they travel in straight lines, stopped by whatever is in the way, like a torch beam.

So lower frequencies reach further around the earth, but they need large antennas and more power. Higher frequencies need only small antennas, but reach only as far as they can see.

That explains each band's job. MF, and the LF just below it, carries the NDBs, whose signals follow the ground beyond the horizon. HF carries long-range voice, by bouncing off the ionosphere. VHF carries everyday voice and the VOR: clear, but short-ranged. And UHF carries the transponder, DME and GNSS, which need precise straight-line signals and tiny antennas.

Question. Which band does the COM radio you use in the circuit work in, and how does its signal travel?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: VHF, 118 to 137 MHz. Its signal travels in straight lines, line of sight, as the space wave. So its range depends on the aircraft's height, and on the terrain between you and the station.

## Three paths for a radio wave (3.10.1 c)

A wave leaving an antenna can reach a distant receiver by three routes. Which one dominates depends on the frequency.

[[diagram:radio-propagation-paths]]
Picture a transmitter standing on the curved surface of the earth, with the ionosphere as a band high above. Three waves leave it. The first, the ground wave, hugs the surface and fades with distance. The second, the HF sky wave, climbs up to the ionosphere, is bent back down, and reaches the ground far away, hop after hop. The third, the VHF and UHF space wave, goes straight. It reaches an aircraft in line of sight. And a wave aimed upwards passes right through the ionosphere and into space.

Take the ground wave first. The ground wave, or surface wave, follows the curvature of the earth. Part of the wave travels along the surface, and the ground drags on it slightly. That tilts the wave front forward, so it bends around the curve. Every bit of ground it travels over absorbs some energy, so it fades with distance.

The ground wave is strong at LF and MF, and fades quickly at higher frequencies. Its range depends on power and frequency. And it is greater over the sea than over land, because sea water conducts electricity far better than dry ground, and absorbs less of the wave. This is how NDBs are received. An NDB's signal reaches you around the curve of the earth, well below the line of sight.

Next, the sky wave. Between roughly 60 and 400 km up lies the ionosphere. It is layers of gas that the sun's radiation has ionised, stripping electrons from the atoms. A radio wave entering an ionised layer is refracted, which means bent. If it is bent enough, it comes back down to earth, far from where it started. That returning wave is the sky wave. Each bounce off the ionosphere and back is a hop, and HF hops can be thousands of kilometres long. That is what gives HF its long range.

The sky wave creates a strange gap. Close to the transmitter you hear the ground wave, which soon fades. The first sky wave comes back down a long way out. In between lies the skip zone, also called dead space, where nothing is heard. It is too far for the ground wave, and too close for the sky wave.

[[diagram:hf-skip-zone]]
Picture an HF transmitter on the left, with three aircraft spread out to its right. The nearest, 30 km away, hears the ground wave, but that wave fades out soon after. Waves sent up too steeply pass straight through the ionosphere. Shallower ones are bent back, and return to earth far away, where the furthest aircraft, 1,500 km out, hears the sky wave. And the middle aircraft, 150 km away, hears nothing. It is in the skip zone: too far for the ground wave, and too close for the sky wave.

And last, the space wave. At VHF and UHF, the ionosphere is not dense enough to bend the waves back, so they pass straight through into space. What remains is the space wave, or direct wave, travelling in a straight line from antenna to antenna. It reaches only as far as the radio horizon. That is line of sight. If the receiver is below the horizon, or behind a hill, the wave goes over its head.

Question. An aircraft 150 km from an HF station hears nothing. Yet one 30 km away, and one 1,500 km away, both hear it clearly. Why?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the aircraft at 150 km is in the skip zone. It is beyond the range of the fading ground wave, but short of where the first sky wave returns. The near aircraft gets the ground wave, and the far one gets the sky wave.

## How far does VHF reach? (3.10.1 b)

Because VHF travels in straight lines, its range is set by geometry. How far can an antenna at your height see, before the earth curves away? The higher you are, the further the horizon. Radio waves bend slightly more than light does in the lower atmosphere, so the radio horizon is a little beyond the visual one.

The rule of thumb is this. The line-of-sight range in nautical miles is about 1.23 times the square root of the height in feet.

For two elevated stations, each has its own horizon, and the ranges add. So the range is 1.23 times the sum of the two square roots: the square root of one height, plus the square root of the other. Use the height above the terrain the ground station sits on.

The square root is the important part. Range grows with height, but more slowly than height does. Double the range needs four times the height. That is why the first few thousand feet of climb make a large difference, and the next few thousand a smaller one.

[[diagram:vhf-line-of-sight-range]]
Picture a curve of range against height for a station at sea level. It rises steeply at first, then flattens. 1,500 ft gives about 48 nm. 5,000 ft gives about 87 nm. And four times the height, from 1,500 ft to 6,000 ft, only doubles the range, from 48 to about 95 nm. Terrain, transmitter power and antenna shielding often make the real range less.

Let's work one: VHF range from 5,000 ft. You are calling a ground station at sea level. Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
First, the square root of 5,000 is 70.7. Then 1.23 times 70.7 is 87 nm. More exactly, 86.97. So at 5,000 ft over flat country, you can expect to work a sea-level station out to about 87 nm, if the transmitter power and the terrain allow.

Now a station on a hill. The aircraft is at 4,000 ft, and the ground station is on a 1,000 ft hill.
[[pause 6]]
The aircraft's term is the square root of 4,000, which is 63.2. The station's term is the square root of 1,000, which is 31.6. Add them: 94.8. Times 1.23, that is 117 nm, or more exactly {116.6|one hundred and sixteen point six}. Putting the station on a hill added about 39 nm, compared with a sea-level site. That is why VHF repeaters, and air traffic control transmitters, sit on hilltops and towers. So the answer is about 117 nm.

Now try one yourself. You are in the circuit area at 1,500 ft, calling a station at sea level. What is the theoretical range?
[[pause 6]]
The square root of 1,500 is 38.7. So the range is about 1.23 times 38.7, which is 48 nm, or 47.6 more exactly. At circuit and low-level training heights, VHF reaches only a few tens of miles. And less behind hills.

The actual range is often less than the formula gives, for three reasons. First, terrain blocks the line of sight. Hills between you and a Perth station, when you fly east of the scarp, cast a radio shadow you can fly into. Second, transmitter power is limited, so a weak signal may fade into noise before the geometric horizon. And third, the antenna may be shielded by the airframe in a turn, with the wing or the fuselage between the antenna and the station.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Range does not rise in proportion to height. Doubling your height multiplies the range by only the square root of 2, about 1.4. To double the range, you need four times the height.

Question. A pilot at 1,000 ft, behind a ridge near Toodyay, cannot raise Perth, although the formula says the station is well within range. Why, and what is the remedy?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the formula assumes a clear line of sight. The ridge is blocking it, and the aircraft is in its radio shadow. Climbing, if it is safe and permitted, or moving clear of the ridge, restores the line of sight.

## What weakens or spoils the signal (3.10.1 d)

Six factors affect propagation and reception. Each acts on a particular band more than the others, and the pattern follows from the paths you have just heard.

The first is terrain. Hills and mountains block VHF and UHF, casting a shadow behind them. Terrain also absorbs the ground wave, more over land than over sea. And mountains can reflect low-frequency signals. That bends an NDB signal, and gives the ADF a false bearing.

The second is the ionosphere. It is made by the sun, so it changes with the sun. By day, ionisation is strong, and the layers can bend back higher frequencies. By night, ionisation fades, and only lower frequencies come back, while higher ones go straight through into space. Its height and density also vary with the season. So the practical rule for HF is: by day, use higher HF frequencies. At night, use lower ones.

The ionosphere also causes the NDB's night effect. After dark, sky waves from the NDB come back down, and interfere with the ground wave. That makes the ADF needle wander.

The third is sunspot activity. The sun's activity rises and falls over an eleven-year cycle. Solar flares and high sunspot counts disturb the ionosphere. That causes HF fade-outs, noise, and unpredictable range. VHF, which ignores the ionosphere, is largely unaffected.

The fourth is electrical interference. Anything electrical can radiate noise. Alternator whine. Ignition noise. Faulty strobes. And personal electronic devices. It affects VHF, and especially LF and MF, where the receiver is listening for weak, low-frequency signals in a band where electrical noise is strong. A whine in your headset that rises and falls with engine RPM is usually the alternator.

The fifth is thunderstorms. Lightning is a huge, brief electrical discharge, and it radiates across a wide range of frequencies. It causes static crackle, worst on LF, MF and HF. And an ADF may point to the storm, because the lightning is a stronger signal than the NDB.

And the sixth is power attenuation. Signal strength falls with distance. Spreading out from the antenna, the same power covers a growing area. So the strength falls with the square of the distance. That is the inverse square law: twice as far, a quarter of the strength. The signal is also absorbed by the atmosphere, terrain, water, and the airframe. And a low battery reduces transmit power. So a pilot on battery alone, after an alternator failure, may be heard over a shorter range than usual.

So, to group them. Terrain is the enemy of VHF and UHF. The ionosphere and sunspots act on HF, and on the NDB at night. Electrical interference and lightning are worst at the low frequencies. And attenuation weakens every band with distance.

Question. Why does a thunderstorm 40 nm away crackle in the HF receiver, but barely affect your VHF COM?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: lightning radiates strongly at low and high frequencies, and LF, MF and HF receivers pick it up as static. VHF is much less affected. The same effect can swing an ADF needle towards the storm.

## Antennas (3.10.1 e)

An antenna is the part of the system that turns electrical current into radio waves, and back again. You'll find more on that in the lesson on radio system components. An antenna works best when its length is a set fraction of the wavelength, half-wave or quarter-wave. Then the current in it resonates with the wave, like a guitar string whose length matches the note.

Feed that into the wavelength rule, and the shapes of aircraft antennas follow. VHF wavelengths are a couple of metres, so VHF antennas are short whips or blades. HF wavelengths are tens of metres, so HF antennas are long wires, or use tuned couplers that make a shorter wire behave electrically like the right length. LF and MF wavelengths are hundreds of metres, far too long for any aircraft. So the NDB receiver uses loop and sense antennas, which sense the direction and presence of the signal, rather than resonating with it.

Antennas are also polarised: the wave's electric field lines up with the antenna. VHF COM antennas are vertical, and VOR antennas horizontal. A receiving antenna works best when it matches the transmitting one.

In use, antennas must be clean, secure and undamaged. And bonding to the airframe matters, because a poor electrical connection weakens the signal and adds noise. Remember also that shielding by the fuselage or the wing can blank a signal in a turn. In a steep turn, the antenna on the belly may face away from the station.

Question. Why is a VHF COM antenna a short blade, while an aircraft HF antenna is a long wire, or needs a coupler?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: an antenna works best at a set fraction of the wavelength, a quarter or a half. VHF wavelengths are about 2.5 m, so a quarter is about 60 cm. HF wavelengths are tens of metres, so the antenna must be long, or electrically lengthened with a coupler.

## Limits of VHF and HF (3.10.2)

Put the two communication bands side by side, and their strengths and weaknesses are mirror images.

VHF travels by line of sight. Its range is limited by height and terrain, so low down, or behind hills, you are out of range. Within range, though, it is clear, low-noise, and little affected by weather, because it ignores the ionosphere and suffers less from static. That makes it the band for normal air traffic control, the CTAF, and the area frequencies.

HF travels by sky wave, so its range is thousands of kilometres. But it is noisy and fading. It is affected by the time of day, the season, and solar activity, because it depends on the ionosphere. It has a skip zone. And choosing the wrong frequency for the time of day gives no contact at all. HF is used in remote and oceanic areas, beyond VHF coverage.

The HF and VHF communications services are described in AIP GEN 3.4. Verify the current coverage and frequencies there.

## Bringing it together

A ferry flight from Perth towards Kalgoorlie, in an aeroplane with VHF and HF.

At 1,500 ft leaving Perth, the formula gives you about 48 nm to a sea-level station. Climbing to the cruise widens that, which is why you climb before you lose contact.

East of the scarp, you descend into a valley, and the area frequency goes quiet. That is terrain shadow, fixed by climbing.

Beyond the reach of the VHF stations, you change to HF. In the afternoon, a daytime frequency works. After dark it fades away, and you change to a lower one, because the weakened ionosphere now bends back only lower frequencies.

A thunderstorm to the south fills the HF with crackle, and swings the ADF needle towards it, while the VHF stays clean.

Every behaviour on that flight came from one fact. Frequency decides the path a wave takes.

## Key points

Let's recap.

Radio waves are electromagnetic waves, travelling at 300,000,000 metres per second. The wavelength in metres is 300 divided by the frequency in megahertz.

The bands. MF, 300 kHz to 3 MHz: NDBs, by ground wave. HF, 3 to 30 MHz: long-range voice, by sky wave. VHF, 30 to 300 MHz: COM at 118 to 137 MHz, and the VOR and ILS, by line of sight. UHF, 300 MHz to 3 gigahertz: DME, transponder, GNSS and glideslope. The higher the frequency, the more it behaves like light.

The paths. The ground wave follows the earth, further over sea. The sky wave is refracted by the ionosphere, 60 to 400 km up, and leaves a skip zone. The space wave is line of sight.

VHF range is about 1.23 times the square root of the height in feet. For two stations, the ranges add.

The six factors. Terrain blocks VHF. The ionosphere changes between day and night. Sunspots disrupt HF. Electrical noise hits LF and MF. Lightning causes static on LF, MF and HF, and swings the ADF. And signals fall with the square of distance.

Antennas are a half or a quarter of a wavelength, polarised, and must be clean, secure and bonded.

And the limits. VHF is clear and low-noise, but line of sight, limited by height and terrain. HF reaches thousands of kilometres, but it is noisy and fading, with a skip zone. Use higher HF frequencies by day, and lower ones at night.

A final exam tip. VHF range depends mainly on height: double the range needs four times the height. HF needs a frequency chosen for the time of day: high by day, low at night. Have the two formulas by heart. Wavelength equals 300 divided by megahertz. And range is about 1.23 times the square root of the height in feet. Convert kilohertz to megahertz before dividing. And expect a matching question: the NDB with the ground wave, HF with the sky wave, and VHF and UHF with line of sight.
