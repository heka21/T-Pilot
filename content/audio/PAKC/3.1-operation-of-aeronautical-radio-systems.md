This is PAKC 3.1, Operation of aeronautical radio systems. By the end you will be able to say letters, numbers and times the way the radio expects them, use your call-sign correctly, work the radio outside controlled airspace, and explain why a clipped transmission is dangerous. There are a few questions along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (PAKC 3.1)

The exam wants seven things from you, all about using the radio correctly. First, the phonetic alphabet, and transmitting numbers the standard way. Second, using aircraft call-signs correctly. Third, the standard radio procedures outside controlled airspace. Fourth, saying a time correctly. Fifth, listening effectively. Sixth, establishing and keeping two-way communication. And seventh, what a clipped transmission is, and the harm it does.

## Why this matters

On a Saturday morning, the Jandakot training area, Murrayfield, Serpentine and Northam all have aircraft talking, and several CTAFs share a frequency. A CTAF is a common traffic advisory frequency. On a busy frequency, a letter misheard is a wrong runway. A dropped call-sign is an instruction followed by the wrong aeroplane. And a missed hold short is a runway incursion.

Standard phraseology lets every listener pick out the meaning through engine noise, static and accents. The exam asks how numbers, call-signs and times are spoken, what the standard words mean, the readability scale, and why a clipped transmission is dangerous.

## Letters and numbers

Start with the phonetic alphabet. Over a noisy radio, letters that sound alike blur together. B, C, D, E, G, P, T and V all end in the same ee sound. The cure is to replace each letter with a word chosen so that no two can be confused, even by a listener whose first language is not English. That is the phonetic alphabet, and it is used worldwide.

Here it is, letter by letter. {A|eigh}, Alfa. B, Bravo. C, Charlie. D, Delta. E, Echo. F, Foxtrot. G, Golf. H, Hotel. I, India. J, Juliett. K, Kilo. L, Lima. M, Mike. N, November. O, Oscar. P, Papa. Q, Quebec. R, Romeo. S, Sierra. T, Tango. U, Uniform. V, Victor. W, Whiskey. X, X-ray. Y, Yankee. And Z, Zulu.

You use it for registrations, taxiway names, ATIS codes, as in information Golf, and anything you need to spell. Alfa is spelled with an f, and Juliett with two t's. Those spellings are deliberate, so that speakers of other languages pronounce them correctly.

Numbers have the same problem. Five and nine sound alike through static, and three is easily lost. So radiotelephony uses a set pronunciation for each digit, given in the AIP. The three that differ most from everyday speech are tree, for 3, fife, for 5, and niner, for 9. The rest are spoken clearly, with the stress on the first syllable: wun, too, fow-er, six, sev-en, ait, and zero. The decimal point is day-see-mal, usually written decimal.

Most numbers are then sent digit by digit. That covers headings, QNH, frequencies, transponder codes, and wind direction and speed. The exception is altitudes, heights and cloud bases in whole thousands and hundreds. They use the words {tou-sand|tow-sand} and hun-dred. The words themselves flag that this is a height.

Here is how that sounds. An altitude of 2,500 ft is two thousand fife hundred. {FL125|Flight level one two five} is flight level one two fife. A heading of 090 is heading zero niner zero. A QNH of {1013|one zero one three} is QNH one zero one tree. A frequency of {126.7 MHz|one two six decimal seven megahertz} is one two six decimal seven. A squawk of {3412|three four one two} is squawk tree four one two. And a wind of {270|two seven zero} degrees at 15 knots is wind two seven zero degrees one fife knots.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Altitudes in whole thousands and hundreds use thousand and hundred. Headings, frequencies, QNH and codes are digit by digit. A distractor will offer two five zero zero for an altitude. Or thirty-five hundred, which is everyday speech, not phraseology.

Question. How would you say a QNH of {1019|one zero one nine}, and a heading of {350|three five zero}?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: QNH one zero one niner, and heading tree fife zero. Both are digit by digit, using the radiotelephony pronunciations for 3, 5 and 9.

## Call-signs

Your call-sign is how everyone on frequency knows which aeroplane is talking, and which one an instruction is for. An Australian private aeroplane uses its registration without the nationality prefix, VH. So {VH-ABC|V H eigh B C} is Alpha Bravo Charlie.

On first contact with ATC, and in broadcasts at a non-controlled aerodrome, put the aircraft type in front: Cessna Alpha Bravo Charlie. The type tells others what to look for, and how fast it is coming.

Once you are in two-way contact, call-signs may be abbreviated. But never abbreviate your call-sign until the ground station has abbreviated it first. The controller knows what else is on frequency, and you do not. And if another aircraft with a similar call-sign is on frequency, say Alpha Bravo Charlie and Alpha Bravo Delta, always use the full call-sign. Airline flights use their company designator and flight number instead of a registration: {Qantas 512|Qantas five one two}.

Question. You are {VH-PLK|V H P L K}, and {VH-PLM|V H P L M} is in the same circuit. Why does it matter that both of you use full call-signs?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the two call-signs differ only in the last letter. So a shortened or partly heard call-sign could fit either aeroplane. Full call-signs make sure each instruction or broadcast is linked to the right aircraft, and that each pilot acts only on what was meant for them.

## Radio procedures outside controlled airspace

Outside controlled airspace, in Class G, nobody gives you a clearance or separates you from other traffic. You and the other pilots separate yourselves, by looking out, and by telling each other where you are and what you intend. The radio procedures are built around that.

[[diagram:controlled-vs-non-controlled-at-a-glance]]
Picture two columns compared side by side. On the left, controlled airspace, Class C and D and the control zones: a clearance is required, and ATC separates traffic. On the right, non-controlled Class G: no clearance, pilots separate themselves by lookout and radio, flight information is available on request, and CTAF procedures apply at aerodromes. In Class G, lookout and radio broadcasts do the job that clearances do in controlled airspace.

There are three parts to the procedure. The first is to maintain a listening watch on the right frequency. En route, that is the area frequency, Centre, shown on the VNC, the {ERC-L|E R C low} or in ERSA. Near a non-controlled aerodrome, it is that aerodrome's CTAF, from ERSA. With two COM radios, you can monitor both as you depart.

The second is to make broadcasts where they are needed to avoid a collision. A broadcast is a transmission to all aircraft, addressed to nobody in particular, and needing no reply. The recommended points are before taxiing, before entering the runway, inbound by 10 nm, on joining the circuit, and others as needed. The lesson on reporting position in the circuit teaches each call, and why it sits where it does.

And the third is to make reports to ATS when you want a service: a SARTIME, traffic information, or a weather update from Centre. A report is addressed to a particular station, and expects an answer.

Every CTAF broadcast follows one format. Location traffic. Type. Call-sign. Position. Level. Intentions. And the location again. The aerodrome name is said at the start and the end, because several CTAFs often share one frequency. A listener who missed the start still learns which aerodrome the call was about.

So it sounds like this. Northam traffic, Cessna {172|one seventy-two} Alpha Bravo Charlie, 10 miles south-west, {2,500|two thousand fife hundred}, inbound, estimate overhead at {15|one fife}, Northam.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. In Class G there is no clearance, and nobody to request one from at a non-controlled aerodrome. You broadcast your position and intentions to other traffic. You report to ATS only when you want a service.

## Saying the time

Aviation runs on Coordinated Universal Time, UTC, on the 24-hour clock. That way a flight plan, a NOTAM and a forecast mean the same instant to everyone, whatever their local time zone. Perth local time, Western Standard Time, is UTC plus 8, with no daylight saving. So {0915|zero nine one five} UTC is {1715|seventeen fifteen} WST.

[[diagram:utc-and-wst-strip]]
Picture two time strips, one above the other, lined up so that the same instant sits in the same column. The WST strip runs 8 hours ahead of the UTC strip. So the WST date changes at {1600|sixteen hundred} UTC. For example, {1530|fifteen thirty} WST is {0730|zero seven thirty} UTC the same day. And {0545|zero five forty-five} WST on Tuesday 7 October is {2145|twenty-one forty-five} UTC on Monday 6 October.

On the radio, a time is normally given as the minutes past the hour only: estimate overhead at one fife. The hour is usually obvious from context. Where there is any chance of confusion, near the change of the hour or for a time well ahead, give the hour as well: one two one fife. A time check is given to the nearest minute: time one two four fife.

Let's work one. You plan to be overhead Northam at {1048|ten forty-eight} WST. What do you say on the CTAF? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 4]]
Subtract 8 hours, and it is {0248|zero two four eight} UTC. On the CTAF you would say, estimate overhead at four eight. If you were giving it as a full time, it would be zero two four eight, never ten forty-eight.

Question. You estimate arriving at {0710|zero seven ten} WST. What is that in UTC, and how would you normally say it?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: {0710|zero seven ten} WST minus 8 hours is {2310|twenty-three ten} UTC, on the previous day. Normally you would say only the minutes: one zero. If the hour is needed, two tree one zero.

## Listening effectively

Half of good radio work is listening. There are five habits.

The first: listen before you transmit. Before you press the PTT, the press-to-talk switch, listen for a few seconds, to make sure you will not cut across someone mid-exchange. A controller who has just asked a question is waiting for the answer, not for your call. After changing frequency, listen before you speak, for the same reason.

The second: set the radio up so you can hear. Volume and squelch are set before departure, as the lesson on the pre-flight radio test explains. A squelch set too tight makes a busy frequency sound quiet.

The third: build a picture of the traffic. Place each aircraft you hear relative to you. For example: the Warrior inbound from the north will join crosswind about when I turn downwind.

The fourth: process every call, not just your own. Listen for your call-sign, of course. But a conflicting aircraft will not use your name.

And the fifth: write things down, and anticipate. Write down clearances, the ATIS, frequencies and the QNH. Knowing what ATC is likely to say next makes it easier to hear correctly what they do say. That might be a frequency change after departure, or a sequence when inbound. But never hear what you expected, instead of what was said.

## Establishing and keeping communication

Start with the first call. Making contact with a station follows a set pattern. The station you are calling. Your call-sign. Then your position and message, or the word request if the message is long. For example: Melbourne Centre, Cessna {172|one seventy-two} Alpha Bravo Charlie, request traffic. Then wait for the reply before giving the rest of the message. A short first call makes sure the controller is ready to listen before you give the details.

If there is no reply, wait at least about 10 seconds before calling again. The controller may be dealing with another aircraft, or another frequency. Then check the basics. Are you on the right frequency? Is the volume up? Is the MIC selector on the radio you think? And are you within range? Fault-finding is taught in its own lesson.

Next, readback. Once you are in contact, some messages must be read back. You repeat them, so the controller can hear that you received them correctly. The AIP requires readback of several items. ATC route clearances. Any clearance or instruction to enter, land on, take off from, backtrack on, cross, or hold short of a runway. The runway in use. Altimeter settings. SSR codes, which are transponder codes. Level instructions. And heading and speed instructions. Check the AIP for the complete current list.

End a readback with your call-sign. That tells the controller which aircraft has acknowledged. A readback is a two-way check. It catches both your mishearing, and the controller's slip of the tongue.

Then the standard words. A small set of words carries a precise meaning, so that a short reply cannot be misunderstood. There are seven.

Roger means, I have received all of your last transmission. Wilco means, I understand your message and will comply. Affirm means yes. Negative means no, or permission not granted. Say again means, repeat all, or the following part, of your last transmission. Standby means, wait and I will call you. And correction means, I made an error, and the correct version is this.

Roger is not yes, and it is not I will comply. It only acknowledges receipt. Do not say Roger Wilco, because Wilco already includes Roger. And never answer a readback item with Roger alone.

Last, the readability scale. When you ask for a radio check, the reply grades how well you were heard, from 1 to 5. 1 is unreadable. 2 is readable now and then. 3 is readable but with difficulty. 4 is readable. And 5 is perfectly readable.

Question. The tower tells you to hold short of {runway 24|runway two four}. Is Roger an acceptable reply?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: no. A hold short instruction must be read back in full, ending with your call-sign: hold short {runway 24|runway two four}, Alpha Bravo Charlie. Roger only says you received something, not that you received it correctly.

## Clipped transmissions

A transmission is clipped when its first or last words are cut off. It happens when you start speaking before the press-to-talk switch is fully pressed and the transmitter has come up to power. Or when you release the switch before you have finished the last word.

The words that get clipped are exactly the ones that matter most. The first words of a call are usually the call-sign. The last are often the key word: a runway number, not, hold short, or your call-sign at the end of a readback.

There are three consequences. First, the wrong aircraft acts on an instruction, or nobody does. With the call-sign gone, two pilots may each assume it was theirs, or neither will. Second, the meaning reverses. A lost not, or a lost hold short, can turn a message into its opposite. And third, the frequency clogs with every say again.

Prevention is a habit. Press the switch, pause about half a second, speak, finish, then release. A stuck microphone is the opposite failure. The switch stays keyed, and blocks the frequency for everyone. That is in the lesson on fault finding.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. If a clearance arrives with its call-sign clipped, do not assume it was yours, even if you were expecting it. Ask: station calling Alpha Bravo Charlie, say again. Acting on someone else's clearance is how two aeroplanes end up on the same runway.

## Bringing it together

You are departing Northam. Before taxi, you listen on the CTAF, and place a Cessna joining crosswind for {runway 23|runway two three}. Then you broadcast your taxi call.

Climbing out, you call: Melbourne Centre, Cessna {172|one seventy-two} Alpha Bravo Charlie, request SARTIME. You wait for the answer. You give your time as zero tree tree zero. And you read back the QNH: QNH one zero one niner, Alpha Bravo Charlie.

A moment later you hear: harlie, traffic, Piper, 2 o'clock, 5 miles, same level. The call-sign is clipped. And a Kilo Lima Charlie is also on frequency. So you reply: station calling Alpha Bravo Charlie, say again.

## Key points

Let's recap.

Letters use the phonetic alphabet. Numbers are digit by digit, with tree, fife and niner. The exception is altitudes in whole thousands and hundreds, which use {tou-sand|tow-sand} and hun-dred.

Call-signs: the registration without VH, with the type in front on first contact. Abbreviate only after the ground station does.

In Class G, keep a listening watch on the area frequency or the CTAF. Broadcast to avoid collision. Report to ATS for a service.

Time is UTC, on the 24-hour clock. Normally the minutes only, with the hour added if there is any doubt. WST is UTC plus 8.

Listen before you transmit, build a traffic picture, and process every call.

The first call is the station, your call-sign, and request. Wait about 10 seconds before calling again. Read back the AIP items, ending with your call-sign. Roger is received. Wilco is will comply. Readability runs from 1 to 5.

And clipping loses the call-sign or a key word. Press, pause, speak, release.

A final exam tip. A clipped call-sign is the classic trap. If a clearance arrives without a clear call-sign, do not assume it was yours. Ask: station calling Alpha Bravo Charlie, say again. Have the readability scale by heart: 3 is readable with difficulty, and 5 is perfectly readable. Know that Wilco already includes Roger. And know that altitudes in whole thousands and hundreds use thousand and hundred, while everything else is digit by digit.
