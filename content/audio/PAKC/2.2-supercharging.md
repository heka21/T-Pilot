This is PAKC 2.2, Supercharging. By the end you will be able to say why an engine is supercharged, how a gear-driven supercharger and an exhaust-driven turbocharger differ, how the wastegate and its back-ups keep the boost in check, and what you do about an overboost. There are a few questions along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (PAKC 2.2)

The exam wants four things from you. First, why an engine is supercharged: what squeezing the intake air is meant to achieve. Second, the usual ways of supercharging an aero engine: the gear-driven supercharger, and the exhaust-driven turbocharger. Third, the devices that stop the intake being boosted beyond the engine's limits. And fourth, what you do when supercharging takes the engine past one of its limits.

## Why this matters

Sooner or later a PPL takes you further than a trainer comfortably goes. A turbocharged Cessna {206|two oh six} or {210|two ten}, for a charter-style trip to a Pilbara mine strip. Or a turbocharged Arrow, for a fast run from Jandakot to Esperance at 11,500 ft, above the afternoon cumulus.

These aeroplanes look like the ones you learnt on, but the engine behaves differently. It holds its power where a normally aspirated engine would be gasping. And in exchange, it can be pushed past its limits by a careless hand on the throttle on a cold morning.

The exam asks why supercharging is done, how the two methods differ, what device keeps the boost in check, and what you do about an overboost. In the previous lesson, on piston engines, you met the effect of a turbocharger on performance with altitude. This lesson opens up the machinery.

## Why squeeze the air

Start with the problem supercharging solves. Go back to the idea that runs through the lesson on piston engines. An engine is an air pump. The power it makes depends on the mass of air it can push through its cylinders each minute. At a given RPM, each cylinder swallows a fixed volume per stroke. So the mass depends on the density of the air arriving at the intake valve.

A normally aspirated engine relies only on the atmosphere to push air into its cylinders. So it can never give its cylinders air denser than the air outside. In fact it always gives them slightly less, because the air loses a little pressure squeezing past the air filter, the carburettor and the throttle butterfly. As you climb and the outside air thins, the engine's power falls with it, at about 3 percent per 1,000 ft of density height.

[[diagram:power-loss-with-altitude]]
Picture a graph of power at full throttle against height. The line starts at full sea-level power on the ground, and slopes steadily away as the height rises, roughly 3 percent for every 1,000 ft. By about 7,000 to 8,000 ft, full throttle gives only about 75 percent. Supercharging is the cure for that curve.

Supercharging removes that ceiling. A compressor placed in the intake squeezes the air before it reaches the cylinders. So the engine is fed air denser than the atmosphere around it. More mass per cylinderful means more fuel can be burnt, and more power made.

Now, two different aims. Engine makers use that extra density for one of two purposes, and the exam expects you to know both.

The first aim is to maintain sea-level power at altitude. The compressor is used only to make up what the thinning atmosphere takes away. So the engine is given air about as dense as it would get at sea level, and no more. Its maximum power is the same as an unboosted version of the engine, but it keeps that power as it climbs. This is sometimes called turbo-normalising, because it restores the normal sea-level manifold pressure.

The second aim is to boost power above the normally aspirated value, at all heights, including on the ground. Here the engine is built to take more than one atmosphere of pressure in its intake. It has stronger internal parts. It often has a lower compression ratio, to keep away from detonation. And its rated manifold pressure is well above sea-level atmospheric pressure. This is ground boosting. A smaller, lighter engine then gives the power of a larger one.

Both aims show on one instrument, the manifold pressure gauge. It reads the pressure in the intake pipe downstream of the throttle, in inches of mercury. A normally aspirated engine can never show a manifold pressure above the outside air pressure. At sea level on a standard day, that is about 30 inches. A supercharged engine can show well above it. A ground-boosted engine typically shows 35 to 40 inches of mercury or more at take-off power. A reading above the outside pressure is what tells you a compressor is working.

So what does the extra power buy? Thinner air offers less drag at the same true airspeed, so the same power pushes the aeroplane faster. A turbocharged single holding 75 percent power at 12,000 ft cruises noticeably faster than at 4,000 ft, on the same fuel flow. Altitude also lifts you above much of the weather and turbulence, and it gives a longer glide if the engine stops. A normally aspirated engine cannot exploit this, because by 12,000 ft it is down to roughly two-thirds of its power.

The engine cannot hold its power for ever, though. Each compressor has a limit on how much it can squeeze. Above a certain height it has nothing more to give. That height is the critical altitude, which you met in the lesson on piston engines. It is the highest altitude at which the system, at full throttle, can still hold the rated manifold pressure. Above it, the engine loses power with height like any other.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Supercharging feeds the engine, not the aeroplane. The wing and the propeller still work in the outside air. So take-off and landing distances at a hot or high aerodrome still grow with density height, even though the engine is making full power.

Question. On the ground before start, a turbocharged aeroplane and a normally aspirated one are parked side by side at Jandakot. Both show about 30 inches of manifold pressure. Why are they the same?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: with the engine stopped, no air flows. So there is no pressure drop across the throttle, and no compressor is turning. Both gauges read the outside air pressure. Only once the turbocharged engine is running at high power can its compressor raise the manifold pressure above the outside pressure.

## The two ways of supercharging

Both methods use the same kind of compressor, a centrifugal compressor. It is a small wheel with curved vanes, called the impeller, spinning at very high speed inside a snail-shaped housing. It turns at tens of thousands of RPM.

Air enters near the hub. The vanes fling it outwards, and it leaves the rim at high speed. The housing around the rim gradually widens, so the fast-moving air slows down. And as it slows, its speed is turned into pressure. The faster the impeller spins, the more it squeezes the air.

The two methods differ only in what spins the impeller.

The first is the gear-driven supercharger, also called a mechanical supercharger. Its impeller is driven through gears from the engine's crankshaft. It works from the moment the engine starts. And it responds instantly to the throttle, because its speed is locked to the engine's.

That direct drive has two drawbacks. First, it takes power to turn the impeller, and that power comes out of the crankshaft before it reaches the propeller. So part of the extra power the supercharger produces is spent driving the supercharger itself. Second, the boost is tied to engine RPM, rather than to what the engine needs. So it is either too much at low level, or not enough high up, unless a gearbox or a clutch varies the drive. On modern light aeroplanes, gear-driven superchargers are rare.

The second method is the turbocharger. It spins the impeller with energy that would otherwise be thrown away. The engine's exhaust leaves the cylinders hot and under pressure. In a normally aspirated engine, all of that energy goes straight out of the exhaust pipe.

In a turbocharged engine, the exhaust is led first through a turbine, a second bladed wheel, which it spins as it rushes past. The turbine and the compressor impeller are mounted on the same shaft. So the exhaust spins the turbine, the turbine spins the compressor, and the compressor squeezes the intake air.

Follow the air through a typical installation. Outside air enters through the filter. It passes through the compressor, where it is squeezed and heated. It may pass through an intercooler. Then it goes through the throttle and the fuel metering, a carburettor or fuel injection, to the intake manifold and the cylinders. After combustion, the exhaust gas goes to the turbine, spins it, and leaves through the exhaust pipe.

In most light aeroplane installations, the compressor squeezes the air before it reaches the carburettor or the injector. Some designs instead place the carburettor before the compressor, so that the compressor squeezes the fuel and air mixture.

Because it uses waste energy, a turbocharger costs the engine little. It takes no power from the crankshaft. The only cost is a slight rise in the back pressure the pistons push the exhaust against. That is why it is the common choice on light aeroplanes.

It brings three characteristics of its own.

The first is turbo lag. The turbine has to speed up before the compressor delivers extra boost. And it only speeds up once the engine is producing more exhaust. So when you open the throttle, the manifold pressure takes a few seconds to follow. Then it may keep rising after you stop moving the lever. That is why throttle movements on a turbocharged engine are slow and smooth.

The second is heat. The turbine sits in exhaust gas hot enough to make its housing glow. And the compressor heats the intake air as it squeezes it. So turbocharged engines run hotter. Many show a turbine inlet temperature gauge, with its own limit.

And the third is bearings that need oil. The shaft spins on bearings that are lubricated and cooled by engine oil. A shaft running at tens of thousands of RPM, next to a red-hot turbine, depends on that oil flow.

Now the intercooler. Squeezing air heats it. Hot air is less dense than cool air at the same pressure, so part of the benefit of compression is lost. And hot intake air brings the charge closer to detonation, where the last part of the mixture explodes instead of burning smoothly. You met detonation in the lesson on piston engines.

So many turbocharged installations pass the compressed air through an intercooler. It is a radiator, in which outside air cools the charge before it reaches the cylinders. A cooler charge means a denser charge, more power for the same manifold pressure, and a wider margin from detonation.

So, the two methods side by side. The gear-driven supercharger is driven by the crankshaft, through gears. The turbocharger is driven by a turbine in the exhaust. The supercharger takes power from the crankshaft. The turbocharger uses waste exhaust energy, at the cost of a slight back pressure only. The supercharger responds to the throttle instantly. The turbocharger has a few seconds of lag. The supercharger's boost is tied to engine RPM. The turbocharger's is tied to the exhaust flow, which the wastegate controls. And where you meet them: gear-driven superchargers on old radials and historic aircraft, and turbochargers on most supercharged light aeroplanes.

Question. Why is a turbocharger more efficient than a gear-driven supercharger?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the gear-driven supercharger takes power from the crankshaft to spin its compressor, so it spends some of the power it creates. The turbocharger spins its compressor with energy in the exhaust gas that would otherwise be wasted. It costs the engine only a slight increase in exhaust back pressure.

## Keeping the boost in check

First, why boost must be limited. A turbocharger at full speed can squeeze far more air into the engine than the engine can survive. That is especially so low down, where the air is already dense.

Too much manifold pressure means too much air and fuel in each cylinder. That means too high a peak pressure when it burns, and a charge so hot and compressed that it detonates. Pistons, rings and cylinder heads are damaged within minutes. So every supercharged engine has a rated manifold pressure, marked by a red line on the manifold pressure gauge. And it has devices that keep the compressor from exceeding it.

You might expect the throttle to do that job. On a turbocharged engine, it is the wrong tool for the whole job. Throttling the air after the compressor has squeezed it wastes the work the turbine did. It heats the charge. And it leaves the turbine spinning hard for nothing. The better way is to control how hard the turbine is driven in the first place.

The device that does this is the wastegate. It is a valve in the exhaust system that lets exhaust gas bypass the turbine, and go straight out of the exhaust pipe.

Think of it in three positions. Wastegate open: most of the exhaust takes the easy route around the turbine. The turbine turns slowly, the compressor does little, and the boost is low. Wastegate closed: all of the exhaust is forced through the turbine. The turbine spins at full speed, and the compressor delivers maximum boost. And in between, the share of exhaust going through the turbine, and so the boost, is set by how far the wastegate is open.

Now picture a climb in a turbo-normalised aeroplane. On the ground at full throttle, the dense sea-level air needs almost no help. So the wastegate is nearly open. As you climb and the outside air thins, the wastegate closes progressively. It sends more exhaust through the turbine, and makes the compressor work harder to keep the manifold pressure at its rated value.

At the critical altitude, the wastegate is fully closed. Every bit of exhaust is driving the turbine, and the compressor has no more to give. Above that, the manifold pressure falls as you climb.

So the wastegate's position is to the turbocharger what the throttle's position is to a normally aspirated engine at full throttle height. Once it reaches its stop, the reserve is gone.

Who moves the wastegate? There are three ways of setting it, and the POH tells you which your aeroplane has.

The first is a fixed wastegate. The engineers set it to a fixed opening, or replace it with a fixed bypass hole. It sends a fixed share of the exhaust through the turbine. So the pilot limits the boost with the throttle, and must watch the manifold pressure closely. That matters most on take-off on a cold day, and during the climb, when it changes with every few hundred feet.

The second is a manual wastegate. The pilot moves it with a cockpit control. The pilot sets the throttle for the power wanted, and closes the wastegate progressively in the climb. It works, but it is easy to get wrong.

The third is an automatic wastegate controller, which does the job for you. In the common systems, the wastegate is moved by an actuator worked by engine oil pressure. Oil pressure pushes the wastegate towards closed, and a spring pushes it open. A controller senses the pressure in the intake. It bleeds off oil to let the wastegate open, or holds oil in to close it. That way the boost stays where it should be as altitude and power change.

The automatic varieties have different names. An absolute pressure controller holds a set manifold pressure. A density controller senses the pressure and temperature of the compressed air, and limits the boost by its density. That stops the engine being over-boosted on a cold day, when the air is denser. And a sloped controller, on some installations, lets the maximum manifold pressure rise slightly with altitude, to make up for the hotter, thinner charge high up. Whatever the name, the job is the same: to limit the boost automatically to the engine's rating.

That oil pressure design has a useful property. If oil pressure to the actuator is lost, the spring opens the wastegate. The turbine slows, and the engine is left with roughly normally aspirated power. That is a loss of boost, but not an overboost.

Controllers can fail, though, and wastegates can stick. So two further devices stand behind them.

The first is a manifold pressure relief valve, often called a pop-off valve. It is a spring-loaded safety valve in the intake. If the manifold pressure rises past a set value above the rated limit, it opens and vents the excess to the atmosphere. It is a last line of defence, not a control.

The second is the warning. Some installations have an overboost warning, a light or an aural alert. And every gauge has the red line, which tells you when the limit has been passed. On a type with neither a warning nor an automatic controller, the red line and your eyes are the only protection.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. The wastegate controls boost by controlling the turbine, through the exhaust. It does not throttle the intake. A distractor will describe it as a valve that bleeds compressed air from the intake. That is the pop-off valve. And the pop-off valve is the emergency back-up, not the normal control.

Question. In a climb with an automatic controller, which way does the wastegate move? And what has happened when it reaches its stop?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: it closes progressively. It sends more exhaust through the turbine, so that the compressor works harder to hold the rated manifold pressure in thinner air. When it is fully closed, the aeroplane has reached its critical altitude. Above that, the manifold pressure and the power fall with height.

## When supercharging takes the engine past its limits

An overboost is any manifold pressure above the red line. It is the commonest supercharging exceedance. And its usual causes follow directly from the machinery you have just met. There are three.

The first is opening the throttle too quickly, especially for take-off. The controller needs a moment to catch up with the rising exhaust flow. So the manifold pressure can overshoot the limit before it settles.

The second is a cold, dense morning, with a fixed or manual wastegate. The dense air gives the compressor more to work with. So a throttle setting that was fine on a warm day overboosts on a cold one.

And the third is a controller fault, or a wastegate stuck closed. Then the turbine is driven flat out whether you want it or not. And the manifold pressure rises past the red line as soon as you use high power, particularly low down.

Why does it matter? Too much manifold pressure means too much charge in each cylinder. The peak pressure when it burns is higher than the engine was built for. And the hot, highly compressed charge is close to detonation.

[[diagram:detonation-vs-normal-combustion]]
Picture two cylinders side by side. On the left, normal combustion: a smooth flame front spreads out from the spark plug, and the pressure rises smoothly. On the right, detonation: after the spark, the rest of the charge explodes and hammers the piston, and the pressure shows a sharp spike. High manifold pressure, especially with low RPM or a lean mixture, is one of the listed causes. And the effects are rough running, a sharp rise in cylinder head temperature, a loss of power, and damage within minutes.

Detonation hammers the pistons, burns the edges of the piston crowns, and can crack cylinder heads. You may hear or feel nothing until the damage is done. What you will see is the cylinder head temperature rising quickly. Even an overboost too brief to cause detonation over-stresses the engine's moving parts. That is why engine manufacturers specify inspections after an overboost beyond a given amount, or a given time.

So what do you do? The actions follow from the causes. When the manifold pressure goes above the red line, there are four steps.

First, reduce the throttle smoothly, to bring the manifold pressure back within limits. The throttle is your direct control over the pressure in the manifold, whatever the wastegate is doing.

Second, check the mixture is correctly set, rich at high power as the POH requires, because the extra fuel cools the charge. And keep the RPM up, because high manifold pressure with low RPM is a detonation condition.

Third, monitor the cylinder head temperature, the exhaust gas temperature, and the turbine inlet temperature where it is fitted. Reduce power further if they are high.

And fourth, note the exceedance and how long it lasted. After landing, record it in the maintenance release, so that the engineers can carry out whatever inspection the engine manufacturer specifies.

If the cause is a controller fault, expect it to happen again. Fly the rest of the flight at a power setting that keeps the manifold pressure below the red line. Move the throttle slowly. And watch the gauge, rather than trusting the lever position. Your POH may give a specific procedure for a failed controller or wastegate. If it does, that takes precedence.

Here's another trap the exam likes to set. It offers reduce the RPM as the cure for an overboost. Reducing the RPM does not reduce the manifold pressure much. And high manifold pressure at low RPM is exactly the condition that invites detonation. The cure is the throttle. When reducing power on an engine with a constant-speed propeller, the usual order is throttle back first, then RPM.

Finally, looking after a turbocharged engine. Several other limits exist because of the turbocharger, and they are part of handling one well. There are three habits.

The first is smooth, slow throttle movements. Turbo lag means the manifold pressure follows the throttle a few seconds late. Moving the lever slowly, and pausing, lets you see where it will settle before you go further. And it avoids the overshoot that causes most take-off overboosts.

The second is watching the temperatures in the climb. A turbocharged engine can climb at high power for much longer than a normally aspirated one. That means a long time hot. So climb at the POH's recommended airspeed and mixture, with the cowl flaps open if they are fitted, to keep the cylinder head and turbine inlet temperatures within limits.

And the third is cooling the turbocharger before shutdown. After landing, the turbine is still very hot. If you stop the engine at once, the oil supply to the turbocharger bearings stops with it. The oil left in the hot bearings can cook into carbon, which wears the bearings and eventually seizes the shaft. So let the engine idle for a few minutes before shutdown, for the time the POH gives. The turbine cools while the oil is still flowing.

Question. You advance the throttle for take-off on a cold Perth winter morning, in an aeroplane with a fixed wastegate. The manifold pressure passes the red line. What do you do? And why was a cold morning the problem?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: reduce the throttle smoothly until the manifold pressure is within limits. Keep the mixture rich and the RPM high. Watch the engine temperatures. And record the overboost in the maintenance release after the flight. A cold morning was the problem because cold air is dense. So with a fixed wastegate, the same throttle setting gives more boost than on a warm day.

## Bringing it together

You are flying a turbocharged single with an automatic wastegate controller, from Jandakot to Karratha, planning 11,500 ft. Before take-off, you review the manifold pressure red line and the turbine inlet temperature limit.

It is a cool morning. So you advance the throttle slowly for take-off, and watch the manifold pressure settle at the take-off figure, rather than shoving the lever to the stop.

In the climb, the controller closes the wastegate bit by bit. The manifold pressure stays put, where a normally aspirated aeroplane's would be falling an inch per 1,000 ft. At 11,500 ft you are still below the critical altitude, so you set cruise power with manifold pressure to spare.

Halfway, the manifold pressure creeps up a couple of inches above your setting, towards the red line, with no throttle movement. That is a controller fault. You throttle back to bring it within limits, and check the mixture and the RPM. You note the time and the peak reading. And you fly the rest of the trip at a lower setting, moving the throttle gently.

At Karratha, you idle for the POH cooling time before shutdown. Then you write up the overboost and the controller fault in the maintenance release.

## Key points

Let's recap.

Why supercharge. Supercharging compresses the intake air, so the cylinders get air denser than the atmosphere. It is done for one of two aims. Either to hold sea-level power up to the critical altitude, which is turbo-normalising. Or to boost power above the normally aspirated value at all heights, which is ground boosting. Either way, the manifold pressure gauge can then read above the outside pressure.

The two methods. A gear-driven supercharger spins its compressor from the crankshaft. It responds instantly, but it costs crankshaft power. A turbocharger spins its compressor with an exhaust-driven turbine on a common shaft. It uses waste energy, but brings lag, heat, and bearings cooled by oil. An intercooler cools the compressed charge.

Keeping the boost in check. The wastegate limits boost by letting exhaust bypass the turbine. Open for little boost, closed for full boost, and fully closed at the critical altitude. It may be fixed, manual or automatic, and the automatic ones are absolute pressure, density, or sloped controllers. A pop-off valve, and the red line or an overboost warning, back it up.

And the overboost. Reduce the throttle. Keep the mixture rich and the RPM up. Watch the cylinder head, exhaust gas and turbine inlet temperatures. And record the exceedance and how long it lasted in the maintenance release. Move the throttle slowly, and let the turbocharger cool before shutdown.

A final exam tip. The wastegate controls boost by controlling the turbine, not by throttling the intake. Closed means all the exhaust drives the turbine, and maximum boost. An overboost is cured by reducing the throttle, not the RPM. And a significant overboost must be recorded in the maintenance release for the engineers. Know the two purposes: maintain sea-level power at altitude, or boost above it. And know the two methods: the crankshaft-driven supercharger, and the exhaust-driven turbocharger.
