This is PAKC 3.4, Reporting position in the circuit and for local flights. By the end you will know the standard names for every part of the circuit, the shape of every CTAF broadcast, the calls a local flight makes on the way out and on the way back, and how those calls change when there is a tower. There are a few questions along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (PAKC 3.4)

The exam wants one thing from you, in several parts. You need the standard words for reporting where you are in the circuit. And you need the calls a local flight makes on departure, in the training area and on return, both at an aerodrome without a tower and at one with a tower.

## Why this matters

On a Saturday morning the circuit at Murrayfield can hold four aeroplanes, a gyroplane, and someone inbound from Serpentine. The only thing joining them into one picture is the radio.

If every pilot names the same legs with the same words, and puts the same items in the same order, a ten-second broadcast tells everyone where you are, how high you are, and what you will do next. If someone invents their own words, the picture breaks, and lookout is all that is left.

At Jandakot the tower does the sequencing. But it can only do that if your calls give it what it needs, at the points where it expects them.

The exam asks you to pick the correct form of a broadcast. It asks when the inbound call is due, and why the aerodrome name is said twice. And it asks what a downwind call at a towered aerodrome contains. Every call in this lesson follows the AIP wording, and CASA's advisory circular on operations near non-controlled aerodromes, as the lesson gives them. Check the current AIP and Visual Flight Rules Guide before relying on any of them.

## Naming the legs of the circuit

Every circuit is the same shape: a rectangle, with the runway along one long side. Because the shape is standard, its parts can have standard names. And a name tells other pilots both where you are, and roughly how high.

[[diagram:circuit-pattern]]
Picture a left-hand circuit from above, with the runway along the bottom. There are five legs. The first is upwind: straight ahead after take-off, climbing along the runway direction. The second is crosswind: after the first turn, at right angles to the runway, still climbing to circuit height. The third is downwind: parallel to the runway, flying the opposite way to the landing direction, at circuit height, which is 1,000 ft above the aerodrome. The fourth is base: at right angles again, descending, setting up for the last turn. And the fifth is final: lined up with the runway, descending to land.

Around those five legs sit a few more terms. Joining crosswind, and joining downwind, say that you are entering the pattern on that leg, rather than flying it from take-off. Late downwind means you are past the landing threshold on the downwind leg. That tells other pilots you will turn base soon. And straight-in approach means you are approaching along the extended centreline, without flying the rest of the circuit.

Distances on final are given in nautical miles, as in "5 miles final". Left or right is added only where the circuit direction is not obvious. For example, at an aerodrome where one runway has a right-hand circuit, you would say "right downwind runway {24|two four}".

[[widget:circuit-3d]]
Now imagine flying that circuit in three dimensions, and then swapping the runway in use. The whole pattern flips. But the leg names stay the same. That is exactly why they work as position reports.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Upwind is not "into wind on approach". It is the leg flown straight ahead after take-off. A pilot who calls "upwind" when they mean final points every listener at the wrong end of the runway.

Question. You are on the downwind leg, already past the landing threshold. What do you call your position, and why does the distinction help others?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: late downwind. It tells others that your base turn is close. So an aircraft about to turn base, or about to join, will expect you close ahead, rather than at the start of the leg.

## Calls at a non-controlled aerodrome

At a non-controlled aerodrome, nobody sequences the traffic. The pilots do it themselves, with two tools. The first is lookout. The second is the CTAF, the common traffic advisory frequency. That is the frequency published in ERSA on which everyone near that aerodrome broadcasts. Murrayfield, Serpentine and Northam all work this way, and their CTAF frequencies are in ERSA.

Under CASR Part 91, a pilot must broadcast when it is reasonably necessary to avoid a collision risk. And pilots must carry and use a radio at certain aerodromes. The current list is in the AIP and the Visual Flight Rules Guide. CASA's advisory circular then sets out the recommended broadcasts. Those are the points at which a call most often prevents a conflict.

Every CTAF broadcast follows one format. Location traffic. Type. Call-sign. Position. Level. Intentions. And location again. Say it with me once more: location traffic, type, call-sign, position, level, intentions, location.

Each item has a job. The location first tells listeners which aerodrome the call is about. Traffic says it is addressed to all aircraft, not to one particular station. The type lets others picture what they are looking for, and how fast it will be. The call-sign identifies you. Position and level say where you are. Intentions say what you will do next. That is the part another pilot needs to plan around.

And the location again at the end matters, because the same frequency, often {126.7|one two six decimal seven}, is shared by many aerodromes. A pilot who tunes in halfway through your call missed the first word. The last word tells them whether the call is about their aerodrome, or one 40 nm away.

[[diagram:ctaf-broadcast-anatomy]]
Picture an inbound call laid out in a row, split into its eight items, with a reason written beside each one. "Northam traffic, Cessna {172|one seven two} Alpha Bravo Charlie, 10 miles south, 2,500, inbound, estimate circuit at 25, Northam." The aerodrome name sits at both ends, like a pair of brackets around the call. That is because many aerodromes share one frequency, and a pilot who tunes in late still hears the last word.

Now the recommended calls themselves. There are seven points at which to call.

First, before taxiing. "Northam traffic, Cessna {172|one seven two} Alpha Bravo Charlie, taxiing runway {23|two three} for the south-west training area, Northam."

Second, before entering the runway. "Northam traffic, Cessna Alpha Bravo Charlie, entering and rolling runway {23|two three}, departing to the south-west, Northam."

Third, inbound. That call is made by 10 nm, and earlier for fast aircraft. "Northam traffic, Cessna {172|one seven two} Alpha Bravo Charlie, 10 miles south, 2,500, inbound, estimate circuit at 25, Northam."

Fourth, joining the circuit. "Northam traffic, Cessna Alpha Bravo Charlie, joining crosswind runway {23|two three}, Northam."

Fifth, a straight-in approach, if you use one. The call contains "3 miles final, straight-in approach runway {23|two three}". And you use it only when no other traffic is disadvantaged.

Sixth, base or final, when it is needed for separation, for example if other traffic is about. The call contains "turning base runway {23|two three}".

And seventh, clear of the runway. That one is optional, but useful when traffic is waiting.

Each call sits where it does for a reason. The taxi call warns aircraft about to land that someone will soon be on the runway. It also tells anyone else taxiing where you are going. The entering the runway call is the last warning before you occupy the runway. An aircraft on short final that hears it can go around, rather than land on top of you. The inbound call gives circuit traffic time to look for you. The joining call tells them exactly where you will appear. Base and final calls are made when other traffic is about, and needs to know where you are in the sequence. And clear of the runway tells a waiting aircraft that it may line up.

So why 10 nm for the inbound call? At 100 kt, 10 nm gives others about 6 minutes' warning. The arithmetic is 10 nautical miles divided by 100 knots, which is a tenth of an hour. Times 60, that is 6 minutes. Six minutes is enough for a pilot in the circuit to hear you, look for you, and fit their circuit around your arrival. A faster aircraft covers the 10 nm sooner. That is why the inbound call comes earlier for fast aircraft.

Before the inbound call, listen to the CTAF for a while. Learn which runway is in use, and who is in the circuit. Then your call can state your joining intention in terms that fit what is already happening.

Let's work one: a training flight departing Northam. Cessna {172|one seven two} Alpha Bravo Charlie is at Northam, with runway {23|two three} in use, departing for the south-west training area. What calls does it make, and in what order?

There are four steps. First, listen on the CTAF before starting, to learn the runway in use and the traffic. Second, before taxiing: "Northam traffic, Cessna {172|one seven two} Alpha Bravo Charlie, taxiing runway {23|two three} for the south-west training area, Northam." Third, at the holding point, after the run-up, look along the approach path and listen. And fourth, before entering the runway: "Northam traffic, Cessna Alpha Bravo Charlie, entering and rolling runway {23|two three}, departing to the south-west, Northam."

So the answer is two calls, each bracketed by "Northam". Together they give every pilot within range a complete picture of where you are and what you will do next.

Now try the return yourself. The same aeroplane is 12 miles south of Northam, at 2,500 ft, returning at about 100 kt. It expects to reach the circuit at 25 minutes past the hour. What is the inbound call, when do you make it, and what is the joining call for a crosswind join on runway {23|two three}? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 8]]
First, listen to the CTAF to confirm the runway in use and the traffic. The inbound call is due by 10 nm, so make it now, or within the next mile. "Northam traffic, Cessna {172|one seven two} Alpha Bravo Charlie, 10 miles south, 2,500, inbound, estimate circuit at 25, Northam."

Then, on joining: "Northam traffic, Cessna Alpha Bravo Charlie, joining crosswind runway {23|two three}, Northam." And if other traffic is about, add "turning base runway {23|two three}" at the base turn. At 100 kt, the 10 nm call gives about 6 minutes' warning before you reach the circuit.

[[diagram:overhead-join]]
What if you are unsure of the runway in use? Then you can join from overhead. Picture a left-hand circuit seen from above. You cross overhead the aerodrome, above circuit height. You descend on the dead side, the side away from the circuit, down to circuit height, 1,000 ft above the aerodrome. Then you cross the upwind end of the runway, and join the crosswind leg. So your joining call names the crosswind leg.

Question. A CTAF broadcast starts with the aerodrome name. Why say it again at the end?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because one frequency is often shared by several aerodromes. A listener who tunes in, or starts paying attention partway through, missed the first word. The name at the end tells them which aerodrome the call is about.

Here's another trap the exam likes to set. A straight-in approach is allowed only when no other traffic is disadvantaged. Calling "straight-in" does not give you priority over aircraft already in the circuit. If anyone would have to extend, or go around, for you, join the circuit instead.

## Calls at a towered aerodrome

At a towered aerodrome such as Jandakot, the tower controls the circuit and does the sequencing. So your calls change character. You are no longer telling everyone where you are so that they can sort themselves out. You are telling the controller what you need, so that the controller can sort it out for you. Position reports are made when asked, or at standard points.

The key one is downwind. "Alpha Bravo Charlie, downwind, touch and go." You state either "full stop" or "touch and go". The downwind call is the controller's cue to fit you into the landing sequence, and your intention matters to that. A touch and go stays in the circuit. A full stop leaves the runway, and frees a slot.

The tower replies with a sequence. "Alpha Bravo Charlie, number two, follow the Warrior on base." Number two means one aircraft lands ahead of you. "Follow the Warrior on base" tells you which one to look for. Your job is to find it, follow it, and turn base behind it.

Final is reported if the tower asks for it, or where local procedures require it. "Alpha Bravo Charlie, final."

Then comes the clearance. You read back the landing or take-off clearance in full. "Cleared touch and go runway {24 right|two four right}, Alpha Bravo Charlie." Reading it back in full lets the controller hear that you have the right runway and the right clearance. Jandakot has parallel runways. So "{24 right|two four right}" against "{24 left|two four left}" is exactly the kind of slip a readback catches.

Let's work through a Jandakot circuit exchange. Alpha Bravo Charlie is in the Jandakot circuit for runway {24 right|two four right}, intending to land and stay in the circuit. There are five steps.

First, on downwind: "Alpha Bravo Charlie, downwind, touch and go." Second, the tower: "Alpha Bravo Charlie, number two, follow the Warrior on base." Third, you find the Warrior, follow it, and turn base behind it. Fourth, the tower clears you, and you read it back in full: "Cleared touch and go runway {24 right|two four right}, Alpha Bravo Charlie." And fifth, if no clearance has come by short final, you do not land. You query the tower, or go around.

So the downwind call carried your intention. The sequence told you whom to follow. And the full readback confirmed the runway.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. At a towered aerodrome, you never land without hearing "cleared to land", or the touch and go clearance you asked for. Being number one, and seeing a clear runway, is not a clearance.

Question. Why does the downwind call at a towered aerodrome include "touch and go" or "full stop"?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because the controller is building the landing sequence, and the two intentions use the runway differently. A touch and go stays in the circuit. A full stop vacates. Stating it on downwind gives the controller time to plan.

## Local flights: out to the training area and back

A local flight leaves the circuit for a nearby training area, and returns to the same aerodrome. Its calls bracket the flight. Getting out. Staying in touch while you are there. And getting back in.

Start with departing a towered aerodrome. From Jandakot, the sequence is: get the ATIS, the automatic terminal information service, then call Ground with the departure request. "Jandakot Ground, Cessna Alpha Bravo Charlie, received Echo, request taxi for south-west training area, VFR, two POB."

Every item has a purpose. "Received Echo" tells Ground you have the current ATIS, so they need not read you the runway and weather. The request says where you are going, so the taxi route and the departure can be planned. "VFR" and "two POB", meaning two persons on board, complete the picture for search and rescue. On leaving the control zone by the published route, the tower will say "frequency change approved", or hand you to Centre.

[[diagram:class-d-tower-hours]]
One thing to check first: is the aerodrome controlled today? Picture the same regional towered aerodrome twice, side by side. On the left, during the tower's published hours, the zone is Class D, a clearance is required, and the tower controls the circuit. On the right, outside those hours, the airspace reverts to Class G, or Class E above, and the aerodrome becomes a CTAF aerodrome. Then the CTAF calls apply. The hours are in ERSA, and may be changed by NOTAM.

In the training area, keep a listening watch on the area frequency, and on any training area frequency listed in ERSA. Some areas have local broadcast procedures, and ERSA shows them. The listening watch is what lets you hear another aircraft reporting its position in your area. And it is what lets air traffic services reach you.

Now returning. To a towered aerodrome, listen to the ATIS, then call the tower at the inbound reporting point. "Jandakot Tower, Cessna Alpha Bravo Charlie, at the reporting point, 1,500, received Golf, inbound." The inbound reporting points and altitudes are on the Perth VTC, the visual terminal chart, and in ERSA. The call has the same logic as the CTAF inbound call: position, level and intention. Plus the ATIS letter.

To a CTAF aerodrome, make the inbound call by 10 nm, and then the joining call, exactly as in the Northam example.

Last, the SARTIME. A SARTIME is the time by which, if you have not reported, search and rescue action starts. If you have nominated a SARTIME for the local flight, either with Centre or with a responsible person, you must cancel it on landing. Nominating it is a safety net for a solo flight. Forgetting to cancel it launches a search for an aeroplane that is already in the hangar.

[[diagram:sartime-timeline]]
Picture a timeline running from left to right. Before flight, you nominate a SARTIME in UTC, with a sensible margin after your planned arrival. If you are delayed, you extend it by radio or phone before it expires. On landing, you cancel it at once. Nothing happens until the SARTIME passes. But if it passes without being cancelled or amended, the alerting phases begin: attempts to contact you, then a communications search, then a physical search. So cancel on landing.

Question. In the Jandakot taxi request, what does "received Echo" tell Ground, and why does it save time?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: that you have listened to ATIS information Echo, the current broadcast of runway, wind and conditions. Ground need not repeat that information. So the exchange is shorter, and the frequency stays clear.

## Bringing it together

It is a Sunday local flight from Jandakot. You listen to the ATIS, information Echo, and request taxi from Ground for the south-west training area, VFR, two POB. The tower clears you for take-off, and you read the clearance back in full, including "{24 right|two four right}". Leaving the zone by the published route, the tower approves the frequency change. In the training area you keep a listening watch on the area frequency.

On the way home you divert to Serpentine for a few circuits. You listen on its CTAF to learn the runway in use. You make the inbound call by 10 nm, with position, level and intention, bracketed by "Serpentine". Then a joining call naming the leg. And a "turning base" call, because two others are in the pattern.

Back towards Jandakot, you take the new ATIS, Golf. You call the tower at the inbound reporting point, at the published altitude. On downwind you say "touch and go" or "full stop". The tower gives you a sequence, you find the aircraft to follow, and you land only after the clearance. On the ground, the last call is to cancel your SARTIME.

Every call used the same leg names and the same order of items. Only the listener changed.

## Key points

Let's recap.

The legs are upwind, crosswind, downwind, base and final. Add joining crosswind, joining downwind, late downwind and straight-in. Distances on final are in miles.

A CTAF broadcast goes: location traffic, type, call-sign, position, level, intentions, location. The inbound call is made by 10 nm.

The recommended calls are: taxiing, entering the runway, inbound, joining, base or final when traffic needs it, and clear of the runway.

At a tower: call downwind with "touch and go" or "full stop", follow the sequence, read clearances back in full, and never land without a clearance.

And local flights: get the ATIS, make the request to Ground, keep a listening watch in the area, call inbound at the reporting point with the ATIS letter, and cancel the SARTIME.

A final exam tip. The inbound call at a non-controlled aerodrome is made by 10 nm. And every CTAF broadcast both starts and ends with the aerodrome name. At a towered aerodrome, the downwind call states your intention, touch and go or full stop. And you never land without hearing "cleared to land". Expect a question offering four versions of a broadcast. Pick the one in the order location traffic, type, call-sign, position, level, intentions, location.
