This is PAKC 3.8, Radio failure procedures. By the end you will know where the radio failure procedure lives in ERSA, and what to set, what to say, where to go and how to arrive when your radio goes quiet, with or without a tower. There are a few questions along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (PAKC 3.8)

The exam wants one thing from you, in four parts. Find the radio failure procedures in ERSA, and apply them to a flight. What to set. What to say. Where to go. And how to arrive.

## Why this matters

A radio that goes quiet is one of the commonest abnormal situations a student pilot meets. And it rarely happens at a convenient moment. It happens on the way back from the training area south of Jandakot, with the Class D zone ahead. Or halfway between Northam and York, with a CTAF full of other aeroplanes you can no longer hear.

Nothing about the aeroplane's ability to fly has changed, so this is not an emergency. But your two safety nets around other traffic have gone at once: hearing them, and being heard by them.

The procedure in ERSA exists so that controllers and other pilots can predict what you will do, without talking to you. The exam gives you an ERSA extract or a scenario, and asks what you do next. So you need to know where the text lives, and the logic behind each step.

## Finding the procedure in ERSA

ERSA, the En Route Supplement Australia, is mostly aerodrome entries. But its front section holds the procedures you might need in a hurry. The emergency section is printed on pink pages, so you can find it by feel. It holds the distress and urgency calls, the transponder emergency codes, the communications failure procedures, interception, light signals, and search and rescue.

The communications failure procedures are set out in separate paragraphs. There are paragraphs for VFR flights and for IFR flights, and for flights in controlled airspace and outside it. You read the paragraph that matches your flight rules and your airspace, and you ignore the rest.

[[diagram:transponder-emergency-codes]]
Picture three transponder code windows, side by side, with the pink pages behind them. The first reads {7700|seven seven zero zero}: emergency. The second reads {7600|seven six zero zero}: radio failure. And the third reads {7500|seven five zero zero}: unlawful interference. All three sit in the ERSA emergency section, together with the radio failure procedure and the light signals. So one section answers every question in this lesson.

Practise finding the emergency section in your own copy on the ground. And accept that the text changes with ERSA amendments. What follows is the shape of the procedure as the cited sources gave it when the lesson was written. The exam and the cockpit both use the current ERSA text. So verify each step against the current ERSA emergency section, and the AIP.

Question. You are VFR in Class G, and the emergency section has paragraphs for IFR in controlled airspace and for VFR. Which do you use?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the VFR paragraph that applies outside controlled airspace. The procedure differs with flight rules and airspace, because what air traffic control expects of you differs. So the first step in extracting the procedure is choosing the paragraph that matches your flight.

## Is it really a failure?

Before you treat the radio as dead, find out whether it is. A radio installation is a chain. The microphone and headset. The audio panel that routes the signal. The radio itself, with its frequency and volume settings. The power that feeds it. And the antenna outside. A break anywhere in the chain looks the same from the seat: silence. And most breaks are in the parts you can reach.

So run the fault-finding check from the lesson on fault finding, in order. Volume up. Squelch opened, and you should hear background hiss. The frequency in the active window, not standby, and the right one. The audio panel selections: the right COM selected for the MIC and for receive. The headset and microphone plugs fully home. The circuit breakers in. Then try COM 2, and the hand microphone. Many apparent failures end here.

If the checks find nothing, think about which half has failed. That decides how much good each later step does. There are three possibilities.

First, the receiver has failed, and the transmitter works. You hear nothing, but everyone hears you. Your broadcasts still let other pilots and air traffic control know where you are.

Second, the transmitter has failed, and the receiver works. You hear everything, but nobody hears you. You can still listen to the ATIS, the CTAF traffic, and anything air traffic control says to you.

And third, both have failed, as with an electrical failure. You are a silent aircraft. You must rely on the transponder code, if it still has power, your lookout, and the published procedures.

From the cockpit, you usually cannot tell which of the first two you have. So the procedure assumes the worst of each half. You keep listening, in case the receiver works. And you keep transmitting, in case the transmitter does.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. A radio failure on its own is not a distress situation. The code is {7600|seven six zero zero}, not {7700|seven seven zero zero}, and the procedure is calm and planned. If something else is wrong as well, such as an engine problem or a sick passenger, the emergency procedures from the lesson on distress and emergency messages apply on top.

## The VFR radio failure procedure, step by step

Each step answers one question. How do the people around you find out what is going on? And how do you stay safe without them? There are six steps.

First, squawk {7600|seven six zero zero}, if a transponder is fitted. Radar controllers see the radio failure code.

Second, listen on the appropriate frequencies, including the ATIS and any navaid voice channel. Your receiver may work, even if your transmitter does not.

Third, transmit blind. Make your normal calls, prefixed with "{TRANSMITTING BLIND|transmitting blind}", and repeated, because your transmitter may work even if your receiver does not. "Transmitting blind, Northam traffic, Cessna Alpha Bravo Charlie", and then the rest of the call.

Fourth, remain in VMC, visual meteorological conditions. In controlled airspace, a VFR flight that has lost communications should stay in VMC, and follow the ERSA procedure for its situation. Generally, that means proceeding in accordance with the last clearance received and acknowledged, where that is what ERSA requires. Or leaving controlled airspace by the most direct route. Then land.

Fifth, land at the most suitable aerodrome.

And sixth, report your arrival to air traffic services by telephone, as soon as possible, so that a search is not started. And cancel any SARTIME.

That procedure comes from the ERSA emergency section and the AIP. Verify it against the current text. Now the reason for each step.

Why {7600|seven six zero zero}? A transponder answers radar interrogations with a four-digit code, each digit 0 to 7. In altitude mode, it also sends your pressure altitude. A few codes are reserved, so that their meaning appears on every radar screen at once. {7600|Seven six zero zero} means radio failure. The moment you select it, the controller's display flags your aircraft. And they know two things without a word from you. You may not hear instructions. And you will be following the published procedure. They can then move other traffic around you. Remember the three reserved codes as a set. {7500|Seven five zero zero}, unlawful interference. {7600|Seven six zero zero}, radio failure. {7700|Seven seven zero zero}, emergency.

Why listen? Listening costs nothing, and it may give you everything. The ATIS tells you the runway and the conditions at your destination. And a working receiver lets you hear other traffic, and any instruction air traffic control tries to give you.

Why transmit blind? Transmitting blind means making a broadcast you cannot confirm anyone received. You make the same calls you would normally make, at the same points: position, intentions, joining, turning final. Each is prefixed "{TRANSMITTING BLIND|transmitting blind}", and sent twice, because without a readback you cannot know whether the first one got through. The prefix tells every listener not to reply and expect an answer. And it tells air traffic control that you will not acknowledge their calls.

Why remain in VMC and land? In visual conditions you can see and avoid other traffic yourself. That replaces the separation the radio was helping with. If you flew into cloud, you would be invisible to other pilots, and unable to coordinate with anyone. Landing at the most suitable aerodrome limits the time you spend as a silent aircraft. Most suitable is your judgement. Often it is the nearest non-controlled aerodrome with a good runway. Sometimes it is your destination, if it is close and you know its procedures well.

And why telephone afterwards? The search and rescue system is triggered by silence. If you held a SARTIME, a time by which you promised to report your safe arrival, or you were on a flight plan with air traffic services, the system cannot tell a quiet radio from a crashed aeroplane. Telephoning air traffic services, or the tower you were dealing with, as soon as you are on the ground closes the loop. It stops a search being launched for you.

[[diagram:sartime-timeline]]
Picture a timeline running from left to right. Before flight you nominate a SARTIME, with a margin after your planned arrival. If you are delayed, you extend it before it expires. On landing, you cancel it. Nothing happens until the SARTIME passes. But silence after it starts the alerting phases: attempts to contact you, then a communications search, and eventually a physical search. A phone call on landing stops it.

Question. After the checks, you can hear the Jandakot ATIS clearly, but the tower never answers you. Which half of the radio is probably working, and does it change what you do?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the receiver is probably working, so the transmitter, or the microphone, is the suspect. You still squawk {7600|seven six zero zero}, and you still transmit blind, because you cannot be sure the transmitter is dead. And you listen closely, because any instruction air traffic control gives you will reach you.

## In controlled airspace

Controlled airspace adds a complication. You were flying on a clearance, and air traffic control was relying on you to do what you had read back.

The fourth step gives the general shape. Stay in VMC. Follow the last acknowledged clearance where ERSA requires it, or leave controlled airspace by the most direct route. Then land.

This is the part most likely to differ in detail between amendments, and between classes of airspace. That is why the exam asks you to extract it from ERSA, rather than recite it. Read the paragraph, then apply it to the scenario. And verify it against the current ERSA emergency section.

The logic behind it is predictability. A controller who has lost contact with you plans around the last thing you agreed to do. If you keep doing it, or leave the controlled airspace by the shortest path, they can keep other traffic clear of you.

## Arriving at a non-controlled aerodrome without a radio

At a non-controlled aerodrome, separation is entirely up to the pilots, using lookout, and radio broadcasts on the CTAF, the common traffic advisory frequency. Without a working receiver, you do not know who is in the circuit. Without a working transmitter, they do not know you are coming. So your arrival has to give you the information the radio normally would.

Make blind broadcasts as usual. Plan to overfly the aerodrome above circuit height, and check the AIP for the recommended height. From there, check the wind indicator, the runway in use, and the other traffic. Then descend on the non-active side, and join the circuit, keeping a very good lookout. Other traffic will not know you are there.

[[diagram:overhead-join]]
Picture a left-hand circuit from above. You cross overhead the aerodrome, above circuit height. You read the windsock and spot the traffic. Then you descend on the dead side, the side away from the circuit, down to circuit height. And you cross the upwind end of the runway, and join the crosswind leg at circuit height.

Each part of that has a reason. Overhead, and above the circuit, you are clear of the traffic flying it. And from there you can read the windsock and any ground signals, to find the runway in use, and see the aeroplanes already in the pattern. Descending on the non-active side, the dead side, keeps you out of their way while you lose height. Joining at circuit height, in the normal direction, puts you where other pilots expect to see aircraft. And expect no help from anyone. There is no tower, so there are no light signals.

Question. Why overfly the aerodrome above circuit height before joining, when your radio has failed?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: without the radio, you do not know the runway in use, or where the circuit traffic is. Overhead and above the circuit, you are clear of that traffic, and you can read the windsock and see the other aircraft. Then you descend on the dead side, and join where they expect you.

## Arriving at a controlled aerodrome: light signals

At a towered aerodrome, the tower can still control you without a radio, using a signal lamp. It is a focused light the tower can aim at a single aircraft, and switch between green, red and white, steady or flashing. You cannot reply on the radio, so watch the tower.

The colours follow a pattern you met in the lesson on conditions of flight. Green is permission. Red is prohibition. White sends you to a specific place. And a flash is a weaker, or preparatory, version of the steady light.

Start with an aircraft in flight. Green flashes: return for landing. Steady green: authorised to land, if you are satisfied that no collision risk exists. Steady red: give way to other aircraft, and continue circling. Red flashes: the aerodrome is unsafe, do not land. White flashes: land at this aerodrome after receiving a steady green, and then, after receiving green flashes, proceed to the apron. And a red pyrotechnic, a red flare, means: notwithstanding any previous instructions, do not land for the time being.

Now an aircraft on the ground. Steady green: authorised to take off. Steady red: stop. Green flashes: authorised to taxi. Red flashes: taxi clear of the landing area in use. And white flashes: return to the starting point on the aerodrome.

Those signals come from the AIP and the ERSA emergency section. Verify them against the current text.

Listen to the in-flight signals as a sequence. Green flashes say "come back, and set up for a landing". A steady green is the landing clearance itself. And even then, you land only if you are satisfied there is no collision risk. A steady red says "not yet: give way, and keep circling". Red flashes are stronger. The aerodrome itself is unsafe, so you do not land there at all. White flashes tell you this is where you are to land, and that you will be sent to the apron afterwards, with the steady green, and then green flashes, still to come.

To acknowledge, check the AIP for the exact wording. In flight by day, rock the wings. At night, flash the landing lights or the navigation lights. On the ground by day, move the ailerons or rudder. At night, flash the landing or navigation lights.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. It pairs green flashes and steady green as distractors for each other. In flight, green flashes mean return for landing. Only a steady green authorises the landing. On the ground, green flashes mean taxi, and a steady green means take off.

Question. You are in the circuit with a failed radio, and you see white flashes from the tower. What do they mean, and what are you still waiting for?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: land at this aerodrome. You are still waiting for a steady green before you land. And then, after landing, for green flashes before you taxi to the apron.

## Using the procedure: two scenarios

Here is the procedure applied twice, once with a tower, and once without.

Let's work the first: a radio failure inbound to a controlled aerodrome. You are VFR, inbound to Jandakot from the training area. After listening to the ATIS, you hear nothing on the tower frequency. The checks reveal nothing. What do you do?

There are five steps. First, squawk {7600|seven six zero zero}, and keep listening on the tower and ATIS frequencies. The ATIS still gives you the runway in use. Second, transmit blind your position and intentions, prefixed "{TRANSMITTING BLIND|transmitting blind}", and repeated. Third, stay clear of the control zone if ERSA directs it, or continue as ERSA permits for your situation. Verify the current ERSA paragraph for VFR in controlled airspace. Fourth, if you continue, watch the tower for light signals, and land only after a steady green. And fifth, telephone the tower after landing.

The tower sees {7600|seven six zero zero} on radar, and perhaps hears none of your calls. But it can still sequence you with the lamp. Your job is to be predictable, and to watch.

Now try the non-controlled case yourself. You are returning to Northam, a non-controlled aerodrome, from the west. Ten miles out, you make your inbound call on the CTAF, and you hear nothing at all. No replies, no other traffic. Yet it is a Saturday, and you heard three aircraft there when you left. What do you do, from here to the hangar? Pause if you'd like to work it through.
[[pause 8]]
There are seven steps. First, check: volume, squelch, with hiss present, the active frequency correct, the audio panel MIC and receive on the right COM, the plugs, the circuit breakers, then COM 2 and the hand microphone.

Second, nothing found, so squawk {7600|seven six zero zero} if a transponder is fitted. Third, keep listening on the CTAF, in case the receiver recovers, or partly works. Fourth, broadcast blind at every normal point: "Transmitting blind, Northam traffic", and so on, each call sent twice. Fifth, stay in VMC, and continue to Northam. It is the most suitable aerodrome, since you are almost there.

Sixth, overfly above circuit height, checking the AIP for the recommended height. Read the windsock, find the runway in use and the traffic, descend on the dead side, and join crosswind at circuit height, with a heightened lookout. There is no tower, so there are no light signals to expect. And seventh, after landing, telephone air traffic services if you hold a SARTIME or a flight notification, and cancel it.

The difference from the Jandakot case is who sequences you. At Northam, nobody does. So the overhead join, and your eyes, replace the CTAF entirely.

## Bringing it together

Picture the worst version. A flat battery takes both radios and the transponder at once, near Serpentine, on the way home to Jandakot. You cannot squawk {7600|seven six zero zero}, and you cannot transmit, so air traffic control has no idea. You are a silent, unidentified aeroplane.

The ERSA logic still tells you what to do. Stay in VMC. Do not enter controlled airspace, unless ERSA permits it for your situation. Choose the most suitable aerodrome. Perhaps that is Serpentine itself, non-controlled and right below you. Overfly, and join from the dead side, with your eyes working hard. Land. And telephone, so that nobody starts looking for you.

Question. Why does the procedure have you both listen out and transmit blind, when at least one of them is probably useless?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: from the cockpit, you cannot tell whether the receiver or the transmitter has failed. Doing both guarantees that whichever half still works is used. Listening catches the ATIS and any instructions. Transmitting tells others where you are.

## Key points

Let's recap.

The procedure is in the ERSA emergency section, the pink pages. Pick the paragraph for your flight rules and your airspace, and verify it against the current text.

Check the radio first: volume, squelch, frequency, audio panel, plugs, breakers, and COM 2.

Then: squawk {7600|seven six zero zero}. Listen, to the ATIS, the tower, and the CTAF. Transmit blind, "{TRANSMITTING BLIND|transmitting blind}", twice. Remain in VMC. Follow the ERSA procedure in controlled airspace. Land at the most suitable aerodrome. And telephone on arrival, and cancel the SARTIME.

At a non-controlled aerodrome: overfly above circuit height, descend on the dead side, and join with a heightened lookout.

At a controlled aerodrome: watch for light signals. Steady green, land. Green flashes, return for landing. Steady red, give way and keep circling. Red flashes, do not land. And rock the wings by day to acknowledge.

A final exam tip. {7600|Seven six zero zero} is radio failure, {7700|seven seven zero zero} is emergency, and {7500|seven five zero zero} is unlawful interference. Transmitting blind means you assume your transmitter may work, even though you hear nothing. A steady green to an aircraft in flight means cleared to land. Flashing green means return for landing. Expect a scenario, or an ERSA extract. Decide which paragraph applies: VFR or IFR, inside or outside controlled airspace. Then read it literally. And verify the procedure and the light signals against the current ERSA emergency section before the exam.
