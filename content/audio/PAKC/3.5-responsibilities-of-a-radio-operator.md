This is PAKC 3.5, Responsibilities of an aeronautical radio operator. By the end you will know what you may and may not do with the messages you overhear, which transmissions you have no right to make, and the reason behind each rule. There are a few questions along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (PAKC 3.5)

The exam wants two things from you, both about your duties as a radio operator. First, what you must do about the secrecy of the communications you hear. And second, what you must do about transmissions you are not authorised to make.

## Why this matters

The first time you press the transmit button on a training flight out of Jandakot, you become a radio operator. Your duties are set by Commonwealth law, as well as by CASA. Nobody checks those duties at the holding point. They live in how you behave on the frequency, every day.

You will overhear a great deal. Other pilots' mistakes. Company messages. A controller sorting out someone's lost clearance. And you will be tempted to fill the air, with a cheery "thanks, see you" to the tower, or a chat with a mate on the CTAF at Murrayfield.

This lesson explains what you may and may not do with what you hear, and what you may and may not put on the air, and why each rule exists. The exam tests it with short scenario questions, where one answer is the courteous-sounding wrong one. So the reasons matter more than the lists.

## Who counts as a radio operator

Anyone who transmits on an aeronautical frequency is acting as an aeronautical radio operator. And the law treats that as a privilege you must hold.

There are two ways to hold it. A Part 61 flight crew licence holder may operate the aircraft radio as part of the licence privileges, subject to the English language proficiency requirement. A person who is not a licensed pilot needs an aeronautical radio operator certificate. Check CASR Part 61 for the current wording.

The English language requirement exists for a plain reason. The aeronautical band is the one place where pilots and controllers of every background must understand one another. Quickly, under pressure, with no chance to look up a word. The privilege is tied to being able to do that.

Either way, using the aeronautical band brings legal duties, from two sources. The first is the Radiocommunications Act {1992|nineteen ninety-two}. That is Commonwealth law, governing all use of the radio spectrum in Australia, aviation included. It is where secrecy and unauthorised transmissions become legal obligations, rather than good manners. The second is AIP GEN 3.4, which sets out the radiotelephony procedures: how calls are made, the standard words, and the discipline expected on the frequency.

Question. Your friend in the right seat is not a pilot, and wants to make the inbound call at Northam. May they?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: not unless they hold an aeronautical radio operator certificate, or a flight crew licence that includes the radio privilege. Transmitting on the aeronautical band is a privilege that has to be held. It does not come with sitting in an aircraft. Check CASR Part 61 for the current wording.

## Aeronautical radio is a safety service

Before the two rules themselves, here is one idea that explains both. Aeronautical radio is a safety service, not a public broadcast. It exists to keep aircraft apart, and to get help to those in trouble. Everything on it is either safety information, or the routine traffic that supports safe operations. And every second of air time belongs to that purpose.

Two consequences follow. First, a message is meant for the station it is addressed to. The people who happen to hear it have no right to use it for anything else. That is the secrecy of communications.

Second, a frequency is a shared resource that only one station can use at a time. Aviation VHF voice is simplex: while one station transmits, nobody else can be heard. You'll find more on that in the lesson on radiotelephone controls. So anything that does not need to be said costs everyone else air time. And anything false or misleading poisons the picture that every pilot builds from what they hear. That is the basis of the rules on unauthorised transmissions.

## Secrecy of communications (3.5.1 a)

Imagine you are cruising at 3,500 ft south of Serpentine, listening on the area frequency. You hear an exchange between another aircraft and the controller about that pilot's airspace infringement, with their call-sign, and a request to phone after landing. You know the aircraft. It belongs to a rival school. The thought crosses your mind that this would make a good story at the club.

It would also break the law. A radio receiver lets you hear everything on the frequency. But hearing a message does not make it yours.

So what is the duty? As AIP GEN 3.4 and the Radiocommunications Act frame it, it comes down to three things. First, do not divulge or publish the content of a message you overhear that was not intended for you. Divulge means telling anyone. Publish means putting it out to the public. Second, do not use it for personal gain. Information overheard on the frequency is not yours to profit from. And third, do not record and broadcast or post aviation communications in a way that breaches the legislation. Check the Radiocommunications Act for the current detail.

The word secrecy can mislead you into thinking you must not listen. That is not the rule. You are expected to listen, because a listening watch is how you build your picture of traffic. The duty is about what you do with what you hear. You may hear. You may not pass on.

Why does the rule exist? Pilots and controllers need to speak plainly. A pilot reporting a problem, admitting a mistake or asking for help must not hesitate because the call might end up on social media, or in a newspaper. If every transmission were public property, people would start guarding their words. And a frequency where people guard their words is a frequency where problems are reported late. The secrecy duty protects the candour that safety depends on. Some traffic also concerns people's private affairs, and the people involved did not choose to have their business broadcast.

Now, what secrecy does not stop. It does not stop you acting on information relevant to safety. Much of what you hear is addressed to you, even though your call-sign is not in it. There are three cases.

The first is broadcasts to all stations. A CTAF call that begins "Northam traffic" is addressed to every aircraft in the area. Its whole purpose is that you hear it and act on it. Using it to plan your join is exactly what it is for.

The second is traffic you need to avoid. If you hear a controller give another aircraft a clearance that puts it near you, that is information you may, and should, use for your own safety.

And the third is distress traffic. If you hear a distress message, you should relay it, if the station it was addressed to does not appear to have heard it. Secrecy never stops you helping an aircraft in danger. The words for relaying are in the lesson on distress and emergency messages.

So the line runs between two things. Using information for the safety purpose it was sent for is allowed, and often required. Spreading it beyond the frequency, for any other purpose, is not.

Picture that line drawn down the middle of a page. On the allowed side: you may, and should, listen. And you may use a message for the safety purpose it was sent for. Acting on broadcasts to all stations, avoiding traffic you hear about, and relaying a distress call that has not been answered. Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Secrecy does not mean "do not listen". And it does not mean "ignore what is not addressed to you". You must keep a listening watch, act on broadcasts to all stations, and relay distress traffic. What you must not do is divulge, publish or profit from a message that was not intended for you.

Question. On the Jandakot tower frequency, you hear the controller tell another pilot they landed without a clearance. After landing, a friend asks what all that was about. What may you say?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: nothing about the content of that exchange. It was not addressed to you, and it had no safety purpose for you once you were on the ground. So passing it on, or posting it, would breach the secrecy duty. You may of course have used any traffic information in it while you were flying.

## Unauthorised transmissions (3.5.1 b)

Now turn from listening to talking. A transmission is unauthorised when it is one the operator has no right to make. That can be because of its content, because of the frequency it is made on, because it carries no identification, or because the equipment is not approved. The lesson lists six kinds. Let's take them one at a time, each with its reason.

The first kind is unnecessary or superfluous messages. Chatting, jokes, "thanks a lot", and repeated radio checks.

This is the one most pilots break without thinking, because it feels polite. A tower controller at Jandakot on a busy Saturday may be working a dozen aircraft on one frequency. Each "thanks very much, have a good day" takes two or three seconds. During those seconds, nobody else can be heard. If one of those blocked calls was "entering and rolling", or "going around", the courtesy has cost safety. The correct way to say thanks is a clean readback, or a short acknowledgement with your call-sign. Then silence.

Repeated radio checks are the same. One radio check before departure, made properly, tells you what you need to know. That is in the lesson on the routine pre-flight radio test. Asking again on every frequency change adds nothing.

The second kind is profane, obscene or offensive language. The aeronautical band reaches every receiver in range. That includes ground stations, other aircraft, and anyone with a scanner. It is a public utility used by professionals, and the law treats it that way.

The third kind is false or misleading messages. And above all, never a false distress or urgency signal.

A false position report, or a misleading intention, puts a wrong picture into every listener's head. And other pilots plan their separation around it. A false MAYDAY, or a false PAN PAN, is worse. It launches search and rescue resources: aircraft, crews, rescue coordination staff, and sometimes police and volunteers. While they are chasing a hoax, they are not available for a real emergency. And they themselves take risks to respond. That is why a false distress signal is an offence, not just bad practice.

A genuine distress call that turns out to be unnecessary is a different thing entirely. If you declare a MAYDAY for an engine failure, and the engine then recovers, you cancel it, and nobody blames you. The rule is against falsehood, not against caution.

The fourth kind is messages without identification. Every transmission must include your call-sign. A transmission without one is unauthorised, because the listener cannot tell who it is from, cannot reply, and cannot judge whether it applies to them. An anonymous "turning base" on a busy CTAF could be any of three aeroplanes. And every pilot in the circuit has to guess.

The fifth kind is using a frequency that is not appropriate to the purpose. The lesson's examples are the clearest. Using {121.5|one two one decimal five} MHz for routine chat. Or using a CTAF for company messages.

Each frequency is reserved for a job, and other people are listening on it for that job. {121.5|One two one decimal five} MHz is the international emergency frequency. Aircraft in trouble call on it, and many aircraft and ground stations monitor it, so that a distress call is heard. Chatting on it buries the call it exists for. A CTAF is the frequency every aircraft near that aerodrome uses to keep track of the others. So a long company message about fuel orders or passenger arrangements blocks the position reports that keep people apart.

And the sixth kind is unapproved equipment. Do not transmit from a radio that is not approved or licensed for aeronautical use. An approved aircraft radio is built to transmit on the exact frequency selected, with the right power and a clean signal. An unapproved handheld, or a modified set, can drift off frequency, splatter onto neighbouring channels, or interfere with navigation aids.

Let's work one. You are in the Northam circuit, on the CTAF, with two other aircraft. Here are five possible transmissions. For each one, decide whether you may make it, before you hear the verdict.

The first: "Northam traffic, Cessna Alpha Bravo Charlie, joining crosswind runway {23|two three}, Northam." That is authorised. It is a broadcast to all stations, identified, on the right frequency, for a safety purpose.

The second, on short final: "Thanks for the heads-up, mate, nice day for it." That is unauthorised. It is unnecessary and superfluous, and it blocks the frequency at the busiest point of the circuit.

The third: "Northam traffic, turning base runway {23|two three}, Northam." That is unauthorised as made. There is no call-sign, so nobody knows which of the three aircraft is turning base. Adding the type and call-sign makes it a correct call.

The fourth: a message to your school, asking them to have the fuel truck ready, sent on the CTAF. Unauthorised. It is a company message, on a frequency reserved for traffic separation.

And the fifth: the same fuel request sent on {121.5|one two one decimal five} MHz, "because nobody else is using it". Unauthorised. The emergency frequency is reserved for emergencies, and somebody may need it at any moment.

The test for each is the same. Is it necessary, identified, truthful, on the right frequency, and from approved equipment?

Picture that test as a row of six gates, one after another, that a call must pass through before you press the PTT, the push-to-talk button. Is it necessary? Is it in clean language? Is it true? Is it identified with your call-sign? Is it on the right frequency for its purpose? And is it from an approved radio? One "no" at any gate makes the call unauthorised. And notice that the polite "thanks" fails at the very first gate. A call that passes every gate is made once, short, and in standard words.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. It likes the polite option. "Thank you, tower, have a good afternoon" sounds like good airmanship. But it is an unnecessary transmission. Courtesy on the radio is brevity. A correct readback with your call-sign is all the thanks a controller needs.

Question. Why is a false MAYDAY treated as an offence, rather than merely poor practice?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because it launches real search and rescue resources. Those are then not available for a genuine emergency, and their crews take risks to respond. A false distress signal costs safety elsewhere, so the law treats it as an offence.

## Good practice that follows from the same duty

The rules say what you must not do. Good radio practice follows from the same two ideas, a shared frequency and a safety purpose, and it makes it easy to stay inside them. There are four habits.

First, listen first. Before you press the PTT, listen long enough to know that the frequency is clear, and that you are not about to answer a question that was asked of someone else. Never cut across a transmission in progress. On a simplex channel, two stations transmitting together produce a squeal, and both messages are lost.

Second, never transmit over distress traffic. Once a MAYDAY is on the air, that frequency belongs to the aircraft in distress, and to the station helping it. Anything else you have to say can wait, or go elsewhere. The controlling station can impose silence formally, as the lesson on distress and emergency messages explains. But you should not need to be told.

Third, plan the message before you press the PTT. Know what you will say, in standard phraseology, in the standard order. The standard phraseology in AIP GEN 3.4 exists so that every listener can decode your call instantly. The CTAF broadcast format, from the lesson on reporting position in the circuit, is a good example of how much a short, ordered call can carry.

And fourth, keep messages short, and use the minimum power and time necessary. The band is shared. Short transmissions leave gaps for others, including the pilot who needs to say something urgent.

Let's work one: planning a call before pressing the PTT. You are approaching 10 miles south of Northam at 2,500 ft, returning from the training area, and the inbound call is due. The CTAF is busy. How do you go about it?

There are five steps. First, listen for a few seconds. Two aircraft are in the circuit on runway {23|two three}, and the frequency is now quiet. Second, plan the call in the standard order: location traffic, type, call-sign, position, level, intentions, location. Third, check it against the rules. It is necessary, because an inbound call by 10 nm is recommended. It is identified, it is truthful, and it is on the CTAF, where the traffic is. Fourth, transmit once, cleanly. "Northam traffic, Cessna {172|one seven two} Alpha Bravo Charlie, 10 miles south, 2,500, inbound, estimate circuit at 25, Northam." And fifth, release the PTT, and listen again.

The call is short, standard and complete. So nobody needs to ask you to repeat it, and the frequency is free again within seconds.

Question. You are about to report downwind at Jandakot when you hear a MAYDAY from another aircraft on the tower frequency. What do you do with your downwind call?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: hold it. A frequency carrying distress traffic belongs to the aircraft in distress and the station helping it, so you do not transmit over it. Keep flying your circuit safely, and keep listening. Make your call only when the frequency is free, and it will not interfere.

## Bringing it together

It is a Saturday solo from Jandakot to the training area and back. At the holding point, you listen to the tower before calling, and your call is planned and identified.

Over the scarp, you hear a company aircraft discussing a passenger's missed connection with its base. That is none of your business, and it stays that way.

You notice another student in the area chatting with a friend on the area frequency about lunch plans. That is the unnecessary transmission the rules forbid, and you do not join in.

On the way back, a pilot on the frequency reports smoke in the cockpit, with a MAYDAY. You stop transmitting, keep listening, and hear Centre answer. Had Centre not answered, you would have relayed it.

After landing, you resist the temptation to post a recording of the drama.

Every choice in that flight came from two ideas. Messages belong to the station they were meant for. And the air time belongs to safety.

## Key points

Let's recap.

Who may transmit: a licence holder, subject to English language proficiency, or a person with a radio operator certificate. The duties come from the Radiocommunications Act {1992|nineteen ninety-two}, and AIP GEN 3.4.

Secrecy: you may hear, but you must not divulge, publish or profit from a message not intended for you. You may act on safety information and on broadcasts to all stations. And you should relay distress.

Unauthorised transmissions: nothing unnecessary. Nothing profane. Nothing false or misleading, and above all no false distress or urgency signal, which is an offence. Nothing without your call-sign. Nothing on the wrong frequency: {121.5|one two one decimal five} MHz is not for chat, and a CTAF is not for company messages. And nothing from an unapproved radio.

And good practice: listen first, never transmit over distress traffic, plan the message, and keep it short.

A final exam tip. Secrecy means you may hear, but you may not pass on, messages not addressed to you. The exception is safety: distress and urgency traffic, and broadcasts addressed to all stations. For unauthorised transmissions, look for the option that is unnecessary, unidentified, false, or on the wrong frequency. And be wary of the polite-sounding distractor: "thanks a lot" is superfluous. A false distress signal is an offence. Check the current Radiocommunications Act and AIP GEN 3.4 wording before the exam.

That's the end of PAKC 3.5.
