This is PAKC 2.1, Piston engines, at private pilot level. By the end you will be able to say what full throttle height is and why it changes with the power you choose, work out how much power the engine has left on a hot day, say where best power and best economy sit on the mixture scale, and explain how a turbocharged engine differs from a normally aspirated one. There are worked examples and a few questions along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (PAKC 2.1)

The exam wants three things from you. First, what full throttle height means. Second, how rising altitude and rising temperature change the power an engine can make. And third, how engine performance depends on the strength of the mixture, and on density height and altitude, both for a normally aspirated engine and for a turbocharged or supercharged one.

## Why this matters

As a private pilot you will start flying further and higher. Jandakot to Kalgoorlie at 8,500 ft, to stay above the afternoon bumps. Or over the Darling Scarp to a station strip in the Wheatbelt, on a 40 degree February day. Both flights ask the engine for power in air it was never rated in. And the throttle can no longer give you whatever you ask for.

At some height the throttle is fully open, and the power you planned on is simply not available. On a hot day that height arrives much lower than the book suggests. And if you hire a turbocharged aeroplane, the rules change again.

You met the engine itself in the lesson on piston engine aircraft, and the mixture control in the RPLA lesson on the piston engine. This lesson adds the altitude picture. The exam asks what full throttle height is, and why it changes with the power setting. How density height eats engine power. Where best power and best economy sit on the mixture scale. And how a turbocharged engine differs from a normally aspirated one.

## An engine is an air pump

Start with what you already know. On every induction stroke the piston moves down, and the cylinder fills with a fuel and air mixture. That mixture is then compressed, burnt and exhausted.

The fuel is never the limit. The carburettor or the injector can supply as much fuel as you like. What limits each power stroke is how much air gets into the cylinder, measured by mass. That is because the oxygen in that air is what burns the fuel. Twice the mass of air means you can burn twice the fuel, and make roughly twice the power.

So the question, how much power can this engine make, is really a different question. How many kilograms of air can it pump through itself each minute? At a given RPM, the cylinders swallow a fixed volume on each stroke. The mass in that volume depends on the density of the air arriving at the intake.

Everything in this lesson follows from that one idea. Anything that lowers the density of the air reaching the cylinders lowers the power the engine can make.

Between the outside air and the cylinders sits the throttle butterfly. It is a disc in the intake, which the throttle lever opens and closes. When it is partly closed, it restricts the flow. So the pressure in the intake pipe downstream of it, the manifold pressure, is lower than the outside air pressure.

Many PPL aeroplanes, especially those with a constant-speed propeller, have a manifold pressure gauge. It reads this pressure in inches of mercury. It is the most direct measure you have of how much air is being offered to the cylinders.

With the engine stopped there is no flow, so nothing restricts it, and the gauge reads the outside air pressure. That is about 30 inches at a sea-level aerodrome on a standard day. 29.92 inches is the same pressure as {1013.25 hPa|ten thirteen point two five hectopascals}.

Question. With the engine stopped on the ground, what should the manifold pressure gauge read, and why?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: about the outside air pressure, close to 30 inches at a sea-level aerodrome. With no air flowing, there is no pressure drop across the throttle butterfly, so the intake pipe is at the outside pressure. A gauge reading well below that is faulty.

## What altitude and temperature do to power

Climb away from Jandakot, and the air around you thins. Low down, the pressure falls by about 1 hPa for every 30 ft. That is roughly 1 inch of mercury every 1,000 ft. And the density falls with it.

Each cylinder still swallows the same volume per stroke. But that volume now holds less air by mass. So it can burn less fuel, and the power stroke is weaker. A normally aspirated engine is one that relies on atmospheric pressure alone to push air into the cylinders. It loses power steadily from the moment it leaves the ground.

Temperature does the same thing by a different route. Warm air at the same pressure is less dense than cool air, because its molecules are moving faster and spread further apart. On a 38 degree afternoon, the air at the aerodrome holds noticeably less oxygen per litre than on a 15 degree morning at the same pressure.

The engine cannot tell the difference between thin air caused by height and thin air caused by heat. It responds only to density. Humid air costs a little more again, because water vapour displaces some of the oxygen-carrying air. But that effect is small next to height and temperature.

The single number that combines all of this is density height. It is the height in the International Standard Atmosphere at which the air would be as dense as the air the engine is breathing now. You learnt to calculate it in the lesson on take-off and landing performance, from the pressure height and the temperature. An engine at a density height of 5,000 ft makes the same power whether it is at 5,000 ft on a standard day, or at a Wheatbelt strip on a scorching afternoon.

[[diagram:power-loss-with-altitude]]
Picture a graph with height running up the side, and the power available at full throttle running across, as a percentage of sea-level power. The line starts at 100 percent on the ground, and slopes steadily back as height increases. It loses roughly 3 percent for every 1,000 ft. By about 7,000 to 8,000 ft, full throttle gives only about 75 percent.

So the rule of thumb for a normally aspirated engine is a loss of about 3 percent of sea-level power for every 1,000 ft of density height. It is a rule of thumb, not a law. Your POH cruise tables give the real figures for your engine. But it is close enough to show how fast power disappears. Roughly 75 percent is all that is left at about 8,000 ft. And about 55 percent at 15,000 ft.

Picture setting a pressure and a temperature, and watching the density height move. Low QNH and heat both push the density height up. And heat is the bigger of the two.

Let's work one: the power available on a hot afternoon. A Wheatbelt strip has an elevation of 1,000 ft. The QNH is {1008 hPa|ten oh eight hectopascals}, and the temperature is 36 degrees. Use the rule of thumb, 3 percent of power lost per 1,000 ft of density height. How much power does the engine have at full throttle? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 8]]
Step one, the pressure height. {1013|ten thirteen} minus {1008|ten oh eight}, times 30, is 150 ft. So the pressure height is 1,000 plus 150, which is 1,150 ft.

Step two, the standard temperature at 1,150 ft. That is 15, minus 2 times 1.15, which is 12.7 degrees.

Step three, the deviation from standard. 36 minus 12.7 is 23.3 degrees above.

Step four, the density height. 120 times 23.3 is 2,796 ft. Add the 1,150, and the density height is 3,946 ft. Call it 3,950 ft.

Step five, the power. 3 percent times 3.95 is 11.9 percent. 100 minus 11.9 leaves about 88 percent.

So on the take-off roll, with the throttle wide open, the engine is making about seven-eighths of its rated power before you have left the ground. The take-off run is longer, and the climb shallower. And both get worse as you climb. The answer once more: about 88 percent.

Now one for you to try. You are cruising at a pressure height of 9,500 ft, and the outside air temperature is plus 10 degrees. Roughly what percentage of sea-level power can the engine make at full throttle?
[[pause 8]]
The standard temperature at 9,500 ft is 15, minus 2 times 9.5, which is minus 4 degrees. So plus 10 is 14 degrees warmer than standard. The density height is 9,500 plus 120 times 14. That is 9,500 plus 1,680, which is 11,180 ft. The power is about 100, minus 3 times 11.18. That is 100 minus 33.5, so about 66 percent. If your flight plan assumed a 75 percent cruise, the engine cannot deliver it here.

Temperature has a second effect that the 3 percent rule does not show. An engine is cooled by air flowing over its cylinder fins, and by the oil. On a hot day the cooling air is hotter. So for the same power, the cylinders and the oil run hotter, and the margin before the cylinder head temperature and oil temperature limits shrinks.

In a long climb on a hot day, the engine is making less power, and is closer to its temperature limits, at the same time. That is why the POH may call for a faster, shallower cruise climb on hot days. You get more airflow over the engine, for a little less climb rate.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. It describes two aerodromes, a high one on a cool day and a low one on a hot day, and asks which gives better engine performance. Do not compare elevations. Work out, or estimate, the density height of each. The one with the lower density height wins, whatever its elevation.

Question. Why does a hot day cost engine power, when the pressure is the same as on a cool day?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: warm air is less dense than cool air at the same pressure. So each cylinderful holds less air by mass, can burn less fuel, and makes a weaker power stroke. The engine responds to density height, and heat raises density height.

## Full throttle height

Picture a climb out of Jandakot towards your cruising level. You are in an aeroplane with a constant-speed propeller and a manifold pressure gauge. The POH cruise table says that 65 percent power at your chosen RPM needs, say, 24 inches of manifold pressure at sea level. You set it.

As you climb, the outside pressure falls about an inch per 1,000 ft. So the manifold pressure falls too, and the power with it. To hold 65 percent, you open the throttle a little more every few hundred feet. That lets more of the thinner air through the butterfly, so that the cylinders keep getting the same mass of air.

That cannot go on for ever. At some height the throttle reaches its stop. The butterfly is fully open, there is no restriction left to remove, and the engine is only just making 65 percent. That height is the full throttle height for 65 percent power.

Above it, the manifold pressure and the power can only fall. They fall at about an inch, and 3 percent, per 1,000 ft of density height, because there is no more throttle to give.

Most trainers have a fixed-pitch propeller and no manifold pressure gauge. There you see the same thing on the tachometer instead. Above full throttle height, the RPM sags in the cruise with the throttle wide open, and nothing you do with the throttle brings it back.

Three consequences follow, and the exam uses all of them.

The first: full throttle height belongs to a power setting. Each power setting has its own. A lower power needs less air, so the throttle can stay partly closed, with a reserve to open, up to a greater height. The full throttle height for 65 percent is higher than the full throttle height for 75 percent. And the full throttle height for 100 percent is sea level, in density height terms, because at sea level full power already needs a wide-open throttle.

The second: full throttle height is a density height. The engine responds to density, so the full throttle height for any power is reached at a lower pressure height on a hot day than on a standard day. On a hot day, the power you planned may run out a couple of thousand feet below where the POH table says it should. That table is drawn for the standard atmosphere.

And the third: above full throttle height, the cruise power is whatever full throttle gives. Light aeroplanes are routinely cruised at full throttle at altitude. It does the engine no harm, because the thin air limits the power to well below its rating. You control the power by your choice of altitude and RPM, and by leaning. And the POH cruise table tells you what you will get.

Let's work one: the full throttle height for 65 percent on a hot day. The engine is normally aspirated, and the rule of thumb is 3 percent of power lost per 1,000 ft of density height. Today the air at cruising levels is 15 degrees warmer than standard. At what pressure height does the throttle reach its stop? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 8]]
Step one, the power that may be lost before 65 percent is all that is left. 100 minus 65 is 35 percent.

Step two, the density height at which that happens. 35 divided by 3 is 11.7. So about 11,700 ft density height.

Step three, the height the hot air adds. 120 times 15 is 1,800 ft.

Step four, the pressure height at full throttle height. 11,700 minus 1,800 is 9,900 ft.

On a standard day you could hold 65 percent up to about 11,700 ft. Today the throttle hits the stop at about 9,900 ft, on the altimeter set to {1013|ten thirteen}. So plan a lower cruise level, or accept less power and a slower cruise. The answer once more: about 9,900 ft.

Now try it the other way round. On a standard day, about what density height is the full throttle height for 75 percent power? And is it above or below the full throttle height for 65 percent?
[[pause 6]]
For 75 percent, the engine may lose 100 minus 75, which is 25 percent. 25 divided by 3 is 8.3, so about 8,300 ft. A typical trainer's POH puts it at about 7,000 to 8,000 ft. That is below the 11,700 ft for 65 percent. The higher the power setting, the lower its full throttle height.

Question. Why is the full throttle height for 65 percent power higher than for 75 percent?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: 65 percent needs less air, so at any height the throttle needs to be open less far for it. You keep a reserve of throttle up to a greater height, before the butterfly is fully open and the reserve is gone.

## Mixture strength and power

The mixture control has already been taught as a handling skill in the RPLA lesson on the piston engine. Lean in the cruise by watching the RPM. Go full rich for the descent and landing. The PPL question is a performance one. How does the strength of the mixture change the power and the economy you get?

Start with the chemically correct ratio, and why the engine is not run at it. Petrol and air burn completely, with no fuel and no oxygen left over, at a ratio of about 1 part fuel to 15 parts air, by weight. That is the chemically correct mixture, also called the stoichiometric mixture. It is natural to assume the engine makes its best power there. It does not, quite.

Best power comes from a mixture slightly richer than chemically correct. With a little surplus fuel, every molecule of oxygen in the cylinder finds fuel to burn quickly. The burn is fast and complete. And the extra fuel evaporating in the cylinder cools the charge, so more of it can be packed in. You get the most power for a given throttle setting.

Best economy comes from a mixture slightly leaner than chemically correct. With a surplus of air, every drop of fuel finds oxygen and burns completely. Less power comes out of each stroke, but more of it per litre of fuel. You get the most kilometres per litre and the longest endurance, at a small cost in speed.

[[diagram:mixture-rich-to-lean]]
Picture a graph with the mixture running from full rich on the left to lean on the right. Engine power is a hump. It peaks a little rich of the correct mixture, and falls away on both sides. On the rich side, to the left, the engine runs cool, burns a lot of fuel, blows black smoke and fouls its plugs. On the lean side, to the right, the cylinder head and exhaust gas temperatures climb, and detonation becomes a risk.

Either side of that working band, performance falls away. Too rich wastes fuel, which goes out of the exhaust unburnt. It lowers the power, fouls the spark plugs with soot, and shortens your endurance.

Too lean slows the burn and lowers the power. It raises the cylinder head and exhaust gas temperatures. And at high power it invites detonation, where the last part of the charge explodes instead of burning smoothly, and hammers the piston. Further lean still, the engine runs rough and stops.

[[diagram:detonation-vs-normal-combustion]]
Picture two cylinders side by side. On the left, normal combustion. A smooth flame front spreads out from the spark plug, and the pressure rises smoothly. On the right, detonation. After the spark, the rest of the charge explodes and hammers the piston, and the pressure shows a sharp spike. The causes are a mixture too lean at high power, high manifold pressure with low RPM, fuel of too low an octane, and a high cylinder head temperature. The effects are rough running, a sharp rise in cylinder head temperature, a loss of power, and damage within minutes.

Now, why altitude makes the mixture rich. The carburettor meters fuel by the volume of air flowing through its venturi. That is because the pressure drop that draws fuel from the jet depends on the speed of the airflow, not on the air's density. As you climb, each litre of air through the venturi weighs less, but it draws roughly the same fuel. So the mixture gets richer with height.

Left at full rich, the engine at 8,000 ft is well over-rich. It is down on power, burning fuel it does not need, and fouling its plugs. Leaning restores the correct ratio for the thinner air. That is why leaning gives back power, as well as saving fuel.

[[diagram:carburettor-cross-section]]
Picture a carburettor cut in half, with air entering at the bottom and flowing up through a narrow throat, the venturi, where it speeds up and its pressure drops. Off to one side is the float chamber, where a float and a needle valve hold the fuel at a constant level. The low pressure in the venturi draws fuel from that chamber, through the main jet, and out of a nozzle into the airflow. Above the venturi sits the throttle butterfly. The point is that the venturi draws fuel in proportion to the volume of air passing. So thinner air gets the same fuel, and the mixture goes rich.

That also explains the one exception to full rich for take-off. At a hot, high strip, the air is so thin that a full rich mixture is over-rich even at take-off power. The engine cannot make its best available power. So some POHs call for leaning for take-off at high density heights, usually by leaning for maximum RPM at full throttle during the run-up. Where your POH says so, do it. Where it does not, take off full rich, because the extra fuel is what keeps a hard-working engine cool.

Question. Which side of the chemically correct mixture gives best power, and which gives best economy?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: best power is slightly rich of chemically correct. The surplus fuel makes sure all the oxygen is used quickly, and it cools the charge. Best economy is slightly lean of it. The surplus air makes sure all the fuel is burnt, giving the most energy per litre, at a small cost in power.

## Density height and the two kinds of engine

The final element asks you to compare two engines. How does density height affect a normally aspirated engine? And how does it affect a turbocharged or supercharged one?

The mechanism of the turbocharger is the next lesson, on supercharging. Here you need only its effect. A compressor in the intake squeezes the air before it reaches the cylinders. So the engine can be given air that is denser than the air outside.

Take the normally aspirated engine first. It is at the mercy of density height from the ground up. Every 1,000 ft of density height takes about 3 percent of its power. That shows up in three phases of flight.

First, take-off. At a high or hot aerodrome, the take-off is made with less than full rated power. Combine that with the thinner air the wing and propeller are working in, and the run is longer.

Second, the climb. Climb performance falls steadily. The rate of climb depends on the spare power: the power available, minus the power needed to fly level. And the power available is shrinking with every 1,000 ft. Eventually the rate of climb falls to 100 ft per minute. That is the service ceiling. Then it falls to zero, the absolute ceiling, where the engine at full throttle can only just hold the aeroplane level.

And third, the cruise. Cruise power is capped by full throttle height. Above it you cruise at full throttle, and accept whatever the thin air gives.

Now the turbocharged or supercharged engine. Add a compressor, and the picture changes. On the ground and low down, the compressor could deliver far more air than the engine can safely use. So a control, the wastegate, limits the boost to the engine's rated manifold pressure. You will meet the wastegate in the next lesson. As you climb, the control lets the compressor work harder and harder to keep that manifold pressure. And the engine goes on making its rated power, sea-level power or close to it, whatever the density height outside.

That continues up to the critical altitude. That is the highest altitude at which the compressor, working flat out with the throttle fully open, can still hold the rated manifold pressure. For a light aeroplane turbocharger it is typically 12,000 to 20,000 ft. Above the critical altitude, the compressor has nothing left to give. From there the power falls with density height, just as a normally aspirated engine's does. So the critical altitude is the turbocharged engine's equivalent of full throttle height. It is the point where the reserve runs out.

Three points stop you overrating the turbocharger.

The first: the aeroplane still feels density height. The turbocharger feeds the engine, not the wing or the propeller blades. At a high density height, the wing still needs a higher true airspeed to lift off. So the take-off run is still longer than at sea level, even though the engine is making full power.

The second: compressed air is hot air. Squeezing air heats it, and hot intake air is less dense, and closer to detonation. That is one reason turbocharged engines have tighter temperature limits, and why some have an intercooler to cool the charge. On a hot day the margins are smaller still.

And the third: the mixture still needs managing. The turbocharger keeps the air dense going into the engine. But the leaning procedure, and the high-power full rich rule, still apply as the POH sets them.

So, the two engines side by side. As density height rises, the normally aspirated engine's power falls from the surface up, about 3 percent per 1,000 ft. The turbocharged engine holds rated power up to its critical altitude, and then it falls. The normally aspirated engine's reserve runs out at full throttle height, one for each power setting. The turbocharged engine's runs out at the critical altitude, for its rated manifold pressure.

At a hot, high strip, the normally aspirated engine gives less power and a longer run. The turbocharged one gives full power, but still a longer run, because the wing feels the density height. And the pilot's main job differs. In the normally aspirated aeroplane, open the throttle in the climb, and lean. In the turbocharged one, watch the manifold pressure and the temperatures, and never over-boost.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. A distractor says a turbocharged engine is unaffected by altitude. It is unaffected only up to its critical altitude. Above that, it loses power like any other engine. And the aeroplane's take-off and landing distances still grow with density height, because the wing needs a higher true airspeed in thin air.

Question. What is the critical altitude of a turbocharged engine, and how does it relate to full throttle height?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: it is the highest altitude at which the turbocharger, at full throttle, can still maintain the rated manifold pressure. Like full throttle height, it marks the point where the reserve is used up. Above it, power falls with density height, as in a normally aspirated engine.

## Bringing it together

You are planning Jandakot to Kalgoorlie on a February afternoon, in a normally aspirated aeroplane. The forecast is 15 degrees above standard at cruising levels, and the POH cruise table assumes 75 percent power.

The rule of thumb puts the full throttle height for 75 percent at about 8,300 ft density height on a standard day. Today, 15 degrees of extra heat adds 1,800 ft. So the throttle reaches its stop at about 6,500 ft pressure height.

At 8,500 ft you will be making well under 75 percent. So your true airspeed and your fuel flow will both be lower than the 75 percent row shows. Your timings should come from the row for the power you will actually get.

Leaned correctly in the cruise, you get best power or best economy, as you choose. Left at full rich, you get neither.

A friend's turbocharged aeroplane would hold its planned power all the way to 8,500 ft and beyond. But it would still need a longer take-off run out of Kalgoorlie on the way home, because its wing feels the heat just as yours does.

## Key points

Let's recap.

Full throttle height is the height at which the throttle is fully open, and the engine is only just making the selected power. Each power setting has its own. A lower power setting has a higher full throttle height. And on a hot day, every full throttle height arrives lower.

Power depends on the mass of air the engine pumps. Height and heat both lower the air's density, so both lower power. For a normally aspirated engine, that is about 3 percent of sea-level power per 1,000 ft of density height. Heat also reduces the cooling margins.

The mixture. Best power is slightly rich of the chemically correct ratio, which is about 1 part fuel to 15 parts air by weight. Best economy is slightly lean of it. Too rich wastes fuel and fouls the plugs. Too lean at high power overheats the engine and invites detonation. The carburettor makes the mixture richer with height, and leaning restores it.

The two kinds of engine. A normally aspirated engine loses power from the surface up. A turbocharged or supercharged engine holds rated power up to its critical altitude, typically 12,000 to 20,000 ft, then loses it like a normally aspirated one. Either way, the airframe still feels density height.

A final exam tip. Full throttle height is specific to a power setting. The full throttle height for 65 percent power is higher than for 75 percent, because less power needs less air. Have the rule of thumb ready: about 3 percent of power per 1,000 ft of density height, so about 75 percent at 8,000 ft. Know that best power is slightly rich, and best economy slightly lean. And remember that a turbocharger holds sea-level power only up to its critical altitude.
