This is PAKC 3.6, Function of the components of an aeronautical radio system. By the end you will be able to follow your voice from your lips to the tower and back, and say in one line what each part of the radio installation does, and why. There are a few questions along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (PAKC 3.6)

The exam wants you to say what each part of an aircraft radio installation does. There are eight parts. The power source and the battery master switch. The radio master, also called the avionics master. The fuses and circuit breakers. The microphone. The transmitter. The receiver. The antenna. And the headphones and speaker.

## Why this matters

When you call Jandakot Ground for taxi, your words make a journey through eight different parts before they reach the controller's ear. And the reply makes the same journey back. Each part does one job, and each can fail in its own way.

If you know what each one does, a silent radio stops being a mystery. You can work out which part has stopped doing its job. And you know which ones you can fix from the seat, and which you cannot. The fault-finding routine and the radio controls, each in its own lesson, both rest on this one.

The exam asks for the function of a named component. Most often that is the avionics master, the receiver, and the type of modulation. So you need a clear one-line answer for each, backed by the reason.

## The system as a chain

Think of the radio as a chain. On transmit, electrical power feeds the radio. The microphone turns your voice into an electrical audio signal. The transmitter turns that audio into a radio-frequency signal. And the antenna radiates it into the air.

On receive, the chain runs the other way. The antenna collects signals. The receiver picks out the one you want, and turns it back into audio. And the headset or speaker makes it audible.

Upstream of all that sits the power supply, with its switches and protection. The battery and alternator. The master switch. The avionics master. And the fuses and circuit breakers.

[[diagram:radio-system-block-diagram]]
Picture a block diagram. Along the top runs the power: the battery and the alternator feed the main bus through the master switch. The avionics master then feeds the avionics bus, and each radio has its own circuit breaker. Below that, two paths. The transmit path runs from the microphone and its press-to-talk switch, to the transmitter, to the antenna. The receive path runs from the antenna, to the receiver with its squelch, to the headset or speaker. Notice that the transmitter and receiver share one box and one antenna, but they are separate paths.

Any break in the chain stops communication. And a break anywhere looks the same from the seat: silence. That is why fault finding follows the same path.

Notice also that the transmitter and receiver usually live in the same box, the COM radio, which is called a transceiver. They share one antenna. But they are separate circuits that can fail separately. That is why, in the lesson on radio failure procedures, you transmit blind.

## Power: the battery, the alternator and the master switch (3.6.1 a)

There are two sources of electrical power. The battery stores electrical energy chemically. It supplies the large current needed for starting the engine. And it is the reserve if the alternator fails.

Once the engine runs, the alternator takes over. In older aircraft it is a generator. Driven by the engine, it generates electricity, supplies the whole electrical load, radios included, and recharges the battery.

The master switch connects the battery to the main bus. The main bus is the bar or wire from which all the aircraft's electrical circuits draw their power. Master off, the bus is dead, and so is every radio.

The function that matters most for radio is what happens when the alternator fails. The battery then carries everything alone. With the alternator failed, a light-aircraft battery may run the radios for perhaps 30 to 60 minutes. Less with heavy loads, such as lights and pitot heat. So shed non-essential load early, and check your POH for your own aircraft. The ammeter or voltmeter, and any low-voltage warning, tell you when this has happened. The lesson on fault finding works through the decision that follows.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. With the alternator failed, the radios do not stop at once. And they do not run indefinitely. They run on the battery for a limited time: roughly 30 to 60 minutes in a typical light aircraft, and less with heavy loads. That time is your POH's figure, not a fixed rule.

Question. In the cruise, the ammeter shows a discharge, and the low-voltage light is on. What is now powering the radio, and what follows from that?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the battery alone, because the alternator has stopped supplying the load. The battery is running down. So you shed non-essential equipment at once, to stretch the time the radio will keep working, and you plan to land within that time.

## The radio (avionics) master (3.6.1 b)

Between the main bus and the radios sits a second switch: the radio master, or avionics master. It is a separate switch, or relay, between the main bus and the avionics bus. The avionics bus is the circuit that feeds the radios, the navigation equipment, the audio panel and the transponder.

It has two jobs. The first is protection during engine start and shutdown. When the starter motor engages, it draws a very large current. When it releases, and when the alternator comes on or off line, the system voltage can spike or dip sharply. Modern radios are built from solid-state circuits: transistors and microprocessors that can be damaged by those transients. The avionics master lets all the radios be switched off together for engine start and shutdown, so the spike never reaches them. The rule is simple. Avionics master on after start. Off before shutdown.

The second job is one switch in an emergency. In an electrical emergency, such as smoke from behind the panel or an electrical fire, the avionics master lets the pilot remove power from all the avionics at once, without hunting for individual knobs.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. The avionics master's main purpose is to protect the avionics from voltage transients during engine start and shutdown. It does not save the battery. And it does not isolate the alternator. Those are distractors. Its second purpose is a single switch to kill all the avionics in an electrical emergency.

Question. Why is the avionics master switched off before you turn the starter?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because the starter's heavy current causes voltage spikes and dips on the bus, and the solid-state circuits in the radios can be damaged by them. With the avionics bus isolated, the transient never reaches the radios. You switch it on once the engine is running.

## Fuses and circuit breakers (3.6.1 c)

Each radio draws a modest current through its own wiring. Now suppose a wire chafes through its insulation and touches the airframe, or a component fails. A short circuit or an overload lets far more current flow than the wire was designed for. The wire heats, and it can start a fire behind the panel.

Fuses and circuit breakers protect each circuit from overload and short circuit, by breaking the circuit when the current is excessive. Each radio, and each major circuit, has its own, rated for that circuit's normal current.

A fuse is a thin link of metal that melts when too much current passes. It protects once, and must then be replaced. If your aircraft uses fuses, carry spares of the correct rating. A higher-rated fuse would let a dangerous current through before it blew.

A circuit breaker is a switch that opens itself when the current is excessive. It pops out, to show that it has tripped. And it can be reset by pushing it back in.

Reset a breaker once only. A single trip may be a momentary surge. But a breaker that trips again indicates a real fault. Holding it in, or resetting it again and again, forces current through a faulty circuit. That is the very thing the breaker exists to prevent.

Question. The COM 1 circuit breaker pops. You reset it, and two minutes later it pops again. What does that tell you, and what do you do?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: it indicates a real fault in that circuit, such as a short or an overload, not a passing surge. Leave it out, treat COM 1 as failed, and use COM 2 if one is fitted. Resetting it again risks overheating the wiring.

## The microphone and the press-to-talk switch (3.6.1 d)

Now the signal path itself, starting at your mouth. Sound is a pressure wave in the air. The microphone converts your voice, which is sound pressure, into an electrical audio signal. That is a small voltage that varies in step with the sound.

A cockpit is loud, and the engine and propeller noise reaches the microphone along with your voice. So aviation headset microphones are noise-cancelling. They respond to sound arriving close to the mouth, and they reject the engine noise from all around. That only works when the boom is positioned close to your lips. Move it away, and your voice weakens, while the cabin noise does not.

The microphone does not transmit by itself. The press-to-talk switch, the PTT, on the control yoke or stick, keys the transmitter. While it is held, the transmitter is on, and the microphone's audio goes out on the air. Release it, and the radio returns to receiving. A PTT that sticks keeps the transmitter on, and that blocks the frequency for everyone. The lesson on radiotelephone controls covers how to spot it.

## The transmitter (3.6.1 e)

The audio signal from your microphone is a low-frequency electrical wave. It runs at the same few hundred to a few thousand cycles per second as the sound of your voice. On its own, it cannot travel through the air any distance. It needs a carrier.

The transmitter generates a radio-frequency carrier on the selected frequency, and modulates it with the audio from the microphone. The carrier is a steady radio wave at, say, {118.1|one one eight decimal one} MHz. To modulate it means to vary some property of that carrier in step with the audio, so that the voice rides on the radio wave.

Aeronautical VHF voice uses amplitude modulation, AM for short. The strength of the carrier varies with the voice. Loud parts of your speech make the carrier stronger, and quiet parts make it weaker. Its frequency stays fixed.

AM has a property that matters for safety. When two stations transmit together on the same frequency, an AM receiver lets a listener hear the clash, as a squeal or a garble. So listeners know that both calls may need repeating. Receivers using FM, frequency modulation, tend to lock on to the stronger signal and suppress the weaker one. A listener might hear one clean call, and never know that a second pilot had also transmitted. With AM, the blocked call announces itself. That is a useful safety feature.

[[diagram:am-modulation]]
Picture three traces, stacked one above the other. At the top, the audio from the microphone: a slow, wandering wave. In the middle, the transmitter's carrier: a steady, fast radio wave on the selected frequency, for example {118.1|one one eight decimal one} MHz. At the bottom, the modulated result. The carrier's strength rises and falls in step with the audio, while its frequency stays fixed, so the outline of the result is the shape of your voice. And beneath it all, two stations transmitting at once on the same frequency. Their carriers clash, and an AM receiver produces a squeal, so listeners know that a call was blocked.

Question. What does the transmitter do to your voice, and why is AM useful when two pilots press the PTT at the same moment?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: it generates a radio-frequency carrier on the selected frequency, and varies the carrier's strength with your voice. That is amplitude modulation. With AM, two simultaneous transmissions are heard as a squeal or garble. So listeners know a call was blocked, and can ask for a repeat.

## The antenna (3.6.1 g)

The antenna is where electrical signals become radio waves, and back again. On transmit, it converts the transmitter's radio-frequency current into radiated electromagnetic waves. On receive, it collects passing waves, and delivers the tiny currents they induce to the receiver. A COM radio uses one antenna for both.

An antenna works best when its length is matched to the wavelength of the signal. The lesson on radio waves explains why. VHF COM antennas are typically a quarter-wavelength whip or blade, about 60 cm long at 125 MHz, mounted on top of or under the fuselage.

On the walk-around, you can match each antenna to its job. There are three kinds to find. First, the COM antennas: whips or blades on the top or bottom of the fuselage, one per COM radio. Second, the NAV antennas. Often that is a V-shaped antenna on the fin, called a cat's whisker, feeding the VOR and ILS receivers. And third, the transponder and the DME. They use short UHF stubs underneath the fuselage. They are short because their frequencies are high, and their wavelengths short.

[[diagram:aircraft-antenna-locations]]
Picture a light aeroplane from the side. On top of the fuselage is a COM antenna, a blade or whip about 60 cm long, a quarter of a wavelength. A second COM antenna sits underneath, one for each COM radio. The V-shaped cat's whisker on the fin is the NAV antenna, for the VOR and ILS receivers. And the shortest stubs, underneath, belong to the UHF transponder and DME.

The antenna is the one part of the radio system that is outside the aeroplane. A damaged, loose or ice-covered antenna reduces range. That is why the antennas are on the pre-flight walk-around. Look for cracks, bends, a loose base, or a missing whip.

## The receiver (3.6.1 f)

At any moment, the antenna is collecting a jumble of signals: every station on every frequency within range, plus electrical noise. The receiver turns that jumble into one clear voice. Its job has three parts.

First, select. It picks the wanted frequency out of all the signals the antenna collects. That is what tuning does. Second, amplify. The signal arriving from a distant station is extremely weak. And third, demodulate, to recover the audio. That means stripping the voice back off the carrier, reversing what the distant transmitter did.

The two words pair up, and the exam pairs them too. The transmitter modulates. The receiver demodulates.

The receiver has one more circuit. With no station transmitting, an amplified receiver produces a steady hiss of background noise. The squelch circuit mutes the output when no signal is present, so you hear silence between calls. And it opens when a strong enough signal arrives. Opening the squelch on purpose, to hear the hiss, is a quick test that the receiver and the audio path are alive. The lesson on fault finding explains how.

Question. What are the three things the receiver does to the signal arriving from the antenna?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: it selects the wanted frequency from everything the antenna collects. It amplifies the weak signal. And it demodulates it, to recover the audio. The squelch then mutes the output when no signal is present.

## Headphones and speaker (3.6.1 h)

The last link turns the receiver's audio back into sound. The headphones and the speaker both convert the audio signal back into sound. That is the reverse of what the microphone did.

Headphones, in practice a headset with the microphone on a boom, are far better in a noisy cockpit. They put the sound directly at your ears, and seal out much of the engine noise, so you hear the call clearly. They also give hearing protection against hours of engine noise. The cabin speaker is a back-up, if the headset fails. It is harder to hear over the engine, but it works with nothing plugged in.

[[diagram:anr-noise-cancelling]]
How does a headset cut the engine noise? Picture an ear cup in cross-section. First, passive protection. The ear seal and the shell block the noise, but only if the seal is intact. Then active noise reduction, ANR. A small microphone inside the cup hears the low-frequency engine and propeller drone. The electronics play an opposite sound wave, so the two cancel, and much less reaches the ear.

ANR headsets need their own battery or aircraft power, so check them before flight. If the ANR power fails, many still work as ordinary passive headsets. But the drone returns.

Question. Your headset fails in flight. What can you still use to hear, and to transmit?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the cabin speaker lets you hear the radio. And the hand microphone, if one is fitted, lets you transmit. Both are back-ups for exactly this failure. Selecting them may need a change on the audio panel.

## Following one call through the system

Now let's put the chain together, with a call you will make on almost every flight. The engine is running at Jandakot. You call Ground for taxi, and Ground replies. Follow the call through eight links.

First, power. The master is on, the alternator is charging, and the avionics master was switched on after start, so the start transient never reached the radios. The breakers for COM 1 and the audio panel are in.

Second, the microphone. You hold the boom close to your lips and speak. It turns your voice into an electrical audio signal, and rejects the engine noise.

Third, the PTT. Pressing it keys COM 1's transmitter.

Fourth, the transmitter. It generates a carrier on the Ground frequency, and amplitude-modulates it with your voice.

Fifth, the antenna. The blade on the fuselage radiates the signal, and Ground's antenna collects it.

Sixth, you release the PTT. Ground replies, and your antenna collects the reply.

Seventh, the receiver. It selects the Ground frequency, amplifies the weak signal, and demodulates it to recover the controller's voice. The squelch opens, because a signal is present.

And eighth, the headset turns that audio back into sound at your ears.

Eight links, each doing one job. If any one fails, the conversation stops. And the job that was not done points you at the cause.

Now try one yourself. You are listening to the Perth ATIS on COM 2 before departure, and you hear nothing at all. In signal order, what does the ATIS broadcast pass through on its way to your ears, and what does each part do? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 8]]
There are four stages. First, power: the battery or alternator, the master, the avionics master and the COM 2 breaker. They run the radio. Second, COM 2's antenna, which collects the ATIS signal from the air. Third, the COM 2 receiver. It selects the ATIS frequency, amplifies it, and demodulates it to recover the audio, and the squelch opens because a signal is present. And fourth, the headset or speaker, which turns the audio back into sound.

The microphone, the PTT and the transmitter play no part, because you are only receiving. So a silent ATIS points at power, at the COM 2 antenna, at the COM 2 receiver or its settings, or at the headset and the audio routing.

## The typical light-aircraft fit

A training aeroplane usually carries more than one radio, all built from the same blocks. Here is the pattern first. Some sets transmit and receive. Some only receive. And one replies to radar.

The COM radios, COM 1 and COM 2, transmit and receive VHF voice, from {118.000|one one eight decimal zero zero zero} to {136.975|one three six decimal nine seven five} MHz.

The NAV radios, NAV 1 and NAV 2, are the VOR and ILS receivers. They cover {108.00|one zero eight decimal zero zero} to {117.95|one one seven decimal nine five} MHz, and they receive only. The ADF receives NDBs, roughly 190 to 1,750 kHz. The NAV and ADF sets are receivers only. They have antennas and receivers, but no transmitter, because a navigation aid on the ground does the transmitting.

The transponder is both. It receives the secondary radar's interrogation, and transmits a reply with a code and the altitude.

The ELT, the emergency locator transmitter, is an emergency beacon on 406 MHz, with {121.5|one two one decimal five} MHz for homing.

And the audio panel and intercom select what you hear, and which radio transmits, and they give the crew an intercom. The audio panel is the switchboard between the headsets and all those radios. The lesson on radiotelephone controls covers it.

## Key points

Let's recap, part by part.

Power. The battery starts the engine and is the reserve. The alternator carries the load. The master connects the battery to the bus. With the alternator failed, you have perhaps 30 to 60 minutes, by your POH's figure.

The avionics master protects the radios from start and shutdown spikes. And it kills all the avionics at once.

Fuses melt, and breakers pop. Reset a breaker once only.

The microphone turns voice into audio, and it must be close to the lips. The PTT keys the transmitter.

The transmitter makes a carrier and modulates it. VHF voice is AM.

The receiver selects, amplifies and demodulates. The squelch mutes it between calls.

The antenna radiates and collects. A quarter wave, about 60 cm.

And the headset and speaker turn audio back into sound. The speaker is the back-up, and ANR needs power.

A final exam tip. Remember the reason for the avionics master: it protects the avionics from voltage transients during engine start and shutdown. The receiver demodulates. The transmitter modulates. Aeronautical VHF voice is AM. And with the alternator failed, the radios run on the battery for a limited time only, so shed load early.
