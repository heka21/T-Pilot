This is PAKC 3.9, Radiotelephone controls. By the end you will be able to picture every control between your mouth and the antenna, set up the COM radio, the audio panel and the transponder before taxi, and predict where a call will go from a given set of selections. There are a few questions along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (PAKC 3.9)

The exam wants two things from you. First, a description of the controls you use to transmit and receive on an aeronautical radio: the COM set, the press-to-talk switch, and the transponder. And second, what each audio panel selection does.

## Why this matters

A radio call that never left the aeroplane looks exactly like one that nobody answered.

Most of the radio problems a student meets at Jandakot, or on a Northam CTAF, are not faults at all. They are a knob in the wrong place. The tower frequency still in standby. The MIC selector left on COM 2 after a call to Centre. The squelch wound so tight that a weak station is cut out. Or the intercom swallowing the radio.

Each control does one job, in a chain that runs from your mouth to the antenna and back to your ears. Once you can picture that chain, you can set it up correctly before taxi, and find the fault in seconds when something goes quiet. The exam asks you to describe the controls, and to predict what happens with a given set of selections, especially on the audio panel.

## The chain from your mouth to the antenna

Start with what has to happen when you speak. Your voice goes into the microphone on your headset, which turns it into an electrical signal. That signal goes to the audio panel, a switchboard between the headsets and the radios. The audio panel sends it to whichever COM radio, the communication radio, is selected to transmit. The COM radio is keyed by the press-to-talk switch, the PTT. It turns the voice into a radio-frequency signal on the selected frequency, and feeds it to its antenna, which radiates it.

Receiving runs the same chain backwards. The antenna picks up signals. The COM radio tuned to the frequency extracts the voice. And the audio panel passes that audio to your headset, or to the cabin speaker, if you have selected that radio for listening.

Two consequences follow, and they explain most of this lesson.

First, every link has a control, and every control can be set wrong. Silence tells you that a link is broken. It does not tell you which one.

And second, aviation VHF voice is simplex. One frequency carries both directions, and only one station can usefully transmit at a time. While your PTT is pressed, your own receiver on that frequency is silent, so you cannot hear anyone else. And if two stations transmit together, listeners hear a squeal or a garble, or only the stronger one.

Question. Why can't you hear another station that transmits while you are holding the PTT down?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: aviation VHF voice is simplex. One frequency carries both directions, and while your transmitter is keyed, your own receiver on that frequency is muted. Two stations transmitting at once also block each other for everyone else listening.

## The COM radio face

A typical light-aircraft COM radio has the same handful of controls. It does not matter whether it is a stand-alone set, or the COM half of a combined COM and NAV unit, or of a GPS unit. Your POH supplement, in section 9, describes your exact unit. The functions are common to all of them.

There are seven controls. The on-off and volume knob powers the radio, and sets the audio level. The squelch mutes background noise when no signal is present. The frequency selector sets the frequency in the standby window. The flip-flop button swaps the standby frequency into the active window. Memory recall gives stored frequencies, on some units. The TX indicator shows when the transmitter is keyed. And many radios recall {121.5|one two one decimal five}, the emergency frequency, with a long press of the flip-flop. Now, why each control is there.

Start with the volume. The volume knob, usually combined with the on-off switch, sets how loud the received audio is before it reaches the audio panel. On many sets it is the only on-off switch. So "volume fully down" and "radio off" can be one click apart. If you hear nothing, the volume is the first thing to check. And the squelch is how you check it.

Next, the squelch. A receiver with nothing to receive does not stay quiet. It amplifies whatever random electrical noise is around, and you hear a constant hiss, which is static. Listening to that for an hour is tiring, and it hides real calls. The squelch is a gate. It keeps the audio muted until an incoming signal is stronger than a threshold, and then opens to let the voice through.

Setting the threshold is a trade-off. Too low, and the hiss leaks through between calls. Too high, and weak stations never open the gate, so you do not hear them at all. That might be a distant aircraft, or a ground station near the edge of its range.

Many radios set the threshold automatically, and give you a pull-to-test, or a push button, that opens the squelch fully. Opening it is useful twice over. If you hear hiss, the receiver, the volume and the audio path are working. And the hiss is a known sound to set the volume against. On a radio with a manual squelch knob, turn it until you hear the hiss, then back it off until the hiss just stops.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. A squelch set too tight looks like a quiet frequency. If a busy CTAF seems strangely silent, open the squelch before you assume nobody is about.

Now frequency selection, and the active and standby windows. Most modern COM radios show two frequencies. The active frequency, on the left or on top, is the one the radio is transmitting and receiving on now. The standby frequency is a scratch pad. The concentric frequency knobs change only the standby. The larger outer knob steps whole megahertz, and the smaller inner knob steps the kilohertz part. When the standby is ready, the flip-flop button, also called the transfer button, swaps the two. The old active frequency moves to standby, so going back is one press away.

This design lets you dial the next frequency without losing contact on the current one. And it is why a frequency you have dialled but not transferred does nothing. You transmit and receive on the active frequency only.

A standard habit is to pre-set the next frequency in standby as soon as you know it. The tower, while you are on the ATIS. Or the area frequency, while you are on the CTAF. Then transfer it at the right moment.

Picture the face of a typical COM radio. On the left, the active window shows {126.700|one two six decimal seven}. That is the frequency the radio transmits and receives on. On the right, the standby window shows {120.025|one two zero decimal zero two five}, the scratch pad. Below the standby window sit the concentric knobs, outer for megahertz and inner for kilohertz, and they change only the standby. Between the windows is the flip-flop button that swaps them. The volume knob is often the on-off switch, and pulling it opens the squelch to test for hiss. And a small TX indicator lights while the transmitter is keyed.

Australian VHF COM uses 25 kHz spacing. A frequency like {120.025|one two zero decimal zero two five} MHz is a valid 25 kHz channel. Some radios also offer 8.33 kHz spacing, which is used in Europe. Australia does not require it.

Then memory, {121.5|one two one decimal five}, and the TX light. Some units store frequently used frequencies as memory channels, saving dial-twisting on regular routes. Many also give a quick way to tune {121.5|one two one decimal five} MHz, the international emergency frequency, often with a long press of the flip-flop button. Check your POH for your unit.

The TX indicator is a small "{TX|T X}", or "T", in the display, that lights while the transmitter is keyed. Glance at it when you make a call. It confirms that the radio you meant to use is the one transmitting. If it stays lit after you release the PTT, the microphone is stuck in the transmit position.

That is more than your problem. A stuck microphone keeps transmitting a carrier, and every word said in the cockpit, on the frequency. It blocks the frequency for every other aircraft and for the ground station. Unplug the offending microphone, or cycle the PTT, until the light goes out.

Question. You have dialled the tower frequency and made your call, but the tower does not answer. The frequency is shown on the radio. What should you check first, and why?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: whether the tower frequency is in the active window, or still in standby. The knobs only change the standby, and the radio transmits and receives on the active frequency only. So an untransferred frequency means your call went out on the old one.

## The press-to-talk switch

The press-to-talk switch, on the control column or on the hand microphone, keys the selected transmitter. The sequence is: press, pause, speak, release. In two-crew or training aircraft, each seat usually has its own PTT.

The pause matters. A transmitter takes a moment to come up to power after it is keyed, and other radios take a moment to open their squelch. Speak before that, and the first word, often the call-sign, is lost. That is a clipped transmission, which the lesson on operating aeronautical radio systems covers. Releasing before you finish clips the end. Release fully afterwards, and check that the TX light has gone out. Otherwise you become the stuck microphone.

The PTT keys whichever transmitter the audio panel's MIC selector has chosen. It does not know which radio you are thinking about.

## The audio panel

The audio panel routes audio between the radios, the headsets and the speaker. It is where the most interesting mistakes happen, because it separates what you hear from where you transmit.

It has six typical groups of controls. First, the MIC selector, the transmit selector. It chooses which COM transmits: COM 1, COM 2, and sometimes COM 3 or HF. Selecting a COM for transmit normally also selects it for receive.

Second, the receiver select buttons: COM 1, COM 2, NAV 1, NAV 2, ADF, DME, and marker. Pressing a button lets you hear that receiver. This is how you monitor the area frequency on COM 2 while transmitting on the CTAF on COM 1. Or listen to a navaid's ident.

Third, the speaker or phone selector, which routes the audio to the cabin speaker, or to the headsets.

Fourth, the intercom volume and squelch knob, the VOX, and the isolation modes. Pilot isolate means the pilot hears only the radios. Crew means the pilot and co-pilot can talk, while the passengers hear music. And all means everyone.

Fifth, on many panels, the marker beacon lights, and a high or low sensitivity setting.

And sixth, the fail-safe. If the audio panel loses power, COM 1 is usually connected directly to the pilot's headset, so you can still talk and listen. Check your POH.

The key idea is this. You transmit on one radio, but you can listen to as many as you like. The MIC selector is a single choice: one COM at a time is connected to the microphones and the PTT. The receiver buttons are independent switches. You can hear COM 1, COM 2 and a NAV ident at once, mixed together in your headset.

That makes monitoring possible. On a CTAF departure, you can broadcast on the CTAF on COM 1, while listening on COM 2 to the area frequency, for traffic and for anyone calling you.

It also makes the classic error possible. You hear the CTAF clearly, so you assume you are talking on it. But the MIC selector is still on COM 2 from your last call to Centre, and your broadcast has gone out on the area frequency instead.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. You transmit on whichever COM the MIC selector chooses, regardless of which ones you are listening to. Hearing a frequency proves nothing about where your next call will go. The MIC selector, and the TX light, do.

Now navaid idents and the marker. The NAV, ADF and DME buttons let you hear the Morse identification of a navigation aid, which you must check before using it. Turn the receiver button on to identify the aid. Then turn it off again, so the Morse does not drown out air traffic control. The marker beacon lights and tone belong to instrument approaches. For VFR, you will mostly leave the marker audio off, or set to low sensitivity.

Next, the intercom and isolation. The intercom lets the people on board talk to each other through their headsets. A voice-activated squelch, the VOX, opens each microphone only when someone speaks loudly enough, so that the engine noise is not piped into every headset. Set it like the radio squelch, just above the point where engine noise opens it.

The isolation modes decide who hears what. In pilot isolate, the pilot hears only the radios, and is cut out of the cabin chat. That is useful during a busy arrival. Crew links the front seats, while the passengers hear only themselves, and music if it is fitted. And all puts everyone on the same intercom.

And last, when the audio panel fails. The audio panel is electrically powered, and it sits between you and every radio. So its failure could silence everything. That is why most panels are built with a fail-safe path. With no power, COM 1 is wired straight through to the pilot's headset and microphone. You lose the selections, the intercom and COM 2. But you can still talk and listen on COM 1. Check your POH for your panel. This is also why the fault-finding check, in the lesson on fault finding, includes the audio panel switch and its circuit breaker.

Question. You want to hear the Perth ATIS on COM 2, without losing the tower on COM 1. What audio panel selections do you make?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: leave the MIC selector on COM 1, the tower. And press the COM 2 receiver button, so you hear COM 2 as well. Turn COM 2 off again once you have the ATIS, so it does not mask tower calls.

## Setting up before you taxi

Putting the controls together, here is a standard set-up at a non-controlled aerodrome. Let's work it through. You are departing from a non-controlled aerodrome. The CTAF is in ERSA, and the area frequency is on the VNC, the visual navigation chart. There are five steps.

First, the COM radios. COM 1 active on the CTAF, from ERSA. COM 2 active on the area frequency, from the VNC.

Second, the audio panel. MIC on COM 1. Receive on both COM 1 and COM 2.

Third, volume and squelch, set on each radio. Open the squelch, set a comfortable volume against the hiss, then close it.

Fourth, the transponder. Code {1200|one two zero zero}, and {ALT|alt}, which is Mode C or S, before entering the runway, unless a different code is assigned.

And fifth, the taxi call, on COM 1. If you then need to call Centre, switch the MIC selector to COM 2. And remember to switch it back for the next CTAF broadcast.

The pattern is: talk on the CTAF, listen on both, and treat every change of the MIC selector as something you must undo.

Now try a different case yourself. You are about to leave the Jandakot circuit area, on a flight to a country strip. COM 1 has Jandakot tower active. You want the area frequency ready next. And you want to check the identity of a navaid on NAV 1 on the way. What selections do you make? Pause if you'd like to work it out.
[[pause 8]]
There are four parts. First, COM 1. Tower stays active. Dial the area frequency, or the destination CTAF, into standby, ready to flip-flop when the tower releases you.

Second, the MIC stays on COM 1, with receive on COM 1 selected. Nothing changes on the audio panel when you flip-flop, because the frequency changes inside the same radio.

Third, NAV 1. Tune the aid, press the NAV 1 receiver button to hear the Morse ident, check it against the chart, and then turn the button off.

And fourth, COM 2 can hold the next frequency after that. Select it for receive if you want to monitor it, with the MIC still on COM 1.

Every change that affects where you transmit, meaning the MIC selector and the active frequency, is checked against the TX light on the next call.

## Transponder controls

The transponder is a radio too. It receives interrogations from ground radar on 1,030 MHz, and replies on 1,090 MHz with a code, and, in {ALT|alt} mode, your pressure altitude.

It has three groups of controls. First, the code selector: four digits, each from 0 to 7. Second, the mode selector: OFF, standby, ON and {ALT|alt}, and sometimes {GND|ground}. And third, the IDENT button, which you press only when air traffic control asks you to "squawk ident". When changing codes, avoid passing through {7500|seven five zero zero}, {7600|seven six zero zero} and {7700|seven seven zero zero}.

The code identifies your aircraft on the controller's screen. Standby keeps the transponder warm, but silent. It is used on the ground at many aerodromes until you enter the runway. ON replies with the code only. {ALT|Alt} adds the altitude, and it is the normal setting in flight: Mode C or S. The IDENT button makes your return stand out on the screen for a few seconds, so the controller can pick you out. That is why it is used only on request.

Why avoid the reserved codes while changing digits? Because each intermediate setting is transmitted as you dial. Say you roll from {1200|one two zero zero} to {7234|seven two three four} by turning the first digit up through 7 first. That could briefly show {7200|seven two zero zero}, which is harmless. But other paths pass through {7600|seven six zero zero} or {7700|seven seven zero zero}, and trigger an alarm on the controller's screen.

[[diagram:transponder-emergency-codes]]
Picture three code windows, side by side. {7700|Seven seven zero zero}: emergency. {7600|Seven six zero zero}: radio failure. {7500|Seven five zero zero}: unlawful interference. They are the three codes you never pass through while changing codes.

Question. You are squawking {7300|seven three zero zero}, and you are cleared to squawk {3500|three five zero zero}. Which digit do you change first, and why?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the first digit. Each intermediate code is transmitted as you dial. Change the second digit first, and the code steps through {7400|seven four zero zero} and {7500|seven five zero zero}, the unlawful interference code, while the 7 is still set. Change the 7 to a 3 first, and the steps are {3300|three three zero zero}, {3400|three four zero zero} and {3500|three five zero zero}. None of them is an emergency code.

## Key points

Let's recap.

The COM radio. The volume knob is often the power switch too. The squelch mutes the hiss, and pull-to-test opens it. The outer knob sets megahertz and the inner knob kilohertz, in the standby window. The flip-flop makes it active. And you transmit and receive on the active frequency only. The TX light shows the transmitter is keyed.

The PTT: press, pause, speak, release.

The audio panel. One MIC selection decides where you transmit. Any number of receiver buttons decide what you hear. Add the speaker or phones selector, the intercom with its VOX squelch and isolate modes, and a fail-safe that connects COM 1 to the pilot's headset.

And the transponder. A four-digit code, each digit 0 to 7. Modes OFF, standby, ON and {ALT|alt}. IDENT only on request. And avoid passing through {7500|seven five zero zero}, {7600|seven six zero zero} and {7700|seven seven zero zero}.

A final exam tip. You transmit on whichever COM the MIC selector chooses, regardless of which one you are listening to. Many "no reply" problems are a call made on COM 2 when you meant COM 1. Expect questions that give you a set of selections, such as the MIC on one COM, receivers on several, and a frequency in standby, and ask what happens when you press the PTT. Work along the chain, from the microphone to the active frequency.
