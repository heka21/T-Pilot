This is PAKC 3.3, Fault finding and corrective action. By the end you will have a routine for finding a radio fault in flight, know what each check tells you and why it comes where it does, and know what to do when the fault cannot be cleared. There are worked examples and a few questions along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (PAKC 3.3)

The exam wants two things from you. First, the routine procedure for finding a radio fault. And second, the corrective action a pilot takes once the fault is found, or when it cannot be cleared.

## Why this matters

Sooner or later the radio goes quiet on you. It might be on the ground at Jandakot, when Ground does not answer your taxi call. Or at 3,000 ft over the Avon Valley, inbound to Northam, when the CTAF that was busy a minute ago falls silent.

In almost every case, the radio itself is fine. A plug has worked loose. A switch is in the wrong place. The frequency is in the standby window. Or a breaker has popped.

A pilot with a routine finds that in a minute, without taking their eyes off the horizon for long. A pilot without one fiddles, gets distracted, and flies through the Serpentine circuit unannounced. The exam asks what to check first for a given symptom, what to do with a breaker that pops, and what to do when nothing works. This lesson gives you the routine, the reasoning behind its order, and the corrective actions that follow.

## Fly first, then fault-find

Before any checklist, one rule. The aeroplane comes first. A dead radio is an inconvenience. A dead radio plus a wandering heading in a busy training area, or a climb into the base of the Class C steps above Jandakot, is a problem. So level off, or settle into the circuit. Trim, and look out. Only then start working through the checks, a step at a time, looking up between each one.

That rule shapes the routine. Every check in it is quick, done from the seat, and can be interrupted and resumed. It finds the simple causes. If they are cleared and the radio still does not work, you stop fault-finding, and switch to the radio failure procedure.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Fault-finding is not a reason to stop looking out. In a CTAF circuit with a silent radio, other pilots cannot hear you, and you cannot hear them. So your lookout is now the only collision avoidance you have. Do one check, look out, do the next.

## Think of the radio as a chain

A radio message travels along a chain of parts. Going out, it goes from your voice into the microphone. Then through the audio panel to the selected transmitter, which needs electrical power. Then out through the antenna to the other station. Coming in, it runs the other way. Antenna, receiver, audio panel, headset or speaker, and your ear. The lesson on radio system components takes each of those parts one at a time. Here you only need their order.

Said as one line, the chain is: power, radio, audio panel, microphone or headset, antenna, and the other station. A break anywhere stops everything downstream of it. So you test one link, and the result tells you which half of the chain the fault is in.

The routine also puts the commonest causes first. Plugs, switches and settings fail far more often than electronics. And they are the only things you can fix in flight. Most radio failures in light aircraft are switch or plug problems, not dead radios.

Question. Why does the fault-finding routine follow the signal path, rather than starting wherever you first suspect a fault?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: one break in the chain silences everything after it. So testing the links in order tells you which part of the chain is at fault. Starting from power and working outwards also puts the commonest and simplest causes first.

## The routine fault-finding check

Here is the routine, in its order. There are nine steps. Learn the order first, and then why each item sits where it does.

First, power. Are the master switch and the avionics master on? Is the radio switched on, with its display lit? Is the ammeter or voltmeter normal? A low voltage warning means the alternator has failed, and the radios may be next.

Second, the circuit breakers. Look for a popped breaker for the COM, the audio panel or the intercom. Reset it once only. If it pops again, leave it out. There is a fault, and possibly an electrical fire risk.

Third, the frequency. Is the right frequency in the active window, not the standby? Has it been mis-set by one digit, {127.6|one two seven decimal six} instead of {126.7|one two six decimal seven}?

Fourth, volume and squelch. Turn the volume up, and open the squelch. If you hear hiss, the receiver and audio path are working. You may simply be out of range, or nobody is talking.

Fifth, the audio panel. Check the correct MIC selector for transmitting, and the receiver selected for listening.

Sixth, the headset and microphone. Are the plugs fully in, in the correct sockets? Microphone and phone plugs swapped is common. Try the other headset socket, a spare headset, or the hand microphone and the cabin speaker.

Seventh, a stuck microphone. If the transmit light, often a T or TX on the display, stays on, the press-to-talk switch is stuck. You cannot hear anyone, and you are blocking the frequency for all. Unplug it, or switch to the other COM.

Eighth, the other radio. Change to COM 2, and try again.

And ninth, range and terrain. VHF is line of sight. Climb if it is safe, or try a station closer or higher.

Now the reasons, starting with power and breakers. Power is first, because without it nothing else can be tested. The ammeter or voltmeter tells you whether the alternator is charging. If it is not, the battery is carrying the whole electrical load, and it will run down, and the radios will die with it. That is a corrective action in its own right. Switch off what you do not need, keep one COM, and plan to land while you still have it. The battery endurance is in the lesson on radio system components.

The circuit breakers come next, because a popped breaker is the commonest cause of a radio that has gone dark on its own. A circuit breaker is a switch that opens itself when too much current flows, to protect the wiring from overheating.

A single trip may be a momentary spike, which one reset clears. A real fault, such as a chafed wire touching the airframe, trips it again. Then the breaker is telling you that a circuit behind the panel is overheating. Holding it in, or resetting it again and again, defeats the protection, and can start an electrical fire. So: one reset, and if it pops again, leave it out.

Question. Why do you reset a popped circuit breaker only once?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: a single trip may be a passing spike. A second trip means there is a real overload or short in that circuit. Resetting it again forces current through a faulty circuit, which can overheat the wiring and start a fire. So you leave it out, and treat the radio as failed.

Next, frequency, volume and squelch: the hiss test. With power confirmed, look at the frequency. Modern COMs show an active frequency, the one you transmit and receive on. And a standby frequency, which you preset and then flip across.

Setting the next frequency in standby and forgetting to flip it is a very common slip. So is a transposed digit: {127.6|one two seven decimal six} set instead of {126.7|one two six decimal seven}. Either one leaves you on a perfectly good frequency that nobody you want is using.

Then comes the most useful single test in radio fault-finding. Turn the volume up, and open the squelch. Squelch is a gate that mutes the receiver's output when no signal is present, so you do not have to listen to background noise all flight. When you open it, the gate is forced open. If the receiver, the audio panel and your headset are all working, you will hear hiss, the noise of an empty channel.

That hiss splits the chain in two. Hiss but no stations means everything from the receiver to your ears works. So the fault is out in front of the radio. It is the wrong frequency, or you are out of range or shielded by terrain, or simply nobody is talking.

No hiss at all means the problem is between the radio and your ear. It is the power to the radio, the breaker, the audio panel selection, or the headset or its plugs. One action has halved the search.

[[diagram:squelch-hiss-test]]
Picture the receive chain as a line running from left to right. On the left is the other station. Then range and terrain, and the frequency set in the active window. Then the receiver, the audio panel and the headset, with power, the breaker and the volume feeding the radio from below. Open the squelch. Hiss but no stations puts the fault out in front of the radio, towards the left. No hiss puts it between the radio and your ear, towards the right.

Next, the audio panel and headset. The audio panel chooses which receivers you hear, and which transmitter your microphone keys. With two COMs, it is easy to be listening to COM 1 while the MIC selector is on COM 2. You then hear everyone on the CTAF, and nobody hears your broadcasts, because they are going out on the area frequency. Check that the MIC selector and the receiver buttons are where you think they are.

The headset plugs are the next weak point. A headset has two plugs, a larger one for the headphones and a smaller one for the microphone, and they go into separate sockets. A plug that is half out, or the two plugs swapped, gives exactly the symptoms of a dead radio.

If the plugs look right, try the other headset socket, or a spare headset. Or bypass the headset entirely, with the hand microphone and the cabin speaker. If the hand microphone and speaker work, the radio is fine, and the headset is the fault.

Next, the stuck microphone. This one fault hurts everyone else as well as you. If the press-to-talk switch sticks closed, the radio transmits continuously, and the TX indicator stays lit. While a radio transmits, it cannot receive. So you hear nobody. And your open carrier blocks the frequency for every other aircraft on it. Unplug the switch or the headset, or change to the other COM, at once.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. A silent frequency can be a stuck microphone, not a quiet day. If the TX light is on and you are not pressing the button, you are the reason nobody else can talk.

Then the other radio, and then range. If one COM still will not work, the second one is a complete second chain from the audio panel outwards, with its own transmitter, receiver and antenna. Changing to COM 2 tests all of them at once.

Last comes range. VHF travels in straight lines, like light, so a hill or the curve of the earth blocks it. Low behind the Darling Scarp, you can lose a coastal plain station that was readable minutes earlier. Climbing, where airspace and cloud allow, extends the line of sight. That is covered in the lesson on radio waves.

Let's work one. You have departed a country strip for the training area, and changed frequency. Your call gets no answer, and you have heard nothing for five minutes. Pause if you'd like to work through the routine.
[[pause 6]]
Step one, fly. Level at your planned altitude, trimmed, looking out.

Step two, power. The COM display is lit, and the ammeter shows a charge. Power is fine.

Step three, breakers. All in, so no tripped circuit.

Step four, frequency. The active window shows {127.6|one two seven decimal six}. The frequency you meant to set was {126.7|one two six decimal seven}. Transposed digits.

Step five, correct it, and open the squelch. Hiss is heard, then other traffic. Fault cleared.

The radio was never faulty. Reading the active window digit by digit would have caught it before the first call.

Question. You open the squelch and hear a strong hiss, but no stations. What does that tell you, and what do you check next?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the receiver, the audio panel and the headset are all working, so the fault is in front of the radio. Check that the right frequency is in the active window. Then consider range and terrain. Climb if you can, or try a closer station.

## Matching the symptom to the cause

Each symptom points at a part of the chain. Take each one as a short piece of reasoning, not a fact to memorise. There are six.

Nothing heard, and no hiss with the squelch open. The likely cause is the radio or the audio turned off, a circuit breaker, or the headset. Check the power, the breaker and the plugs, and try the speaker.

Hiss but no stations. The likely cause is the wrong frequency, or being out of range. Check the frequency, climb, or try another station.

Others cannot hear you, but you hear them. The likely cause is the microphone plug, the wrong MIC selector, the press-to-talk switch, or the antenna. Check the plugs and the selector, use the hand microphone, or the other COM.

A constant TX light, with no reception. That is a stuck press-to-talk switch. Unplug the switch or the headset, and change radio.

Weak, noisy reception. The likely cause is the squelch set high, range, or the antenna. Adjust the squelch, climb, and report it to maintenance.

And a whine that varies with RPM. That is alternator noise. Report it for maintenance.

Now look at the logic. No hiss, and hiss but no stations, are the squelch test from before. They cannot hear you but you hear them means receive is fine. So the fault is in the parts used only for transmitting: the microphone plug, the MIC selector, the press-to-talk switch, and possibly the antenna. And a whine that rises and falls with RPM is electrical noise from the alternator getting into the audio. It does not stop you communicating, but it is a defect to report.

Try this one. You have just swapped headsets with your instructor before departing Murrayfield. You hear the CTAF clearly. But two of your broadcasts get no response, and an aircraft in the circuit asks whether anyone is on the runway. Which part of the chain is at fault, and what do you check?
[[pause 6]]
First, you hear others clearly, so the receive path, the frequency and the power are fine. The fault is on the transmit side only.

Second, check the MIC selector on the audio panel. It is still on COM 1, the CTAF. So it is not the selector.

Third, check the headset plugs. The microphone plug is only half in after the swap. Push it home.

And fourth, if that does not fix it, try the other headset socket, or the hand microphone, then COM 2.

So the swap disturbed the plug. Until the fix is confirmed, assume nobody has heard you, and look out accordingly.

## When the fault cannot be cleared

Sometimes the routine ends with no fix. At that point you stop fault-finding, and change from diagnosing to coping. There are four corrective actions.

First, squawk {7600|seven six zero zero}, if you have a transponder and are, or were about to be, communicating with ATC.

Second, follow the radio failure procedure in ERSA EMERG. Stay in VMC, land at the most suitable aerodrome, and report arrival. That procedure has its own lesson, on radio failure.

Third, transmit blind, in case your transmitter works even though your receiver does not.

And fourth, afterwards, record the defect on the maintenance release, or in the aircraft's defect log, so the next pilot is not caught out.

[[diagram:transponder-emergency-codes]]
Picture three code windows side by side. {7700|Seven seven zero zero} is emergency. {7600|Seven six zero zero} is radio failure. And {7500|seven five zero zero} is unlawful interference. All three are listed in the ERSA EMERG section, the pink pages, along with the distress and urgency calls and the radio failure procedures. For a radio you cannot fix, the one you need is {7600|seven six zero zero}.

Each action has a reason. {7600|Seven six zero zero} tells a radar controller, without a word, that you have lost communications. They can then keep other traffic clear, and expect you to follow the ERSA EMERG procedure, rather than improvise.

Transmitting blind recognises that a fault may be on one side of the chain only. If your receiver has failed but your transmitter works, your broadcasts still tell others where you are, even though you cannot hear their replies. And reporting arrival stops a search being started for an aeroplane that is safely on the ground.

The last step happens on the ground, and it is easy to skip when the drama is over. A radio defect not written up is a trap set for the next pilot. That may be a student solo on their first navigation exercise. Write the defect on the maintenance release, or in whatever defect system your operator uses, in plain words. What failed, when, and what you tried.

Let's work one: an alternator failure on the way to Northam. You are cruising at 4,500 ft east of the Scarp. The low voltage light comes on, and the ammeter shows a discharge. What do you do? Pause if you'd like to think it through.
[[pause 6]]
Step one, fly. Maintain heading and altitude, and look out.

Step two, diagnose. A low voltage warning plus a discharge means the alternator has failed, and the battery is carrying the load.

Step three, act on power. Switch off non-essential equipment, and leave one COM on. That is load shedding.

Step four, decide. The radios will last only as long as the battery. So plan to land at the nearest suitable aerodrome, while you can still make your calls.

Step five, if the radio then dies. Squawk {7600|seven six zero zero} if you had been talking to ATC, follow ERSA EMERG, transmit blind, and stay in VMC.

And step six, after landing. Report arrival if required, and write the alternator and radio defect on the maintenance release.

The fault-finding found the cause early. And that bought time to land with the radio still working.

Question. If your receiver may have failed, but your transmitter may still work, why make blind transmissions?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: other aircraft and ATC might still hear you, even though you cannot hear them. Starting your normal calls with transmitting blind tells them where you are and what you intend. So they can keep clear.

## Bringing it together

You are returning from a solo in the Jandakot training area. You call the tower at the inbound point, and get nothing. You hold clear of the zone boundary, trim, and look out.

Power: the display is lit, and the ammeter is charging. Breakers: all in. Frequency: the tower is in the standby window, and the ATIS in the active. You flip it, and call again. Still nothing.

You open the squelch. No hiss. That points behind the radio. So you check the audio panel, which has COM 1 selected for both MIC and receive. Then the headset. The headphone plug has worked out of its socket. You push it home. The hiss returns, and then the tower, asking you to report your position.

Two simple faults, stacked one on the other, both found by working in order. Had the routine found nothing, the next steps were {7600|seven six zero zero}, ERSA EMERG, and blind transmissions. Either way, a loose socket goes on the maintenance release.

Question. You have flipped the right frequency into the active window, and you still hear nothing. Opening the squelch gives no hiss. Which part of the chain do you now look at?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the part between the radio and your ears. That is the radio's power and breaker, the audio panel receiver selection, and the headset plugs. No hiss means the receive audio is not reaching you. So the frequency was not the whole problem.

## Key points

Let's recap.

Fly first. Then work along the chain, from the simplest cause. Power. Breakers. The frequency in the active window. Volume and squelch. The audio panel. The headset and plugs. A stuck microphone. The other COM. And range.

The squelch hiss test tells you whether the fault is behind the radio or in front of it. Reset a breaker once only.

And if nothing works: squawk {7600|seven six zero zero} if you were talking to ATC. Follow the ERSA EMERG radio failure procedure. Transmit blind. Stay in VMC, land at the most suitable aerodrome, and report arrival. Then write the defect on the maintenance release.

A final exam tip. A popped circuit breaker is reset once. If it trips again, do not hold it in. The protective device is telling you there is a fault, and repeated resetting can start an electrical fire. And expect symptom questions. No hiss points to power, breakers, the audio panel or the headset. Hiss without stations points to the frequency or range. And a constant TX light is a stuck microphone.

That's the end of PAKC 3.3.
