This is PAKC 3.7, Distress and emergency messages. By the end you will be able to tell a distress call from an urgency call, choose the right frequency and transponder code, and say a MAYDAY or a PAN PAN with every item in the right order. There are a few questions along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (PAKC 3.7)

The exam wants two things from you. First, how a distress message differs from an emergency message, which the AIP calls an urgency message. And second, the standard words used for each.

## Why this matters

Most pilots never make a MAYDAY call. And that is exactly why the words have to be learned on the ground.

When the engine goes quiet over the wheatbelt east of Northam, you will have perhaps two minutes between the silence and the paddock. Most of that belongs to flying the aeroplane. There is no time to work out what to say, or in what order. The radio part of an emergency has to be a reflex. The right signal word. The right frequency. The items in a fixed order. Then back to flying.

Just as important is choosing between the two levels of emergency call, because each tells the people listening something different about how fast they need to act.

The exam asks you to tell the two apart in a scenario, to put the items of a distress message in order, to pick the right frequency, and to recognise the other standard phrases. Every call format in this lesson follows AIP GEN 3.4 and the ERSA emergency section, as the lesson gives them. Verify them against the current AIP and ERSA.

## Two levels of trouble

Start with what the people on the ground need to know when you call. A controller who hears that an aircraft is in trouble has to decide, in seconds, how much to drop. Clear the frequency? Alert search and rescue? Scramble a rescue helicopter? Or just give you priority and a bit of help? The first word of your call answers that question for them.

There are two levels. And the test that separates them is one phrase: grave and imminent danger, requiring immediate assistance.

Distress is the top level. The aircraft is threatened by grave and imminent danger, and requires immediate assistance. The signal is MAYDAY MAYDAY MAYDAY. It has absolute priority over all other traffic.

Urgency is the level below. There is a condition concerning the safety of the aircraft, or of a person on board or in sight, which does not require immediate assistance. The signal is PAN PAN, PAN PAN, PAN PAN. It has priority over all traffic except distress.

The word "emergency" in the syllabus covers this second level. AIP GEN 3.4 calls it urgency. Both are emergencies in the everyday sense. But only distress means "help now".

So how do you apply the test? The useful question is not "how bad is this?". It is "do I need help now, or do I need people to know and stand by?"

Here are the distress examples. An engine failure with a forced landing. A fire. A structural failure. The pilot incapacitated, with no other pilot on board. And being lost, with the fuel nearly exhausted. For distress, you squawk {7700|seven seven zero zero}.

And here are the urgency examples. A rough-running engine that is still producing power. A sick passenger. Being lost, but with fuel to spare. An alternator failure. And a bird strike, with the aircraft still controllable. For urgency, you squawk {7700|seven seven zero zero} if it is appropriate.

Listen to the pairs in those lists, because they show the test working. Lost with fuel nearly exhausted is distress. Lost with fuel to spare is urgency. The problem is the same: being lost. But in the first case the danger is imminent, because the engine will stop soon, wherever you are. In the second you have time, and what you need is help finding your position, not a rescue.

Likewise, an engine failure forcing a landing is distress. But a rough-running engine that is still producing power is urgency. You are still flying, still in control of where you go. You just want people to know, in case it gets worse.

Picture that test as a fork in the road. At the top, one question: is the aircraft threatened by grave and imminent danger, requiring immediate assistance? The left branch is yes: distress, MAYDAY three times, absolute priority, squawk {7700|seven seven zero zero}. The right branch is no, but there is a safety concern for the aircraft, or for someone on board or in sight: urgency, PAN PAN three times, priority over all except distress. Notice that the same problem can sit on either branch. And an arrow runs from the urgency branch across to distress, because a PAN can be upgraded if things get worse. A MAYDAY is cancelled when the danger is over.

Notice that the urgency definition includes "or in sight". A PAN PAN can be about someone else: an aircraft or a vessel you can see, that appears to be in trouble.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. The test is not how frightening the situation feels. It is whether immediate assistance is needed. A sick passenger, an alternator failure, or being lost with fuel to spare are all serious. But you can still fly, and choose where to go. That is a PAN PAN. Engine failure, fire, structural failure, or fuel about to run out: that is a MAYDAY.

Question. You are lost over the Darling Range, with two hours of fuel. Distress or urgency? And what would change your answer?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: urgency. The situation concerns the safety of the aircraft, but with two hours of fuel the danger is not imminent. So it is a PAN PAN. If the fuel became nearly exhausted while you were still lost, the danger would become grave and imminent, and you would upgrade to a MAYDAY.

## Declare early, upgrade or cancel

A PAN can be upgraded to a MAYDAY if the situation worsens. And a MAYDAY should be cancelled once the danger is over. That flexibility is what lets you call early.

So do not hesitate. Declaring early gets help moving, and it costs nothing if it is cancelled. The reasoning is lopsided. If you call and the problem clears, you cancel, and the cost is a few minutes of a controller's attention. If you do not call and the problem gets worse, the search and rescue system starts late, perhaps only when your SARTIME expires. And the people who could have helped you are hours behind.

Pilots who hesitate usually do so out of embarrassment. And embarrassment is not a safety factor.

## Which frequency, and what else to set

Your engine has just failed. Which frequency do you call on?

Use the frequency you are already on: the area frequency, the tower, or the CTAF. The reason is simple. Someone there is already listening. A controller working the area frequency hears you immediately. Aircraft on a CTAF can see you, and pass on your position. Changing frequency costs time, and it may land you on a frequency nobody is monitoring.

If there is no reply, use {121.5|one two one decimal five} MHz, the international emergency frequency. Many aircraft and ground stations monitor it.

Two more actions go with the call. First, squawk {7700|seven seven zero zero}, so that radar shows an emergency. The controller's screen flags your aircraft and its position, even if your call is not heard. And second, activate the ELT, the emergency locator transmitter, if a forced landing is imminent or has happened. The ELT sends a signal that satellites and search aircraft can detect. That is how searchers find you once you are on the ground.

Picture three transponder code windows, side by side. The first reads {7700|seven seven zero zero}: emergency. That is the one that goes with a MAYDAY. The second reads {7600|seven six zero zero}: radio failure. And the third reads {7500|seven five zero zero}: unlawful interference. All three are listed in the emergency section of ERSA, the pink pages, along with the distress and urgency calls and the other emergency procedures.

The full set of emergency procedures, codes and calls is in that ERSA emergency section. You'll find more on it in the lesson on emergencies and search and rescue. A radio failure on its own is not a distress situation. That is {7600|seven six zero zero}, and the procedure in the lesson on radio failure procedures.

Question. You are on the area frequency when the engine fails. Why make the MAYDAY there, rather than switching straight to {121.5|one two one decimal five}?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because someone on the frequency you are already using is already listening. So you will be heard at once, without spending time changing frequency. You use {121.5|one two one decimal five} MHz if there is no reply.

## What goes in a distress message

When a controller hears MAYDAY, they need enough information to send help to the right place. Who you are. What is wrong. What you will do. And where you are. The message delivers those in a fixed order, so that even if it is cut off, the most important items have already gone out.

After MAYDAY MAYDAY MAYDAY, give as much as time allows, in this order. First, the name of the station addressed, if you know it and time permits. Second, the call-sign, and the aircraft type helps the search. Third, the nature of the emergency. Fourth, the intentions of the pilot in command. Fifth, the position, level and heading. And sixth, any other useful information: persons on board, endurance, and the aircraft's colour. That order comes from AIP GEN 3.4 and ERSA. Verify it against the current text.

Each item earns its place. The signal, said three times, makes certain the call is recognised even through noise, and it gets everyone else off the frequency. The station addressed directs the call. But you skip it if you do not know whom to call, or if there is no time. The call-sign identifies you, and adding the type tells searchers what to look for from the air. The nature of the emergency tells responders what kind of help is needed. Intentions tell them what you are going to do. For a forced landing, that is where you will be. Position, level and heading narrow the search area. Level and heading let them work out roughly where you will come down.

Other information comes last, because it is useful but not essential. Persons on board tells rescuers how many people to find. Endurance tells them how long you have. And the aircraft colour helps them spot you.

An urgency message has the same structure, preceded by PAN PAN three times.

Let's work one: a MAYDAY for an engine failure near Northam. Cessna {172|one seven two} Alpha Bravo Charlie has two on board. The engine fails 8 miles north-east of Northam. You are passing 2,000 ft, heading {230|two three zero}, gliding to a paddock, and you are on the Melbourne Centre frequency. Pause if you'd like to build the call yourself.
[[pause 8]]
First, fly. Best glide, paddock chosen. Then press the PTT on the frequency you are already on.

Now the items in order. The signal: "MAYDAY MAYDAY MAYDAY". The station addressed: "Melbourne Centre". The call-sign and type: "Cessna {172|one seven two} Alpha Bravo Charlie, Alpha Bravo Charlie, Alpha Bravo Charlie". The nature of the emergency: "engine failure". The intentions: "forced landing in a paddock". Position, level and heading: "8 miles north-east of Northam, passing 2,000, heading {230|two three zero}". And other useful information: "two POB".

Put together, it sounds like this. "MAYDAY MAYDAY MAYDAY, Melbourne Centre, Cessna {172|one seven two} Alpha Bravo Charlie, Alpha Bravo Charlie, Alpha Bravo Charlie, engine failure, forced landing in a paddock, 8 miles north-east of Northam, passing 2,000, heading {230|two three zero}, two POB."

Then squawk {7700|seven seven zero zero}, go back to flying the aeroplane, and activate the ELT as the landing becomes certain.

[[diagram:distress-message-anatomy]]
Picture that same MAYDAY written out in a line and cut into its parts, in the AIP order. First the signal, MAYDAY three times. Then the station addressed, Melbourne Centre. Then the call-sign and type. Then the nature of the emergency, engine failure. Then the intentions, forced landing in a paddock. Then position, level and heading. And last, the other useful information, two POB. The most essential items come first, in case the call is cut short. A PAN PAN message uses the same order.

The order is the thing to learn. Say the items in this sequence every time, and you will not have to think about it when it matters.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Exam options often swap the order of the middle items. After the signal and the station, it is call-sign, nature of the emergency, intentions, position. "Position before nature", and "intentions last", are the usual distractors.

Question. Why does the call-sign come before the nature of the emergency, and why do the extra details, such as persons on board, come last?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: responders must first know who is calling. Then what is wrong, what you will do, and where you are. That is what they need to start helping. Persons on board, endurance and colour help the search, but they are the least essential. So they come last, in case the message is cut short.

## The urgency message

An urgency message uses exactly the same structure as the distress message, with PAN PAN said three times in place of MAYDAY. Because the danger is not imminent, you usually have time to give every item. That includes endurance, which matters more here. The controller wants to know how long you have to sort the problem out.

Let's work one: a PAN PAN for a rough-running engine. Cessna {172|one seven two} Alpha Bravo Charlie has two on board, at 3,500 ft, 10 miles west of York. The engine is running rough, but still producing power. You decide to divert to York. You have two hours of fuel.

The items in order. The signal: "PAN PAN, PAN PAN, PAN PAN". The station: "Melbourne Centre". The call-sign and type: "Cessna {172|one seven two} Alpha Bravo Charlie". The nature: "rough-running engine". The intentions: "diverting to York". Position and level: "10 miles west of York, 3,500 ft". And other information: "two POB, endurance two hours".

Put together: "PAN PAN, PAN PAN, PAN PAN, Melbourne Centre, Cessna {172|one seven two} Alpha Bravo Charlie, rough-running engine, diverting to York, 10 miles west of York, 3,500 ft, two POB, endurance two hours."

The engine is still working, you are in control, and you have a plan. So this is urgency, not distress.

Now try the next step yourself. Five minutes later, over York, the rough engine stops altogether. What do you do on the radio?
[[pause 6]]
The danger is now grave and imminent, because the aircraft cannot stay airborne. So you upgrade to distress. On the same frequency, after flying the aeroplane: "MAYDAY MAYDAY MAYDAY". Then the station, the call-sign, the nature, which is engine failure, the intentions, which is a forced landing, at York if you can reach it, then position, level and heading, and persons on board, in the standard order.

Squawk {7700|seven seven zero zero} if you have not already. And activate the ELT if the landing will be off an aerodrome. The PAN had already given Centre most of the picture. That is exactly why calling early pays. So the answer: upgrade the PAN to a MAYDAY, on the same frequency.

## Other standard phrases

Three more phrases complete the set. Each has a fixed form and a fixed meaning.

The first is {MAYDAY RELAY|mayday relay}, said three times. You use it when you are passing on a distress message from another station that cannot be heard by air traffic control. You are acting as a link. Give the distressed aircraft's details, not your own, and then identify yourself. The order matters, because the controller must not mistake you for the aircraft in trouble. This is the safety exception to the secrecy duty, from the lesson on the responsibilities of a radio operator. You are expected to pass on a distress message.

The second is {STOP TRANSMITTING, MAYDAY|stop transmitting, mayday}. The controlling station uses it to impose silence on stations interfering with distress traffic. When you hear it, do not transmit until it is cancelled. The frequency belongs to the aircraft in distress.

The third is {DISTRESS TRAFFIC ENDED|distress traffic ended}, or "cancel distress". It means the danger is over. For the pilot who declared the MAYDAY, the lesson's form is: "MAYDAY Alpha Bravo Charlie, cancel distress, engine has restarted, continuing to York."

That cancellation is as important as the MAYDAY. Until it is made, rescue resources may still be on their way.

Question. You hear the controller say "{STOP TRANSMITTING, MAYDAY|stop transmitting, mayday}". You were about to report your position. What do you do?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: do not transmit until the silence is cancelled. The controller has imposed silence because distress traffic is in progress. Any call, however routine, could block the aircraft in distress.

## After a forced landing

The radio work does not end when the aeroplane stops. If you can, tell air traffic services that you are safe: on the radio, by phone, or through another aircraft. And keep the ELT available. If nobody has heard from you, it is how searchers will find you.

[[diagram:sartime-timeline]]
Picture a timeline running from left to right. Before flight you nominate a SARTIME, with a sensible margin after your planned arrival. If you are delayed, you extend it before it expires, and on landing you cancel it. If the SARTIME passes in silence, the alerting phases begin: attempts to contact you, then a communications search, then a physical search. A call or a phone message that you are safe stops it.

A cancelled distress needs no apology. An undeclared one may delay the search. That single idea is the attitude the whole lesson is building.

## Bringing it together

A calm Sunday flight from Jandakot to Northam, two on board. Over the hills east of the scarp, the engine starts running rough, but keeps producing power. You are in control, and you have fuel. So on the area frequency you make a PAN PAN, in the standard order, with your intention to divert.

Then the engine stops. Now the danger is grave and imminent. You upgrade to MAYDAY on the same frequency, squawk {7700|seven seven zero zero}, and go back to flying the glide.

Another pilot hears Centre struggling to read you. They make a {MAYDAY RELAY|mayday relay}, with your details first and their own call-sign after. The controller imposes silence with "{STOP TRANSMITTING, MAYDAY|stop transmitting, mayday}".

Before you reach the paddock, the engine catches and runs smoothly. You climb away, and cancel distress. If it had not restarted, you would have landed, activated the ELT, and told air traffic services you were safe as soon as you could.

Every phrase used came from the same small set, in the same fixed order.

## Key points

Let's recap.

Distress is MAYDAY, said three times. Grave and imminent danger, immediate assistance needed, and absolute priority.

Urgency is PAN PAN, said three times. A safety concern that does not need immediate assistance, with priority over everything except distress.

Use the frequency you are on, then {121.5|one two one decimal five} MHz. Squawk {7700|seven seven zero zero}. And activate the ELT if a forced landing is imminent or has happened.

The message order is: station addressed, call-sign, nature, intentions, position with level and heading, then other information, meaning persons on board, endurance and colour. Urgency uses the same order.

Upgrade a PAN if it worsens. Cancel a MAYDAY when it is over.

And the other phrases. {MAYDAY RELAY|Mayday relay} passes on another aircraft's distress. {STOP TRANSMITTING, MAYDAY|Stop transmitting, mayday} imposes silence. And "cancel distress", or {DISTRESS TRAFFIC ENDED|distress traffic ended}, ends it.

A final exam tip. The test is grave and imminent danger requiring immediate assistance. If immediate help is needed, MAYDAY. If the situation is serious but under control, PAN PAN. Use the frequency you are already on first, then {121.5|one two one decimal five}. Learn the message order as a chant: station, call-sign, nature, intentions, position, the rest. And verify the phraseology against the current AIP GEN 3.4 and the ERSA emergency section before the exam.
