This is PAKC 3.2, Routine pre-flight test of an aircraft radio-telephone. By the end you will be able to test the radio before flight step by step, from switching it on to the radio check, and say why each step sits where it does. There are a few questions along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (PAKC 3.2)

The exam wants you to describe the pre-flight radio test step by step, and it names eight parts. The transmit and receive selectors. Switching the radio on. Selecting the right frequencies. Setting the squelch. Selecting and checking the navigation radios. Using the microphone properly. The intercom and passenger address system. And voice-activated, or VOX, systems.

## Why this matters

A radio fault found on the apron at Jandakot costs five minutes and a word with the engineer. The same fault found when you call the tower before the control zone boundary, or join a busy circuit at Murrayfield, is a radio failure, with all its procedures, at the worst moment.

The pre-flight test proves three things before you move. That you can transmit. That you can receive. And that every switch and knob is where you think it is. Most radio failures turn out to be a selector, a volume knob or a squelch set wrong, and this test catches them on the ground.

The controls themselves are explained in the lesson on radiotelephone controls. This lesson turns them into a routine. And the exam asks about the squelch method, the avionics master, VOX, microphone technique and the readability scale.

## Turning the radio on

The first item happens before the engine is even running. Start the engine with the avionics master off.

The avionics master is a switch that feeds power to all the radios and navigation equipment at once. While the starter motor is cranking, it draws a very large current and pulls the battery voltage down. When it disengages, the voltage can jump as the circuit recovers. Those surges can damage solid-state avionics. Keeping the avionics off isolates them.

After start, check that the alternator is charging, on the ammeter or voltmeter, with no low-voltage light. That tells you the electrical system has settled. Then switch the avionics master on.

Each radio has its own on and off switch, often combined with the volume knob. Turn each one on. Many units run a self-test, and show a start-up page and their software version, before settling on the normal display. Watch for any failure message or warning flag.

At the end of the flight the order reverses. The avionics master goes off before you shut the engine down, for the same reason.

Question. Why is the avionics master switched off for engine start, and when does it go on?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the starter draws a heavy current, and the voltage can surge when it disengages, which can damage solid-state avionics. The avionics master goes on only after start, once the alternator is charging and the electrical system is stable. And it goes off again before shutdown.

## Choosing where you talk and where you listen

The radios do not connect straight to your headset. Between them is the audio panel. And it has two kinds of selector that do different jobs.

The transmit selector, often labelled MIC, chooses the one radio your microphone and press-to-talk switch are connected to: COM 1 or COM 2. You can transmit on only one at a time.

The receive selectors are separate buttons, one for each receiver: COM 1, COM 2, NAV, ADF and marker. Each one you press adds that radio to what you hear. So you can listen to several at once.

The test, then, is to set them deliberately. Select the transmitter you intend to use first, usually COM 1, and the receivers you want to hear. Then check the two agree. Make sure you are transmitting on the radio you are listening to.

The classic error is to hear a frequency clearly on COM 2, while the MIC selector is on COM 1. Every call goes out on the wrong frequency, and nobody answers. Hearing a frequency proves nothing about where you are transmitting. The TX indicator on the radio face lights while that radio is transmitting. It confirms the selection when you make your test call.

## Selecting the frequencies

The frequencies you need come from ERSA, and for the area frequency, from the VNC or the {ERC-L|E R C low}. Look them up while planning.

[[diagram:ersa-entry-anatomy]]
Picture an ERSA aerodrome entry, laid out as a set of blocks from top to bottom. At the top is the header, with the elevation, position, variation, time zone and operator. Below it is the runway table. Then comes the communications block, with the navigation aids. And last, the remarks and local traffic regulations. The communications block lists the CTAF, the ATIS, and the tower or ground frequencies.

Most radios show an active frequency, the one in use, and a standby frequency. Dial the frequency into the standby window with the frequency knobs, and check it. Then press the transfer button, the flip-flop, to make it active. You transmit and receive on the active frequency only.

There are two typical set-ups. At a non-controlled aerodrome, put COM 1 on the CTAF, and COM 2 on the area frequency. Then you can broadcast on one and monitor the other. At a towered aerodrome, the ATIS goes in first. Then ground, or delivery, with the tower frequency waiting in standby for the run-up.

Australia uses 25 kHz channel spacing in the VHF communication band. That band runs from {118.000|one one eight decimal zero zero zero} to {136.975 MHz|one three six decimal nine seven five megahertz}. Europe uses 8.33 kHz spacing, which is not required here, though many modern radios can select it. If a frequency will not dial up the way you expect, or the display looks odd, check the radio is set to 25 kHz mode.

Before you rely on a frequency, read it back to yourself against ERSA. A single digit wrong, {126.7|one two six decimal seven} instead of {127.6|one two seven decimal six}, puts you on someone else's frequency. And you may hear traffic there, and believe all is well.

## Setting the volume and squelch

A receiver with no signal to receive does not stay silent. It amplifies random noise into a constant hiss. The squelch is a gate that mutes the audio until a signal stronger than a threshold arrives, and then lets it through. Set too tight, it shuts out weak stations. Set too loose, you listen to static. Getting the squelch and volume right is the core of the receive test.

The method has three steps. First, open the squelch until you hear the hiss. On many sets you turn the squelch knob, or pull or press a test control that opens it fully.

Second, set the volume to a comfortable level against that hiss. The hiss proves that the receiver, the audio panel and the headset are all working. And it gives you a steady sound to set the level by.

And third, close the squelch until the hiss just disappears, and no further.

Why set the volume with the squelch open, or while listening to the ATIS? For a simple reason. If you set it on a silent frequency, you cannot tell whether the radio is quiet because nobody is talking, or because the volume is turned down. Many modern radios set the squelch automatically. But the test button still lets you check the volume against the hiss.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. The squelch is set by opening it until noise is heard, then closing it until the noise just stops. Turning it as far as it will go to remove all noise also removes the weak stations. And a busy frequency then sounds quiet.

Question. You are about to set the volume on COM 2, tuned to the area frequency, and you hear nothing. Why open the squelch before touching the volume?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: silence could mean nobody is transmitting, or that the volume is down. Opening the squelch produces hiss. That proves the receiver and the audio path are working, and gives you a sound to set the volume against. Then close the squelch until the hiss just stops.

## Setting up the navigation radios

The navigation receivers are part of the radio test too. For each one you plan to use, there are three checks.

First, tune and identify the aid. Select its receive button on the audio panel, and listen to the Morse identification. Check it against the code printed on the chart or in ERSA. Then deselect it, so the Morse does not drown out the COM. A navigation aid must be identified before you use it. A wrong frequency, or an aid off the air for maintenance, can give a believable but false indication.

Second, check the indication behaves sensibly. A VOR's course deviation indicator should centre, with a sensible TO or FROM flag, when you turn the course selector. An ADF needle should point roughly towards the beacon. And where a VOR check point is published for the aerodrome, check the VOR against it.

And third, for a GNSS unit, check the database is current, the unit has passed its self-test, and the satellite page shows a position.

## Using the microphone

Good microphone technique decides whether anyone understands you, however good the radio.

Start with position. Hold a hand microphone, or position a boom microphone, close to the lips. About 1 to 2 cm away, and slightly to the side. Close, so that your voice is much louder than the engine noise around it. Slightly to the side, so your breath does not hit the microphone and produce a roar on every word.

Most headset microphones are noise-cancelling. They are designed to pick up sound from very close, and to reject sound from further away. That works only when the microphone is that close.

[[diagram:anr-noise-cancelling]]
Picture a headset in two halves. On one side, passive protection: the ear seal and the shell block the noise, but only if the seal is intact. On the other side, active noise reduction: a microphone hears the low drone of the engine and propeller, and the electronics play an opposite sound wave, so that the two cancel and much less reaches the ear. Both depend on fit. An intact seal for your ears, and a microphone right at the lips for everyone listening to you.

Then technique. Press the press-to-talk switch fully. Pause about half a second, so the transmitter comes up to power. Speak at a normal level and a steady pace. Finish, then release. Do not shout. Shouting distorts the signal, and makes it harder to understand, not easier. Starting to speak too early, or releasing too soon, clips your transmission, as the lesson on operating the radio explains.

And care. Keep the cord clear of the controls, and check the plugs are fully home in their sockets. A plug half out often gives you reception, but no transmission.

## The intercom and passenger address

The intercom links the headsets, so the people on board can talk to each other. Check each headset socket you will use. Plug in, speak, and check you can hear each other. Check the intercom volume, and its squelch, which on most intercoms is the voice-activation threshold.

Some aeroplanes have a passenger address function, which sends audio to the cabin speaker. Check it is not selected while you are talking to ATC. Otherwise your calls and their replies fill the cabin, and passengers may talk over them.

Brief your passengers on the intercom. When the radio is active, they stay quiet. A passenger chatting over a tower clearance can make you miss it. And on most intercoms, their voice is mixed with the radio in your headset. Some audio panels have isolation modes, which cut the pilot out of the passenger intercom during busy phases.

## Voice-activated systems

Intercoms are usually voice-activated, which is called VOX. Each microphone is live only when the sound reaching it exceeds a threshold. Below the threshold the microphone is muted, so engine noise is not piped into every headset.

Set the threshold like a squelch, with the engine running, because engine noise is what you are setting it against. Too low, and engine noise keeps the intercom open, filling every headset with roar. Too high, and the first word of every sentence is lost before the microphone opens. Or a quiet speaker never opens it at all. The aim is that the engine alone does not open the intercom, but normal speech does.

VOX controls only the intercom. Transmitting to ATC or on the CTAF still requires the press-to-talk switch. So you can talk freely on board without broadcasting it.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. VOX opens the intercom microphone, not the transmitter. Speaking loudly does not put you on the radio. Only the press-to-talk switch does.

## The radio check

With everything set, you test the whole chain with a call. Make the radio check on the appropriate frequency. At a towered aerodrome, that is ground. At a non-controlled aerodrome, you can ask another station, or rely on hearing your broadcast answered or acknowledged. In the air, Centre will give a check if workload permits.

It sounds like this. Jandakot Ground, Cessna Alpha Bravo Charlie, radio check one two zero decimal three. And the reply: Alpha Bravo Charlie, Jandakot Ground, readability five.

Saying the frequency in the request lets the station tell you if you are on the wrong one. The reply grades how well you were heard on the readability scale. 1 is unreadable. 2 is readable now and then. 3 is readable but with difficulty. 4 is readable. And 5 is perfectly readable. Anything below 4 is worth investigating before you leave the apron. Check the microphone position, the plugs, and the other COM radio.

Question. You hear the ground controller perfectly, but your radio check gets no reply. Which part of the chain is proven, and what do you check first?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: reception is proven. The receiver, the volume and the audio path all work. Transmission is not. Check the MIC selector is on the radio tuned to ground. Watch for the TX light when you press. And check the microphone plug is fully in.

## Bringing it together

You are departing Jandakot on a weekday morning. The engine is started with the avionics master off. The alternator is charging. Then the avionics master goes on, and both COMs and the NAV are powered and self-tested.

On the audio panel, the MIC is on COM 1, with COM 1 and COM 2 receive selected. You dial the ATIS into COM 1 standby, and flip it active. You open the squelch, set the volume against the hiss, close the squelch until the hiss just stops, and copy the ATIS. Ground goes active on COM 1, with the tower in standby.

You tune the VOR you will use on NAV 1, identify it by its Morse, then switch the ident off. Your passenger's headset works on the intercom. The VOX threshold is set above the engine noise. And you have briefed them to stay quiet when the radio is busy.

Microphone at your lips, you press, pause, and call ground with a radio check. Readability five comes back. And the TX light confirmed COM 1 was transmitting.

## Key points

Let's recap.

Selectors: select the transmitter, the MIC, and the receivers on the audio panel. Check you transmit on the radio you are listening to.

Switching on: the avionics master is off for start and shutdown, and on only once the alternator is charging. Watch each radio's self-test.

Frequencies come from ERSA and the charts. Dial them into standby, and transfer them to active. Australia uses 25 kHz spacing.

Squelch: open it to hear hiss, set the volume against the hiss, then close the squelch until the hiss just stops.

Navigation radios: tune and identify every aid by its Morse, and check the indication. Check the GNSS database and satellites.

Microphone: 1 to 2 cm from the lips, slightly to the side. Press, pause, speak normally, release.

Intercom: check each headset, keep the passenger address off during radio calls, and brief passengers to be quiet when the radio is active.

And VOX: set the threshold above engine noise but below normal speech. VOX works the intercom only, never the transmitter.

A final exam tip. The squelch is set by opening it until noise is heard, and then closing it until the noise just stops. The avionics master is off for engine start and shutdown, and on only after the engine is running and the electrical system is stable. VOX controls the intercom, not the transmitter. And the readability scale runs from 1, unreadable, to 5, perfectly readable.

That's the end of PAKC 3.2.
