This is PAKC 2.3, Flight instruments, at private pilot level. By the end you will be able to take indicated airspeed all the way to true airspeed, say which way each pressure instrument errs and by roughly how much, apply the southern-hemisphere compass rules, and use a compass correction card. There are worked examples and a few questions along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (PAKC 2.3)

The exam wants seven things from you. First, the pitot-static system, the alternate static source, and pressure error. Second, indicated, calibrated and true airspeed, and how one becomes the next. Third, how the three pressure instruments err, and what an instantaneous VSI gains you. Fourth, how the magnetic compass is built. Fifth, its turning and acceleration errors in the southern hemisphere. Sixth, the compass correction card. And seventh, the pre-flight checks of the primary instruments.

## Why this matters

The RPLA lesson taught how each instrument works and fails. Flying further and higher as a private pilot, the small errors you could ignore in the circuit start to matter.

Cruising at 8,500 ft to Kalgoorlie, the ASI reads nearly 20 kt less than your true airspeed. Plan with the wrong one, and every time and fuel figure in the plan is wrong. Crossing the Stirling Range on a cold winter morning, the altimeter over-reads by enough to eat into your terrain margin. And with the DI drifting, the compass is your heading reference. In the southern hemisphere it lies in a particular way every time you turn.

The exam asks for the airspeed chain, a true airspeed calculation, the directions of the errors, and the southern-hemisphere compass rules.

## The pitot-static system and its imperfections

The pitot-static system is the pitot tube, the static vent or vents, the lines joining them, and the three instruments they feed: the ASI, the altimeter and the VSI.

[[diagram:pitot-static-system]]
Picture the plumbing. The pitot tube faces into the airflow, and its line runs to the airspeed indicator and nowhere else. The static vent sits on the side of the fuselage, and its line branches to all three instruments: the airspeed indicator, the altimeter and the vertical speed indicator. Inside the cockpit, an alternate static source can stand in for a blocked vent. So pitot pressure goes only to the ASI, and static pressure feeds all three.

Air ramming into the pitot tube comes to rest, so the tube holds the surrounding pressure, plus the pressure of stopping the moving air. That is pitot pressure, also called total pressure. The flush static vent senses only the surrounding air's own pressure. That is static pressure. The ASI measures the difference, which is dynamic pressure. Dynamic pressure is a half, times the air density, times the speed squared. So it grows with air density, and with the square of the speed.

Now pressure error. The static vent has an impossible job. It must measure undisturbed air pressure, from a hole in an aeroplane that is disturbing the air around it. The fuselage speeds the flow up in some places and slows it in others, and pressure changes with it. No spot is right at every speed and attitude. The flow pattern changes with airspeed, flap setting and angle of attack, so the error changes too.

That difference, between the pressure the vent senses and the true static pressure, is pressure error. It is also called position error, because it depends on where the vent sits. The pitot tube can add a little of its own, when the airflow meets it at an angle.

Pressure error affects all three pressure instruments, but it shows most on the ASI. And it is largest at low speed with flap extended, because that is when the angle of attack is greatest and the flow around the fuselage most distorted. The ASI also carries a small instrument error from its own mechanism. The manufacturer measures both in flight testing, and publishes the correction as the airspeed calibration table, in section 5 of the POH.

Next, the alternate static source. Ice, insects or a wasp's nest can block the static vent. So many aeroplanes have an alternate static source. It is a valve under the panel that lets the instruments take static pressure from inside the cabin instead.

Air flowing fast over the cabin lowers the pressure outside the doors and windows, and the cabin leaks towards it. So in an unpressurised aeroplane in flight, the cabin pressure is slightly lower than true static.

Lower static pressure is what the instruments would see if you were higher and faster. So on the alternate source, the altimeter reads slightly high, and the ASI reads slightly high. And the VSI shows a brief climb at the moment you select it, then settles. The instruments are usable but less accurate, and the POH gives the corrections.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Pressure error is not a blockage, and not a fault. It is present in every serviceable aeroplane at every speed. It changes with speed and configuration. And the only cure is the POH calibration table.

Question. Why is pressure error usually largest at low speed with full flap?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the flow around the fuselage, and so the pressure at the static vent, depends on angle of attack and configuration. At low speed with flap down, the angle of attack is high and the flow pattern most distorted. So the vent is furthest from sensing true static pressure.

## From indicated to true airspeed

There are three airspeeds. Indicated airspeed, IAS, is what the needle shows. It is dynamic pressure, on a dial that assumes sea-level density. The wing also responds to dynamic pressure, so stall and limit speeds are given as indicated airspeeds.

Calibrated airspeed, CAS, is IAS corrected for pressure error and instrument error, from the POH calibration table. In the cruise, most light aeroplanes show CAS and IAS within a couple of knots. The gap opens at low speed with flap down.

True airspeed, TAS, is your actual speed through the air. It is CAS corrected for air density, which depends on pressure height and temperature. TAS is what navigation uses. Add the wind to it, and you get groundspeed, the speed over the ground.

[[diagram:airspeed-chain-ictg]]
Picture four boxes in a row, linked by arrows: indicated, calibrated, true, and groundspeed. For example, at 8,000 ft an indicated 100 kt is about 116 kt true. And a true airspeed of 105 kt into a 20 kt headwind gives 85 kt over the ground. Each step has one correction.

Why is TAS higher than CAS at height? Climb into thinner air and the density falls. So to make the same dynamic pressure, and the same reading, the aeroplane must be moving faster through the air. At height, the ASI reads less than the true speed. Heat does the same, because warm air is less dense. Density height, from the lesson on piston engines, combines both.

The rule of thumb is that TAS exceeds CAS by about 2 percent for every 1,000 ft of density height. As a formula: TAS is about CAS, times 1 plus 0.02 times the density height in thousands of feet. It is close enough for planning.

Let's work one: TAS on a warm day. The pressure height is 6,000 ft, the outside air temperature is plus 20 degrees, and the IAS is 105 kt. The POH calibration table gives a CAS of 103 kt. Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 8]]
Step one, the standard temperature at 6,000 ft. 15 minus 2 times 6 is 3 degrees.

Step two, the deviation. 20 minus 3 is plus 17 degrees.

Step three, the density height. 120 times 17 is 2,040. Add the 6,000, and the density height is 8,040 ft.

Step four, the TAS. 103, times 1 plus 0.02 times 8.04, is 103 times 1.161. That is {119.6|one hundred and nineteen point six}, call it 120 kt.

With 105 kt on the dial, you are moving through the air at about 120 kt. Plan the leg with 120 kt.

Now one for you to try. The pressure height is 4,000 ft, the outside air temperature is plus 23 degrees, and the CAS is 100 kt. What is the TAS?
[[pause 8]]
The standard temperature at 4,000 ft is 15 minus 2 times 4, which is 7 degrees. So the deviation is 23 minus 7, plus 16 degrees. The density height is 4,000 plus 120 times 16, which is 5,920 ft. The TAS is about 100 times 1.118, so about 112 kt. The flight computer gives about the same.

[[widget:density-height]]
Picture setting a pressure height and a temperature. Heat pushes the density height up fast. And every 1,000 ft of density height adds about 2 percent to your true airspeed.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. The chain runs in a fixed order. IAS, then CAS, for instrument and pressure error. Then TAS, for density. Then groundspeed, for wind. A distractor will define CAS as TAS corrected for wind, or IAS corrected for density. Density belongs to the CAS to TAS step only.

Question. At the same CAS and the same pressure height, is your TAS higher on a 35 degree day or a 15 degree day, and why?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: higher on the hot day. Warm air is less dense, so the density height is greater. The aeroplane must move faster through the air to produce the same dynamic pressure, and the same reading.

## How the pressure instruments err

The RPLA lesson worked through the blockages, and here they are as a recap. This section adds leaks, manoeuvres, lag, the instantaneous VSI, and temperature error.

[[diagram:pitot-static-blockages]]
Picture a grid of the three instruments against three faults. Pitot tube and drain both blocked: the ASI acts like an altimeter, over-reading in a climb and under-reading in a descent. With the drain clear, the ASI falls to zero. Static vent blocked: the altimeter freezes, the VSI reads zero, and the ASI under-reads in a climb and over-reads in a descent. On the alternate static, the altimeter and ASI read slightly high.

Take the ASI first, and leaks. A leak lets the wrong pressure in. A static line that leaks inside the cabin feeds the instruments with cabin pressure. So they behave as if the alternate static source were selected, and read slightly high. A pitot line that leaks loses some of its ram pressure. So the difference the ASI measures is too small, and the ASI under-reads.

Then manoeuvre-induced errors. The pressure at the static vent depends on the flow around the fuselage. An abrupt manoeuvre changes that flow suddenly: a sharp pull-out from a dive, a push-over, a sideslip. For a few seconds afterwards, the ASI, the altimeter and the VSI may all read incorrectly. Fly the attitude, and let them settle.

Now the VSI. With a static blockage, the capsule and the case both hold the same trapped pressure. So the VSI reads zero, whatever the aeroplane does.

Then lag. The case pressure catches up with the capsule only through a calibrated leak, so the VSI is always slightly behind. It shows the trend at once, but takes about 6 to 9 seconds to settle on the true rate. And on entering a climb or descent abruptly, it may briefly show the opposite trend, because the pitch change itself disturbs the static pressure. Use it for the trend, and confirm with the altimeter.

The instantaneous VSI, the IVSI, adds an accelerometer: a small weighted piston in a cylinder, held by springs, connected to the capsule line. When the aeroplane starts to climb or descend, the piston's inertia moves it, pumping a small pressure change into the capsule at once. As the vertical acceleration stops, the piston recentres, and the calibrated leak takes over. The IVSI removes most of the lag and the reverse indication. But it is still a static instrument, and a blocked vent stops it too.

Last, the altimeter. With a static blockage, the case pressure stops changing. So the altimeter freezes at the height where the vent blocked.

It lags too. In a rapid climb or descent, the capsules and linkage are a few seconds behind. Level off with that in mind.

Then an incorrect subscale setting. Each hectopascal of error moves the reading about 30 ft. And a setting too high makes the altimeter read too high. Flying from high to low pressure without resetting does the same. From high to low, look out below. The arithmetic is in the RPLA lesson.

[[widget:altimeter-subscale]]
Picture turning the subscale knob and watching the needle. Each hectopascal between the subscale and the real QNH moves the reading about 30 ft. Set too high a number, and you are lower than the altimeter says.

Then non-standard temperature. The altimeter is calibrated with the ISA, the standard atmosphere, which assumes a particular temperature at every height. With the correct QNH set, it reads correctly at the station that supplied the QNH. But the air between that station and you may be colder or warmer than standard.

Cold air is denser and more compact. So the pressure falls off faster with height, and the pressure levels are squeezed closer together. The pressure level your altimeter calls 4,000 ft sits physically lower than 4,000 ft. In cold air, you are lower than indicated. In warm air, the levels are stretched apart, and you are higher than indicated. The memory aid extends the pressure one. From high to low, or hot to cold, look out below.

The size of the error, as a rule of thumb, is about 4 percent of the height above the QNH station, for every 10 degrees the air differs from standard. It is small near the ground and grows with height. So it matters most for terrain clearance on a cold day. In a Western Australian summer, the error is in the safe direction.

Let's work one: a cold morning over the Stirling Range. It is a winter morning, and the air is about 10 degrees colder than standard. The correct QNH is set, from a station near sea level, and you are indicating 4,500 ft. What is your true altitude? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
The height above the QNH station is about 4,500 ft. The error is 4,500 times 0.04, times 10 over 10, which is 180 ft. The air is cold, so you are lower than indicated. Your true altitude is 4,500 minus 180, which is 4,320 ft.

The altimeter is set correctly and serviceable, and you are still 180 ft lower than it says. Over peaks of about 3,600 ft, the clearance you think is 900 ft is nearer 720 ft.

Now try the opposite case. On a summer afternoon, the air is 15 degrees warmer than standard. The QNH is correct and comes from a station near sea level, and you are indicating 6,500 ft. Roughly what is your true altitude?
[[pause 6]]
The error is 6,500 times 0.04, times 15 over 10. That is 6,500 times 0.06, which is 390 ft. Warm air stretches the pressure levels apart, so you are higher than indicated. Your true altitude is about 6,890 ft. On a hot day, the error is in the safe direction.

And non-standard pressure. The altimeter only knows the datum you give it. When the pressure changes along your route, the subscale error applies until you reset the area QNH.

Question. The area QNH is {1009|ten oh nine}, but the subscale still shows the {1016|ten sixteen} you set at departure. Is the altimeter reading high or low, and by about how much?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: high, by about 7 times 30, which is 210 ft. The subscale is set too high, so the altimeter over-reads, and you are about 210 ft lower than it shows.

## How the magnetic compass is built

Start with the magnets. Under the card of the direct-reading magnetic compass are two or more small magnetised needles, fixed to the card and balanced on a pivot. A freely suspended magnet lines up with the Earth's field, so the needles, and the card, align with the horizontal part of that field, and point to magnetic north. The aeroplane turns around the card, and you read the heading against the lubber line.

Then the fluid. The bowl is filled with a light, clear fluid, usually a kerosene-like oil. It damps oscillation, so the card settles quickly instead of swinging after every turn or bump. And it reduces friction: the card floats, taking weight off the pivot, so it turns freely.

The fluid must fill the bowl, and be free of bubbles. A bubble means fluid has leaked. The card is no longer fully damped and floated, so it swings and sticks, and the pivot can corrode. A compass with a bubble is unserviceable.

Now pendulosity, and why it causes errors. The Earth's field does not lie flat. Away from the equator, its lines slope into the ground, steeply near the magnetic poles. This slope is dip. In the southern hemisphere, the south-seeking end of a free magnet is pulled downwards. A card tilted by dip would rub, stick, and read wrongly.

The cure is to make the magnet assembly pendulous. It hangs below its pivot, like a pendulum, so that gravity holds it roughly level against the pull of dip. To balance that pull, its centre of gravity sits a little to the equator side of the pivot. In the southern hemisphere, that means the north end.

That pendulous design is also the cause of the compass's errors. Whenever the aeroplane accelerates, turning or changing speed, it pushes the pivot. But the assembly's centre of gravity lags behind, like a passenger thrown sideways in a car. Because the centre of gravity is offset from the pivot, that lag rotates the magnets. And the card shows a heading change that has not happened.

## Turning and acceleration errors in the southern hemisphere

All the rules that follow come from one fact. In the southern hemisphere, the assembly's centre of gravity is on the north side of the pivot. And it lags behind whenever the aeroplane accelerates.

Acceleration errors first. Fly due east and accelerate. The pivot is pushed forward, towards the east. The centre of gravity, on the north side of the pivot, lags behind, towards the west. That swings the north end of the magnets backwards. The card rotates, and the compass shows a heading change towards south. Heading west, acceleration again shows a turn towards south. Decelerate, and the centre of gravity surges forward instead, showing a turn towards north.

The southern-hemisphere memory aid is SAND. South on Acceleration, North on Deceleration. The errors are largest on east and west headings, and nil on north and south. Heading north or south, the centre of gravity is directly ahead of or behind the pivot. So pushing it back or forward produces no rotation.

Now turning errors. In a turn, the aeroplane is accelerating towards the centre of the turn, and the lagging centre of gravity swings outwards.

Turning through south, the north end of the assembly is behind the pivot. The outward swing rotates the magnets the same way the aeroplane is turning. So the card turns with the aeroplane, and the reading changes too slowly. The compass is sluggish: it lags. At higher latitudes it may even start by showing a turn the wrong way. So you must roll out early, before the compass shows the heading you want. Undershoot south.

Turning through north, the north end is ahead of the pivot. Now the swing rotates the magnets against the turn. The card moves the opposite way to the aeroplane, and the reading changes too fast. The compass is lively: it leads. So you must roll out late, after it has passed the heading you want. Overshoot north.

The turning errors are largest when turning through north or south, and nil when turning through east or west, where the outward swing is along the magnets' axis. The usual allowance is about 30 degrees at a rate one turn in mid latitudes, and less at lower latitudes. On headings in between, allow a proportion of it. Northern-hemisphere books give the opposite rules, {UNOS|you-noss} and {ANDS|ands}. So check which hemisphere a text was written for.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Learn the action, not just a slogan. Some sources say overshoot south, undershoot north, describing the card rather than the pilot, and mixing up which way it leads or lags. An answer that rolls out early through south and late through north is the southern-hemisphere answer, whatever words surround it.

Because of both errors, the compass is read only in steady, straight, level, unaccelerated flight. And the directional indicator is reset to it only then.

Question. In level flight, heading {270|two seven zero}, near Perth, you open the throttle to climb power. What does the compass do, and what is the real heading doing?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the compass swings towards south. That is SAND, south on acceleration. So it shows a turn to the left, onto a lower heading, even though the real heading has not changed. Wait until the speed is steady before reading it.

## The compass correction card

The compass also feels the aeroplane itself. The steel in the engine, the magnetos, the radios, and the currents in the wiring all make magnetic fields of their own. The result is deviation: the difference between the heading the compass shows and the true magnetic heading. Because the aeroplane's own field turns with it, deviation changes with heading.

[[diagram:true-magnetic-compass]]
Picture three arrows fanning out from one point, each called north. True north runs along the meridian. Magnetic north is offset from it by the variation. And compass north is offset again, by the deviation from the aeroplane itself. For example, a track of {120|one two zero} degrees true, with variation 3 degrees east, gives {117|one one seven} magnetic. Deviation of 2 degrees west then gives {119|one one nine} compass.

An engineer measures deviation in a compass swing, lining the aeroplane up on known magnetic headings and adjusting small compensating magnets. What is left is recorded on the compass correction card, also called the deviation card, mounted beside the compass. Its purpose is to tell you which compass heading to steer, to achieve a given magnetic heading.

Picture a card with two rows. The top row is the magnetic heading you want, every 30 degrees from 000 to {180|one eight zero}. The bottom row is the compass heading to steer. For 000, steer 002. For 030, steer 033. For 060, steer 064. For 090, steer 092. So far the compass reads more than magnetic, by a few degrees. For {120|one two zero}, steer {120|one two zero}: no deviation. Then it reads less. For {150|one five zero}, steer {148|one four eight}. And for {180|one eight zero}, steer {176|one seven six}.

Where the compass reads more than magnetic, deviation is west. Where it reads less, deviation is east. The memory aid is: deviation east, compass least. Deviation west, compass best. The card must be current. The compass is re-swung after avionics changes, or any work that alters the aeroplane's magnetic field.

Let's work one: the compass heading to steer. Use that card. Your flight plan gives a magnetic heading of 075. What do you steer? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
Step one, the card entries either side. For 060, steer 064. For 090, steer 092.

Step two, the deviation at each. At 060 it is 4 degrees, and the compass reads more, so 4 degrees west. At 090 it is 2 degrees west.

Step three, 075 is halfway between. So the deviation is about 4 plus 2, over 2, which is 3 degrees west.

Step four, the compass heading to steer. 075 plus 3 is 078.

Steer 078 on the compass, and the aeroplane is flying 075 magnetic.

The card works in reverse too, when you want to know the magnetic heading you are actually flying. Try this one. In steady level flight the compass reads {162|one six two}. Using the card, what is your magnetic heading?
[[pause 6]]
A compass reading of {162|one six two} lies exactly halfway between the steer figures {148|one four eight}, for {150|one five zero}, and {176|one seven six}, for {180|one eight zero}. Deviation there runs from 2 degrees east, to 4 degrees east. So halfway it is about 3 degrees east. Compass least, so magnetic is more. {162|one six two} plus 3 is {165|one six five} magnetic.

Question. Why does deviation change with heading, when variation does not?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: deviation comes from the aeroplane's own magnetic fields, which turn with the aeroplane. So their effect on the compass changes as the heading changes. Variation is a property of the place on the Earth, the same whichever way you point.

## Checking the instruments before flight

The RPLA lesson gave the checks, and the reason for each. The PPL exam adds a few figures.

[[diagram:gyro-instruments-panel]]
Picture three gyro instruments side by side. On the left, the attitude indicator, and in the middle, the directional indicator, reset to the compass about every 10 to 15 minutes. Both are normally vacuum driven. On the right, the turn coordinator, with its ball, normally electric. You check each one, and the suction gauge, after start.

The pressure instruments are checked before start. The ASI reads zero at rest, or roughly the wind if you are parked facing it. The pitot cover is off, and the tube, the drain and the static vents are clear, because a blocked vent will not show on the ground. And where the POH calls for it, check the pitot heat warms the tube.

For the altimeter, set the QNH and compare the reading with the aerodrome elevation. It must be within 100 ft for VFR. That figure is in the AIP, so verify it against the current text. The subscale should move freely. And where two altimeters are fitted, they should agree.

The VSI reads zero on the ground. If it shows a small steady value, note it, and treat that as the zero in flight.

The gyro instruments are checked after start, and on the taxi. The attitude indicator erects within about 5 minutes of start, with no warning flag. It shows level with the aeroplane level, and tilts no more than about 5 degrees in taxi turns. The suction gauge is in the green.

The directional indicator is set to the compass. It turns the correct way in taxi turns, and stays within a few degrees of the compass when straight, with no flag. The turn coordinator shows a turn in the direction of the taxi turn, with the ball to the outside, and no flag.

Then the magnetic compass. The bowl is full, and the fluid clear with no bubbles. The card swings freely and settles. It turns the correct way in taxi turns, and reads close to the runway direction when you line up. And the deviation card is present and legible.

After take-off, the airspeed is alive on the roll, and the altimeter and VSI show the climb.

Question. On the taxi, the attitude indicator banks about 15 degrees in each taxi turn. Is it serviceable, and what might be the cause?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: no. A serviceable attitude indicator should stay within about 5 degrees in taxi turns. A gyro that tilts like this may not be up to speed, from low suction or a worn instrument. Check the suction gauge, and have it looked at before you rely on it.

## Bringing it together

On a June morning, you plan Jandakot to Albany at 7,500 ft. The planning: a CAS of 105 kt, a pressure height of 7,500 ft, and air 5 degrees colder than standard. The standard temperature at 7,500 ft is 0 degrees, so the temperature is about minus 5 degrees. The density height is about 7,500 minus 600, which is 6,900 ft. That gives a TAS of about 105 times 1.138, or about 119 kt.

Before start, the altimeter reads within 30 ft of the Jandakot elevation, and the compass is full and clear. En route, the cold air puts you about 7,500 times 0.04 times 0.5 lower than indicated. That is 150 ft. You reset the DI from the compass only in straight, level, unaccelerated flight.

Turning onto a southerly heading with the compass alone, you roll out early, because turning towards south the compass lags. And descending towards Albany's higher ground, with a stale QNH and cold air, you remember that both altimeter errors point the same way. Lower than you think.

## Key points

Let's recap.

Pitot pressure feeds only the ASI, and static feeds all three. Pressure error is the vent's failure to sense true static, largest at low speed with flap, and the POH table removes it. The alternate static source reads slightly high.

IAS is the dial. CAS corrects it for instrument and pressure error. TAS corrects CAS for density, about 2 percent more per 1,000 ft of density height. Add the wind for groundspeed.

A pitot leak makes the ASI under-read. Abrupt manoeuvres upset all three for a few seconds. The VSI lags 6 to 9 seconds, and the IVSI's accelerometer removes most of that. A static blockage freezes the altimeter and zeroes the VSI. Subscale error is 30 ft per hectopascal. And cold air puts you lower, by about 4 percent per 10 degrees. Hot to cold, look out below.

The compass fluid damps and floats the card, and a bubble makes it unserviceable. The pendulous, offset magnet assembly counters dip, and causes the errors. In the southern hemisphere: SAND, on east and west headings. In turns, it lags through south, so roll out early, and leads through north, so roll out late. Deviation east, compass least. Deviation west, compass best.

And the pre-flight checks. ASI at zero, covers off. Altimeter within 100 ft of the elevation, for VFR. VSI at zero. Attitude indicator erect within about 5 minutes, and within about 5 degrees in taxi turns. Compass full, no bubbles, card present.

A final exam tip. The southern-hemisphere compass rules are the ones examined, and northern-hemisphere books give the opposite. Have the airspeed chain in order: IAS, CAS, TAS, groundspeed. And have three figures ready: 2 percent per 1,000 ft for TAS, 30 ft per hectopascal for the subscale, and hot to cold, look out below, for temperature error.

That's the end of PAKC 2.3.
