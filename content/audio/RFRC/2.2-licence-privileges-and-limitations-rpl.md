This is RFRC 2.2, Licence privileges and limitations, for the recreational pilot licence. By the end you will know what the RPL lets you do and where it stops, what keeps it current, which medical certificates it can rest on, and what you may sign on the maintenance release. You will also be able to work through "may this pilot do this?" scenarios one limit at a time. There are a few questions along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (RFRC 2.2)

The exam wants four things from you. First, what the RPL does not let you do: the limits on the aircraft, the conditions, the distance, the airspace and the height. Second, what you must do to keep using the licence: the flight review, recent experience for passengers, a current medical, and the documents. Third, which medical certificates an RPL holder may use, and the limits each one brings. And fourth, what a licence holder may do about the daily inspection, the maintenance release and defects.

## Why this matters

The recreational pilot licence is the first licence most WA students hold, and many fly on it for years. Weekend flights from Serpentine or Murrayfield. A friend in the right seat. A coffee at a country strip. It is a real licence with real privileges. But every privilege comes with a fence, and the fences are where pilots get into legal trouble without meaning to. A passenger carried with no recent landings. A flight review that lapsed last month. A second passenger on a medical that allows one.

The exam asks this subtopic in two ways: as straight recall of a handful of figures, and as "may this pilot do this?" scenarios. This lesson explains why each fence is where it is. Then the figures make sense, and the scenarios become easy to reason through.

## What the RPL lets you do, and where it stops

Start with what the RPL is for. It was designed for flying close to home, in simple aeroplanes, in good weather, with the option to grow it into something bigger one endorsement at a time. Every limit follows from that design. The licence assumes the least demanding conditions, and you earn your way out of each limit by training for it.

Here is the core. The holder of a recreational pilot licence with an aeroplane category rating may fly as pilot in command of a single-engine aeroplane, certified for single-pilot operation, with a maximum take-off weight of not more than 1,500 kg. That maximum take-off weight is often shortened to MTOW. And the RPL holder may carry passengers. The rules are in CASR Part 61, the licensing part of the Civil Aviation Safety Regulations. Verify the current text.

Around that core sit seven limits. The first: day VFR only, which means day, under the visual flight rules. No night flying, and no flight in IMC, the instrument meteorological conditions. The RPL training contains no instrument flying beyond the basics, and no night flying. So the licence stops at the conditions you were trained for.

The second: private operations or flight training only. No aerial work, and no commercial operations. Nobody pays you to fly, and you do not fly anyone for hire.

The third: within 25 nm of the departure aerodrome, or in a designated flight training area, unless you hold a recreational navigation endorsement. The basic licence teaches you to fly the aeroplane, not to navigate it. 25 nm keeps you in country you can find your way back from.

The fourth: non-controlled aerodromes and Class G airspace only, unless you hold the controlled aerodrome endorsement and the controlled airspace endorsement. Talking to a tower and following clearances are separate skills, trained and tested separately.

The fifth: below 10,000 ft, unless you hold the flight above 10,000 ft endorsement, and the aircraft and the oxygen rules allow it.

The sixth: only aircraft within your class rating, which is single-engine aeroplane, and with any design feature endorsements the aeroplane needs. There are five design features: tailwheel, retractable undercarriage, manual propeller pitch control, pressurisation, and floats. A tailwheel aeroplane handles differently on the ground. A retractable undercarriage can be forgotten. Each feature has its own training.

And the seventh: no aerobatics unless you hold the aerobatic endorsement. And no formation, spinning or other such activity without the relevant endorsement.

[[diagram:rpl-privileges-map]]
Picture the bare licence on the left, and the endorsements lined up on the right. On the left is the RPL as it is first issued: single-engine, single-pilot, not more than 1,500 kg, day VFR, private or training, within 25 nm, non-controlled aerodromes and Class G, below 10,000 ft, and passengers allowed. On the right are the endorsements, each one lifting one of those limits: recreational navigation, controlled aerodrome, controlled airspace, flight above 10,000 ft, design features, and aerobatics.

The pattern is the point. Some limits can be lifted by an endorsement on the RPL itself. Distance, with the navigation endorsement. Controlled aerodromes and airspace. Height, above 10,000 ft. Design features. And aerobatics. Others belong to the licence, and no endorsement removes them. Those are four: single-engine, not more than 1,500 kg, day VFR, and private operations. To get past those you need a higher licence. The PPL, the private pilot licence, removes the weight, distance and airspace limits. And the CPL, the commercial pilot licence, allows commercial operations.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. The 1,500 kg is the aeroplane's maximum take-off weight, the certified figure in the flight manual. It is not what the aeroplane weighs today. A single with an MTOW of 1,600 kg, flown light with one person on board, is still outside the RPL.

Let's work one: may this RPL holder fly this flight? Sam holds an RPL with the recreational navigation endorsement and the controlled aerodrome endorsement, but not the controlled airspace endorsement. Sam wants to take a friend from Murrayfield to York, in a four-seat single-engine aeroplane of 1,100 kg MTOW, on a Saturday morning. Pause if you'd like to try it first.
[[pause 6]]
Take the limits one at a time. First, the aircraft. It is single-engine and single-pilot, and 1,100 kg is not more than 1,500 kg. That is within the licence.

Second, the conditions. Day VFR, and a private flight with a friend. That is within the licence.

Third, the distance. York is well beyond 25 nm, but Sam holds the recreational navigation endorsement. Allowed.

Fourth, the airspace. The route must stay in Class G, and remain clear of controlled airspace, because Sam has no controlled airspace endorsement. The controlled aerodrome endorsement alone does not cover the airspace en route.

And fifth, the passenger. Allowed by the licence, subject to the recency and medical rules that come next.

So the answer: the flight is legal, if Sam plans a route that stays outside controlled airspace, and meets the passenger rules.

Question. An RPL holder adds every endorsement available. Can they now fly a light twin at night?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: no. Single-engine, day VFR and the 1,500 kg MTOW limit belong to the RPL itself, and no endorsement lifts them. A twin needs a multi-engine class rating and a higher licence. And night flying needs a night VFR rating on a PPL or higher.

## Keeping the privileges current

A licence, once issued, does not expire. But skills fade, and the regulations recognise two different kinds of fade. Your general handling fades slowly, over years. Your take-off and landing feel fades quickly, over weeks. So there are two currency rules, with two different time windows, matched to what they protect.

The first rule is the flight review, and it answers the question: can you still fly at all? You must complete an aeroplane flight review with an instructor at least every 24 months. The review is valid to the end of the 24th month after it was done. Verify the current text.

The review is a flight and a briefing, in which an instructor checks that your handling, your knowledge of the rules and your decision making are still at licence standard. It is not a pass-or-fail flight test in the same sense as the licence test. But if the instructor is not satisfied, you do not get the sign-off until you are.

Without a current flight review, you cannot fly as pilot in command at all, with or without passengers. You may still fly dual with an instructor, which is exactly how you would get back to standard.

The second rule is recent experience, and it answers the question: can you carry someone else? To carry a passenger, you must have done at least 3 take-offs and 3 landings in the previous 90 days, in the same category of aircraft. Verify the current text.

The logic is that take-offs and landings are where most accidents happen, and where your skill decays fastest. Ninety days is short enough to catch a pilot who has not flown for a season. Three of each is enough to shake off the rust. If you are not current, the fix is simple. Fly solo circuits first, then take your passenger.

The rule is about passengers. Alone in the aeroplane, you may fly with a current flight review even if your last landing was four months ago. The regulations accept that risk to yourself, but not to someone who is trusting you.

[[diagram:rpl-recency-timeline]]
Picture two look-back windows, both ending today, one short and one long. The short window reaches back 90 days. To carry a passenger, you need at least 3 take-offs and 3 landings inside it, in the same category of aircraft. The long window reaches back 24 months. To fly as pilot in command at all, you need an aeroplane flight review inside it, valid to the end of the 24th month after it was done.

Then there are the other things you must keep in order. There are five. First, a medical certificate that is current and appropriate to the licence. That comes in the next section. Second, your logbook, kept up to date, because it is the evidence of the flight review and the recent experience. The logbook has its own lesson, on documentation. Third, the licence document, carried with your medical certificate and photo identification when you fly. Check the current requirement for an electronic licence. Fourth, English language proficiency, which is assessed as part of the licence. And fifth, the endorsements and class ratings for the aircraft and the operation.

Let's work one: is this pilot current to take a passenger? Today is 3 October. Priya's logbook shows a flight review completed on 18 August, two years ago. Her last three flights were on 2 June, with two take-offs and two landings. On 24 August, with one of each. And on 20 September, with one of each. Pause if you'd like to try it first.
[[pause 6]]
Step one, the flight review. It was completed in August two years ago. So it is valid to the end of the 24th month after it, which is the end of August this year. It has expired. Priya may not fly as pilot in command at all.

Step two, recent experience, just for interest. The 90 days before 3 October reach back to early July. So the June flight does not count. Only the August and September flights count. That is 2 take-offs and 2 landings, short of 3.

So there are two problems, and they come in an order. First, Priya needs a flight review with an instructor. That will also count towards recent experience. Then, if the review does not bring the total to 3 take-offs and 3 landings in 90 days, she flies some solo circuits before the passenger flight. The answer again: the review has expired, and recency is 2 and 2, short of 3.

Question. Why is the passenger rule 90 days, but the flight review 24 months?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: they protect against different kinds of skill fade. Landing and take-off feel decays within weeks, so recency for passengers looks back 90 days. General handling and knowledge decay slowly, so the full review is needed only every 24 months.

## The medical certificates

Every licence rests on a medical certificate, because no amount of skill helps a pilot who loses consciousness. The RPL is unusual in giving you a choice of three. And the choice follows one principle: the easier the medical is to get, the tighter the limits that come with it.

A GP who knows you can assess you to a driving standard. But a driving standard was written for a car. So the flying it allows is restricted to aircraft and operations close to driving in risk.

[[diagram:rpl-medical-certificates]]
Picture three cards side by side, one for each certificate. Let's take them from the hardest to get to the easiest.

The first card is the Class 2 medical certificate. It is issued by a DAME, a designated aviation medical examiner, with CASA. A DAME is a doctor trained in aviation medicine and approved by CASA, who examines you to an aviation standard. It is the standard private pilot medical. In return for that harder exam, it carries no operational limits beyond the licence's own. It is valid 4 years if you are under 40, and 2 years if you are over 40. Check the current validity periods.

The second card is the Basic Class 2. It is issued by any GP, to the commercial driver medical standard. That is stricter than the standard for a private car. Its limits are private operations, day VFR, below 10,000 ft, piston aircraft up to 8,618 kg, and no more than 5 passengers.

The third card is the RAMPC, the recreational aviation medical practitioner's certificate. It is issued by any GP, to the private driver standard, which is the easiest to meet. So it carries the tightest limits. A single-engine piston aircraft up to 1,500 kg. Day VFR. Below 10,000 ft. No more than 1 passenger. No aerobatics. And private operations. The rules are in CASR Part 67, and on CASA's pages for the RAMPC and the Basic Class 2. Verify the current limits.

So the passenger figures run 1 for the RAMPC, up to 5 for the Basic Class 2, and no extra limit for the Class 2.

Now notice that the licence limit and the medical limit stack. You are bound by whichever is tighter.

[[diagram:tighter-limit-applies]]
Picture two comparisons. In the first, the Basic Class 2 would allow piston aircraft up to 8,618 kg. But the RPL allows only 1,500 kg. So 1,500 kg applies. In the second, a four-seat aeroplane has three passenger seats, and the RPL allows passengers. But a RAMPC allows no more than 1 passenger. So 1 applies, however many seats the aeroplane has. The tightest limit wins.

Next, fitness on the day. A certificate says you were fit when you were examined. It says nothing about today. Whatever the certificate, you must not fly if you know or suspect you are temporarily unfit. That covers illness, injury, medication and fatigue. And you must tell CASA or a DAME of a medically significant condition that could affect your ability to fly safely. The rules on alcohol, drugs and temporary unfitness are taught in the lesson on conditions of flight.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. "No more than 1 passenger" on a RAMPC is a limit of the medical, not of the licence or the aeroplane. A four-seat aeroplane and a valid RPL do not let a RAMPC holder take two friends.

Now try one yourself. Jo holds an RPL with the navigation endorsement, and a RAMPC. Jo plans a day VFR flight to Northam, in a 1,150 kg single-engine piston aeroplane, with a partner and their teenage son. May Jo take both?
[[pause 5]]
The answer: no. The aircraft, the conditions and the distance are all within the licence and the RAMPC. But the RAMPC allows no more than 1 passenger, and Jo plans two. Jo can take one of them. Or Jo can obtain a Basic Class 2, which allows up to 5 passengers, or a Class 2, before taking both.

Question. Why does a medical issued to a private driving standard come with more limits than one issued by a DAME?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: a driving standard assesses fitness for a car, not for flight. So it is accepted only for flying close to driving in risk: one passenger, a light single-engine piston aeroplane, day VFR. The DAME's aviation examination supports the full privileges of the licence.

## Daily inspections, the maintenance release and defects

In the lesson on documentation, you met the maintenance release as the document that tells you whether the aeroplane may fly. This part is about what you, as a licence holder, may write on it, and what you may not. The dividing line is simple. Engineers certify airworthiness. Pilots certify what they have personally checked and observed.

Start with the daily inspection. Before the first flight of each day, the aeroplane needs a daily inspection. That is a thorough check, to the manufacturer's or the operator's daily inspection schedule. It is more than the walk-around before each flight.

A pilot licence holder may carry out and certify the daily inspection, on three conditions. The aircraft is a Class B aircraft, used for private or aerial work. It is an aircraft they are about to fly. And they are trained and authorised to do it by the operator or school. The rule is regulation {42ZC|forty-two Z C} of the Civil Aviation Regulations {1988|nineteen eighty-eight}. Verify the current text. The certification goes in Part 3 of the maintenance release, the daily inspection and time-in-service record, and it covers that day.

The reason you may do it is practical. Aeroplanes fly from places where no engineer is present at {0700|oh seven hundred}. The reason you must be trained and authorised is that the inspection is only worth what the inspector knows to look for.

[[diagram:daily-vs-preflight-inspection]]
Picture two inspections side by side. On the left is the daily inspection. It is done once a day, before the first flight, to the daily inspection schedule. It is done by a licensed engineer or an authorised pilot. It is certified on the maintenance release, and it is valid for the rest of the day. On the right is the pre-flight inspection. It is done before every flight, as the walk-around in the POH, by the pilot in command, and it is not certified.

Next, signing the maintenance release. A pilot cannot issue a maintenance release. Only a licensed engineer or an approved organisation can, because issuing it certifies that the scheduled maintenance has been done properly. What a pilot does sign on the maintenance release is three things. First, the daily inspection certification, if authorised. Second, the flight time after each flight, which keeps the hours remaining accurate. And third, any defects found.

Here's another trap the exam likes to set. An exam option that says the pilot may "issue" or "renew" the maintenance release is wrong. The pilot signs on the release, for the daily inspection, the flight time and defects. But the pilot never issues it.

Last, reporting defects. A pilot who finds a defect must endorse it in Part 2 of the maintenance release. The endorsement is how the defect reaches the engineer and every later pilot. A defect you mention only to a friend in the clubhouse does not exist, as far as the next pilot is concerned.

[[diagram:defect-endorsement-flow]]
Picture a flow from left to right. It starts with a defect found, either on the ground, or in flight and then written up after landing. Next, it is endorsed in Part 2 of the maintenance release, the defect section. The entry has a description, the date and the time in service, and your signature and licence number. Then the aircraft stays on the ground. It cannot fly until a licensed engineer rectifies the defect and signs it off, or endorses it as a permissible unserviceability, with any conditions.

Beyond the maintenance release, the pilot in command must also report defects that affect safety to the operator. And major defects and reportable matters go through the transport safety reporting system. That means the ATSB, the Australian Transport Safety Bureau, for accidents and incidents, which is covered in the lesson on emergencies and search and rescue. And it means CASA's defect reporting, for airworthiness defects.

Question. You are authorised to do daily inspections on the club aeroplane. During one, you find a cracked exhaust. What may you certify, and what must you do?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: you must not certify the aircraft as serviceable. Endorse the defect in Part 2 of the maintenance release, and tell the operator. The aircraft stays on the ground until a licensed engineer rectifies it, or endorses it as a permissible unserviceability.

## Bringing it together

Alex holds an RPL with the navigation endorsement, and a Basic Class 2. The flight review was done fourteen months ago. Alex last flew six weeks ago, with two landings at Serpentine. Now Alex wants to take two friends to Narrogin, in the club's 1,050 kg four-seater, on Sunday.

Alex goes through the limits in turn. The licence: single-engine, under 1,500 kg, day VFR, private, and beyond 25 nm with the endorsement. With a route planned to stay in Class G, that is fine. The flight review: well within 24 months. The medical: the Basic Class 2 allows up to 5 passengers, so two are fine. Recent experience: only 2 take-offs and 2 landings in the last 90 days. So Alex flies a couple of solo circuits on Saturday.

On Sunday morning Alex, trained and authorised by the club, does the daily inspection and signs it, and checks Part 2. After the flight, Alex writes the time in the maintenance release, and the flight in the logbook. Every rule in the lesson came up, and each one had a reason Alex could see.

Question. Which two currency checks must an RPL holder pass before taking a passenger, and which one also stops a solo flight?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: a flight review within 24 months, and 3 take-offs and 3 landings in the previous 90 days. Only the flight review also stops a solo flight. Recent experience applies to carrying passengers.

## Key points

Let's recap.

The limits. Single-engine, single-pilot, MTOW not more than 1,500 kg, day VFR, private or training. Within 25 nm without the navigation endorsement. Class G and non-controlled aerodromes without the controlled airspace and controlled aerodrome endorsements. Below 10,000 ft. Design features and aerobatics need their endorsements. Endorsements lift some limits, but single-engine, 1,500 kg, day VFR and private operations stay.

Currency. A flight review every 24 months, to act as pilot in command at all. 3 take-offs and 3 landings in the previous 90 days, to carry a passenger. A current medical, and an up-to-date logbook. And carry the licence, the medical and photo identification.

Medicals. The Class 2, from a DAME, with no extra limits. The Basic Class 2, from a GP to the commercial driver standard, up to 5 passengers, piston aircraft up to 8,618 kg. The RAMPC, from a GP to the private driver standard, 1 passenger, single-engine piston up to 1,500 kg, no aerobatics. Do not fly when unfit, and report significant conditions.

The maintenance release. A trained and authorised pilot may certify the daily inspection. A pilot signs for the daily, the flight time and defects, but never issues the release. Defects go in Part 2, and to the operator, with serious matters to the ATSB and CASA.

A final exam tip. These are the numbers the exam likes. 1,500 kg. 25 nm. 3 take-offs and landings in 90 days. A flight review every 24 months. And 1 passenger with a RAMPC. In scenario questions, check each limit in turn: aircraft, conditions, distance, airspace, recency, and medical. Don't look for the one that jumps out. The distractor is usually the limit you skipped. And check the current CASR Part 61 wording before your exam, because the licensing rules are amended from time to time.

That's the end of RFRC 2.2.
