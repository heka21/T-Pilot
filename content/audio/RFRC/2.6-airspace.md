This is RFRC 2.6, Airspace. The sky is divided into volumes with different rules, and none of the boundaries can be seen out of the window. By the end you will know what each class of airspace means for a VFR aeroplane, which charts show where controlled airspace is, and how to find out whether an area is active today. There are a few questions along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (RFRC 2.6)

The exam wants two things from you. First, how controlled airspace differs from non-controlled airspace, and what each class means for a VFR aeroplane. And second, which documents show where controlled airspace is, and how you tell whether a prescribed area is active or not.

## Why this matters

Fly south from Jandakot, and you are under Perth's Class C steps. Fly north-east, and you pass near RAAF Pearce's military zone and restricted areas. Head inland, and you are soon in Class G, with nobody controlling anything. The sky over the Perth basin is divided into volumes with different rules, and none of the boundaries are visible out of the window.

As an RPL holder without the controlled airspace endorsement, you must stay out of controlled airspace altogether. And every pilot must stay out of an active restricted area without a clearance.

An airspace infringement means flying into controlled or restricted airspace without a clearance. It is among the occurrences most often reported in Australia. And it usually comes from a pilot who did not know where the boundary was, or did not check whether an area was active.

The exam asks two things. What the difference between the classes means for you. And which document tells you where and when. This lesson answers both from first principles.

## Why airspace is divided at all

Picture the sky near a major airport on a weekday morning. Airliners are descending in cloud on instrument approaches. Others are climbing out at 250 knots. Light aeroplanes are on training flights, and a helicopter is on a news job. If every one of them relied on seeing the others, an airliner coming out of cloud at 2,000 ft would have seconds to spot a Cessna it had no idea was there. Near busy airports, see and avoid alone is not enough. So a controller takes on the job of keeping aircraft apart.

Away from the busy airports, the traffic is light, and most of it is VFR. The cost of controlling every flight would buy little safety. There, pilots keep themselves apart by looking out, and by talking on the radio.

That is the whole idea of airspace classification. Controlled airspace is where an air traffic controller issues clearances and instructions, and you may not enter or operate without a clearance. Non-controlled airspace is where there is no clearance. You fly by the rules of the air, broadcast on the appropriate frequency, and avoid other traffic yourself.

There are two terms you will see on charts and in the AIP. A CTA, a control area, is controlled airspace that starts at some height above the ground. A CTR, a control zone, is controlled airspace that starts at the surface, around a controlled aerodrome. That way, aircraft taking off and landing there are inside it.

## The classes, one by one

Australia uses the ICAO classes, labelled by letter. ICAO is the International Civil Aviation Organization, and the classes are set out in the AIP. Verify the current text. The letters are not a ranking of importance. Each one is a different package of what ATC, air traffic control, does, and what it requires from you.

Think of each class as answering three questions for a VFR aeroplane. Do I need a clearance? Who keeps me apart from other aircraft? And what must I carry?

Here is the pattern first. Classes A, C, D and E are controlled. Class G is the only non-controlled class. In Class C and Class D, you need a clearance. In Class E, a VFR flight does not need a clearance, but it is still controlled airspace. And Class A is closed to VFR altogether. Now the reasoning behind each one, from the top down.

Class A is the high-level airspace where the airliners cruise. It is the upper airspace, generally from FL180 along the east coast and FL245 elsewhere. Verify that on the current charts. Everything there is IFR, under the instrument flight rules, and fully separated. A VFR aircraft relying on see and avoid at jet speeds has no place in it. So VFR flight is not permitted.

Class C surrounds the major airports, such as Perth. It also covers much of the en route airspace above Class E or G. It is the busiest airspace a light aeroplane is likely to enter, with fast IFR traffic mixing with VFR. So ATC takes on more. A clearance is required before entry. ATC separates IFR from VFR. And ATC gives VFR flights traffic information about other VFR. Because ATC needs to see you and talk to you to do this, a transponder and a radio are required.

Class D is the airspace around regional towered aerodromes, such as Jandakot. The traffic is mostly light aircraft at lower speeds, many of them in the circuit. A clearance is still required. And the tower controls the circuit. It tells you which runway, when to turn, and when you are cleared to land. ATC separates IFR from IFR, and gives traffic information to VFR. But it does not separate VFR from VFR, or VFR from IFR. The tower tells you who is around, and you keep your own spacing with what you can see.

Class E is the in-between class. It is controlled for IFR, not for VFR. IFR flights receive clearances and separation, but VFR flights do not need a clearance. They must meet the VMC minima, the larger cloud clearances you met in the lesson on conditions of flight. And they must carry a serviceable transponder, squawking {1200|one two zero zero}, and a radio, keeping a listening watch on the area frequency. That is so that ATC, and the IFR traffic it is separating, can see and hear them. Only an aircraft without an engine-driven electrical system is exempt from the transponder.

Class G is non-controlled. No clearance is required, and pilots are responsible for their own separation, using lookout and radio. A flight information service, and traffic information, are available on request. But nobody is controlling you. At non-controlled aerodromes in Class G, you use CTAF procedures. That means broadcasting your position and intentions on the CTAF, the common traffic advisory frequency. Most of WA's airspace below the en route steps is Class G, and most of your early flying will be in it.

Picture two columns, compared row by row. On the left is controlled airspace: Class C and D, and the control zones. A clearance is required. ATC separates traffic. In Class C, it separates IFR from VFR. In Class D, it separates IFR from IFR, with traffic information to VFR. Class C requires a transponder and radio. And an RPL holder needs the controlled airspace and controlled aerodrome endorsements. On the right is non-controlled Class G. No clearance. Pilots separate themselves by lookout and radio. And Class A is IFR only.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Class E is controlled airspace, even though a VFR flight needs no clearance to enter it. The exam likes to offer "Class E is non-controlled" as a distractor. Class G is the only non-controlled class.

Question. In Class D, the tower clears you to land behind a Piper on final. Who is responsible for keeping you apart from the Piper?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: you are. In Class D, ATC separates IFR from IFR, and gives traffic information to VFR. It tells you about the Piper and sequences you. But keeping your spacing behind it is your job, by sight.

## How the classes fit together in three dimensions

The classes are volumes, stacked and nested.

[[diagram:airspace-cross-section]]
Picture a slice through the sky over an imaginary region, not a real place. At a major airport, Class C starts at the surface, in the control zone. Then it extends outwards in steps. These are rings of controlled airspace whose lower limit rises with distance from the airport, like an upside-down wedding cake. Under each step, between the ground and the step's lower limit, the airspace is usually Class G. You may fly there without a clearance, as long as you stay below the step. Above the steps, and away from the airports, sits Class E in some areas, with Class A at high level. At a regional tower, such as Jandakot, a Class D control zone sits from the surface up to its published upper limit, often tucked beneath the Class C steps. And there is a restricted area in the picture too.

This is why the boundary you must watch is often vertical, not horizontal. Flying south from the Perth area, you can be in Class G at 1,500 ft, with Class C starting at 2,500 ft above you. Climb through that lower limit without a clearance, and you have infringed controlled airspace, even though you never crossed a line on the ground.

So who may fly where? An RPL holder needs the controlled airspace and controlled aerodrome endorsements before flying in Class C or D, or at a towered aerodrome. That is covered in the lesson on licence privileges and limitations. Without those endorsements, your airspace is Class G and non-controlled aerodromes.

Alongside the classes are areas set aside for particular activities. These are the prohibited, restricted and danger areas, together called PRD areas. And there is other special-use airspace, such as military operating areas.

[[diagram:prd-areas-entry]]
Picture three panels side by side. In the first, a prohibited area. It may not be entered at all, and the aircraft's track stops at the boundary. In the second, a restricted area. It is treated like controlled airspace. When it is active, you need the controlling authority's clearance to enter it. Without one, stay out. Military flying, weapons ranges and rocket launches use them. In the third, a danger area. It warns of an activity that could be hazardous, for example parachuting or gunnery. You may enter, but knowing the risk is your responsibility. So: prohibited means never. Restricted means only with a clearance, when active. And danger means you may enter, but the risk is yours to know.

Question. You are in Class G, below a Class C step whose lower limit is 3,500 ft. Do you need a clearance, and what limits your climb?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: no clearance is needed while you stay in Class G, below the step. The step's lower limit caps your climb. You must stay below 3,500 ft, unless you obtain a clearance to enter the Class C, and hold the endorsement to do so.

## Finding the boundaries: the charts

Since the boundaries cannot be seen, you find them on charts. There are four documents that show controlled airspace. First, the Visual Terminal Chart, the VTC, at the largest scale, around the major airports. Second, the Visual Navigation Chart, the VNC, for wider areas. Third, the En Route Chart Low, the ERC-L. And fourth, the Terminal Area Chart.

They show the lateral boundaries and the vertical limits of each control area step, and of the control zones. A label such as {"C LL 4500"|C L L forty-five hundred} means Class C with a lower limit of 4,500 ft. The step begins at 4,500 ft, and you are in Class G below it, unless another class sits there.

The WAC, the World Aeronautical Chart, shows no airspace. It is a topographic chart for finding your way. Using it alone near Perth is how infringements happen. The AIP sections ENR 2 and 3, and the Designated Airspace Handbook, describe the airspace in words. You rarely need them as a VFR pilot, but they are the legal definition behind the charts.

[[diagram:chart-features-panel]]
Picture four chart panels side by side. The first is the WAC, at {1:1,000,000|one to one million}. It shows topography, towns, roads, railways, rivers and aerodromes, but no airspace. The second is the VNC, at {1:500,000|one to five hundred thousand}. It adds controlled airspace, PRD areas, frequencies and reporting points to the topography. The third is the VTC, at {1:250,000|one to two hundred and fifty thousand}. It has detailed topography, with control zones, control area steps, PRD areas, VFR routes and frequencies. And the fourth is the ERC-L. It shows airspace, air routes, navigation aids, frequencies and PRD areas, but no topography at all.

Here's another trap the exam likes to set. The WAC is a good navigation chart, and it shows no airspace at all. An exam option offering the WAC for airspace boundaries is wrong every time.

## Is it active? Hours, ERSA and NOTAM

A chart shows where an airspace is. It does not tell you whether it is in force today. Many controlled zones and restricted areas operate only at certain times, and outside those times the rules change. So the second half of the job is checking when.

Start with control zones at regional towers. Control zones and Class D airspace at regional towers may only operate during the tower's published hours. A regional tower is staffed for the busy part of the day, not all night.

[[diagram:class-d-tower-hours]]
Picture the same regional towered aerodrome twice, side by side, seen from the side. On the left, during the tower's published hours, the control zone is Class D. A clearance is required, and the tower controls the circuit. On the right, outside those hours, the airspace reverts to Class G, or Class E above. And the aerodrome becomes a CTAF aerodrome, where you fly non-controlled procedures and broadcast on the CTAF. The hours are in ERSA for the aerodrome. And they may be changed by NOTAM, for example for a staff shortage or a public holiday.

Next, restricted and danger areas. They show their hours of activity in the ERSA PRD section, and in the AIP, at ENR 5.1. The hours come in one of four forms. {H24|H twenty-four}, which means always. HJ, which means sunrise to sunset. Specified UTC times. Or "by NOTAM", which means active only when a NOTAM says so. A NOTAM can activate, deactivate or change them. And if an area is active and you do not have a clearance, you must stay out.

Temporary restricted areas are set up for bushfires, air shows or VIP movements. They are promulgated by NOTAM, and sometimes by AIP Supplement. A bushfire in the Perth Hills in summer can create one overnight.

Military control zones and restricted areas, around bases such as RAAF Pearce, are active at the published hours, and otherwise as notified.

Put that together, and the check before every flight near controlled or restricted airspace is a sequence of four steps. Each step is answered by a different source.

[[diagram:airspace-active-check]]
Picture four steps in a row. Step one: the chart, the VTC, VNC or ERC-L, for the boundaries and limits. Where is it? Step two: ERSA, its PRD section, and AIP ENR 5.1, for the hours of activity and the controlling authority. When is it normally active, and who controls it? Step three: the NOTAMs, for activations, changes, and temporary restricted areas. Is today different? And step four: if in doubt, call the controlling authority or ATC on the published frequency. At the end of the row, the rule: an active restricted area needs a clearance, and without one, you stay out.

[[diagram:flight-documents-flow]]
Now picture that sequence applied to one restricted area. The VNC, VTC or ERC-L shows its identifier and its vertical limits: where and how high. The ERSA PRD section and AIP ENR 5.1 give its hours, its controlling authority, and its conditional status. NOTAMs can activate it or change it. And when in doubt, you call the controlling authority.

Let's work one: can you route through that restricted area on Saturday? You plan a Saturday flight north-east of Perth. Your VNC shows a restricted area across your direct track. Work through the four steps. Pause if you'd like to try it first.
[[pause 6]]
Step one, the chart. The VNC gives the area's boundary, its vertical limits, and its identifier. Your planned altitude is inside those limits, so it matters whether the area is active.

Step two, the ERSA PRD section. You look up the identifier. Suppose the entry says the area is active "by NOTAM", with the controlling authority named. That means it is inactive unless a NOTAM says otherwise.

Step three, the NOTAMs. You check the area NOTAMs for Saturday. Suppose there is one activating it from {0100|zero one hundred} to {0800|zero eight hundred} UTC. WA's local time is UTC plus 8, which is covered in the lesson on time. So that is {0900|oh nine hundred} to {1600|sixteen hundred} WST, Western Standard Time. The area is active for your whole flight.

Step four, the decision. Without a clearance, you must stay out. Plan a route around it, or a time outside the activation, or contact the controlling authority before the flight.

Had there been no NOTAM, the "by NOTAM" entry would have meant the area was inactive, and you could route through. So the answer again: with that NOTAM, the area is active from {0900|oh nine hundred} to {1600|sixteen hundred} local, and you stay out.

Now try one yourself. On a Sunday, you plan to land at a regional towered aerodrome at {1830|eighteen thirty} local, after its ERSA tower hours have ended for the day. No NOTAM changes the hours. What airspace will you be in, and which procedures do you use?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: outside the tower's hours, the Class D zone reverts to Class G, or Class E above, and the aerodrome becomes a CTAF aerodrome. You use non-controlled procedures. You broadcast your calls on the CTAF, and arrange your own separation. No clearance is needed. And an RPL holder without the controlled aerodrome endorsement could land there at that time.

Question. A restricted area's ERSA entry says {H24|H twenty-four}. A NOTAM says it is deactivated this weekend. Is it active on Saturday?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: no. ERSA gives the standing hours, but a NOTAM can activate, deactivate or change a PRD area, and it is the more recent information. On Saturday, the area is inactive.

## Bringing it together

You hold an RPL with the navigation endorsement, but no controlled airspace endorsement. You want to fly from Murrayfield to Northam and back. Out of the window, there is nothing to tell you where you may fly. On the VTC and VNC, there is everything.

You trace your route. Suppose it passes under Class C steps near the coast. So you plan an altitude comfortably below each step's lower limit, reading each "C L L" label in turn.

North-east of Perth, the chart shows the military zone and restricted areas around RAAF Pearce. You look up the ones near your track in the ERSA PRD section, for their hours and controlling authority. Then you check the NOTAMs, to see whether anything is active on the day. One is active by NOTAM during your planned times, so you route around it.

Northam is a non-controlled aerodrome in Class G, so you plan CTAF calls there. The flight stays in Class G throughout, as your licence requires. And every boundary was found on paper before engine start.

Question. Which three sources together tell you where a restricted area is, when it is normally active, and whether today is different?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the chart, the VTC, VNC or ERC-L, for where. ERSA's PRD section, or AIP ENR 5.1, for the normal hours and the controlling authority. And the NOTAMs, for any change today.

## Key points

Let's recap.

The classes. Controlled airspace is Classes A, C, D and E. There, ATC issues clearances and separates some or all traffic, and you need a clearance to enter, except as VFR in Class E. Class C: a clearance, IFR separated from VFR, and a transponder and radio. Class D: a clearance, the tower controls the circuit, and traffic information to VFR. Class E: controlled for IFR, no clearance for VFR, but a transponder and radio are required. Class G, the only non-controlled class: no clearance, separate yourself, information on request, and CTAF at aerodromes. PRD areas sit alongside, and an active restricted area needs a clearance.

The documents. The VTC, the VNC, the ERC-L and the Terminal Area Chart show the boundaries and limits. {"C LL 4500"|C L L forty-five hundred} means Class C from 4,500 ft. The WAC shows none. ERSA gives the tower hours and the PRD hours, along with AIP ENR 5.1, and NOTAM changes them. Outside tower hours, a Class D zone becomes Class G, or E above, and a CTAF aerodrome. Chart for where, ERSA for when and who, NOTAM for today, and call if in doubt.

A final exam tip. Class G is the only non-controlled class. Class E is controlled airspace, but VFR flights do not need a clearance in it. The WAC never shows airspace. And ERSA plus NOTAMs tell you whether an area is active. When a question asks "which document", decide whether it is asking where, which is a chart, when, which is ERSA, or what has changed, which is NOTAM. And confirm the class boundaries and requirements against the current AIP and charts, because the airspace around Perth is redesigned from time to time.

That's the end of RFRC 2.6.
