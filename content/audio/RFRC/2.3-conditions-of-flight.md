This is RFRC 2.3, Conditions of flight. These are the rules you fly by on every single flight: who gives way, how to behave around an aerodrome, the weather you need to fly visually, the alcohol rules, light signals, minimum heights and last light. By the end you will have a short list of figures by heart, and a reason for each one. There are a few questions along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (RFRC 2.3)

The exam wants six things from you. First, the rules of the air: right of way, the rules on and near an aerodrome, take-off and landing separation where there is no tower, smoking, the visual flight rules and weather minima below 10,000 ft, and altimetry. Second, the rules on alcohol and drugs, and on being temporarily unfit to fly. Third, what the tower's light signals mean. Fourth, the minimum heights over towns and elsewhere. Fifth, the limits on aerobatics and on flying over crowds. And sixth, the requirement to land before the end of daylight.

## Why this matters

These rules start before you even reach the aeroplane. They decide whether last night's drinks let you fly at all, how high you cross the Darling Scarp, and who gives way when you and another aeroplane arrive at Serpentine's circuit together. Most of them exist because someone died without them. And most of them have a logic you can rebuild once you see it. Every avoiding turn goes the same way. Every minimum height is a margin to glide clear or to see and avoid. Every light signal pairs a colour with a meaning you could guess.

The exam tests this subtopic almost entirely by recall of specific figures. So by the end you need a short list by heart: 8 hours. 500 ft. 1,000 ft within 600 m. 3,000 ft. 600 m and 1,800 m. 5,000 m, 1,500 m and 1,000 ft. And 10 minutes. This lesson gives each one its reason, so that it sticks. One caution: these are exactly the figures CASA adjusts, so confirm each one against the current Visual Flight Rules Guide.

## Rules of the air: who gives way

Two aeroplanes in the same piece of sky need a rule that both pilots know without talking. The rules of the air are that rule, and they rest on one organising idea. When in doubt, turn right. And the less manoeuvrable aircraft has priority.

Before the details, one principle overrides all of them. The pilot in command is always responsible for avoiding a collision. That holds whatever clearance you have been given, and whatever the other aircraft ought to be doing. Having right of way never excuses you from looking out, and giving way anyway if the other pilot has not seen you.

[[diagram:right-of-way-rules]]
Now picture four small scenes, each with two aeroplanes. In the first, they are converging at about the same level. The rule is that the aircraft that has the other on its right gives way. Think of a roundabout, where traffic from your right has priority.

In the second scene they are head-on, or nearly so. Here both aircraft alter heading to the right. If both turn the same way, they pass clear. The rule only works because everyone turns right.

In the third, one aeroplane is overtaking another. The aircraft being overtaken has right of way. The overtaking aircraft alters heading to the right, and stays clear until it is well past. That applies whether it is climbing, descending or level.

In the fourth, two aircraft are both approaching to land. The lower one has right of way. But it may not use that to cut in front of, or overtake, an aircraft already on final. More generally, an aircraft landing or on final approach has right of way over aircraft in flight and on the ground.

Two more rules sit underneath those scenes. Between different types, power-driven aeroplanes give way to airships, gliders and balloons, and to any aircraft towing something or carrying a sling load, because those can manoeuvre least. And an aircraft in distress has right of way over everything.

On the ground the same logic applies. Aircraft on the manoeuvring area give way to aircraft taking off or landing. Converging, give way to the one on your right. Head-on, both turn right. Never fly or taxi so close to another aircraft that you create a collision hazard. And no formation flying without prior arrangement between the pilots.

Question. Two aeroplanes are converging at the same level. One sees the other at its two o'clock. Which one gives way, and which way does it turn?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the one that sees the other on its right gives way, and it turns right, to pass behind. The other one holds its heading and speed, while still watching in case the first pilot has not seen it.

## On and around the aerodrome

An aerodrome without a tower works because everyone flies the same pattern, in the same direction, at the same height. The rules make that pattern law.

[[diagram:circuit-pattern]]
Picture the standard circuit from above, as a long rectangle with the runway along one side. You take off and climb straight ahead on the upwind leg. You turn onto crosswind, then downwind, flying parallel to the runway in the opposite direction. Then base, descending, and finally onto final approach and the runway. The side of the runway away from the circuit is called the dead side.

The circuit is flown in the published direction, which is left-hand unless the ERSA entry or the ground signals show right-hand circuits for that runway. All turns in the circuit are in the circuit direction. Standard circuit height is 1,000 ft above aerodrome level for most light aeroplanes. Very low performance types use 500 ft, and high performance aircraft use 1,500 ft, as the Part 91 Manual of Standards sets out. Take off and land into wind on the runway in use, conform to the pattern of the other traffic, keep a lookout, and vacate the runway promptly after landing.

Turns after take-off have their own rule, because the aeroplane is low, slow and heavy, and the circuit below is full of traffic. You climb straight ahead to at least 500 ft above aerodrome level before turning. The exceptions are a turn needed to avoid terrain, or to follow a published procedure. Either way, the turn is then made in the circuit direction. Five hundred feet is the height from which a recovery from a mishandled turn still has a chance.

At a non-controlled aerodrome you make the standard calls on the CTAF, the common traffic advisory frequency. You call when taxiing, entering the runway, inbound, joining, turning base, and whenever else safety needs it. Those calls are how other pilots build a picture of where you are.

[[diagram:overhead-join]]
One common way to join the circuit is from overhead. Picture crossing above the aerodrome, higher than circuit height. You descend on the dead side, the side away from the circuit, down to circuit height. Then you cross the upwind end of the runway, join the crosswind leg, and carry on downwind with everyone else.

Question. How high must you be before the first turn after take-off, and what are the exceptions?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: at least 500 ft above the aerodrome. The exceptions are a turn needed to avoid terrain, or to follow a published departure procedure. And the turn is still made in the circuit direction.

## Separation for take-off and landing at a non-controlled aerodrome

Without a tower, you are your own controller for runway spacing, and the rules set the minimum. The logic is runway occupancy and wake. The aircraft ahead must be clear of the runway, or clear of your path, before you commit.

[[diagram:takeoff-separation]]
Picture the runway from the side, with you lined up at one end and another aircraft ahead of you. You must not take off until that preceding aircraft has done one of four things. First, it has landed and vacated the runway. Second, it has taken off and either crossed the upwind end of the runway, or started a turn. Third, if the runway is longer than 1,800 m, it is airborne and at least 1,800 m ahead of your intended lift-off point. And fourth, if both aircraft have a maximum take-off weight under 2,000 kg, it is airborne and at least 600 m ahead of your intended lift-off point.

Landing works the same way. You must not land until the aircraft ahead has vacated the runway and is taxiing clear, or has taken off and crossed the upwind end or started a turn, or is airborne the same 1,800 m or 600 m ahead of your touchdown point, under the same conditions.

The 600 m figure is the one you will use most. Two training aeroplanes at Murrayfield are both under 2,000 kg. So once the one ahead is airborne and 600 m down the runway, you may roll. But if the one ahead is a 2,500 kg twin, the 600 m rule is off. You wait for it to cross the upwind end, or start its turn.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. The 600 m rule needs both aircraft under 2,000 kg, not just yours. And the 1,800 m rule depends on the runway being longer than 1,800 m, not on the distance alone.

Question. You are number two for take-off, behind a 1,100 kg aeroplane that has just lifted off. When may you start your take-off roll?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: once it is airborne and at least 600 m ahead of your intended lift-off point, because both aircraft are under 2,000 kg. Or earlier, if it has already crossed the upwind end or started a turn. If your own aeroplane were over 2,000 kg, the 600 m option would not apply.

## Smoking

This one is short and absolute. Smoking in an aircraft is prohibited during take-off, landing and refuelling. It is also prohibited whenever the no-smoking signs are on, and whenever the pilot in command says so. Nobody smokes anywhere near an aircraft being refuelled. Fuel vapour, oxygen and an open flame are the reasons.

## VFR and visual meteorological conditions

The visual flight rules, VFR, rest on a bargain. You may fly without air traffic control keeping you separated, because you can see and avoid other aircraft and terrain. The visual meteorological conditions, VMC, are the minimum weather in which that bargain holds. You need enough flight visibility to see another aircraft in time to turn away. And you need enough distance from cloud that an aircraft popping out of it does not appear in front of you with no time to react. That aircraft would be flying under the instrument flight rules, IFR, and it is allowed to be in there.

To fly VFR below 10,000 ft you must stay in VMC. You navigate by visual reference to the ground or water, at least when you are below 2,000 ft above it. And you meet the minima for the class of airspace you are in.

[[diagram:vmc-minima-by-airspace]]
Picture a cloud with an invisible box drawn around it, a keep-out zone. Here is the pattern. Visibility is the same in every case: 5,000 m. The distance from cloud is 1,500 m horizontally and 1,000 ft vertically. That applies in Class C, in Class E, and in Class G when you are up high. There are two exceptions.

The first exception is Class D, where the box is smaller. Keep 600 m horizontally, 1,000 ft above cloud and 500 ft below it.

The second exception is low in Class G. At or below 3,000 ft above mean sea level, or 1,000 ft above the ground, whichever is higher, you only need to be clear of cloud and in sight of the ground or water. Visibility is still 5,000 m.

Why is the low Class G case relaxed? Close to the ground, in uncontrolled airspace, there is no IFR traffic descending out of cloud on an instrument approach into your path. And the ground itself is your reference. Why are the controlled airspace cases not relaxed? In Class C, D and E there are IFR aircraft in cloud, and the margins give you time to avoid one that emerges. Class D gets smaller margins because the tower knows where the IFR traffic is, and keeps it apart from the VFR circuit by procedure. Above 10,000 ft, the visibility requirement rises to 8 km, because closing speeds are higher. In a control zone you also need a clearance from the tower. And special VFR allows lower visibility, but only with a clearance.

[[diagram:airspace-cross-section]]
To know which minima apply, you need to know which airspace you are in. Picture a slice through the sky near a major airport. A Class C control zone rises from the surface at the airport. Around it, Class C steps sit like an upside-down wedding cake, with their lower limits rising the further out you go. A regional tower has its own Class D zone from the surface. Class E sits above the steps, and underneath them all is Class G, the uncontrolled airspace where most training happens.

Question. You are at 2,500 ft in Class G, over flat ground near Pinjarra, 1,000 m horizontally from a cloud. Are you in VMC?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: yes, provided the flight visibility is at least 5,000 m, and you are clear of cloud and in sight of the ground. At or below 3,000 ft above mean sea level, or 1,000 ft above the ground, whichever is higher, Class G only asks for clear of cloud, not 1,500 m. Climb into Class C or E, and 1,000 m from cloud would not be enough.

## Altimetry below 10,000 ft

Every altimeter in an area must agree, or vertical separation means nothing. The altimetry rules make them agree by giving everyone the same datum.

Before take-off, set the aerodrome QNH. You get it from the tower, the ATIS or the AWIS, the automatic weather broadcast. If none is available, set the altimeter to read the aerodrome elevation, and the subscale then shows the local QNH. The lesson on take-off and landing performance covers that in more depth.

In flight below the transition altitude of 10,000 ft, you fly on QNH. Either the local QNH of a station within 100 nm, or the area QNH from the area forecast. Area QNH is issued for 3-hour periods.

From 10,000 ft up to FL110 is the transition layer. At or above it, you set {1013 hPa|ten thirteen hectopascals} and fly flight levels. Descending, you set QNH again when leaving FL110. The recreational licence does not take you up there, but the exam may ask.

There is also a serviceability check. With the correct QNH set, the altimeter must read the aerodrome elevation within 100 ft for VFR. If it does not, it is unserviceable.

Next, cruising levels. Below 10,000 ft, the hemispherical rule keeps opposite-direction traffic 1,000 ft apart.

[[diagram:vfr-cruising-levels]]
Picture a compass rose split down the middle into an eastern half and a western half. On a magnetic track from {000|zero zero zero} to {179|one seven nine} degrees, the eastern half, a VFR flight above 5,000 ft flies odd thousands plus 500 ft. That means 5,500, 7,500 or 9,500 ft. On a track from {180|one eight zero} to {359|three five nine} degrees, the western half, it flies even thousands plus 500 ft. That means 6,500 or 8,500 ft. Below 5,000 ft, any altitude may be flown, subject to minimum heights and good practice. A memory hook: odd and east go together, because east is the first half of the compass.

Question. You are cruising VFR at 7,500 ft from Perth towards Kalgoorlie, on a magnetic track of {095|zero nine five} degrees. Which QNH do you fly on, and is 7,500 ft a correct level?
[[pause 5]]
The answer: below 10,000 ft you fly on QNH. That is either the local QNH of a station within 100 nm, or the area QNH from the forecast for the current 3-hour period. Track {095|zero nine five} is in the eastern half, {000|zero zero zero} to {179|one seven nine}, so you fly odd thousands plus 500. 7,500 ft is correct.

## Alcohol, drugs and medical fitness

The alcohol rules come in two parts: a time and a level. The time: you must not act as a crew member within 8 hours of drinking alcohol, nor while you are under its influence. The level: for safety-sensitive aviation activities, the blood alcohol limit is 0.02 grams per 100 millilitres, which in practice means none. CASA and the police can test you.

[[diagram:alcohol-and-blood-levels-timeline]]
Eight hours is a minimum, not a guarantee. Picture a timeline of a heavy night. The sixth standard drink is finished at {2300|eleven p m}. The liver removes roughly one standard drink an hour, and nothing speeds it up. So blood alcohol is not near zero until about {0500|five a m}. The 8-hour rule makes {0700|seven a m} the earliest legal time to fly. And the hangover can last well beyond that.

Drugs follow the same idea. You must not fly under the influence of any drug that impairs you. That includes prescription and over-the-counter medicines. A decongestant that makes you drowsy counts. Check any medication with a DAME, a designated aviation medical examiner, before flying.

Then there is temporary medical unfitness. If you are temporarily unfit, through injury, illness, surgery, pregnancy complications, or a condition that needs medication, you must not fly. If the condition lasts more than a short period, or is medically significant, you must tell a DAME or CASA, and they decide when you may fly again. Fitness is your call every day. Your medical certificate does not override a head cold.

Question. A pilot finished their last drink at {2200|ten p m}, and plans to depart at {0700|seven a m}. Is that legal?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: nine hours have passed, so the 8-hour minimum is met. But the pilot must also be under 0.02, and not under the influence. If the night involved several drinks, they may still be over the limit at {0700|seven a m}. The 8 hours is a floor, not a clearance.

## Light signals

If your radio fails in a control zone, or the tower needs to talk to an aircraft without one, it uses a signal lamp. The colours follow a pattern. Green is permission. Red is prohibition. White means go back. And a flashing light is a weaker version of a steady one.

Start with an aircraft in flight. Steady green: cleared to land. Steady red: give way to other aircraft and continue circling. Green flashes: return for landing, and a clearance to land will follow. Red flashes: the aerodrome is unsafe, do not land. White flashes: land at this aerodrome, and after landing proceed to the apron. And a red pyrotechnic, a red flare, means do not land for the time being, whatever you were told before.

Now an aircraft on the ground. Steady green: cleared for take-off. Steady red: stop. Green flashes: cleared to taxi. Red flashes: taxi clear of the landing area in use. White flashes: return to your starting point on the aerodrome.

To acknowledge in flight, rock the wings by day, or flash the landing light at night. On the ground, move the ailerons or rudder, or flash the lights at night.

Question. A steady red light is directed at you on final. What does it mean, and what do you do?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: give way to other aircraft and continue circling. Go around, stay in the circuit, and watch for the next signal. A steady green will clear you to land. On the ground, the same steady red would mean stop.

## Minimum heights

There are two heights, for two reasons. Over people, you must be high enough to glide clear if the engine fails. Elsewhere, you must be high enough not to endanger or disturb anyone on the ground.

[[diagram:minimum-heights]]
Picture two side views. On the left, an aeroplane over a town. Over a populous area, which means a city or town, and also over a public gathering, you must not fly below 1,000 ft above the highest obstacle within a 600 m radius of the aircraft. Notice the datum: the highest obstacle within 600 m, not the ground. Over Perth's city centre, that puts the floor well above 1,000 ft above sea level.

On the right, an aeroplane over open country. Anywhere else, you must not fly below 500 ft above the highest feature or obstacle within a 300 m radius of the point directly below the aircraft. Over flat open country or water, that is 500 ft above the surface.

There are exceptions: taking off and landing, operations CASA has approved such as low-level training in approved areas, a forced landing, and flight needed to avoid weather or terrain. And whatever the height, you must always be able to glide clear of a populous area and land without undue hazard if the engine fails.

Question. What is the minimum height over a town, and from where is it measured?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: 1,000 ft above the highest obstacle within a 600 m radius of the aircraft. Not 1,000 ft above the ground. Elsewhere, the minimum is 500 ft above the highest feature or obstacle within 300 m of the point below you.

## Aerobatics and public gatherings

Aerobatic flight, which the rules call acrobatic flight, has four conditions. The aircraft must be certified for it. It must be day VMC. The pilot must hold the aerobatic endorsement, or be under instruction. And it must be no lower than 3,000 ft above the ground, unless CASA has approved a lower height. Never over a populous area or a public gathering. Passengers may only be carried if the rules for passenger carriage during aerobatics are met.

Flight over a public gathering, such as a sporting fixture, a race meeting, a concert or an air show crowd, comes under the populous area rule. That is 1,000 ft above the highest obstacle within 600 m. And air displays over gatherings need CASA approval.

## Landing before the end of daylight

A VFR flight by a pilot without a night VFR rating must be planned so that the aircraft is on the ground at least 10 minutes before the end of daylight, which is last light. That applies at the destination or the alternate, after allowing for any holding. First and last light are published for each place in the daylight and darkness graphs in the AIP, and online. They depend on the date and the place.

Western Australia's east-west spread makes this bite. Last light at Kalgoorlie is well before last light at Perth on the same evening. So a flight planned on Perth's figure arrives at Kalgoorlie in the dark.

Question. You are planning to arrive at Bunbury, where last light is {1915|nineteen fifteen} local. By when must you be on the ground?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: by {1905|nineteen oh five} local, 10 minutes before last light, with any holding you might need allowed for before that. If the plan does not achieve that, leave earlier or land short.

## Bringing it together

Here is a Saturday in May: Jandakot to Bunbury and back, with every rule in play.

The night before, you stop at one glass at {2100|nine p m}, because you brief at {0800|eight a m}. Eight hours is comfortably met, and so is the limit. At the aeroplane you set the ATIS QNH, and the altimeter reads within 100 ft of the 99 ft elevation. Departing runway {24L|two four left}, you climb straight ahead to 500 ft before the left turn.

Clear of the zone, you climb to 4,500 ft on a track of about {175|one seven five} degrees magnetic. Above 5,000 ft you would need an odd thousand plus 500. But you are below it, so any altitude that meets the minimum heights will do. You fly on the Perth QNH until you are more than 100 nm from it, and then on area QNH.

Over farmland near Harvey, your floor is 500 ft. Over Bunbury itself, it is 1,000 ft above the highest obstacle within 600 m. There is scattered cumulus with tops near 3,500 ft, and 8 km visibility. At 4,500 ft in Class G, above 3,000 ft above sea level, you need 1,500 m horizontally and 1,000 ft vertically from cloud. So you hold 1,000 ft above the tops, or weave 1,500 m around the taller ones.

At Bunbury you join the published circuit, and give way to the aircraft lower on approach. On departure you wait for the Jabiru ahead to be 600 m down the runway before you roll. Last light is {1800|eighteen hundred}, so you plan wheels down at Jandakot by {1750|seventeen fifty} at the latest. Every rule in the lesson has appeared, and none of them needed a tower.

## Key points

Let's recap.

Right of way: turn right to avoid. Give way to the aircraft on your right, to the one you are overtaking, to the lower one on approach, and to anything less manoeuvrable. Circuits are left-hand unless published, at 1,000 ft, with no turn below 500 ft after take-off.

Behind another aircraft, wait until it has vacated, crossed the upwind end or turned. Or until it is airborne 600 m ahead, if both are under 2,000 kg. Or 1,800 m ahead, on a runway longer than 1,800 m. No smoking during take-off, landing or refuelling.

VMC below 10,000 ft: 5,000 m visibility, and 1,500 m and 1,000 ft from cloud. In Class D: 600 m, 1,000 ft above and 500 ft below. Low in Class G: clear of cloud and in sight of the ground. Below 10,000 ft fly on QNH, local within 100 nm, or area QNH. Above 5,000 ft, odd plus 500 on eastern tracks, even plus 500 on western tracks.

Alcohol: 8 hours bottle to throttle, and 0.02 grams per 100 millilitres. No impairing drugs. Do not fly while temporarily unfit, and tell a DAME or CASA if it lasts.

Light signals: green permits, red prohibits, white sends you back, and flashes are the weaker version. Steady green: land, or take off. Steady red: circle, or stop.

Minimum heights: 1,000 ft above the highest obstacle within 600 m over a populous area. 500 ft above the highest feature or obstacle within 300 m elsewhere.

Aerobatics: certified aircraft, endorsed pilot, day VMC, not below 3,000 ft above the ground, never over a populous area or a gathering. Gatherings come under the populous area rule.

And daylight: on the ground at least 10 minutes before last light, holding included.

A final exam tip. The questions in this subtopic are mostly straight recall, so have the figures by heart. 8 hours. 1,000 ft within 600 m, and 500 ft within 300 m elsewhere. 3,000 ft for aerobatics. 500 ft before turning after take-off. 600 m and 1,800 m. 5,000 m, 1,500 m and 1,000 ft. 10 minutes before last light. And odd plus 500 eastbound. Read the condition in each question carefully. "Both aircraft under 2,000 kg" and "runway longer than 1,800 m" are where the distractors live. And confirm each figure against the current Visual Flight Rules Guide, because these are the figures CASA changes when it updates Part 91.

That's the end of RFRC 2.3.
