This is RFRC 2.5, Aerodromes. An aerodrome talks to you in paint and shapes, and this lesson teaches you to read it. By the end you will be able to name the parts of an aerodrome and the legs of the circuit, find the dead side for any runway, and say what each marking, marker, cross and ground signal means. There are a few questions along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (RFRC 2.5)

The exam wants four things from you. First, on a diagram of your training aerodrome, point out the movement area and its parts, and say what the taxiway, runway and helipad markings tell you. Second, on a circuit diagram, name the upwind, crosswind, downwind and base legs, and locate the dead side. Third, say what a white cross on the movement area means. And fourth, recognise and explain the runway, threshold and runway end markings, the cone and gable markers, the taxiway markings, holding points and bays, and two white crosses beside the windsock.

## Why this matters

An aerodrome talks to you in paint and shapes. At a busy field like Jandakot, the markings tell you where you may taxi, where you must stop, and where the landing area begins. At a country strip, with no radio traffic and nobody in the clubhouse, the cones, gables and crosses may be the only information you get about which parts of a gravel runway are safe.

Get them wrong, and you taxi onto an active runway, land on a closed one, or touch down among gliders. The circuit, meanwhile, is the shared pattern that lets a dozen aircraft use one runway without a controller.

The exam shows you diagrams and asks you to name what you see: a leg of the circuit, a marker, a cross. This lesson builds each one from the reason it exists, so the names follow.

## The parts of an aerodrome

Picture the aerodrome from above. There are places where aeroplanes park, places where they move about on the ground, and places where they take off and land. The vocabulary simply names those parts.

The movement area is the part of the aerodrome used for take-off, landing and taxiing. It has two parts. The first is the manoeuvring area: the runways and taxiways, where aircraft move under their own power to and from take-off and landing. The second is the apron, where aircraft park, load and refuel.

The distinction matters because the rules differ. On the apron, aircraft are parked, people walk about, and fuel trucks move. On the manoeuvring area, everything is moving with a purpose. And at a controlled aerodrome, you need a clearance to be there at all. The grass, buildings and roads around them are not part of the movement area.

[[diagram:movement-area-plan]]
Picture a plan of a small aerodrome. The runway and the taxiways are shaded together, as the manoeuvring area. The apron is shaded separately. A dashed line runs around both, and that is the movement area. Outside the line are the grass, the buildings and the road, which are not part of it. So the rule is a sum. The movement area is the manoeuvring area, which is the runways and taxiways, plus the apron.

When you look at your training aerodrome's diagram in ERSA, trace the movement area. Find the apron, follow the taxiways out to each runway, and notice that the runways and taxiways together make up the manoeuvring area.

Now, colour. The single most useful fact about aerodrome paint is its colour. Runway markings are white. Taxiway markings are yellow. If the line under your nosewheel is yellow, you are on a taxiway. If it is white, you are on a runway, and you should have a reason to be there. This colour rule is how pilots who taxi onto the wrong surface realise their mistake.

Runway markings describe the runway to an approaching aircraft. There are five to know.

First, the runway designator. It is the magnetic direction of the runway to the nearest 10 degrees, with the last zero dropped. So runway {24|two four} is {240|two four zero} degrees. Where there are parallel runways, a letter is added: L, C or R, for left, centre or right, as seen by the approaching pilot. Jandakot's parallel runways carry L and R.

Second, the centreline, a dashed white line down the middle. Third, the threshold, shown by the "piano keys", white stripes running along the runway at its start. Fourth, on larger runways, the aiming point, which is two broad bars, and the touchdown zone bars. And fifth, side stripes, marking the edges of the paved runway.

Taxiway markings guide you on the ground. There are three kinds. First, a continuous yellow centreline to follow. Second, holding position markings. These are two solid lines and two dashed lines, across the taxiway. Hold on the solid side. The solid lines face you as you approach the runway, saying "stop here". From the runway side, the dashed lines let you cross freely when you vacate. And third, edge markings where needed, and painted guidance to parking.

A helipad is marked by a white or yellow letter H, inside a circle or a square. It is the touchdown area for helicopters. Keep aeroplanes clear of it, because a helicopter may arrive vertically from above you.

Signs follow a colour code too. Red signs with white letters are mandatory. They mark runway holding positions, and say things like "no entry". Yellow and black signs give information. A location sign, yellow letters on black, names the taxiway you are on. A direction sign, black letters on yellow, with an arrow, points to other taxiways and runways. Red means you must not pass without permission. Yellow tells you where you are and where to go.

[[diagram:taxiway-signs]]
Picture taxiway A, seen from above, leading up to runway {24R|two four right}, with a junction off to taxiway B. Beside the taxiway, before the junction, stand two signs. One is a location sign, a yellow letter A on black, telling you the taxiway you are on. The other is a direction sign, a black letter B on yellow with an arrow, telling you which way to go. Further on, the runway holding position marking crosses the taxiway, two solid then two dashed yellow lines. Beside it stands a mandatory sign, {24R|two four right} in white letters on red. Stop on the solid side, and do not pass without permission.

Let's work one: reading the paint on the way to the runway. You taxi from the apron at your training aerodrome for runway {24R|two four right}. Picture each thing you see, and ask what it is telling you.

Leaving the apron, you pick up a continuous yellow line. You are on a taxiway, part of the manoeuvring area. Next, a direction sign, black letters on yellow, shows an arrow towards {24R|two four right}. That is information, telling you which way to go.

Ahead, two solid yellow lines, followed by two dashed lines, cross the taxiway. Beside them is a red sign with white letters reading {24R|two four right}. That is a runway holding position. The solid lines face you, so you stop before them.

Beyond the lines, the paint turns white. That is the runway. You do not cross the solid lines until you are ready, and at a controlled aerodrome, until you are cleared. Each colour and shape told you something without a word on the radio.

Question. After landing, you approach a holding position marking from the runway side, and you see two dashed lines first. May you cross?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: yes. The dashed side faces the runway, so that vacating aircraft can cross freely. You only stop when the solid lines face you, as you approach the runway.

## The circuit and its legs

A runway can only be used safely by several aircraft at once if they all fly the same pattern, in the same direction, at the same height. That pattern is the circuit. The rules that set it were covered in the lesson on conditions of flight. Here the task is naming its parts.

Standard circuits are left-hand: every turn is to the left. The pattern is built from the take-off and landing direction, which is into wind. Take off on runway {24|two four}, with a circuit height of 1,000 ft above aerodrome level, and there are five legs.

[[diagram:circuit-pattern]]
Picture the circuit from above, a rectangle with the runway along one side, and follow the aeroplane round. First, the upwind leg, also called the departure leg. It is straight ahead after take-off, climbing on runway heading. No turn below 500 ft.

Second, the crosswind leg. That is the first turn, 90 degrees left, still climbing. It is made at or above 500 ft, and you usually reach circuit height before the next turn. This leg runs across the wind, which gives it its name.

Third, the downwind leg. It is parallel to the runway, in the opposite direction to landing, at circuit height, 1,000 ft above the aerodrome. The wind is behind you, hence the name. The downwind radio call is made here, and the pre-landing checks are done on this leg.

Fourth, the base leg. Another 90 degrees left, descending, with flap set. The turn onto base is made when the touchdown point is about 45 degrees behind the wing.

And fifth, final. Aligned with the runway, descending to land. On the far side of the runway, away from the circuit, is the dead side.

The names describe the wind relative to each leg. You take off and land into wind. You cross it on crosswind. And you have it behind you on downwind. That is the whole logic. It means you can rebuild the circuit for any runway. Draw the runway. Draw the arrow for the landing direction. And put every turn to the left of it.

[[widget:circuit-3d]]
Now imagine flying that circuit in three dimensions, and then changing the runway in use. The circuit is built from the landing direction. Swap runways, and the whole pattern flips to the other side, so that the turns stay to the left.

Next, the dead side. The circuit lies on one side of the runway. The other side, where nobody is flying the pattern, is the dead side. It is the side of the runway opposite the circuit direction. For a left-hand circuit, it is to the right of the runway, looking along the landing direction.

The dead side earns its name by being kept clear of circuit traffic. That makes it the safe place to lose height when joining.

[[diagram:overhead-join]]
Picture the same left-hand circuit on runway {24|two four}. A joining aircraft crosses overhead the aerodrome, above circuit height. It descends on the dead side, the side away from the circuit, down to circuit height, 1,000 ft above aerodrome level. Then it crosses the upwind end of the runway, joins the crosswind leg, and continues downwind. That way, the joining aircraft never descends through aircraft already flying the pattern.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. The dead side depends on the circuit direction, not on a fixed side of the aerodrome. If a runway has published right-hand circuits, the dead side is on the left of the runway, looking in the landing direction. Work it out from the pattern. Never work it out from a memory of "the right side".

Let's work one: building the circuit for a different runway. The wind has swung, and the runway in use is now {06|zero six}, with standard left-hand circuits. Where does the circuit lie, which way does each leg point, and where is the dead side? Pause if you'd like to try it first.
[[pause 8]]
Step one, the landing direction. It is {060|zero six zero} degrees, roughly north-east. So final points {060|zero six zero}.

Step two. The turns are left, so the circuit lies to the left of the runway, looking along {060|zero six zero}. That is the north-west side.

Step three, the legs. Upwind continues on {060|zero six zero}, past the far end. Crosswind turns left, to about {330|three three zero}. Downwind runs {240|two four zero}, parallel and opposite to landing. Base turns left, to about {150|one five zero}. And final turns left again, onto {060|zero six zero}.

Step four. The dead side is the other side of the runway, the south-east.

Compare runway {24|two four}. The whole pattern has swapped to the other side of the runway, which is exactly what you see when you change runways in the 3D circuit. So the answer again: for runway {06|zero six}, the circuit is on the north-west side, and the dead side is the south-east.

Question. On which leg are the pre-landing checks normally done, and why there?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: on the downwind leg. It is the longest straight, level part of the circuit before the descent. So there is time to complete the checks before the workload rises on base and final.

## The white cross: do not use

A large white cross on a runway, a taxiway, or any part of the movement area means that area is unserviceable, and must not be used. It is the aerodrome's way of saying "closed", in a language visible from 1,000 ft.

Crosses at each end of a runway, or along it, close the runway. A cross on a taxiway closes that taxiway. A runway closed for a long period may also have its markings obliterated, so that it no longer looks like a runway from the air.

Why a cross, and why white? It can be read from the air in any direction, and it contrasts with both bitumen and grass. The meaning is absolute. There is no "use with caution" version.

Question. A white cross is painted at the entrance of one taxiway leading to the runway. May you use that taxiway?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: no. A white cross on any part of the movement area marks it unserviceable. Use another taxiway. The runway itself may still be open, if it carries no cross.

## Markings and markers in detail

Sealed runways at towns and cities carry painted markings. But many country strips in WA are gravel or grass, where paint will not last. So they use markers instead, which are objects placed on the ground. Both systems answer the same three questions. Where does the usable runway begin and end? Where are its edges? And where is the clear area around it?

Start with the threshold and runway end markings. The threshold is where the landing area begins. On a sealed runway, it is marked by the piano keys, the white longitudinal stripes across the runway end.

Sometimes the threshold is displaced. That means it has been moved along the runway, away from its physical end, usually because of an obstacle on the approach, or weak pavement. A displaced threshold is shown by arrows pointing to the displaced threshold line. The paved area before the line may be used for take-off and roll-out, but not for landing. You must not touch down before the line, because the approach to it has been designed to clear the obstacle.

The runway end is where the usable runway finishes. On a sealed runway, it is shown by a transverse stripe, or by the end of the side stripes. On an unsealed strip, it is shown by the gable or cone markers at the ends.

Next, cones and gables. On an unsealed or unrated runway, two kinds of white marker do two different jobs, and the exam tests whether you can tell them apart.

Cone markers are white cones along the edges of the runway, the landing area. They mark where the usable surface is. Land between the cones.

Gable markers are white, gable-shaped markers, shaped like the roof of a house. They mark the boundary of the runway strip. The runway strip is the graded area around and beyond the runway that must be kept clear of obstacles. It is there so that an aeroplane running off the side has a reasonable surface to stop on. A gable marker is not a runway edge.

So, from the inside out. The runway surface. Then the cones at its edges. Then the strip. Then the gables at the strip's boundary.

[[diagram:runway-markings-and-markers]]
Picture two runways from above, one above the other. The top one is sealed, with white markings. It has the threshold piano keys, the designator {24|two four}, and the aiming point bars. A dashed centreline runs down the middle, with side stripes along the edges. Beside it is a taxiway, with a yellow centreline and a runway holding position marking, two solid and two dashed lines. Hold on the solid side. The bottom runway is unsealed. White cones run along its edges. Further out, gables mark the boundary of the runway strip. And a white cross closes one unserviceable section.

Then there are taxiway markings, holding points and holding bays. You have met the yellow centreline and edge lines, and the runway holding position markings. There are also intermediate holding positions, marked by a single dashed line. That is where you may be asked to wait on a taxiway away from any runway, for example to give way to another aircraft at an intersection.

The runway holding position is where you stop and wait before entering or crossing a runway. It is placed far enough back that an aircraft waiting there is clear of aircraft using the runway. A holding bay is a wider area beside the taxiway, where an aircraft can wait or run up without blocking the taxiway. At a busy aerodrome, the holding bay is where you do your engine run-up, while others taxi past you to the runway.

So, to put the markings together. Everything that belongs to the runway is white: the designator, the centreline, the side stripes, the aiming point and touchdown zone bars, and the piano keys at the start of the landing area. Arrows lead to a displaced threshold line, before which you may take off and roll out, but not land. The runway ends at a transverse stripe or the end of the side stripes, or on an unsealed strip, at gables or cones. Everything that belongs to the taxiway is yellow: the centreline, the edges, the runway holding position of two solid and two dashed lines, and the intermediate holding position of a single dashed line. On an unsealed or unrated runway, cones mark the runway edges, and gables mark the strip boundary. And at holding points you stop before a runway, while in holding bays you wait or run up clear of the taxiway.

Here's another trap the exam likes to set. Gables are further out than cones. An exam option that says gable markers show the runway edge is the classic distractor. They show the edge of the strip, the cleared area around the runway. Landing out at the gables puts you off the prepared surface.

Now one more cross, with a completely different meaning. Two white crosses, displayed beside the primary wind indicator, the windsock, mean gliding operations are in progress. Expect gliders and tug aircraft in the circuit. Expect winch cables across the aerodrome. And expect glider operations on the ground.

A glider cannot go around. A winch cable is nearly invisible from the air. The signal tells you to look for both before you join. Gliding operates at several WA aerodromes, so you may well meet it on a weekend navigation exercise.

The position is part of the meaning. A single white cross on the runway closes it. But crosses in the signal area beside the windsock tell you about activity, not about a closure.

Last, the other ground signals.

[[diagram:ground-signals]]
Picture six tiles, each with one signal. The first is the windsock, the primary wind indicator. It shows the wind direction and roughly its strength, and it is placed where it can be seen from the circuit. The second is a single white cross, meaning that part of the movement area is unserviceable. The third is a double white cross beside the windsock, meaning gliding operations in progress. The fourth is a white dumb-bell in the signal area, meaning use the sealed runways and taxiways only. The fifth is a horizontal white landing T, showing the landing direction where no windsock applies. And the sixth is a right-hand turn arrow, meaning right-hand circuits. Right-hand circuits are also shown in ERSA, and at some aerodromes by that ground signal in the signal area.

Question. Overflying a country aerodrome, you see a white dumb-bell in the signal area. What may you use?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: only the sealed runways and taxiways. The unsealed surfaces are not to be used, typically because they are soft, or under works.

## Bringing it together

You are on a navigation exercise to a gravel strip you have never visited. You arrive overhead, above circuit height, and look down before doing anything else.

The windsock shows the wind favouring runway {32|three two}. Beside it in the signal area are two white crosses: gliding in progress. You scan for gliders and a winch line, and spot a tug on the ground near the far end. There is no white dumb-bell, and no white cross on the runway, so the strip is open.

The ERSA entry showed no right-hand circuit for runway {32|three two}. So the circuit is left-hand, and the dead side is to the right of the runway, looking along {320|three two zero}. You descend on the dead side to circuit height, cross the upwind end, and join crosswind. Then you fly downwind, do your checks, and turn base with the threshold 45 degrees behind the wing.

On final you aim between the white cones, not out at the gables marking the strip boundary. After landing, you taxi to the parking area, keeping clear of the glider launch point. Every marker you saw answered a question before you needed to ask it.

Question. Why does an overhead join descend on the dead side, rather than straight down onto downwind?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the dead side has no circuit traffic. So you can lose height to circuit height without descending through aircraft already flying the pattern. You then join crosswind, level at circuit height, where the other traffic can see you.

## Key points

Let's recap.

The parts of an aerodrome. The movement area is the manoeuvring area, which is the runways and taxiways, plus the apron. Runway paint is white: the designator to the nearest 10 degrees, with L, C or R, the centreline, the piano keys, the aiming point, and the side stripes. Taxiway paint is yellow: the centreline, and the holding positions, where you hold on the solid side. An H in a circle or a square is a helipad. Red signs are mandatory, and yellow signs inform.

The circuit. Left-hand unless published. Upwind straight ahead to at least 500 ft. Then crosswind. Then downwind, parallel and opposite to landing. Then base, with the threshold 45 degrees behind the wing. Then final. The dead side is opposite the circuit, and you descend there when joining from overhead.

The white cross. On any part of the movement area, it means unserviceable: do not use.

The markings and markers. Piano keys start the landing area. Arrows to a displaced threshold mean take-off and roll-out, but no landing before the line. An end stripe or end markers finish the runway. Cones mark the runway edge, and gables the strip boundary. At a holding position, stop before a runway. In a holding bay, wait or run up clear of the taxiway. And two white crosses by the windsock mean gliding.

A final exam tip. Cones mark the runway edge, and gables mark the strip boundary. A white cross means unserviceable, and a double white cross by the windsock means gliding. The dead side is opposite the circuit side. Most questions show a diagram. Name what you see by asking what job it does: an edge, a boundary, a closure, or a warning. Then the label follows. And confirm the marker conventions against the current AIP and the workbook figures, which the exam's diagrams are drawn from.
