This is RFRC 2.1, Documentation. Three documents decide whether you may fly today: your logbook, the rule books, and the aeroplane's maintenance release. By the end you will know what each one is for, which document answers which question, and how to read a maintenance release before you fly. There are a few questions along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (RFRC 2.1)

The exam wants three things from you. First, why you record flight time in a logbook, and what else each entry must carry. Second, the documents that hold the aviation law, the aeronautical information and the general operating rules, and which one answers which question. And third, what the maintenance release is for, and how the pilot uses it before and after flight.

## Why this matters

Three pieces of paper decide whether you may fly today. Your logbook proves you are qualified and current. The rule books say what you may and may not do. And the aeroplane's maintenance release says whether the aeroplane itself is fit to go.

At Jandakot or Murrayfield you will open the maintenance release before every flight. You will write in your logbook after every one. And you will reach for ERSA or the VFRG, the Visual Flight Rules Guide, whenever a question comes up that you cannot answer from memory.

The exam tests this subtopic in a practical way. It describes a question a pilot has, and asks which document answers it. Or it describes what you find in a maintenance release, and asks whether you can fly. This lesson shows you why each document exists, so the "which document" questions answer themselves.

## The logbook: your evidence

Imagine you apply for your PPL flight test, the private pilot licence. The examiner needs to know three things. That you have flown the hours the regulations require. That your cross-country time is real. And that your flight review is current. They were not there for any of it. The only thing they can look at is your logbook, the personal record of every flight you have made. That is the whole reason it exists. The logbook is the legal evidence of your aeronautical experience.

Every pilot must keep a logbook and record each flight in it. That requirement sits in CASR Part 61, the licensing part of the Civil Aviation Safety Regulations, and in the Part 61 Manual of Standards. Verify the current text.

The record proves several different things, and each one is used by someone. First, that you have the hours and experience for a licence, rating or endorsement you are applying for. Second, that you have the recent experience to carry passengers. That is the 3 take-offs and 3 landings in 90 days, which you will meet in the lesson on licence privileges and limitations. Third, that your flight review is current, and that endorsements and flight tests have been passed and signed off. And fourth, the history of your flying, which CASA, a flight examiner or a future employer can ask to see.

Because it is evidence, the logbook is kept for the rest of your flying life. A pilot who gets a commercial licence ten years from now will still be counting hours recorded in this year's training.

So what must each entry show? A useful way to remember the list is to ask what someone checking your experience would need to know about each flight. When. In what. Who was in charge. Where. How long, and of what kind. And was anything signed off.

As a minimum, the entry has seven parts. First, the date. Second, the aircraft type and its registration mark. Third, the name of the pilot in command, and your own role. Your role is one of three: pilot in command, dual, which means receiving instruction, or in command under supervision. Fourth, the places of departure and arrival, or the route. Fifth, the flight time. It is split into day and night, and into single or multi-engine, with dual, solo and in-command time recorded separately. Sixth, instrument time and simulator time, where they apply. And seventh, the nature of the flight, and any flight test, flight review or endorsement, signed by the instructor or examiner where that is required.

Each column answers a later question. Splitting dual from solo matters because licences require a minimum of each. Splitting day from night matters because a night rating needs night hours. And recording the type and registration matters because endorsements and class ratings depend on what you actually flew.

Make the entry as soon as practicable, normally the same day, and keep the running totals accurate. Falsifying a logbook is an offence. A false entry is a false claim of qualification, and someone else will rely on it.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. The logbook is about the pilot, not the aircraft. The aeroplane's hours in service are recorded on the maintenance release and in the aircraft's own records. So an exam option that says the main purpose of your logbook is to track the aircraft's maintenance hours is wrong. Your logbook proves your experience.

Question. Why does a logbook entry separate dual time from solo time?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because licences, ratings and endorsements set minimums for each kind of experience, and the logbook is the evidence that you met them. A total alone would not show how much of it you flew without an instructor.

## Where the rules live

When you start flying, the rules seem to come from everywhere. Your instructor, a CASA booklet, a page of ERSA, a NOTAM. In fact they are arranged in a clear order, from law made by Parliament down to guidance written to help you. Once you see the order, you can tell what any document is for by asking two questions. Is it law? And does it change often?

[[diagram:aviation-law-hierarchy]]
Picture a staircase of documents, stepping down from top to bottom. On the top step is the Civil Aviation Act. Below it are the regulations, then the Manuals of Standards, then the remaining Civil Aviation Orders. All of those are law. Lower down is the operational information that Airservices publishes: the AIP, ERSA, the DAP, the charts and NOTAM. And at the base sits the guidance material, the Advisory Circulars and the Visual Flight Rules Guide, which is not law. Let's walk down the staircase, one step at a time.

Start with the law. At the top is the Civil Aviation Act {1988|nineteen eighty-eight}, the Act of Parliament. It creates CASA, and gives it the power to make the detailed rules. It says almost nothing about how to fly an aeroplane. It sets up the system.

Under the Act sit the regulations. They are made by the government, and they are binding on you. There are two sets, because Australia has been moving from an older set to a newer one for many years.

The first set is the Civil Aviation Safety Regulations {1998|nineteen ninety-eight}, called CASR. These are the main modern regulations, divided into Parts by topic. You will meet five most often. Part 61 is flight crew licensing. Part 67 is medicals. Part 91 is the general operating and flight rules. Parts 141 and 142 cover flight training organisations. And Part 101 covers drones.

The second set is the older Civil Aviation Regulations {1988|nineteen eighty-eight}, called CAR. They are still in force wherever the CASR has not yet replaced them. The one you need to know is Part 4A, which holds the maintenance release rules.

Regulations state the rule in general terms. The detail, the actual numbers and tables, often sits one level down, in a Manual of Standards, the MOS. Each MOS is made under a particular CASR Part. The Part 61 MOS contains this syllabus and the flight test standards. The Part 91 MOS holds the operating detail you fly by: the VMC minima, the fuel rules, altimetry, and the procedures at non-controlled aerodromes. A MOS is law, just as the regulation is. The regulation simply points to it for detail that would be clumsy in a regulation.

Below the MOS are the Civil Aviation Orders, the CAOs. They are the remaining orders made under the CAR. Most have now been replaced by the CASR and its MOS, so you will meet few of them.

That is the law. Next, the operational information. Law tells you what you must do. But to actually fly, you also need facts about the world. Where the runways are. Which frequency to call. Where the restricted areas sit. And what has changed this week. That is aeronautical information. In Australia it is published by Airservices Australia. The legislation itself lives on the Federal Register of Legislation.

There are five sources to know. The first is the AIP, the Aeronautical Information Publication. It is the operational handbook, in three parts: GEN for general, ENR for en route, and AD for aerodromes. It is kept up to date in two ways. AIP Supplements cover temporary changes of some length. And Aeronautical Information Circulars, the AICs, carry explanatory and advance information.

The second is ERSA, the En Route Supplement Australia. This is the book you carry in the aeroplane. It holds aerodrome data, the PRD areas, which are the prohibited, restricted and danger areas, the emergency procedures, and the navigation aids.

The third is the DAP, the departure and approach procedures. It holds the instrument procedures, and as a VFR pilot you will rarely need it.

The fourth is the charts. The WAC, the VNC, the VTC, the ERC and the PCA show terrain, airspace and routes in picture form. Which ones show airspace is covered in the lesson on airspace.

And the fifth is NOTAM, the notices to airmen. A NOTAM carries a temporary or urgent change to any of the others. A runway closed for works. A restricted area activated. A navigation aid out of service.

The order matters when two sources seem to disagree. A NOTAM issued this morning overrides the ERSA entry printed months ago, because the NOTAM is how the change is published.

Last, the guidance. This is material that explains the law but is not law itself. Advisory Circulars explain how to comply with a regulation. The Visual Flight Rules Guide, the VFRG, is CASA's plain-English summary of the rules for VFR pilots, gathered in one book. It is guidance, not law. But it is accurate, it is the book most VFR pilots actually read, and it is permitted in the RPL and PPL exams. If the VFRG and the regulation ever differed, the regulation would win.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. The VFRG is not the source of the rules. Exam options often offer "the VFRG" for a question whose correct answer is the regulation or the MOS. So read the wording. If the question asks where a rule is set out, or contained, look for the CASR Part or the MOS. If it asks where a VFR pilot can find a summary, the VFRG is right.

Now the practical skill: matching a question to a document. Ask what kind of fact you need. There are seven kinds.

[[diagram:where-to-find-it]]
Picture a column of pre-flight questions on the left, each with an arrow across to the document that answers it on the right. A rule about you, your licence, privileges and endorsements, is in CASR Part 61. A rule about your fitness, your medical certificate, is in CASR Part 67. A rule about how you fly, such as weather minima, fuel, altimetry and circuits, is in CASR Part 91 and the Part 91 MOS, summarised in the VFRG. A fact about a place, such as an aerodrome, a PRD area or a frequency, or an emergency procedure, is in ERSA, with the AIP for the full text. Something that has changed recently is in a NOTAM. Where the airspace boundaries are is on the VTC, the VNC or the ERC. And whether the aeroplane is fit to fly is on the maintenance release.

Let's work one. You are planning a solo flight from Murrayfield to Pinjarra, and three questions come up. For each, decide which document answers it. Pause if you'd like to try them first.

The first question: am I, as an RPL holder without a navigation endorsement, allowed to go that far? The second: a friend mentioned the strip has works on the runway this month, so is that true? And the third: is the aeroplane I have booked due for its 100-hourly before I get back?
[[pause 6]]
Here are the answers. The first is about your licence privileges, so the answer is in CASR Part 61, summarised in the VFRG. The second is a temporary change, so you check the NOTAMs for the aerodrome. The ERSA entry would not yet show it. And the third is about the aircraft, so the answer is on its maintenance release. Three questions, three different documents, chosen by asking what kind of fact each one needs.

Question. An ERSA entry shows a runway as available, but a NOTAM issued yesterday says it is closed. Which do you follow, and why?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the NOTAM. ERSA is updated on a fixed cycle. The NOTAM is how temporary or urgent changes are published in between, so it is the more recent and authoritative information.

## The maintenance release

You have checked that you may fly. The other half of the question is whether the aeroplane may. You cannot inspect its engine overhaul history, its airworthiness directives or its last 100-hourly from the walk-around. Someone qualified has done that work, and the maintenance release is how they tell you about it.

The maintenance release is the document that certifies the aircraft is airworthy, and may be flown for a stated period. That period is usually 100 hours or 12 months. The release travels with the aircraft, and the rules for it are in Part 4A of the Civil Aviation Regulations {1988|nineteen eighty-eight}. Verify the current text. It is issued by a licensed aircraft maintenance engineer after the required inspection.

Think of it as the aeroplane's equivalent of your logbook and your medical combined. It says what has been done, when the next work is due, and whether anything is wrong with the aircraft today.

[[diagram:airworthiness-documents-flow]]
Picture the documents that tell you whether the aircraft is serviceable, side by side. There is the maintenance release, the certificate of airworthiness, the certificate of registration, and the approved flight manual or POH, with its weight and balance data. The maintenance release is the key one. Before flight, it has to pass four checks. It is current in hours and date. The daily inspection is certified today. There is no endorsed defect that grounds the aircraft. And no maintenance falls due before the end of the flight.

So what does the release show? It has three kinds of content, and each one answers a different pre-flight question.

[[diagram:maintenance-release-anatomy]]
Picture a simplified form in three parts, a schematic rather than the real thing. Part 1, the release itself, gives the period and the hours for which it is valid. It gives an expiry date and an expiry time in service, whichever comes first, and any maintenance that falls due within the period, with the time or date it is due. That answers the question: is the release still current, and is anything due before I land?

Part 2 holds the defect endorsements. That is the defects found, who recorded them, and the engineer's clearance, or the permissible unserviceability endorsement. It answers the question: is there anything wrong with the aircraft, and has it been fixed, or approved to fly as it is?

Part 3 holds the daily inspection certifications and the time-in-service record, one line for each day the aircraft flies. It answers the question: has today's daily inspection been done and signed?

A permissible unserviceability is a defect that an authorised person has assessed and endorsed as acceptable for flight, often with conditions. For example, a failed cabin light, with no night flight. It is not the same as a defect that has simply been written up and ignored.

Now, how the pilot uses it. Before every flight, the pilot in command reads the maintenance release and confirms four things. First, the aircraft is within its hours and dates. The release has not expired on either count. Second, no maintenance is overdue, and none will fall due before the end of the planned flight. Third, the daily inspection is signed for today. And fourth, any endorsed defect has been cleared, or is a permitted unserviceability whose conditions you can meet.

After flight, the pilot does two things: records the flight time, and endorses any defect found. Recording the time keeps the hours on the release accurate for the next pilot. Endorsing the defect stops the next pilot taking off in an aeroplane with a problem you already know about.

The consequence is blunt. An aircraft with an expired maintenance release, overdue maintenance, or an uncleared defect that affects safety is not airworthy, and must not fly. No amount of urgency, and no instructor's assurance, changes that. What a licence holder may sign on the release, and how defects are endorsed, is covered in the lesson on licence privileges and limitations.

Let's work one: can this aeroplane fly today? You arrive for a 1.5 hour flight, and the maintenance release shows four things. It was issued at 3,812.4 hours, and it is valid to 3,912.4 hours or to 14 March next year, whichever comes first. Today's date is in October, and the hour meter reads 3,909.6. The daily inspection for today is signed. And Part 2 has one defect, "left landing light inoperative", endorsed by the engineer as a permissible unserviceability, day VFR only. May you fly? Pause if you'd like to try it first.
[[pause 6]]
Step one, the hours remaining. 3,912.4 minus 3,909.6 is 2.8 hours. The planned 1.5 hours fits.

Step two, the date. October is before 14 March, so the release is current by date.

Step three, the daily inspection. It is signed for today.

Step four, the defect. It is endorsed as a permissible unserviceability, and its condition, day VFR, is one you meet as a day VFR pilot.

So the aeroplane may fly. But look again at step one. After your flight it will have about 1.3 hours left. So the next pilot, booked for a two-hour navigation exercise, cannot take it until it has had its inspection.

Now try one yourself. Same aeroplane, but this time the hour meter reads 3,911.1, and you are booked for a 1.5 hour flight. May you go?
[[pause 6]]
The answer: no, not on that plan. The hours remaining are 3,912.4 minus 3,911.1, which is 1.3 hours. That is less than the 1.5 hours planned. The maintenance would fall due before the end of the flight, so you may not fly the planned exercise. You could fly a shorter flight that ends with time to spare, or wait until the inspection is done. So the answer again: 1.3 hours left, not enough for 1.5.

Here's another trap the exam likes to set. "Valid for 100 hours or 12 months" means whichever comes first. A release issued eleven months ago, with only 40 hours used, is about to expire on the date, however few hours have been flown.

Question. The release is current and the daily inspection is signed. But Part 2 has a defect with no engineer's clearance and no permissible unserviceability endorsement. Can you fly?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: not if the defect affects safety. An endorsed defect that has been neither cleared nor approved as a permissible unserviceability leaves the aircraft unserviceable, until a licensed engineer rectifies it or endorses it.

## Bringing it together

It is a Sunday morning at Jandakot. Before you leave home, you check the NOTAMs and see that a restricted area near your training area is active. You look up its hours in ERSA, so you can plan around it. The CASR and the MOS have already told you that you need a clearance to enter it.

At the aeroplane you open the maintenance release. It is current in date, with 6.2 hours left before the 100-hourly. Today's daily is signed, and Part 2 is empty. So the aeroplane may fly.

You fly your hour of circuits and steep turns. After shutdown, you write 1.0 hour in the maintenance release, so the next pilot sees 5.2 hours remaining. Then you record the flight in your logbook. The date. The type and registration. Yourself as pilot in command. Jandakot to Jandakot. And 1.0 hour, day, single-engine, in command.

Three documents, each answering a question nobody else could answer for you.

Question. After a flight you notice a cracked fairing. Which two documents do you write in, and what does each entry do?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the maintenance release, where you endorse the defect, so the next pilot and the engineer know about it. And your logbook, where you record the flight time as evidence of your experience. The maintenance release tracks the aircraft. The logbook tracks you.

## Key points

Let's recap.

The logbook. It is the legal evidence of your experience, for licences, ratings, recency and flight reviews. Each entry carries the date, the type and registration, the pilot in command and your role, and the departure and arrival, or the route. It carries the flight time, split day and night, single or multi-engine, and dual, solo and in command. Then instrument and simulator time, the nature of the flight, and any test or endorsement, signed. Record it as soon as practicable. Falsifying it is an offence.

The documents. The law comes first. The Act, then the regulations: CASR, with Part 61 for licences, Part 67 for medicals and Part 91 for operations, and the older CAR, with Part 4A for the maintenance release. Then the MOS, where the Part 91 MOS holds the VMC minima, fuel and altimetry. Then the CAOs. The information comes from Airservices: the AIP, in its GEN, ENR and AD parts, with Supplements and AICs, then ERSA, the DAP, the charts and NOTAM. And the guidance, which is not law: the Advisory Circulars and the VFRG, which is permitted in the exam.

The maintenance release. It certifies the aircraft airworthy for a period, usually 100 hours or 12 months, whichever comes first. Before flight, check four things: current in hours and date, nothing overdue, the daily inspection signed for today, and defects cleared or permissible. After flight, record the time and endorse any defect. If the release has expired, if maintenance is overdue, or if there is an uncleared safety defect, do not fly.

A final exam tip. Know which document answers which question. VMC minima and fuel rules are in the Part 91 MOS, and summarised in the VFRG. Aerodrome data and PRD areas are in ERSA. Licence privileges are in CASR Part 61. And the aircraft's serviceability is in the maintenance release. When a question offers both a law and a summary of it, read whether it asks where the rule is set out, which is the law, or where to find it explained, which is the VFRG. And confirm the current regulation numbers before your exam, because CASA moves rules between the CAR and the CASR as the transition continues.

That's the end of RFRC 2.1.
