This is RFRC 2.7, Emergencies and SAR, which stands for search and rescue. Most flights end exactly as planned, and this lesson is about the ones that do not. By the end you will know what flashing lights on an approaching aircraft mean, how to tell an accident from an incident, what to report, to whom and how soon, how to set and manage a SARTIME, and where the emergency procedures are kept. There are a few questions along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (RFRC 2.7)

The exam wants five things from you. First, what an aircraft is signalling when it switches its navigation or landing lights on and off. Second, the difference between an accident and an incident. Third, what must be reported after an incident or accident, to whom, how, and how soon. Fourth, what a SARTIME is and how you use one. And fifth, the document that holds the emergency procedures.

## Why this matters

Most flights end exactly as planned, and this subtopic is about the ones that do not. A radio failure on the way into Jandakot. A bird strike on climb-out from Northam. A precautionary landing in a paddock east of Merredin, with the nearest farmhouse 20 km away. In each one, what happens next depends on systems you set up before the flight, and on rules you know without looking them up.

Western Australia makes the search and rescue part of this unusually real. Much of the state is empty enough that if you went down without a SARTIME or a flight note, nobody would start looking for a long time.

The exam asks short recall questions about the definitions, the reporting rules, the SARTIME, and where to find the procedures. This lesson gives each one its reason, because in an emergency you act on understanding, not on memorised wording.

## Flashing lights: "I have to land"

Imagine you are holding short at a country aerodrome, waiting to line up. An aeroplane on a wide final starts switching its landing lights on and off, over and over. It has made no radio call. What is it telling you?

An aircraft that repeatedly switches its landing lights or navigation lights on and off is signalling that it is in difficulty, and compelled to land. It might have had a radio failure, for example. And it needs the aerodrome to make way for it. The pilot cannot tell you by radio, so the lights do the talking.

If you see it, give way, and expect the aircraft to land. Stay clear of the runway. And if you are in the circuit, extend or leave the pattern to make room.

The signal works because it is unmistakable. Steady lights are normal. Landing lights going on and off repeatedly are not something anyone does by accident.

Context still matters, though, because lights are used for other signals too. Flashing the landing lights is also the night acknowledgement of a light signal from the tower. You met the light signals in the lesson on conditions of flight. And intercepted aircraft use lights as part of the interception signals, which are in the emergency section of ERSA, called ERSA EMERG. So a single flash in reply to a green light from the tower is an acknowledgement. Repeated, irregular switching on approach is a request for the runway.

Question. Why would a pilot use the lights, rather than the radio, to signal that they must land?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: usually because the radio has failed. The lights are the remaining way to tell the aerodrome and the other traffic that the aircraft is in difficulty and needs to land. So the others should give way.

## Accident or incident

After something goes wrong, the first question the system asks is how serious it was. That decides how fast it must be reported, and whether it will be investigated. The two categories are defined in the transport safety legislation. That is the Transport Safety Investigation Act {2003|two thousand and three}, and its Regulations of {2021|twenty twenty-one}. Verify the current text. And the definitions turn on outcomes, not causes.

Start with an accident. An accident is an occurrence associated with the operation of an aircraft. It happens between the time a person boards with the intention of flight, and the time everyone has disembarked. And it has one of three outcomes. First, a person is killed or seriously injured. Second, the aircraft sustains serious damage or structural failure, affecting its strength, performance or flight characteristics, and normally requiring major repair. Or third, the aircraft is missing, or completely inaccessible.

Notice the time window. An occurrence with an engine running on the apron, but nobody aboard intending to fly, is outside it. And notice that the three outcomes are about people, the airframe, and the aircraft's whereabouts.

Some damage is common, local and repairable, and does not by itself make an occurrence an accident. That is damage limited to the engine, the propeller, the wheels, tyres or brakes, the fairings, or small dents or puncture holes in the skin. Take a propeller strike on landing that bends the propeller tips. It is serious, and it must be reported. But if nothing else was damaged, and nobody was hurt, it does not meet the accident definition on its own.

Now the incident. An incident is any other occurrence, other than an accident, associated with the operation of an aircraft, that affects or could affect the safety of operation. The definition is wide on purpose. The point of reporting incidents is to learn from the near things, before they become the real things.

Between the two sits the serious incident. That is one in which an accident nearly occurred. Examples are a near collision, a runway incursion, and fuel exhaustion short of an accident. Nobody was hurt and nothing was broken, but only luck separated it from an accident.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. The word "serious" is not a measure of how frightening it felt. A serious incident is one where an accident nearly occurred. An accident needs an actual outcome: death or serious injury, serious damage or structural failure, or a missing aircraft. So a hair-raising near miss with no damage is a serious incident, not an accident.

Question. A nosewheel tyre bursts on landing, and the aeroplane stops on the runway with no other damage and nobody hurt. Accident or incident?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: an incident. Damage limited to wheels and tyres does not by itself make an occurrence an accident, and nobody was injured. It still affected the safety of operation, so it is reportable.

## Reporting: who, how and how soon

Reporting goes to the Australian Transport Safety Bureau, the ATSB, not to CASA. That is under the Transport Safety Investigation Regulations. The reason is a separation of jobs. CASA is the regulator, which enforces the rules. The ATSB is the investigator, whose only job is to find out what happened, so it does not happen again. A no-blame investigator gets more honest reports.

The rules sort occurrences into two groups, by urgency.

The first group is the immediately reportable matters. These are the serious ones: accidents, serious incidents, and the listed serious events. The listed events include a death or serious injury, serious damage, a missing aircraft, a near collision, and fuel exhaustion. For these, the responsible person must do two things. The responsible person is the pilot in command, or the operator if the pilot is unable.

First, report to the ATSB as soon as practicable, by the quickest means. That is normally by phone, on the 24-hour number, {1800 011 034|one eight hundred, zero one one, zero three four}. And then, second, submit a written report within 72 hours.

The phone call comes first because an investigation may need to start at once. The wreckage preserved. The weather recorded. Witnesses found while they still remember.

The second group is the routine reportable matters. These are the other incidents that affect safety. For example, an engine failure that ended in a safe landing, an airspace infringement, or a bird strike. For these, a written report within 72 hours, through the ATSB online form, is enough. Nothing needs to be preserved urgently. But the report adds to the picture that lets the ATSB see patterns.

Two other reports often follow. Defects found as a result also go on the maintenance release, as you met in the lesson on licence privileges and limitations. And major defects are reported to CASA, through the defect reporting system. Your operator or flying school will also want its own report.

And preserve the evidence. After an accident, do not move the wreckage, except to rescue people or to prevent further damage, unless the ATSB or the police agree. The position of a switch, or the angle of a control surface, can tell an investigator what happened. Moving it erases the clue.

One branch leads to the immediately reportable matters: accidents, serious incidents, and the listed serious events. Let's work one: what must be reported, and how? There are three occurrences. For each, work out the category first, and the reporting follows. Pause if you'd like to try them first.

The first: on a solo flight from Jandakot, you inadvertently climb into the Class C step above you, notice, and descend out of it. The second: a student and an instructor on a training flight near York have a hard landing in a paddock after the engine stops. The instructor suffers a serious back injury, and the wing spar is cracked. And the third: on final at a country strip, you see a ute cross the runway in front of you, and you go around with metres to spare.
[[pause 8]]
The first. Nobody was hurt, nothing was damaged, and there was no near collision. So it is an incident, and an airspace infringement is a routine reportable matter. A written report to the ATSB within 72 hours.

The second. A serious injury, and structural damage. That is an accident, and it is immediately reportable. The instructor is injured, so the student or the operator phones the ATSB on {1800 011 034|one eight hundred, zero one one, zero three four} as soon as practicable. Then a written report within 72 hours. And nothing is moved, except to help the injured.

The third. No damage and no injury, but an accident nearly occurred. That makes it a serious incident, a runway incursion. It is immediately reportable. Phone first, then the written report within 72 hours.

The method: decide accident, serious incident or incident by the outcome. Then the reporting rule follows from the category.

Now try one yourself. On climb-out from Northam, a large bird hits the windscreen. It cracks, but holds, and you return and land safely. Nobody is hurt. What category is it, and what must you report?
[[pause 5]]
The answer: there is no death or injury, and the damage is limited to a cracked windscreen, with no structural failure. So it is not an accident. No accident nearly occurred, in the sense of a near collision. So it is an incident, and a bird strike is a routine reportable matter. That means a written report to the ATSB within 72 hours. You also endorse the damage on the maintenance release, and tell your operator.

Question. Why does an immediately reportable matter need a phone call as well as the written report?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because an investigation may need to start at once, to preserve the wreckage and the evidence, and to contact witnesses. The phone call reaches the ATSB in minutes. The written report, within 72 hours, follows with the detail.

## SARTIME: making sure someone comes looking

Here is the uncomfortable thought behind search and rescue. You make a forced landing in a remote area. The radio is damaged. You are uninjured, but stuck. Who knows you are missing? If you told nobody when to expect you, the honest answer may be nobody. Not until a family member wonders where you are at dinner time. And even then, they do not know where you were going.

A SARTIME, a search and rescue time, fixes that. It is a time you nominate, in UTC, by which you will report your arrival, or the completion of a flight. You lodge it with Airservices. You can do that through a flight plan, or the NAIPS SARTIME form, or by phone or radio to Flight Information. From then on, the system is waiting for you.

If you have not cancelled or amended the SARTIME by that time, the search and rescue system assumes you are in trouble, and begins the alerting phases. There are three. First, attempts to contact you. Then, a communications search, calling aerodromes and contacts along your route. And then a physical search, by the Australian Maritime Safety Authority's Joint Rescue Coordination Centre. The beauty of the system is that it needs nothing from you once you are down. The clock does the alerting.

[[diagram:sartime-timeline]]
Picture a timeline running from left to right. At the start, before flight, you nominate a SARTIME in UTC, with a sensible margin after your planned arrival. If you are delayed, you extend it, by radio or phone, before it expires. On landing, you cancel it at once. Nothing happens until the SARTIME passes. But if it passes without being cancelled or amended, the alerting phases begin, one after another: attempts to contact you, then a communications search, then a physical search. So cancel on landing.

Now, how to use one. When should you use a SARTIME? On flights over remote or inhospitable country. On long cross-country flights. And on any flight where nobody else would know you were missing. In WA, that covers almost any flight east of the wheatbelt, or along the coast north of Geraldton.

How do you choose the time? Pick a SARTIME that gives a sensible margin after your planned arrival. It should be enough to absorb a headwind or a short hold without triggering a search. But not so long that the search starts hours after you went down. The time is in UTC, so convert carefully. WST, Western Standard Time, is UTC plus 8. That conversion is covered in the lesson on time.

If you are delayed, extend it, by radio or phone, before it expires.

On arrival, cancel it as soon as you land. Failing to cancel wastes search resources, and it is embarrassing at best. The alerting phases start for an aircraft that is safely tied down. And people and aircraft may be committed to a search that was never needed.

The alternative is to leave a flight note with a responsible person, who contacts SAR if you do not arrive. For a private VFR flight, the choice between the two is yours, as pilot in command. A flight note relies on that person remembering, understanding the plan, and acting. A SARTIME is held by a system that does not forget.

Let's work one: setting and managing a SARTIME. You plan a private flight from Jandakot to Hyden, departing at {0930|oh nine thirty} WST, with a planned flight time of 1 hour 50 minutes. Nobody will be waiting for you at Hyden. What SARTIME do you lodge? Pause if you'd like to try it first.
[[pause 8]]
Step one, the planned arrival. {0930|oh nine thirty} plus 1 hour 50 minutes gives {1120|eleven twenty} WST.

Step two, add a margin you choose. Say 30 minutes, for headwinds and a possible hold. That gives {1150|eleven fifty} WST.

Step three, convert to UTC by taking away 8 hours. {1150|eleven fifty} minus 8 hours gives {0350|zero three fifty} UTC. So you lodge a SARTIME of {0350|zero three fifty} UTC through NAIPS.

Step four. En route, a stronger headwind puts your arrival at {1145|eleven forty-five} WST, too close to {1150|eleven fifty}. So you call Flight Information on the radio, and extend the SARTIME to, say, {0420|zero four twenty} UTC, before the original expires.

Step five. On landing at Hyden, you cancel the SARTIME by phone, at once.

So the answer: a SARTIME of {0350|zero three fifty} UTC, extended in flight when the headwind slowed you. The margin is your choice as pilot in command, not a regulatory figure. What the rules require is that you extend before expiry, and cancel on arrival.

And if the worst happens? If you do go down, the SARTIME means help is coming to the route you notified. That is why the standard survival advice is to stay with the aircraft. It is far easier to see from the air than a person. It gives shade. And the search follows your notified route.

[[diagram:survival-priorities]]
Picture the survival priorities, in order. First, protection: first aid and shelter. Second, location: helping rescuers find you. Third, water. And only then, food. Beside them, the reminders. Stay with the aircraft. Activate the ELT, the emergency locator transmitter, or the PLB, the personal locator beacon. Try the emergency frequency, {121.5|one two one decimal five} megahertz. And lay out ground-to-air signals. A V means you require assistance. An X means you require medical assistance. N means no, Y means yes, and an arrow means proceeding in this direction.

Question. You are running late towards a remote strip, and your SARTIME expires in ten minutes. What do you do, and why now?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: extend it by radio or phone immediately, before it expires. If it passes uncancelled, the alerting phases begin, and resources are committed to looking for an aircraft that is not missing.

## Where the emergency procedures are

In an emergency, you do not have time to search the AIP. The ERSA EMERG section, the pink pages, is the in-flight reference for emergency procedures. It is deliberately printed on coloured paper, so you can find it by feel and sight.

It contains six things. First, the distress and urgency calls. MAYDAY means grave and imminent danger, with immediate assistance needed. PAN PAN means urgency: a problem without immediate danger. Second, the transponder emergency codes: {7700|seven seven zero zero} for an emergency, {7600|seven six zero zero} for radio failure, and {7500|seven five zero zero} for unlawful interference. Third, the radio failure procedures. Fourth, the interception procedures and signals. Fifth, the light signals. And sixth, ditching, survival, and the SAR system.

The AIP holds the fuller text, in GEN 3.6 for search and rescue, and ENR 1.12 for interception. But for flight, carry a current ERSA. The EMERG section is the version you can use with one hand while flying the aeroplane.

[[diagram:ersa-emerg-contents]]
Picture the pink pages sorted into three groups, by what a pilot in trouble needs, rather than by page order. The first group is "tell someone". It holds the MAYDAY and PAN PAN calls and their format: callsign, the nature of the emergency, intentions, position, altitude, heading, and persons on board. They are made on the current frequency, then on {121.5|one two one decimal five} megahertz. And it holds the transponder codes. The second group is "deal with the problem". It holds the radio failure procedures, for Class G and for controlled airspace, the interception signals and responses, and ditching and forced landing. The third group is "if you end up on the ground". It holds the SAR phases, how to operate the ELT, and the ground-to-air signals. EMERG also has the light signals from a control tower, and the emergency contact numbers for the ATSB, Airservices and the JRCC, the Joint Rescue Coordination Centre. Check your current ERSA.

The transponder codes follow a logic worth seeing. Each one tells ATC something they need to know, without a word.

[[diagram:transponder-emergency-codes]]
Picture three transponder code windows side by side. {7700|seven seven zero zero} says "I am in trouble". {7600|seven six zero zero} says "I cannot hear you, but I am following the radio failure procedure". And {7500|seven five zero zero} says something a pilot may not be able to say aloud, with a hijacker in the cabin. All three are in ERSA EMERG. So again: {7700|seven seven zero zero} emergency, {7600|seven six zero zero} radio failure, {7500|seven five zero zero} unlawful interference.

Here's another trap the exam likes to set. The AIP holds the full text. But the exam asks for the document that contains the emergency procedures for use in flight, and that is ERSA EMERG. Do not pick the VFRG, or the AIP GEN section, when ERSA EMERG is offered.

## Bringing it together

It is a Saturday flight from Jandakot to Merredin. Before departure, you lodge a SARTIME in UTC, with a margin after your planned arrival. And you know the ERSA EMERG pages are in the seat pocket.

Halfway there, the radio goes silent. You open ERSA EMERG, set {7600|seven six zero zero}, and follow the radio failure procedure.

Arriving at Merredin, you see another aeroplane in the circuit. So on a wide final, you switch your landing lights on and off repeatedly, to show that you are compelled to land. The other aeroplane extends downwind and gives you the runway.

On landing, your nosewheel drops into a soft patch, and the propeller tips touch the ground. Nobody is hurt, and the damage is limited to the propeller. So this is not an accident by the definition. But it affected safety, so it is an incident. That means a written report to the ATSB within 72 hours, a defect endorsement on the maintenance release, and a call to the operator.

And before any of that, because your radio is dead, you borrow a phone and cancel the SARTIME. Every part of the lesson appeared in one bad afternoon, which ended well because each system was in place before it started.

Question. In that story, which single action prevents a search for an aircraft that is safely on the ground?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: cancelling the SARTIME on landing, by phone if the radio is unserviceable. Until it is cancelled, the system will start the alerting phases at the nominated time.

## Key points

Let's recap.

Lights. Landing or navigation lights switched on and off repeatedly mean the aircraft is in difficulty and compelled to land, often with a radio failure. Give way. A single flash at night can be an acknowledgement, and interception signals use lights too.

Accident or incident. An accident is death or serious injury, serious damage or structural failure, or the aircraft missing, between boarding for flight and disembarking. Damage limited to the engine, propeller, wheels, tyres, brakes, fairings or small dents is not by itself an accident. An incident is anything else that affects or could affect safety. And a serious incident is one where an accident nearly occurred.

Reporting. To the ATSB, not CASA. Immediately reportable matters, which are accidents, serious incidents and the listed events: phone {1800 011 034|one eight hundred, zero one one, zero three four} as soon as practicable, then in writing within 72 hours. Routine reportable matters: in writing within 72 hours. Defects go on the maintenance release, and to CASA. And do not disturb the wreckage.

SARTIME. A UTC time by which you report your arrival. Lodge it with Airservices, extend it before it expires, and cancel it on landing. If it is not cancelled, it starts the alerting phases, and a search by the JRCC. The alternative is a flight note with a responsible person.

The emergency procedures are in ERSA EMERG, the pink pages. MAYDAY and PAN PAN. {7700|seven seven zero zero}, {7600|seven six zero zero} and {7500|seven five zero zero}. Radio failure, interception, light signals, ditching, survival and SAR.

A final exam tip. Accidents and serious incidents are reported to the ATSB immediately, and in writing within 72 hours. Routine incidents, in writing within 72 hours. A SARTIME must be cancelled on arrival. And the emergency procedures are in ERSA EMERG. In accident-or-incident questions, test the occurrence against the three accident outcomes, person, airframe and whereabouts, and against the list of damage that does not count. The distractor is usually an occurrence that sounds dramatic, but meets none of them. And verify the reporting rules and the ATSB number against the current Transport Safety Investigation Regulations and ERSA.

That's the end of RFRC 2.7.
