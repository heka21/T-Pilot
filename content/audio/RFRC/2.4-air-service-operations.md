This is RFRC 2.4, Air service operations: the responsibilities of the pilot in command. We will walk through a flight in order, from the passenger list to shutdown, with the reason behind each rule. By the end you will know who you may carry, what you must check and brief before flight, and the rules on seats, belts and the controls once you are airborne. There are a few questions along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (RFRC 2.4)

The exam wants three things from you. First, where in the legislation to find the restrictions on carrying passengers on particular kinds of flight. Second, the pilot in command's duties before flight, such as fuel and oil, refuelling, starting engines and briefing passengers, and in flight, such as operating safely, the pilot in command's authority, and dropping things from the aircraft. And third, the rules for each phase. Before flight, that means locking devices, doors and caps, control checks, frost, instruments, the empty seat's harness, the fuel drain, passengers in control seats, and children. In flight, it means who sits where, seat belts, seat adjustment, and who may handle the controls.

## Why this matters

The moment you sign for an aeroplane as pilot in command, everything about the flight becomes your legal responsibility. The fuel in the tanks. The gust lock you might have missed. The child in the back seat. The friend in the right seat who wants a go at the controls.

Most of the rules in this subtopic were written after a specific accident. An aeroplane that took off with water in the fuel. A door that opened after lift-off. A seat that slid back on the take-off roll and pulled the pilot's hands off the controls.

The exam asks them as recall, such as "when must seat belts be worn?" And it asks them as small scenarios, such as "may you carry a passenger on this flight?" This lesson walks through a flight in order, from the passenger list to shutdown. It explains the reason behind each rule, so that you can rebuild it under exam pressure.

## Who may be carried, and on which flights

Start with the question you face when a friend asks to come along. Is this a flight on which I may carry anyone at all?

A passenger is anyone on board who is not a crew member doing a job on the flight. The restrictions on carrying them come from two places, and knowing which is which is the skill the syllabus asks for. Restrictions about you, your licence, your recency and your medical, are in CASR Part 61 and Part 67. Restrictions about the flight and the aircraft are in CASR Part 91 and its Manual of Standards, the MOS.

The logic behind all of them is the same. A passenger has not chosen to accept the risks a pilot accepts. They cannot judge those risks, and often cannot even see them. So passengers are kept off flights where the risk is unknown or higher than normal. And they are kept off flights flown by pilots who have not shown they can manage the normal risk.

There are six restrictions to know. First, a student pilot flying solo must not carry a passenger. A student has not yet been assessed as competent to take responsibility for anyone else.

Second, a pilot must have made 3 take-offs and 3 landings in the previous 90 days before carrying a passenger. That is in CASR Part 61, and it is covered in the lesson on licence privileges and limitations.

Third, passengers must not be carried on a test flight after maintenance. Nor on a ferry flight under a special flight permit, unless the permit allows it. Nor on any flight where the aircraft's certificate or the operation prohibits them. A test flight exists because nobody yet knows whether the aircraft is right. A special flight permit exists because the aircraft does not fully meet its normal standard.

Fourth, aerobatic flights may carry passengers only where the aircraft, the pilot's qualifications, and the briefing requirements for aerobatic passenger carriage are all met.

Fifth, the medical. Under a RAMPC, the recreational aviation medical practitioner's certificate, no more than 1 passenger. Under a Basic Class 2, no more than 5 passengers.

And sixth, a flight review or flight test is not a passenger flight. The person in the other seat is an instructor or examiner doing a job.

Let's work one: where would you look, and what does it say? Here are three situations. For each, ask whether the restriction is about the pilot or about the flight. Pause if you'd like to try them first.

The first: the club's aeroplane has just had its engine replaced, and the engineer asks you to fly the check flight. Your partner would love to come. The second: you have not flown for four months, and you want to take your brother to Rottnest. The third: an aeroplane is being flown to Kalgoorlie for repairs under a special flight permit, and the owner's son wants a lift.
[[pause 6]]
The first is about the flight. It is a test flight after maintenance. Part 91 says no passengers, so your partner waits on the ground.

The second is about you. It is recent experience, in CASR Part 61. You need 3 take-offs and 3 landings in the previous 90 days first.

The third is about the aircraft and the flight. It is a ferry flight under a special flight permit. Passengers only if the permit itself allows it, so you read the permit's conditions.

The pattern: name what the restriction is about, then go to the Part that governs that thing.

Question. During your flight review, is the instructor in the right seat a passenger?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: no. A flight review or flight test is not a passenger flight. The instructor is there doing a job, so the passenger restrictions, such as recent experience, do not apply to their presence.

## Before flight: fuel, oil and refuelling

Start with fuel and oil. Fuel exhaustion and fuel starvation remain among the most common causes of engine failure in light aeroplanes. And almost every one of them was preventable on the ground. So the rule puts the responsibility squarely on you. The pilot in command must ensure the aircraft carries enough fuel and oil for the planned flight, plus the required reserves. It must be the correct grade. And the quantity must have been checked, not just read from the gauges.

Why not trust the gauges? Light aeroplane fuel gauges are notoriously inaccurate, especially near empty, and on the ground with the wings not level. The physical check tells you what is really there. That might be a dipstick, a calibrated sight gauge, or a known quantity from full tanks. The gauges in flight then tell you whether it is going down as expected.

For a VFR day flight in a piston aeroplane, the fixed fuel reserve is 30 minutes at normal cruise consumption. That is in chapter 19 of the Part 91 MOS. Verify the current text. The fixed reserve is fuel you plan never to use. It is there for the go-around, the diversion around a shower, and the headwind that was stronger than forecast.

A fuel check must be made before departure, and at intervals in flight. It compares the fuel remaining with what the plan says should remain.

Correct grade matters as much as quantity. The wrong fuel can destroy a piston engine within minutes. You'll find fuels and their colours in the lesson on fuels and oils.

Now refuelling. Refuelling puts a large quantity of flammable vapour next to a metal aeroplane that may be carrying a static charge. Each rule removes one way of igniting it.

[[diagram:refuelling-safety-zone]]
Picture the scene from above: an aeroplane parked inside a refuelling zone, with a bowser beside it. Around them are six numbered rules. First, no smoking or naked flames in the zone, so there is no ignition source. Second, engines stopped, so there is no hot exhaust or ignition spark. Third, the aircraft and the bowser bonded, which means earthed, with a lead running between them. The flowing fuel builds up a static charge, and bonding lets it equalise through the wire, instead of as a spark at the nozzle. Fourth, a fire extinguisher at hand. Fifth, passengers off the aircraft, or supervised under the conditions the rules allow. And sixth, no radar or HF transmissions, because these can induce currents strong enough to spark.

After refuelling, two more things. The caps are secured. And a fuel drain check is made, for water and for the correct fuel.

[[diagram:fuel-sample-colours]]
Picture a row of clear fuel testers side by side, each holding a sample. AVGAS {100LL|one hundred low lead} is blue. AVGAS {100/130|one hundred, one thirty} is green. Jet A-1 is clear to straw coloured. A sample with clear globules, or a separate layer, at the bottom contains water. And a clear sample from an AVGAS aeroplane means water or jet fuel. Do not fly until the cause is found.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. "The gauges show full" is not a fuel check. The rule asks you to verify the quantity physically before flight, and then use the gauges in flight to monitor it.

## Before flight: starting and the passenger briefing

First, starting and ground running of engines. A propeller turning at idle is almost invisible, and it will kill anyone who walks into it. So the starting rules all aim at keeping people away from the propeller, and the aeroplane under control. There are five.

First, nobody near the propeller. And the area behind the aeroplane is clear of people, and of loose objects that the slipstream could blow about. Second, brakes set or chocks in, so the aeroplane does not move when the engine fires. Third, call "clear prop" loudly, look, and wait for any response before starting. Fourth, do not run the engine inside a hangar. And fifth, a pilot must be at the controls whenever the engine is running. An engine started by hand-swinging, or running for a check, is never left unattended.

Next, the passenger briefing. Your passenger has never seen the inside of this aeroplane before. If something goes wrong, the thirty seconds you spend before start-up are all they will have to go on. So the rule is that before take-off, the pilot in command must brief passengers.

[[diagram:passenger-briefing]]
Picture six tiles, one for each item in the briefing. One: the use of seat belts and harnesses, and when they must be worn. Two: the location and operation of the exits. Three: the smoking rules. Four: the location of the emergency equipment, such as the fire extinguisher, and life jackets if carried. Five: the brace position. And six: that passengers must follow the pilot's instructions.

A verbal briefing is standard in a light aeroplane. A good one is practical. Show them how the door latch works. Have them fasten and unfasten the harness themselves. Point to the extinguisher. So once more, the six items: belts, exits, smoking, emergency equipment, the brace position, and obeying the pilot.

Question. Why are the aircraft and the bowser bonded before refuelling begins?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: fuel flowing through the hose builds up a static charge. Bonding connects the aircraft and the bowser, so the charge equalises through the wire. Otherwise it could jump as a spark at the filler, in a cloud of fuel vapour.

## The pre-flight checks the law requires

The walk-around in your POH, the pilot's operating handbook, is a checklist. Behind several of its items is a legal requirement, and each one is there because pilots have died without it.

[[diagram:preflight-legal-checks]]
Picture the aeroplane from above, with eight numbered items placed around it. We'll take them in the order you meet them at the aeroplane.

One: locking devices removed. That means control locks, gust locks, pitot covers and engine blanks. A control lock left in means the controls do not move. A pitot cover left on means no airspeed indication. Both have caused accidents within seconds of lift-off.

Two: doors and hatches secured. A door opening after take-off is a known cause of accidents. Not because the door itself is dangerous. Most light aeroplanes fly acceptably with a door ajar. It is because the noise and distraction make pilots stop flying the aeroplane.

Three: tank caps fitted and secure after refuelling. An unsecured cap lets the low pressure over the wing siphon fuel out in flight.

Four: frost, ice and snow removed from the wings, the tail and the control surfaces. Even a thin layer of frost roughens the wing enough to cut lift and raise the stall speed. So the aeroplane must be clean before flight. A winter morning at Narrogin or York can leave frost on wings that looked dry the night before.

Five: flight controls tested for full, free and correct movement before take-off. Correct is the word that catches people. After maintenance, a control can be rigged to move freely in the wrong direction.

Six: instruments checked for serviceability. The altimeter set and reading correctly, the gyros erect and set, and the ASI, the airspeed indicator, at zero.

Seven: the unoccupied control seat's harness secured, before a solo flight in a dual-control aircraft, so it cannot foul the controls. A loose harness buckle can jam behind a control column, or drop into the rudder pedals.

And eight: the fuel system inspection. That is a drain check for water and contamination at each drain point. It is done before the first flight of the day, and after each refuelling, allowing the fuel to settle first. Water is heavier than fuel, and it sinks to the lowest point, which is the drain. If you sample straight after refuelling, the water is still mixed through the tank, and the sample looks clean.

Here's another trap the exam likes to set. Draining straight after refuelling is the classic mistake. Water needs time to settle to the drain points. Sample too early, and you get clean fuel while the water is still in suspension, waiting to reach the engine.

Question. Why does the control check say full, free and correct movement, and not just full and free?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because a control can move fully and freely in the wrong direction if it has been misrigged, for example after maintenance. Checking that each surface moves the right way for each input catches that before take-off.

## Who sits where: control seats, infants and children

First, passengers in a control seat. In a training aeroplane, the right seat has a full set of controls. A passenger sitting there can push the column or a rudder pedal at the worst moment, by accident or out of curiosity. So control seats may be occupied only by a pilot, or by a person the pilot in command permits. And that person must not interfere with the controls.

A passenger in a control seat must be briefed to keep clear of the controls. Feet flat on the floor, away from the pedals. Hands in the lap. And nothing stowed where it can jam the column.

Next, infants and children. The default is simple. Every occupant must have a seat and a seat belt. The one exception is an infant under a prescribed age. An infant may be carried on an adult's lap, restrained by an infant seat belt attached to the adult's belt.

Children must be properly restrained, and seated where they cannot reach the controls. A curious six-year-old in the right seat can reach the mixture knob faster than you can stop them.

Question. A family friend wants to sit in the right seat of your two-seater and "just watch". What must happen before start-up?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the pilot in command must permit it, and brief the friend to keep hands and feet clear of the controls, and not to touch anything unless asked. A person in a control seat must not interfere with the controls.

## During flight: seats, belts and the controls

Once airborne, the rules keep everyone where they can be protected. Each person must occupy a seat with a seat belt or harness. And the pilot in command must occupy a control seat, with the harness fastened, at all times.

[[diagram:seat-belts-in-flight]]
Picture the flight from the side, as a profile from take-off on the left to landing on the right, with some segments highlighted. Those are the times when seat belts must be worn by everyone. There are four. First, during take-off and landing. Second, whenever the aircraft is below 1,000 ft above the ground. Third, in turbulence. And fourth, whenever the pilot in command directs.

The pattern is the phases where something sudden is most likely, and where there is least time to react. That means close to the ground, and in rough air. Near the ground there is no time to brief. And in turbulence, an unrestrained head hits the cabin roof. So the four again: take-off and landing, below 1,000 ft, turbulence, and when the pilot in command says so.

Next, seat adjustment. Seats must be adjusted and locked before take-off and landing, so a seat cannot slide back on the take-off run. When a seat slides back under acceleration, the pilot's hands pull back on the control column as they go. That raises the nose at low speed, which is the recipe for a stall just after lift-off. So check the seat lock pin is engaged, by trying to move the seat after you adjust it.

And last, who may handle the controls. Only a pilot qualified on the type, or a student under instruction, may handle the controls. The pilot in command must not permit an unauthorised person to operate them. Letting a friend "have a go" in the cruise is the scenario the exam has in mind. The friend is not a pilot, and not a student under instruction. So the answer is no, however harmless the moment looks.

## The pilot in command: authority and safety

All of the rules so far put duties on the pilot in command. The other half of the bargain is authority. The pilot in command has final authority over the aircraft and everyone in it. The pilot in command is responsible for the safety of the aircraft, the passengers and the cargo, and for compliance with the rules. And passengers and crew must obey the pilot in command's lawful instructions. That is in CASR Part 91. Verify the current regulation numbers.

Authority and responsibility come together, because one cannot work without the other. You cannot be responsible for the safety of a flight if a passenger can overrule you. And you should not have authority over people's lives without being answerable for how you use it.

In an emergency, the pilot in command may deviate from a rule to the extent necessary for safety, and must report the deviation. Suppose an engine runs rough at 800 ft over a town. You may descend below the populous-area minimum height to make a field. Afterwards, you report what you did and why.

Finally, dropping articles. Nothing may be dropped or thrown from an aircraft in flight that could endanger people or property. The exception is when it is done under a CASA approval, or under the specific rules for the activity. A bottle thrown from 1,500 ft arrives at the ground at lethal speed. Even paper can foul another aircraft's propeller. Approved activities, such as supply dropping, firebombing and parachuting, have their own rules.

Question. A passenger refuses to fasten their seat belt for landing. What gives you the right to insist?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the pilot in command's authority. Passengers must obey the pilot's lawful instructions, and seat belts must be worn for landing. As pilot in command, you are responsible for their safety and for compliance, and the law gives you the authority to match.

## Bringing it together

It is a winter Saturday at Murrayfield. Your passengers are a colleague and her four-year-old daughter.

Before the flight, you confirm your own currency, which is a Part 61 matter. Five landings last month, so passengers are allowed. The aeroplane was refuelled last night. So this morning, with the fuel settled, you drain each point and find clean blue fuel. Then you dip the tanks, to confirm the quantity covers the flight plus the 30-minute reserve.

There is frost on the tailplane, so you wait for the sun to clear it. On the walk-around, the gust lock and pitot cover come off, the caps are secure, and the doors are latched.

The daughter sits in the back, in her own seat with her own belt, where she cannot reach the controls. Her mother sits in front, briefed to keep clear of the controls. You brief them both on belts, the door, no smoking, the extinguisher, the brace position, and doing what you ask.

You call "clear prop", check behind, and start with the brakes on. You test the controls: full, free and correct. Seats locked, belts on for take-off, and kept on in the climb until well above 1,000 ft.

Halfway round, the mother asks if she can steer for a minute. She is not a pilot, or a student under instruction. So you explain, kindly, that the law says no.

Question. Name two rules from this lesson that exist because of what happens at the moment of acceleration on the take-off roll.
[[pause 4]]
The answer: seats must be adjusted and locked, because a seat sliding back pulls the pilot's hands back on the column. And seat belts must be worn for take-off. Doors secured is a third, because a door opening at lift-off distracts the pilot at the worst moment.

## Key points

Let's recap.

Passengers. None for a student pilot solo. None without 3 take-offs and 3 landings in 90 days. None on a test flight after maintenance, or on a special-permit ferry flight unless the permit allows. Aerobatic passengers only when all the conditions are met. 1 passenger on a RAMPC, and 5 on a Basic Class 2. Restrictions about the pilot are in Part 61 and Part 67. Restrictions about the flight are in Part 91 and its MOS.

Duties before flight. Enough fuel and oil, of the right grade, physically checked, with a 30-minute fixed reserve for day VFR in a piston aeroplane. Refuel with no ignition source, engines stopped, aircraft bonded, and an extinguisher ready. Start with nobody near the propeller, and a pilot at the controls. Brief passengers on belts, exits, smoking, emergency equipment, the brace position, and obeying the pilot.

Duties in flight. The pilot in command has final authority and responsibility. The pilot in command may deviate from a rule in an emergency, and reports it. And nothing is dropped that could endanger anyone, without approval.

The checks before flight. Locks off. Doors and caps secure. Controls full, free and correct. Frost removed. Instruments checked. The empty seat's harness secured for solo. Fuel drained after settling, at the first flight of the day and after refuelling. Passengers in control seats briefed. And a seat and belt for everyone, except a lap-held infant.

The rules in flight. Everyone seated and belted for take-off, landing, below 1,000 ft, in turbulence, and when directed. Seats locked. And only qualified pilots, or students under instruction, on the controls.

A final exam tip. The passenger briefing list and the seat belt rule are easy marks. The briefing is belts, exits, smoking, emergency equipment, brace, and obey the pilot. The belts are take-off, landing, below 1,000 ft, turbulence, and when directed. For fuel, remember the 30-minute fixed reserve for a day VFR piston aeroplane, and a physical fuel quantity check before flight, not just the gauges. For "may you carry a passenger?" scenarios, ask what the restriction is about: the pilot, the aircraft, or the flight. The answer follows. And verify the current Part 91 regulation numbers, because the exam may cite them.

That's the end of RFRC 2.4.
