This is RBKA 3.5, Turning. By the end you will know where the force that turns an aeroplane comes from, why a turn needs back pressure and power, why the stall speed rises with bank, how much the structure can take, and why turns stay gentle near the ground. You will also be able to work out the stall speed and load factor at 45 and 60 degrees of bank in your head. There are a few questions along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (RBKA 3.5)

The exam wants six things from you. First, describe a balanced turn. Second, explain the terms g, wing loading and load factor. Third, say what increasing the bank angle in a level turn does to the stall speed, including how fast it rises, and to the load on the structure. Fourth, give the reasons for avoiding steep turns shortly after take-off and in a glide, especially on approach. Fifth, explain why a balanced turn at low level can look like a slip or a skid when you turn downwind or into wind. And sixth, given the level-flight stall speed, work out the stall speed and load factor at 45 and 60 degrees of bank.

## Why this matters

You turn on every flight. Onto crosswind after leaving Jandakot, onto base and final, and in the 45 and 60 degree steep turns of the training area. A turn is also where most stall-spin accidents start, because it quietly raises the stall speed while the nose and the airspeed look normal.

Knowing where the extra lift comes from tells you four things. Why you need back pressure and power. Why the stall speed climbs with bank. Why the structure has a limit. And why a low turn in a strong wind can fool your eyes. The exam asks for the two load factors by heart, a stall speed calculation, the balance ball, and why turns stay shallow low down.

## How an aeroplane turns: tilting the lift

Anything that moves in a circle needs a sideways force pulling it towards the centre of the curve. A car gets it from its tyres gripping the road. Without that grip, the car carries straight on. That inward force is called the centripetal force. An aeroplane has no road to push against. The only large force it can point sideways is the lift from its wings.

So you bank. Lift always acts at right angles to the wings, so when the wings tilt, the lift tilts with them. Now the lift has two jobs, and it helps to split it into two parts. The vertical part points straight up and holds the aeroplane up against its weight. The horizontal part points towards the centre of the turn, and that is the centripetal force that curves the flight path. Bank more, and the horizontal part grows, so the aeroplane turns harder.

[[widget:bank-angle]]
Picture the aeroplane from behind, with its lift drawn as an arrow sticking straight up out of the wings. Roll it into a bank and the arrow tilts with it. Now draw the vertical part of that tilted arrow. It is shorter than the arrow itself.

That is the consequence that drives this whole lesson. If you simply roll into a bank and do nothing else, the lift is the same size as before, but tilted. So its vertical part is smaller than the weight, and the aeroplane starts to sink. To hold height, the vertical part must still equal the weight. That means the total lift must become larger than the weight. The steeper the bank, the more the lift is tilted, and the bigger the total lift has to be to keep the same vertical part.

How do you get more lift at the same airspeed? At a fixed speed, the only thing you can change is the lift coefficient, by raising the angle of attack. That is the back pressure you hold in every level turn.

More lift costs drag. Induced drag, the drag that comes with making lift, grows with the square of the lift at a given speed. In a 60 degree level turn the wing makes twice the lift, so the induced drag is roughly four times as large. Leave the throttle alone and the aeroplane slows down. In a gentle turn you lose a few knots. In a steep turn you must add power to hold the speed. And in a very steep turn even full power may not be enough, so the aeroplane must descend to hold its speed.

Two numbers describe how hard you are turning. The rate of turn is how many degrees of heading you change each second. The radius of turn is the size of the circle you fly. Both depend on only two things: bank angle and true airspeed.

More bank gives a bigger horizontal force for the same aeroplane, so the turn is faster and tighter. Higher rate, smaller radius. More speed at the same bank makes the aeroplane harder to bend round, so the turn is slower and wider. Lower rate, bigger radius. In fact the radius grows with the square of the speed, so doubling the speed makes the circle four times as wide.

[[diagram:turn-radius-vs-bank-and-speed]]
To give you a feel for the sizes, picture three circles drawn on the ground, all flown at 90 kt. At 30 degrees of bank, the radius is about 380 m. At 45 degrees, about 220 m. At 60 degrees, about 125 m. Now fly the same three turns at 120 kt, and every circle is about 1.78 times as wide. A full 360 degree turn at 45 degrees and 90 kt takes about 30 seconds. Notice what is missing: weight. A heavy and a light aeroplane at the same speed and bank fly exactly the same circle.

[[widget:balanced-turn]]
So here is the whole picture in one place. Lift tilts with the wings. Its vertical part must still equal the weight, so the total lift grows. The horizontal part is what turns the aeroplane, so a steeper bank turns faster and tighter.

Question. You roll into a 45 degree bank and change nothing else. What happens, and what two inputs prevent it?
[[pause 5]]
The answer: the lift is tilted, so its vertical part is now less than the weight, and the aeroplane descends. Back pressure raises the angle of attack, so the total lift grows until its vertical part equals the weight again. And extra power replaces the speed lost to the higher induced drag.

## The balanced turn

Banking curves the flight path. But the nose also has to swing round at the same rate as the path, and that swinging is yaw. In a well-flown turn, the tail follows the nose exactly along the curve, and the airflow meets the aeroplane from dead ahead. That is a balanced turn, also called a coordinated turn. The rate of yaw matches the angle of bank. There is no sideways airflow over the fuselage. And the passengers are pressed straight down into their seats, not sideways.

Ailerons alone do not give a balanced turn. As you roll in, the down-going aileron makes more drag than the up-going one, and it yaws the nose out of the turn. That is adverse yaw, and the cure is rudder in the direction of the turn as you roll. Once the bank is set, most light aeroplanes need only a little rudder to stay balanced.

When yaw and bank do not match, the aeroplane moves partly sideways through the air. There are two ways to get it wrong.

A slip is too much bank for the rate of yaw, usually because there is not enough rudder. The tilted lift pulls the aeroplane inwards faster than the nose is coming round. So it slides sideways and down, towards the inside of the turn, towards the low wing.

A skid is the opposite: too much rudder for the bank. The nose is being yawed round faster than the bank can curve the path. So the aeroplane slides towards the outside of the turn, like a car whose rear end steps out on a wet corner.

[[diagram:slip-and-skid-ball]]
You detect both with the balance ball. Picture a small ball in a curved tube of liquid, like a spirit level bent into a smile. It responds to the sideways force you feel in your seat. Now picture three of them, all in a left turn. In the middle one, the turn is balanced and the ball sits in the centre. On the left, the aeroplane is slipping, and the ball has fallen to the inside of the turn, the low wing side. On the right, the aeroplane is skidding, and the ball has been flung to the outside, the high wing side.

The correction is the same for both: step on the ball. That means press the rudder pedal on the side the ball has gone to. In a slip, that is rudder towards the low wing, or take off a little bank. In a skid, it is less rudder in the direction of the turn.

Near the ground, a skid is the worse of the two. The inside wing is moving more slowly, and it is partly blanketed by the fuselage. So at the stall it drops first, and it drops towards the ground. The lesson on stalling and spinning picks that up.

Once you are established, the aeroplane does not always stay at the bank you set. The outer wing flies round a bigger circle than the inner wing in the same time. So it moves faster through the air, and makes a little more lift. That difference tries to roll the aeroplane further into the turn.

How that plays out depends on the bank. In a shallow turn, the aeroplane's natural lateral stability is stronger, and it tends to roll back towards level, so you hold a little aileron into the turn. In a medium turn, the two effects roughly cancel, and the aeroplane stays where you put it. In a steep turn, the outer wing's extra speed wins, and the bank tends to keep increasing. That is the overbanking tendency, and you hold a little aileron against the turn. Left alone, a steep turn steepens, the nose drops, and it begins to wind into a spiral dive.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. The distractor for a slip is: the ball is displaced to the outside. Wrong. In a slip the ball falls to the inside, towards the low wing, because the aeroplane is sliding down and inwards. The rule, step on the ball, works in both cases. So learn where the ball goes, and the correction follows.

Question. In a left turn, the ball is out to the right. Slip or skid, and what do you do?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: a skid. The ball is on the outside of the turn, so there is too much left rudder for the bank. Step on the ball, which here means easing off the left rudder, or adding right rudder pressure, until the ball centres.

## g, load factor and wing loading

You have already met this idea under another name. When the wing makes more lift than the aeroplane weighs, everything in the aeroplane is pressed down harder. Your body into the seat. The engine onto its mounts. The wings upwards against their attachments. The number that measures this is the load factor.

Load factor is lift divided by weight. In straight and level flight, lift equals weight, so the load factor is 1. In a level turn, the vertical part of the lift is the lift times the cosine of the bank angle, and that must equal the weight. Rearrange that, and you get the formula the exam uses. Load factor equals one over the cosine of the bank angle.

You don't need a calculator for the two banks the exam asks about. The cosine of 60 degrees is exactly a half, so at 60 degrees the load factor is one over a half, which is 2. The cosine of 45 degrees is about 0.707, so at 45 degrees the load factor is about 1.41.

g is the same number, expressed as multiples of the force of gravity. Pulling 2 g means a load factor of 2. You, your passenger and every part of the structure feel twice as heavy as on the ground. An 80 kg pilot presses into the seat as if they weighed 160 kg.

Wing loading is the weight divided by the wing area, for example in kilograms per square metre. Take a 1,000 kg aeroplane with a wing of 16 square metres. Its wing loading in level flight is 62.5 kg per square metre. In a manoeuvre, the wing supports the load factor times the weight. So at 2 g, it carries the equivalent of 125 kg on every square metre. A more heavily loaded wing needs a higher angle of attack, or a higher speed, to make its lift. And that is what links the load factor to the stall.

Question. What is the load factor in straight and level flight, and in a level 60 degree turn?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: 1 in level flight, because lift equals weight. In a level 60 degree turn it is one over the cosine of 60 degrees, one over a half, which is 2. The wing must make twice the aeroplane's weight in lift, so that its vertical half still equals the weight.

## Bank angle and the stall speed

Now link this to the stall. A wing stalls at its critical angle of attack. The level-flight stall speed is the speed at which the wing, at that angle, makes lift equal to the weight. In a turn the wing must make the load factor times the weight. At the critical angle, the only way to make more lift is to fly faster. And because lift grows with the square of the speed, the speed only needs to rise by the square root of the load factor.

So here is the rule. The stall speed in a turn equals the level-flight stall speed times the square root of the load factor.

[[diagram:stall-speed-vs-bank]]
Picture two curves climbing as the bank angle increases, one for the load factor and one for the stall speed. Both start almost flat, and then rear up steeply. That shape is what the syllabus means by the rate of increase of stall speed with bank.

At small bank angles the cosine barely changes, so the load factor, and with it the stall speed, rises slowly. 30 degrees of bank adds only about 7 percent to the stall speed. Beyond 45 degrees, the cosine falls away quickly towards zero, and the load factor races upwards. Here are the figures to know. At 45 degrees, a load factor of 1.41, and the stall speed up about 19 percent. At 60 degrees, a load factor of 2, and the stall speed up about 41 percent. At 75 degrees, a load factor of 3.86, and the stall speed up about 97 percent, very nearly double. From 60 to 75 degrees, the stall speed rises by more than it did from 0 to 60. And a level turn at 90 degrees of bank is impossible, because no amount of lift tilted on its side can hold the aeroplane up.

Let's work one. The level-flight stall speed is 50 kt. Find the load factor and the stall speed at 45 and at 60 degrees. Pause if you'd like to try it first.
[[pause 6]]
At 45 degrees, the load factor is one over the cosine of 45, one over 0.707, which is 1.41. The square root of 1.41 is 1.19. So the stall speed is 50 times 1.19, which is 59.5 kt. Call it about 60 kt.

At 60 degrees, the load factor is one over a half, which is 2. The square root of 2 is 1.41. So the stall speed is 50 times 1.41, which is 70.7 kt. Call it about 71 kt.

So: 45 degrees gives about 60 kt at 1.41 g, and 60 degrees gives about 71 kt at 2 g. Now think about what that means in the aeroplane. If you fly a 60 degree steep turn at 80 kt, your margin over the stall has shrunk from 30 kt to about 9 kt. A little too much back pressure takes the rest.

Here is one for you to try. An aeroplane's level-flight stall speed is 58 kt. Find the load factor and the stall speed at 45 and at 60 degrees of bank.
[[pause 8]]
At 45 degrees, the load factor is 1.41. Its square root is 1.19, and 58 times 1.19 is 69.0. So 69 kt, at 1.41 g. At 60 degrees, the load factor is 2. Its square root is 1.414, and 58 times 1.414 is 82.0. So 82 kt, at 2 g. Notice that the 45 degree turn added 11 kt to the stall speed, but the extra 15 degrees, from 45 to 60, added another 13 kt. The penalty grows faster than the bank.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. The load factor at 60 degrees is 2, but the stall speed does not double. It rises by the square root of 2, about 41 percent. Options like doubles, or 100 percent, are written for candidates who forget the square root. The opposite slip is to multiply by the load factor at 45 degrees, 1.41, instead of by its square root, 1.19.

## Bank angle and the structure

The wing does not care whether the extra lift is for a turn, a pull-up or a gust. The spars, the attachments and the skin carry the load factor times the weight.

The designer builds the airframe for a limit load factor. That is the greatest load it must carry in service without any permanent deformation. For light aeroplanes the usual certification limits are plus 3.8 g for the normal category, plus 4.4 g for the utility category, and plus 6 g for the aerobatic category. Check the flight manual for your aircraft. It states the category and the limits, and some aeroplanes are certified in more than one category at different weights.

[[diagram:category-load-limits]]
Picture three bars side by side, one for each category, each with two marks on it. The lower mark is the limit load: 3.8, 4.4 and 6 g. Above the limit lies a safety margin, up to the ultimate load, which is normally 1.5 times the limit. For a normal-category aeroplane, that is 5.7 g. Between the limit and the ultimate load, the structure may deform permanently. Think wrinkled skin, bent ribs or spars, popped rivets. Beyond the ultimate load, it may fail. And a bent wing may look fine from the ground, then fail later at a lower load.

Now put the turn formula against those limits. A level turn at 60 degrees is 2 g, comfortably inside. At 75 degrees, the load factor is one over the cosine of 75, one over 0.259, which is 3.86. That is already over the normal-category limit of 3.8 g. So an over-banked level turn can reach the limit load without feeling violent. And a hard pull out of a spiral dive can exceed it in a second. A suspected overstress must be reported, and the aeroplane inspected before it flies again.

Question. A pilot pulls 4.5 g in a normal-category aeroplane and lands safely. Why can the aeroplane not simply fly again?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: 4.5 g is above the 3.8 g limit load, but below the 5.7 g ultimate load. So the structure probably did not break, but it may have been permanently deformed. Hidden damage could fail at a lower load later, so it must be reported and inspected before further flight.

## Why steep turns are avoided low down

Everything so far comes together near the ground, where you have the least height to recover from a mistake. There are two situations to know: just after take-off, and in a glide.

First, shortly after take-off. The aeroplane is slow, near its best rate of climb speed, Vy, or its best angle of climb speed, Vx. A steep turn raises the stall speed towards that climb speed, so the margin shrinks to a few knots. The turn also costs climb. Tilted lift is not holding the aeroplane up, and the extra induced drag uses power that would otherwise climb, so obstacles may not be cleared. You are low, so a stall, a wing drop or the start of a spin leaves no height to recover. If the engine fails in a steep turn, the nose must come down at once, and the instinctive steep, slow turn back to the runway has killed many pilots. You may also meet the wake turbulence of the aeroplane that departed ahead. So climb straight ahead to a safe height, and keep climbing turns shallow, as your instructor and the flight manual direct.

Second, in a glide, especially on approach. In a glide you have no power to add. When you bank, the vertical part of the lift falls, and the only way to hold the airspeed is to lower the nose. So the rate of descent increases, and sharply as the bank steepens. On approach you have little height to give away. The gliding speed is already low, so the raised stall speed of the turn eats into a small margin. And an overshooting base-to-final turn tempts the wrong fix. Rudder to hurry the nose round, which is a skid. And back pressure to hold the nose up, which is more load factor. That is the stall-spin chain. Instead, accept the overshoot, correct gently, or go around.

Question. Why does the rate of descent increase in a gliding turn, even when you hold the same airspeed?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: in a bank, only the vertical part of the lift opposes the weight, so it is smaller than in a straight glide. Without power to make up the difference, the aeroplane descends faster. And the steeper the bank, the bigger the increase.

## Low-level turns in a strong wind: slip and skid that are not there

The aeroplane flies in the air mass. If the whole body of air is moving at 25 kt from the west, the aeroplane is carried along with it. A turn that is balanced relative to the air, with the ball centred and the airspeed steady, is a perfectly normal turn, whatever the wind is doing. The wind does not change the aerodynamics of the turn at all.

What the wind changes is how the aeroplane moves over the ground. Imagine you are at 500 ft along the coast near Lancelin, in a strong sea breeze. You can see the ground sliding past, and the drift produces two convincing illusions.

The first: turning from into wind to downwind. As you swing through the crosswind part of the turn, the wind is on the outside of the turn, and it blows the aeroplane sideways towards the inside. Against the ground, the aeroplane seems to be slipping inwards. At the same time, the groundspeed rises from airspeed minus the wind to airspeed plus the wind, so the ground appears to rush past faster and faster.

The second: turning from downwind to into wind. Now the wind is on the inside of the turn during the crosswind part, and it drifts the aeroplane outwards. Against the ground, it seems to be skidding outwards. And the groundspeed falls, so the aeroplane feels slow.

The danger is reacting to what you see. On the turn downwind, the rushing ground tempts you to raise the nose, and to add bottom rudder against the apparent slip. Raising the nose bleeds real airspeed towards a stall speed the turn has already raised. And the rudder turns an apparent slip into a real skid. Together, they stall the inside wing at low level. On the turn into wind, the feeling of slowness may tempt you to lower the nose towards the ground. So fly the turn by the airspeed indicator and the balance ball. They measure what the air is doing to the aeroplane.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. It asks what the pilot should do when a low-level downwind turn appears to slip. The answer is nothing different. If the ball is centred and the airspeed is correct, the turn is balanced. Options that add rudder or raise the nose describe the illusion working.

## Bringing it together

You are practising steep turns over the Mundijong training area. Your aeroplane has a level-flight stall speed of 50 kt, and a flight manual limit of plus 3.8 g.

You roll into 60 degrees at 90 kt, with rudder against the adverse yaw, then add power. The load factor is 2, and the stall speed is about 71 kt, so you have 19 kt in hand. The induced drag has roughly quadrupled. Halfway round, the bank creeps towards 70 degrees and the nose drops. That is the overbanking tendency. You check it with opposite aileron, rather than pulling harder, because at 75 degrees a level turn already passes 3.8 g. The ball drifts outwards, a skid, so you ease off the inside rudder.

Back in the Jandakot circuit, the same physics demands gentle turns after take-off and onto final. And in the sea breeze, your references are the ball and the airspeed indicator, not the rushing ground.

## Key points

Let's recap.

A balanced turn has the yaw matching the bank, no sideways airflow, and the ball centred. A slip puts the ball on the inside, from too much bank. A skid puts it on the outside, from too much rudder. Either way, step on the ball.

Load factor is lift divided by weight, and g expresses it as multiples of gravity. Wing loading is weight per unit of wing area, and in effect it rises with the load factor. In a level turn, the load factor is one over the cosine of the bank angle.

As bank increases, the stall speed rises with the square root of the load factor, slowly at first and steeply beyond 45 degrees. The structure carries the load factor times the weight. Beyond the limit, plus 3.8 g normal, plus 4.4 g utility, plus 6 g aerobatic, it may deform. Beyond 1.5 times the limit, it may fail. Check the flight manual.

Avoid steep turns after take-off: you are slow, the stall speed is raised, the climb is reduced, and there is no height to recover if the engine fails. Avoid them in a glide: the rate of descent rises sharply, the speed margin is small, and the skidding final turn waits.

In a strong wind at low level, a balanced turn appears to slip when turning downwind, and to skid when turning into wind, because of drift over the ground. Fly by the airspeed indicator and the ball.

And the numbers. At 45 degrees, a load factor of 1.41, and the stall speed times 1.19. At 60 degrees, a load factor of 2, and the stall speed times 1.41.

A final exam tip. Learn the two numbers. 45 degrees: load factor 1.41, stall speed up 19 percent. 60 degrees: load factor 2, stall speed up 41 percent. Multiply the level-flight stall speed by 1.19 or by 1.41. Expect questions on the ball, slip inside, skid outside. On the structural limit, plus 3.8 g for the normal category, with permanent deformation above it. On steep turns low down. And on the apparent slip when turning downwind, where the answer is to trust the instruments.
