This is RBKA 2.1, Piston engine. By the end you will know when to lean the mixture and when to go back to full rich, how to lean using nothing but the tachometer, how carburettor ice shows itself, and how carburettor heat both proves it is there and clears it. There are a few questions along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (RBKA 2.1)

The exam wants two things from you. First, how you use a manual mixture control on a piston engine driving a fixed-pitch propeller: when to lean, how to lean by RPM, and when to enrich. And second, how engine icing, which means carburettor icing, shows itself in an aeroplane with a fixed-pitch propeller, and how carburettor heat confirms it and clears it.

## Why this matters

On a flight from Jandakot to Northam at 6,500 ft, the engine is working in air about a fifth thinner than at the aerodrome. And unless you move the red knob, it is burning fuel as if it were still at sea level. That costs you endurance you may have planned on, and it fouls the spark plugs.

On the way home, a humid winter morning over the Darling Scarp and a long, low-power descent are exactly the conditions that grow ice in the carburettor. And that can happen with the outside air at 15 degrees.

Both problems announce themselves on the same instrument, the tachometer, the RPM gauge. And both are cured by a control you move with one hand. This is the operating side of the engine you met in the earlier lessons on piston engine aircraft and on malfunctions. The exam asks three things here. What you do with the mixture in each phase of flight. What the RPM does as you lean. And how to read the RPM when you pull the carburettor heat.

## Why a fixed-pitch propeller makes the tachometer your power gauge

Both parts of this subtopic say fixed-pitch propeller, and the reason is worth getting straight before anything else. A fixed-pitch propeller has blades set at one angle, bolted to the crankshaft. The engine and the propeller turn together. The only way for the propeller to turn faster is for the engine to make more power, or for the load on the propeller to fall. That is what happens when you lower the nose and speed up.

So at a steady airspeed, the RPM rises and falls directly with engine power. Anything that changes the power the engine makes shows up as a change in RPM. That includes the mixture, ice in the carburettor, and hot air from the carburettor heat.

On an aeroplane with a constant-speed propeller, a governor would hold the RPM constant. The same changes would show on the manifold pressure gauge instead. That is a PPL topic. In this lesson, the tachometer is the instrument that tells you everything.

There is one condition: hold the airspeed constant. Let the nose drop while you are leaning, and the RPM rises because you have sped up, not because the mixture is better. So leaning, and carburettor heat checks, are done in steady, trimmed flight.

## What the mixture control is for

A piston engine burns a mixture of fuel and air. It runs best within a narrow band of fuel to air ratio, roughly {1:15|one to fifteen} by weight for complete combustion. Too much fuel for the air is a rich mixture, and some of the fuel goes out of the exhaust unburnt. Too little fuel is a lean mixture, and the burn is slow, hot, and eventually ragged.

The carburettor meters fuel by the volume of air flowing through its venturi, the narrow throat in the middle. The pressure drop in the venturi draws fuel from the float chamber, in proportion to the airflow. It is set up so that with the mixture control fully forward, which is full rich, the engine gets a slightly rich mixture at sea level at high power. The extra fuel is deliberate. Fuel that evaporates in the cylinder absorbs heat. So a rich mixture helps keep the cylinders cool when the engine is working hardest, at take-off and in the climb.

Now climb. The air gets thinner. Each litre that passes through the venturi carries less oxygen by weight. But the carburettor is still metering fuel by volume. So it delivers roughly the same amount of fuel into air that weighs less. The mixture becomes richer with height. By the time you are cruising at 6,500 ft, a full rich mixture is well richer than the engine needs.

[[diagram:mixture-rich-to-lean]]
Picture a graph with the mixture running from full rich on the left to lean on the right. Engine power is a hump. It rises to a peak near the correct mixture, and falls away on both sides. On the rich side, to the left, the engine runs cool, wastes fuel, blows black smoke and fouls its plugs. On the lean side, to the right, it runs hot, the exhaust and cylinder temperatures climb, and it invites detonation. The best place is at the top of the hump.

An over-rich mixture has symptoms you can learn to recognise. Slightly rough running. A loss of power, which shows as a lower RPM than the throttle setting should give. High fuel consumption. Cooler than normal temperatures. And sooty, fouled spark plugs, which show up later as a rough magneto check.

The cure is to reduce the fuel flow, so the ratio is right again. That is leaning. The mixture control, usually a red knob or lever, is the device that does it, by restricting the fuel reaching the main jet.

Leaning is not just about economy. A correctly leaned engine runs smoother, and keeps its plugs clean. And the flight manual cruise fuel flows that your flight plan uses for endurance normally assume a leaned engine.

Question. You climb from a sea-level aerodrome to 7,500 ft without touching the mixture. Is the mixture now too rich or too lean, and why?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: too rich. The carburettor meters fuel by the volume of air passing through it. At 7,500 ft each volume of air weighs less, so the same fuel meets less air by weight. You lean to restore the right ratio.

## When to lean, and when to go back to full rich

The general pattern follows from two ideas. The mixture gets richer as the air thins. And the engine needs extra fuel for cooling at high power. Your flight manual, the POH, sets the actual rules for your type. Where it differs from what follows, the POH wins.

Let's go through a flight, phase by phase. There are six phases.

First, start, taxi and run-up: full rich. That gives cool, reliable running. And it means the magneto and carburettor heat checks are done at the setting you will take off with. Some flight manuals allow leaning for the taxi at high-elevation aerodromes.

Second, take-off and the climb at high power: full rich, unless the POH permits leaning at high density altitude. The extra fuel cools the cylinders at high power.

Third, the cruise: leaned, by the method you are about to learn. That applies at or below the POH power limit for leaning, which is commonly 75 percent power. The mixture has become rich with height, and you lean to restore it.

Fourth, the descent: enrich progressively as you come down. The air gets denser, so a leaned mixture becomes leaner still.

Fifth, the pre-landing checks: full rich. That has you ready for a go-around at full power, when you need the cooling fuel.

And sixth, shutdown: idle cut-off, with the mixture control fully back. That stops the engine with no fuel left in the cylinders.

So the pattern is simple. Full rich whenever the power is high or might suddenly need to be. Leaned in the cruise. And enrich on the way down.

Two of those phases deserve a word. The descent is the mirror image of the climb. As the air thickens, the same metered fuel meets more air. So a mixture leaned for 6,500 ft becomes progressively leaner as you come down. If you do nothing, by the time you add power in the circuit it may be lean enough to run rough, or overheat. So you enrich in steps as you descend, and go to full rich in the pre-landing checks. A go-around is the moment the engine goes to full power in a hurry, and it needs the full rich mixture for that.

At shutdown, pulling the mixture fully back to idle cut-off stops fuel at the jet. The engine runs the cylinders dry, and stops with nothing left to fire if someone moves the propeller.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. It reverses the direction: as the aeroplane climbs, the mixture becomes leaner. It does not. Less dense air with the same fuel gives a richer mixture. So you lean in the climb or the cruise, and you enrich in the descent. If you remember only one direction, remember this: thin air, rich mixture.

## Leaning by RPM, step by step

With a fixed-pitch propeller and no exhaust gas temperature gauge, the RPM method is how you find the right mixture. It works because engine power, and so the RPM, peaks at the best-power mixture and falls away on both sides of it. You are using the tachometer to find the top of that hump.

Here is the procedure, in six steps, with what the engine is doing at each one.

Step one: establish the cruise. Set the altitude. Set cruise power with the throttle, at or below the flight manual limit for leaning, commonly 75 percent. Let the speed settle, and trim. The engine is running rich, and the RPM is a little lower than it could be. You need a steady airspeed, so that every RPM change from here on is caused by the mixture.

Step two: move the mixture control slowly towards lean, watching the tachometer. As the fuel flow falls, the surplus fuel that was cooling the burn and going out unburnt is removed. The mixture moves towards the best-power ratio, combustion improves, and the RPM rises slightly.

Step three: notice the peak. The RPM reaches a maximum and stops rising. The engine is now at about the best-power mixture, the most power for this throttle setting.

Step four: keep going a fraction further, and watch for the fall. Leaner than the peak, there is no longer enough fuel for all the oxygen. The burn slows, power falls, and the RPM drops. And the engine begins to run rough, as some cylinders start to misfire. They never get exactly the same mixture as the others.

Step five: enrich at the first sign of an RPM drop or roughness. Bring the mixture back until the RPM is at the peak again and the engine is smooth. Then go a little richer, as your flight manual directs. This is called rich of peak. You give up a sliver of economy to keep a margin against running lean, because the cylinders furthest from the carburettor run leaner than the average.

And step six: re-lean after any significant change of power or altitude. The right setting depends on both. A climb needs further leaning. A descent needs enrichment.

So the whole sequence, in four words: rise, peak, fall, back.

[[diagram:leaning-by-rpm]]
Picture the tachometer reading drawn as a line, as you slowly pull the mixture back. It starts at {2350|twenty-three fifty} RPM. It climbs gently to a peak of {2400|twenty-four hundred}. Then it tips over and falls to {2370|twenty-three seventy}, and the engine roughens. The pilot pushes the mixture back in to the peak, and then just a little richer.

If an exhaust gas temperature gauge, an EGT gauge, is fitted, the flight manual method is usually different. You lean to the peak exhaust temperature, then enrich by a specified number of degrees. It is the same idea: find the peak, then step back to the rich side.

Let's work one. You are cruising at 6,500 ft at a steady airspeed, with the mixture full rich, and the tachometer reads {2350|twenty-three fifty} RPM. Pause if you'd like to say what happens as you lean.
[[pause 6]]
First, you lean slowly, and the RPM rises from {2350|twenty-three fifty} to {2400|twenty-four hundred}.

Second, you keep leaning. The RPM stops rising at {2400|twenty-four hundred}, which is the peak. Then it drops back to {2370|twenty-three seventy}, and the engine roughens.

Third, you enrich. The RPM comes back to about {2400|twenty-four hundred}, and the engine is smooth.

Fourth, you enrich slightly further, as the flight manual directs. The engine is now just rich of peak.

The aeroplane now burns noticeably less fuel per hour than at full rich, which your flight plan may already assume. And the engine is running smoothly, with a margin against overheating.

Now picture the same engine an hour later. You have descended from 6,500 ft to 3,500 ft for the arrival without touching the mixture. The engine is running a little rough, with the RPM slightly lower than usual for the throttle setting. What has happened, and what do you do? Pause and decide.
[[pause 6]]
The mixture was leaned for the thinner air at 6,500 ft. Descending into denser air, the same fuel flow now meets more air by weight. So the mixture has become too lean. It is past the peak on the lean side, which is why the RPM is down and the engine is rough.

The fix is to enrich. Move the mixture control forward until the engine is smooth and the RPM is back up. Then re-lean for 3,500 ft if you are cruising there, or go full rich if you are about to join the circuit. So the answer, in one line: too lean after the descent, and you enrich.

Now, what happens if you get it wrong? Too lean at high power is the dangerous one. A lean mixture burns hotter, and at high power the cylinders are already hot. The cylinder head temperature climbs. And the unburnt part of the charge can explode all at once, instead of burning smoothly outwards from the spark plug. That is detonation. It hammers the piston. It shows as rough running and a sharp rise in cylinder head temperature. And it can damage the engine within a short time. That is why you never lean in a climb at high power unless the flight manual says so. And it is why the mixture goes full rich before a go-around.

[[diagram:detonation-vs-normal-combustion]]
Picture two cylinders side by side. On the left, normal combustion. A smooth flame front spreads out from the spark plug, and the pressure in the cylinder rises smoothly. On the right, detonation. After the spark, the rest of the charge explodes all at once and hammers the piston, and the pressure trace shows a sharp spike. The causes listed beside it are a mixture too lean at high power, high manifold pressure with low RPM, fuel of too low an octane, and a high cylinder head temperature. And the remedy is to enrich, reduce power, lower the nose, and open the cowl flaps.

Too lean at any power gives rough running and a loss of power, and eventually the engine stops. Too rich wastes fuel, reducing your endurance below what you planned. And it fouls the spark plugs, which shows up as a rough magneto check.

Question. While leaning by RPM, the tachometer rises, peaks, then starts to fall, and the engine roughens. What is the engine telling you at each stage?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the rise means surplus fuel is being removed, and the mixture is approaching the best-power ratio. The peak is that best-power mixture. The fall and the roughness mean the mixture is now too lean. So you enrich back to the peak, and slightly richer, as the flight manual directs.

## How carburettor ice forms

Now the second element: ice in the engine's induction system, and how you know it is there. Start with where it forms.

Air entering the carburettor speeds up through the venturi, a narrowing of the passage. Faster air has lower pressure, and lower pressure means lower temperature. At the same place, fuel sprays into the airflow and evaporates. Evaporation takes heat from the air around it. It is the same reason a wet hand feels cold in a breeze.

Together, the pressure drop and the fuel evaporation can cool the air by up to about 20 to 30 degrees. If the air going in is humid, the moisture in it condenses and freezes on the walls of the venturi, and on the throttle butterfly. That is the valve above the venturi that your throttle lever opens and closes.

[[diagram:carburettor-ice-locations]]
Picture a carburettor cut in half, with the air coming in at the bottom and flowing upwards. Ice can form in three places. At the bottom, on the air intake and the filter, is impact ice, which needs visible moisture at or below freezing. In the middle, in the venturi and just past the fuel nozzle, is fuel evaporation ice, where the evaporating fuel takes heat from the air. And at the top, on and behind the partly closed throttle butterfly, is throttle ice, where the pressure drop is largest. Throttle ice is the most common, and the most dangerous.

That cooling is why carburettor ice forms on warm days. With the outside air at 20 degrees, the air in the venturi can be below freezing. The risk is highest in high humidity: visible moisture, rain, or a small spread between the temperature and the dew point. A humid morning after rain, or flying near cloud, can produce ice across a wide range of outside temperatures. Roughly from minus 10 to plus 30 degrees.

Fuel-injected engines have no venturi or butterfly of this kind, and they do not get carburettor ice. That is why this element is specific to carburettor engines.

The risk also depends on the throttle position. At low power, during a descent or a glide approach, the butterfly is nearly closed. The gap the air squeezes through is small, so the local pressure drop is greatest. And a thin layer of ice blocks a large share of a small opening. Low power is also when the engine produces the least heat around the carburettor. That is why the descent and the approach are the classic times for carburettor ice.

## How ice shows itself with a fixed-pitch propeller

Ice in the carburettor narrows the passage the air has to flow through. It does exactly what closing the throttle would do. Less mixture reaches the cylinders, and the engine makes less power. It also disturbs the mixture, because the fuel nozzle and the venturi no longer have the shape they were designed with.

With a fixed-pitch propeller, those changes give you three signs, usually in this order.

First, a gradual, unexplained drop in RPM, with the throttle not moved. This is the first sign, and it is easy to miss, because it is slow. A few tens of RPM, over several minutes.

Second, a loss of performance. If you are holding altitude, the airspeed falls. If you are holding airspeed, the aeroplane loses altitude, or the rate of climb falls.

And third, rough running, as the ice disturbs the mixture. Left alone, this progresses towards the engine stopping.

With a constant-speed propeller, the first sign would be a drop in manifold pressure instead, because the governor holds the RPM. That is the reason the element says fixed pitch.

The danger is in that first sign. A pilot who notices the RPM has crept down often pushes the throttle forward to put it back. The RPM recovers for a while. But the ice keeps growing behind a more open butterfly. Repeat that a few times, and the throttle is fully open with nothing in reserve.

[[diagram:chasing-the-rpm]]
Picture the RPM drawn against time, as a sawtooth sloping downhill. The line falls slowly as the ice builds. Each time the pilot opens the throttle, the line jumps back up for a moment. But the ice keeps building behind the more open butterfly, and the line sags again. At full throttle there is nothing left to give, and the engine stops.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. In a fixed-pitch aeroplane, an unexplained RPM drop is carburettor ice until proven otherwise. The wrong answer is to increase the throttle to restore the RPM. That hides the symptom while the ice grows. The right answer is full carburettor heat.

Question. You are cruising at a constant throttle on a humid morning, and you notice the RPM has slowly fallen by about 100. You are holding altitude. What else would you expect to see, and what do you suspect?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: a falling airspeed, because the engine is producing less power and you are holding height. Possibly some rough running too. And a gradual, unexplained RPM drop at constant throttle in humid air is the classic first sign of carburettor ice.

## Carburettor heat: confirming and clearing the ice

The carburettor heat control replaces the normal filtered intake air with hot air, taken from a muff around the exhaust. The hot air melts ice that has formed, and stops more forming.

It also does two other things, and they are the key to reading the tachometer. First, hot air is less dense, so the engine makes less power. Second, the same fuel meets less air by weight, so the mixture becomes richer. Both make the RPM fall when you apply heat, whether or not there is any ice.

That means applying full heat always gives an RPM drop. What tells you whether there was ice is what the RPM does next, and what it does when you return to cold. It is a decision you make by watching the tachometer through three stages.

[[diagram:carburettor-heat-decision]]
Picture two RPM traces, one above the other, both starting at the moment you pull full heat. The top trace has ice. The RPM has already crept down from {2400|twenty-four hundred} to {2300|twenty-three hundred}. On full heat it falls to {2200|twenty-two hundred}, and the engine runs rough briefly. Then it rises to {2350|twenty-three fifty} as the ice melts. And on returning to cold, it settles at {2400|twenty-four hundred}, higher than before. The bottom trace has no ice. The RPM drops slightly, stays there, and returns to its original value on cold.

So here is the pattern, stage by stage. Immediately on applying full heat, the RPM drops either way. With ice it drops, because of the less dense air and the richer mixture. With no ice it drops slightly.

While the heat stays on, the two cases part company. With ice, the RPM then rises as the ice melts, and the engine may run rough briefly as the water passes through. With no ice, the RPM stays at the lower value.

And on returning to cold: with ice, the RPM rises to higher than before the heat was applied. With no ice, it returns to the original value.

Every step in the ice case has a reason. The first drop is the hot air itself. The roughness is the melted ice going through the engine as water. That is unpleasant, but it means the heat is working. The rise while the heat is still on is the passage opening up as the ice melts, giving back the power the ice had stolen. And the final reading on cold is higher than before you started. That is because the RPM you had before applying heat was already reduced by ice. Clearing it has restored the power you had lost without noticing.

With no ice, there is nothing to melt and nothing to give back. The RPM drops with the hot air, stays down, and returns to exactly where it was.

There are four practical rules, and the flight manual for your type governs them.

First, use full heat, not partial, unless the flight manual says otherwise. Partial heat without a carburettor air temperature gauge can warm the air into the icing range, rather than out of it.

Second, leave it on long enough to clear the ice. That is typically some tens of seconds or more, until the RPM stops rising. Do not return to cold at the first rough running. That roughness is the sign the heat is working.

Third, before a long, low-power descent or a glide approach, most flight manuals call for full carburettor heat applied before you reduce power. The engine is still hot enough then to supply heat to the muff.

And fourth, return to cold before you need full power, for a go-around or a take-off, unless the flight manual says otherwise. Hot air means less power.

Let's work one. You are in the cruise at a constant throttle. The RPM is normally {2400|twenty-four hundred}, but it has crept down to {2300|twenty-three hundred}. You apply full carburettor heat. Pause if you'd like to say what you expect to see, if there is ice.
[[pause 6]]
First, on full heat, the RPM falls to {2200|twenty-two hundred}, because of the hot, less dense air. And the engine runs roughly for a few seconds.

Second, you keep the heat on, and the RPM climbs to {2350|twenty-three fifty} as the ice melts.

Third, after a further 30 seconds, you select cold. The RPM settles at {2400|twenty-four hundred}.

The final reading is higher than the {2300|twenty-three hundred} you had before applying heat. That confirms carburettor ice was present, and that it has been cleared. Keep an eye on the RPM, though. The conditions that made the ice have not gone away, so you may need the heat again. So: {2400|twenty-four hundred} on cold, above the {2300|twenty-three hundred} you started with, means there was ice.

Now one to read yourself. On a dry, clear afternoon you notice what might be a small RPM loss. You apply full heat. The RPM drops by about 75, and stays there, smooth, for 30 seconds. On returning to cold it comes back to the same reading you started with. Was there ice?
[[pause 5]]
The answer: no ice. The drop on applying heat is only the effect of hot, less dense air, and a richer mixture. There was no rise while the heat was on, because there was nothing to melt. And the RPM returned to the original value on cold, not a higher one. If the original loss persists, look for another cause rather than ice. That might be a mixture setting after a change of altitude, or a magneto problem.

Question. Why does the RPM end up higher than before when you return to cold after clearing carburettor ice?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because the reading before you applied heat was already reduced by the ice you had not yet noticed. Melting it restores the airflow, and the power the ice had taken. So the RPM on cold returns to what the engine should have been making all along.

## Bringing it together

It is a winter morning, and you are flying from Jandakot to Northam and back. It is 14 degrees, with a dew point of 12, after overnight rain. That is high humidity.

The mixture is full rich for the take-off and the climb. Level at 6,500 ft, you set cruise power, trim, and lean by RPM. Up to the peak, a little past it until the RPM sags, then back to the peak and a touch richer.

Cruising home at constant throttle, you notice the RPM has drifted down by 100, and the airspeed is a few knots low. You do not touch the throttle. Full heat. The RPM drops, and the engine coughs. Then the RPM climbs. And back on cold, it is above where it was. That was ice.

Before the descent, you apply full heat while the engine is still at cruise power. Then you reduce power, and enrich in steps on the way down. In the pre-landing checks, the mixture goes full rich, ready for a go-around. And the carburettor heat is set as the flight manual directs, so that full power is there if you need it.

## Key points

Let's recap.

The mixture first. Thin air with the same metered fuel makes the mixture richer with height. So you lean in the cruise, at or below the flight manual power limit, commonly 75 percent. You enrich in the descent. It is full rich for take-off, the climb and the pre-landing checks. And idle cut-off to stop the engine.

Leaning by RPM. Lean slowly, and the RPM rises to a peak. Then it falls, and the engine roughens. Enrich back to the peak and slightly richer. And re-lean after any change of power or height.

Getting it wrong. Too lean at high power risks detonation. Too rich wastes fuel, and fouls the plugs.

Carburettor ice. With a fixed-pitch propeller, it shows first as a gradual, unexplained RPM drop at constant throttle. Then a loss of airspeed or height. Then rough running. It can form with the air cooled by 20 to 30 degrees in the venturi, so in humid air it forms on warm days, and it is most likely at low power.

Carburettor heat. Apply full heat. With ice, the RPM drops, may run rough, then rises as the ice melts, and ends higher than before on cold. With no ice, there is a small drop that stays, and then the RPM goes back to the original value on cold.

A final exam tip. Expect five kinds of question. One on the direction of the mixture change with height: climbing makes it richer. One on the RPM sequence while leaning: rise, peak, fall, then return to the peak and slightly rich. One on the pre-landing mixture setting: full rich. One on the first sign of carburettor ice in a fixed-pitch aeroplane: a gradual RPM drop. And one that describes the RPM after carburettor heat, and asks whether there was ice. Higher than before on cold means ice was present. Back to the original means there was none. And remember that carburettor ice can form at outside temperatures up to about 30 degrees in humid air, and is most likely at low power.
