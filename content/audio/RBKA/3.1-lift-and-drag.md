This is RBKA 3.1, Lift and drag. By the end you will know the two angles of attack every pilot should carry in their head, how they turn into the stall speed and the best glide speed, what flap does to lift and drag, how attitude, angle of attack and airspeed are tied together in level flight, and why the aeroplane floats in the flare. There are a few questions along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (RBKA 3.1)

The exam wants four things from you. First, whether lift and drag go up or down when you change the flap setting. Second, the typical angle of attack at which a basic low-speed wing makes its maximum lift, and the angle at which it is most efficient, with its best lift to drag ratio. Third, how those two angles become two speeds: the stall speed and the best glide speed. And fourth, how attitude, angle of attack and airspeed are tied together in level flight.

## Why this matters

Two moments in your training depend on this lesson. The first is the engine failure after take-off from Murrayfield or Serpentine. The only thing you can do with the energy you have left is fly the speed that gives the most distance for the height. And that speed exists because of one particular angle of attack.

The second is the landing that floats halfway down Jandakot's runway, because you arrived 10 kt fast, and the wing, a few feet off the ground, suddenly had less drag than it should.

In between sits flap, which you use on every circuit, and which changes lift, drag, and the attitude the aeroplane flies at. This lesson builds on the lift formula and the drag curve from the basic aeronautical knowledge lesson, also called lift and drag. It turns them into the handful of angles and speeds the exam asks about.

## One wing, one angle, one speed

Start with what you already know. Lift depends on four things. The density of the air. The square of the speed. The wing area. And the lift coefficient, a number that says how hard the wing is working at its present angle of attack. In words, the formula is: lift equals a half, times the air density, times the speed squared, times the wing area, times the lift coefficient.

The angle of attack is the angle between the wing's chord line and the relative airflow. The relative airflow is the air coming at the wing from the direction it is travelling. Tilt the wing to a larger angle, and the lift coefficient rises in almost a straight line. That continues until the air can no longer follow the upper surface, and the lift collapses.

[[diagram:cl-vs-angle-of-attack]]
Picture a graph with the angle of attack along the bottom and the lift coefficient going up the side. The line climbs straight and steady from left to right, until it reaches a peak at about 16 degrees. That is the critical angle. Past it, the line turns down. The airflow has separated, and the lift falls, even though the nose is higher.

Now put the aeroplane in level flight at a fixed weight. Lift must equal weight, so the lift side of the formula is fixed. On a given day, at a given height, the density is fixed too, and so is the wing area. That leaves only two things that can trade against each other: the speed, and the lift coefficient.

Fly fast, and the speed squared is large, so the lift coefficient must be small. That means a small angle of attack. Fly slowly, and the lift coefficient must be large. That means a large angle of attack.

The conclusion is the backbone of this whole subtopic. In level flight at a given weight, each airspeed goes with exactly one angle of attack. And each angle of attack goes with exactly one airspeed. When the syllabus asks how an angle relates to a speed, this is the link.

## The two angles that matter

Of all the angles a wing can fly at, two have names. The exam expects you to know both, for a basic low-speed aerofoil, the kind on a training aeroplane.

The first is about 16 degrees: maximum lift. At about 16 degrees, the lift coefficient reaches its peak. This is the critical angle of attack. The wing is making the most lift it can for a given speed, but it is right at the edge. Increase the angle any further, and the airflow over the top separates, the lift falls, and the drag rises steeply. That is the stall, which the lesson on stalling covers in full. For a typical light aeroplane the critical angle lies somewhere around 15 to 18 degrees. The exam's figure is 16.

The second angle is about 4 degrees: the most lift for the least drag. This one is about efficiency, and it needs one new idea, the lift to drag ratio. It is simply the lift the wing makes, divided by the drag it costs. A wing with a lift to drag ratio of 10 is producing ten units of lift for every unit of drag. The higher the number, the more efficient the wing.

The lift to drag ratio changes with the angle of attack. Picture what happens as you move through the range.

At a very small angle, the wing makes little lift. So to hold the aeroplane up, you must be flying fast. At high speed, parasite drag is large. That is the drag of pushing the shape of the aeroplane through the air, and it rises with the square of the speed. So there is lots of drag for the lift: a poor ratio.

At a large angle, near the stall, the wing makes plenty of lift at a low speed. But the price of making lift, induced drag, is large. Induced drag comes from the air spilling round the wing tips, and the downwash behind the wing. It is greatest at high angles of attack and low speeds. So again, lots of drag for the lift: a poor ratio.

In between, there is an angle where the two kinds of drag are equal, and their total is smallest. In level flight, the lift is fixed at the weight. So the smallest drag means the largest lift to drag ratio. For a basic low-speed aerofoil, that angle is about 4 degrees, the best lift to drag angle. Either side of it, the ratio falls away.

[[diagram:drag-curves-vs-airspeed]]
Picture three curves drawn against airspeed, with speed increasing to the right. Parasite drag starts low on the left and rises more and more steeply to the right. Induced drag does the opposite. It starts high on the left, at low speed, and falls away to the right. Add them together, and the total drag is a U shape. The bottom of the U is where the two curves cross, where the two drags are equal. That is the speed for the best lift to drag ratio. Slower than that is called the back side of the curve.

Notice the two angles are a long way apart. At 4 degrees, the wing is working efficiently, with a large margin to the stall. At 16 degrees, it is making all the lift it can, and it has no margin left. You do most of your flying between them.

So here they are side by side, now that each one has a reason. About 4 degrees gives the best lift to drag ratio: the most lift for the least drag, and the minimum total drag. That makes it the most efficient angle, for the best glide, the best range, and the least thrust needed for level flight. About 16 degrees gives the maximum lift coefficient. That is the critical angle, and any increase beyond it causes the stall.

[[diagram:ld-ratio-vs-angle-of-attack]]
Picture two graphs stacked one above the other, sharing the same angle of attack scale along the bottom. In the top graph, the lift to drag ratio rises quickly to a sharp peak at about 4 degrees, then falls away on either side. In the bottom graph, the lift coefficient climbs in a straight line all the way to its peak at about 16 degrees, then collapses. The two peaks are about 12 degrees apart. The efficient wing has a big margin to the stall.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. It lists pairs of angles, and asks which is the best lift to drag angle and which is maximum lift. Maximum lift is the larger angle, 16 degrees. The most efficient angle is the smaller one, 4 degrees. A wing at maximum lift is not efficient. It is paying a huge induced drag bill for that lift.

Question. Why is the lift to drag ratio poor both at a very small angle of attack and near the critical angle?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: at a very small angle, the wing must fly fast to make enough lift, so parasite drag is large. Near the critical angle, it makes the lift slowly, but induced drag is large. Only around 4 degrees are the two drags balanced, so that the total drag is least for the lift made.

## From angles to speeds: the stall and the best glide

Now put the two ideas together. Each angle of attack corresponds, in level flight at a given weight, to one airspeed. So each of the two key angles has a speed attached to it.

First, the stall speed. It is the speed at which the angle of attack needed to hold the aeroplane up reaches the critical angle, about 16 degrees. At that speed the wing is producing its maximum lift coefficient, and only just making enough lift. Fly any slower, and no angle of attack will give enough lift. The angle needed would be beyond the critical angle, and the wing stalls. So the stall speed is the speed that goes with the maximum lift coefficient.

Second, the best glide speed. It is the speed that puts the wing at the best lift to drag angle, about 4 degrees. To see why that speed gives the best glide, look at the forces when the engine is not helping.

[[diagram:forces-in-a-glide]]
Picture an aeroplane gliding down a straight, sloping path, with no thrust at all. Lift acts at right angles to the path. Drag acts straight back along the path. And weight acts straight down. Lift and drag together make a single resultant, and it is exactly equal and opposite to the weight. The steepness of the path is set by the drag divided by the lift.

So less drag for the lift you are making means a flatter path. The flattest path of all comes when the drag divided by the lift is smallest. That is the same as saying the lift to drag ratio is greatest, at about 4 degrees. And the flattest path gives the greatest distance in still air for the height you have.

A wing with a lift to drag ratio of 9 glides 9 ft forward for every foot it descends. The details of glide distance, and what happens if you glide at the wrong speed, are in the lesson on descents. What matters here is the link. The best glide speed is the speed for the best lift to drag angle. And the minimum drag speed, at the bottom of the drag curve, is that same speed.

Now, what does weight do to both speeds? The angles belong to the wing. They do not change with weight. The speeds do. A heavier aeroplane needs more lift. At the same angle of attack, the only way to make more lift is to fly faster. So at a higher weight, the wing reaches 4 degrees at a higher speed, and 16 degrees at a higher speed. Both the best glide speed and the stall speed rise with weight. And both fall when you are lighter.

Because lift goes with the square of the speed, the speed for any given angle goes with the square root of the weight. In words: the new speed equals the old speed, times the square root of the new weight divided by the old weight.

[[diagram:best-ld-and-critical-angle-speeds]]
Picture a graph of the angle of attack needed for level flight, against airspeed, with two curves: one at maximum weight, and one at a lighter weight. Both curves fall from left to right, high angles at low speed and low angles at high speed. A horizontal line at about 4 degrees crosses them at the best glide speed: 70 kt at maximum weight, and about 63 kt when lighter. Another horizontal line, at about 16 degrees, crosses them at the stall speed, which is also lower when lighter. The angles stay where they are. Only the speeds slide.

Let's work one. The flight manual best glide speed is 70 kt, at the maximum weight of 1,000 kg. You are solo with low fuel, at 810 kg. What speed gives the best lift to drag angle today? Pause if you'd like to try it first.
[[pause 6]]
Step one, the weight ratio. 810 divided by 1,000 is 0.81.

Step two, the square root. The square root of 0.81 is 0.90.

Step three, the speed. 70 times 0.90 is 63 kt.

So at your weight, the best glide speed is about 63 kt. At the lighter weight, the best lift to drag still occurs at about 4 degrees. But less lift is needed, so the speed that gives 4 degrees is lower. The flight manual figure is for maximum weight. Gliding at 70 kt when you are light costs a little distance. The glide angle at the correct speed, though, is the same at both weights. So the answer again: 63 kt at 810 kg.

Here is one for you to try. An aeroplane's best glide speed is 65 kt at 750 kg. What is the speed for the same angle of attack at the maximum weight of 900 kg?
[[pause 8]]
The weight ratio is 900 over 750, which is 1.20. The square root of 1.20 is 1.095. And 65 times 1.095 is 71.2. So about 71 kt.

Heavier means a higher best glide speed, by the square root of the weight ratio. The best lift to drag angle is unchanged. So the best glide angle, and the still-air distance from a given height, are unchanged too.

Here's another trap the exam likes to set. A heavier aeroplane glides less far. It does not, if it is flown at its correct best glide speed. Weight changes the speed at which the best lift to drag angle is reached, not the ratio itself. So the glide angle, and the still-air distance, are the same. The heavy aeroplane simply gets there faster, with a higher rate of descent.

Question. What is the link between the critical angle of attack and the stall speed?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: in level flight at a given weight, each speed needs one angle of attack. The stall speed is the speed at which that angle reaches the critical angle, about 16 degrees, where the lift coefficient is at its maximum. Any slower, and the angle needed would be past the critical angle.

## What flap does to lift and drag

You lower flap on every circuit. It is worth understanding what it does to the wing, rather than just learning the answers.

A trailing-edge flap is a hinged section at the back of the wing that moves down. Lowering it increases the wing's camber, the curvature of the aerofoil. On some types, it increases the wing area too. Fowler flaps are an example. They slide back as well as down.

A more cambered wing turns the airflow more sharply. So at any given angle of attack, it makes more lift. It also makes more drag. The deflected flap presents more of itself to the airflow, and the extra lift brings extra induced drag.

So the answer to the flap question in the syllabus is short. At the same angle of attack and airspeed, lowering flap increases the lift, and increases the drag. Raising flap decreases the lift, and decreases the drag. Both go the same way. With flap down, the lift coefficient is higher at every angle, and so is the maximum lift coefficient.

[[diagram:flap-effect-on-lift-curve]]
Picture the lift curve again, the straight line rising to a peak, drawn twice. The flap up curve is the lower one. The flap down curve sits above it all the way along. More lift at every angle. Its peak is higher, so the stall speed is lower. The same lift is reached further to the left, at a smaller angle of attack, so the nose sits lower. Its peak is also slightly further to the left: the critical angle is a little lower with flap. And beside the curves, a reminder that the drag has increased too.

From that picture come three more consequences.

First, the stall speed is lower with flap down. The maximum lift coefficient is higher, so the wing can make the lift it needs at a lower speed before reaching its maximum. That is the point of flap for landing. You can approach and touch down slower.

Second, the same lift comes at a smaller angle of attack. At a given speed in level flight, the lift needed is the same as before. But the cambered wing makes it at a lower angle. So the aeroplane flies with its nose lower. That is why the view over the nose on a flapped approach is so much better. The critical angle with flap down is also slightly lower, though the stall speed is lower too.

And third, the lift to drag ratio is worse. Flap adds drag in a bigger proportion than it adds useful lift. So the best lift to drag ratio with flap down is lower than with flap up. That is why a flapped glide is steeper, and you use that deliberately on approach.

How much of each you get depends on how far you lower it. The first stage of flap, often about 10 degrees, gives a large increase in lift for a modest increase in drag. Some types use it for take-off, to lift off at a lower speed. Full flap adds relatively little extra lift, but a lot of drag. It steepens the approach and slows the aeroplane down, and it is used for landing. Your flight manual says which settings your type uses, and for what.

Question. You lower flap while holding the same airspeed in level flight. Will the nose need to be higher or lower, and why?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: lower. The more cambered wing makes the lift you need at a smaller angle of attack. And in level flight, a smaller angle of attack means a lower nose attitude. Drag has gone up too, so you also need a little more power to hold the speed.

## Attitude, angle of attack and airspeed in level flight

You cannot see the angle of attack from the cockpit. You can see the attitude, the angle of the nose relative to the horizon. And you can read the airspeed. In level flight those three are locked together, and the reason is the direction of the airflow.

In level flight the flight path is horizontal, so the relative airflow comes straight at the aeroplane, horizontally. The angle of attack is the angle between the chord line and that horizontal airflow. The chord line is fixed to the fuselage at a small angle, called the rigging angle, or the angle of incidence. So in level flight, the angle of attack is simply the pitch attitude, plus that fixed rigging angle. Raise the nose two degrees, and the angle of attack goes up two degrees.

Put that together with the idea that each speed has one angle of attack, and you get the relationship the syllabus wants. There are three cases.

At high airspeed, the angle of attack needed is low, and the nose is low, the attitude flatter. At a medium airspeed, the best lift to drag speed, the angle of attack is about 4 degrees, and the nose is slightly up. And at low airspeed, approaching the stall, the angle of attack is high, approaching 16 degrees, and the nose is high.

For every airspeed, there is one angle of attack, and one attitude, that holds level flight at a given weight and configuration. Slow down, and you must raise the nose, for more angle of attack, to stay level. And you must add power, to overcome the extra induced drag. Speed up, and you must lower the nose, or you climb. That is why your instructor teaches attitude for airspeed. In level flight, a chosen attitude is a chosen airspeed, once the power is set to match.

[[widget:attitude-and-angle-of-attack]]
Now imagine you can move the nose attitude and the flight path separately. The wing stalls at an angle of attack, not at an attitude. The same nose position can be flying happily, or stalled, depending on where the aeroplane is going.

The link only holds in level flight. In a climb or a descent, the relative airflow is inclined to the horizon, because it comes from wherever the aeroplane is going.

[[diagram:angle-of-attack-vs-attitude]]
Picture an aeroplane climbing, seen from the side. Its nose is 10 degrees above the horizon. But its flight path is climbing at 6 degrees, so the relative airflow comes at it from 6 degrees above the horizontal, ahead. Measure the angle of attack from the flight path, not from the horizon. 10 minus 6 leaves about 4 degrees. So a nose 10 degrees up can mean an angle of attack of only about 4 degrees, plus the small rigging angle.

The opposite case is just as important. A nose-low aeroplane being pulled hard out of a dive can be at the critical angle.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. A nose-high attitude means a high angle of attack. Only in level flight. In a climb, the nose is high, but the angle of attack can be small. And in a steep descent or a pull-out, the nose can be below the horizon with the wing at the critical angle. The exam uses attitude as the distractor in stall and angle of attack questions.

Question. You hold level flight at 90 kt, then at 110 kt, at the same weight. Which needs the lower nose attitude, and why?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: 110 kt. At the higher speed, the wing makes lift equal to the weight at a smaller angle of attack. And in level flight, the angle of attack is the attitude plus the fixed rigging angle. So a smaller angle means a lower nose.

## Ground effect: the wing near the runway

There is one place where a wing's drag changes without any change in speed or angle of attack: within about a wingspan of the ground. You meet it in every flare.

Recall where induced drag comes from. A wing making lift pushes air down behind it. That is the downwash. And the high-pressure air underneath spills round the tips, into wing-tip vortices. The downwash tilts the airflow over the wing, which tilts the lift slightly rearwards. That rearward part is induced drag.

Now bring the wing close to the ground. The ground is in the way. The air cannot be pushed down as far, and the tip vortices cannot fully form. The downwash is reduced, the airflow over the wing is less tilted, and the lift stands more upright. The result is less induced drag, and a little more lift at the same angle of attack. This is ground effect.

It is noticeable from about one wingspan up. It becomes significant within about half a wingspan, which for a training aeroplane is the last 15 ft or so of the approach. It is strongest on a low-wing aeroplane, whose wing sits closer to the ground.

[[diagram:ground-effect]]
Picture the same wing twice, seen from behind. On the left, it is more than a wingspan up. It pushes a deep downwash behind it, and big tip vortices curl off each end. On the right, it is within about half a span of the runway. The ground blocks the downwash, and the vortices are squashed flat. So the induced drag falls, and the wing makes a little more lift at the same angle of attack.

Two consequences follow, one for each end of the runway.

On landing, the induced drag falls away just as you close the throttle and flare. With less drag, the aeroplane does not slow down the way it did on the approach, and it floats. Arrive with excess speed, and it floats a long way, using runway you may not have. That is why the approach speed matters so much in the last few hundred feet. Extra knots on final become extra hundreds of metres in the flare.

On take-off, the reverse catches people out. Ground effect lets the aeroplane lift off at a speed at which it could not climb out of ground effect. It flies a few feet up. Then, as it climbs out of ground effect, the induced drag returns, and the aeroplane may sink back, or refuse to accelerate. On a hot day at a high density height, with a heavy aeroplane, this is how strips are run off the end. The cure is to lift off at the flight manual speed. And if you do get airborne early, stay low in ground effect, and accelerate before climbing.

Question. Why does an aeroplane float further in the flare if it arrives at the runway fast?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: in ground effect, induced drag falls, so the aeroplane loses speed more slowly than on the approach. Any excess speed has to be bled off with less drag to do it. So it floats a long way before it is slow enough to touch down.

## Bringing it together

You depart Murrayfield solo on a warm afternoon, at 820 kg, with the first stage of flap set, as your flight manual allows for take-off. The flap gives more lift at every angle, so you lift off at a lower speed. You resist the urge to haul the aeroplane off early, because ground effect would let it fly before it can climb.

At a safe height, you raise the flap. Lift falls at the same angle, so the nose comes up a little to keep climbing.

At 1,500 ft, the engine stops. You lower the nose to the best glide attitude, because the most distance for your height comes from flying the wing at its best lift to drag angle, about 4 degrees. The flight manual says 70 kt at maximum weight. At your weight, the same angle comes at about 63 kt, and you fly close to that, rather than the book figure. You do not raise the nose to stretch the glide. A higher angle of attack takes you off the best lift to drag angle, and steepens the path.

On short final, with full flap, the nose is noticeably lower, the glide steeper, and the stall speed lower. You arrive at the right speed, so the float in ground effect is short.

## Key points

Let's recap.

Flap. Lowering flap increases both lift and drag, at the same angle of attack and speed. Raising it decreases both. Flap raises the maximum lift coefficient, so the stall speed is lower. It gives the same lift at a smaller angle, so the nose is lower. And it lowers the best lift to drag ratio, so the glide is steeper.

The two angles. A basic low-speed wing makes maximum lift at about 16 degrees, the critical angle. And it is most efficient at its best lift to drag ratio, about 4 degrees, where the total drag is least.

From angles to speeds. In level flight, each speed needs one angle of attack. The stall speed is the speed for the critical angle. The best glide speed is the speed for the best lift to drag angle. Both speeds rise with weight, by the square root of the weight ratio. The angles do not change.

Attitude. In level flight, the angle of attack is the attitude plus a fixed rigging angle. High speed: low angle, nose low. Low speed: high angle, nose high. The link breaks in a climb or a descent, because the relative airflow comes from the flight path.

A final exam tip. Have 4 degrees and 16 degrees by heart, and do not swap them. 4 degrees is the best lift to drag ratio and the best glide. 16 degrees is maximum lift and the stall. Flap questions want both increase, for lift and drag, when flap is lowered. And they want nose lower, for the attitude at the same speed. A question about a heavier aeroplane wants the best glide speed and the stall speed higher, and the angles unchanged. And any question that equates attitude with angle of attack is true only in level flight.

That's the end of RBKA 3.1.
