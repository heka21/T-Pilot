This is RBKA 3.8, Structural damage. By the end you will know what damage, including a bird strike, does to the stall and to the handling, why an overstressed aeroplane must not fly again until it is inspected, and how to fly a damaged aeroplane home. There are worked examples and questions along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (RBKA 3.8)

The exam wants one thing from you, in two parts. Describe what structural damage, including a bird strike, does to the stall characteristics of the aeroplane. And describe what it does to the aeroplane's controllability.

## Why this matters

An aeroplane is designed around a smooth, correctly shaped wing, and a structure that has never been bent. Take either away, and the numbers in the flight manual stop being true.

Bird strikes are the damage you are most likely to meet. Waterbirds and raptors work the wetlands around Jandakot. Galahs and corellas flock over country strips in the wheatbelt. And most strikes happen in the climb-out and the approach, low and slow, exactly when you have the least margin.

The other common source is the pilot. A hard pull out of a dive, a fast entry into turbulence, or a heavy landing can bend the airframe without breaking anything you can see.

The exam asks what damage does to the stall and to the handling, and what you do about it. Every answer comes from understanding how the wing makes lift, and how the structure carries it.

## How an aeroplane gets damaged

Damage reaches the airframe in a handful of ways, and they fall into two families: damage from outside, and damage from loads.

Damage from outside first. A bird strike is the most common. The usual impact points are the parts that face forward. The leading edges of the wing and the tailplane. The windscreen. The propeller and spinner. And the engine air intake. Strikes cluster on take-off, climb, approach and landing. And they cluster near coasts, rivers, wetlands, rubbish tips, and aerodromes with large grass areas.

Hail dents every upward-facing and forward-facing surface at once. Ground handling accidents are another source, like a wing tip against a hangar door, or a tug against the tail. So is contact with an obstacle, such as a fence post or a tree on a short strip.

Ice and frost are not damage in the usual sense, but they act like it. They change the shape and the roughness of the wing in exactly the way a dent does.

The second family is damage from loads. Every part of the structure is designed to carry a certain load. Ask more of it, and it bends or breaks. That is overstress, and it is usually the pilot who causes it. By pulling too much g. By flying too fast in turbulence. Or by landing heavily. Because overstress so often leaves no obvious mark, it gets its own chapter.

## Overstress and the flight envelope

Remember from the lesson on turning that the load factor is lift divided by weight. It is the number of g the structure is carrying.

The designer builds the airframe to a limit load factor for its category. For a normal-category aeroplane, that is plus 3.8 g, and minus 1.52 g. For the utility category, plus 4.4 g. For the aerobatic category, plus 6 g. The ultimate load is normally 1.5 times the limit. Between the limit and the ultimate load, the structure may bend permanently. Beyond the ultimate load, it may fail. Check the flight manual for your aeroplane. It states the category and the limits.

[[diagram:load-factor-limits]]
Picture three bars side by side, one for each category, reaching up above zero and down below it. The normal category runs from plus 3.8 down to minus 1.52 g. Utility runs from plus 4.4 to minus 1.76 g. Aerobatic runs from plus 6.0 to minus 3.0 g. Above the normal-category limit there is a band up to the ultimate load, 5.7 g, where the structure bends permanently. Above that, it breaks. For comparison, a level turn at 60 degrees is 2 g, and at 70 degrees, 2.9 g.

Now, the stall protects you, up to a point. There is a natural ceiling on how much g you can pull. The wing cannot make more lift than it makes at the critical angle of attack. And at low speed, that ceiling is low.

From the lift formula, the most lift the wing can make rises with the square of the speed. So the greatest load factor you can reach at any airspeed is that airspeed divided by the level-flight stall speed, squared. At the stall speed itself, you can pull only 1 g. At twice the stall speed, you can pull 4 g before the wing stalls.

Somewhere in between, the stall ceiling reaches the limit load. That speed is the manoeuvring speed, Va. Va equals the stall speed times the square root of the limit load factor. For a normal-category aeroplane, that is the stall speed times the square root of 3.8.

Below Va, if you pull the control column fully back, the wing stalls before it can reach 3.8 g. The stall acts as a safety valve. Above Va, the same full pull reaches the limit load before the wing stalls, and the structure takes it.

[[diagram:v-n-diagram]]
The graph that shows all this is the {V-n|vee en} diagram, or flight envelope. Picture load factor going up the side, and airspeed along the bottom. A stall curve rises from the left, steeper and steeper. The limit load, plus 3.8 g, is a flat line across the top, and the curve meets it at Va, about 101 kt for an aeroplane with a 52 kt stall speed. Below the zero line there is a negative limit, minus 1.52 g. Further right, VNO marks the end of normal operation, and the envelope is closed off by VNE, the never-exceed speed. To the left of Va, a full pull stalls first. To the right of Va, it can overstress the structure.

Let's work one. The level-flight stall speed at maximum weight is 52 kt, and the aeroplane is in the normal category, plus 3.8 g. What is the manoeuvring speed? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
Step one, the square root of the limit load factor. The square root of 3.8 is 1.949.

Step two, the manoeuvring speed. 52 times 1.949 is 101 kt.

So below about 101 kt, a full, abrupt pull stalls the wing before it can overstress the structure. Above it, the same input, or a sharp gust, can exceed 3.8 g. The answer again: 101 kt.

Now the exam's favourite twist, and most candidates guess wrong. Va falls as the aeroplane gets lighter.

Here is why. A lighter aeroplane has a lower stall speed, because the stall speed goes with the square root of the weight. So at any given airspeed, the lighter aeroplane is further above its stall speed, and its wing can make more g before it stalls. Its stall curve moves to the left, and it reaches the 3.8 g line at a lower speed. Put the other way round: the wing's strength is fixed, and a lighter aeroplane needs less lift to reach the same g. So it gets there sooner.

[[diagram:va-vs-weight]]
Picture two stall curves on the same graph. The right-hand one is the aeroplane at maximum weight, meeting the 3.8 g line at 101 kt. The left-hand one is the same aeroplane at 80 percent of that weight, meeting the line at about 90 kt. Between 90 and 101 kt, a full pull stalls the heavy aeroplane first, but it can overstress the light one.

Let's work it. The aeroplane above has a Va of 101 kt at its maximum weight of 1,100 kg. Today it weighs 880 kg. What is the manoeuvring speed today? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
Step one, the weight ratio. 880 divided by 1,100 is 0.80.

Step two, the square root of 0.80, which is 0.894.

Step three, 101 times 0.894 is 90.3. Call it 90 kt.

So flown solo with light fuel, the aeroplane is protected by the stall only below about 90 kt, not the 101 kt you might remember from the flight manual. Many flight manuals publish Va at more than one weight for exactly this reason. Use the figure for the weight you are at. The answer: about 90 kt.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. The distractor says Va increases as weight decreases. It decreases. A lighter aeroplane stalls at a lower speed. So at any speed it can pull more g before the stall saves it, and it reaches the limit load at a lower speed.

Gusts load the structure too. A gust does to the wing what a pull on the control column does. An up-gust suddenly adds an upward part to the relative airflow. That raises the angle of attack with no change of attitude, and the extra lift arrives as extra load factor.

How much extra depends on how fast you are going. The extra lift from a given gust grows roughly in proportion to the airspeed. Fly through the same rough air at 120 kt instead of 90 kt, and every bump hits the structure about a third harder. A lighter aeroplane also feels each gust more, because the same increase in lift is a larger fraction of its weight.

That is why the green arc on the airspeed indicator ends at VNO, the maximum structural cruising speed. The yellow arc above it is for smooth air only. Up to VNO, the structure is designed to take the certification gusts. Above it, it is not. In turbulence, slow down to the flight manual's turbulence penetration speed, which is usually close to Va. Then a strong gust stalls the wing for a moment, rather than overstressing it. You'll find the arcs in detail in the lesson on aircraft limitations.

Question. Why does flying below Va protect the structure from a full control input?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: below Va, the most lift the wing can make before it stalls is less than 3.8 times the weight. So a full pull stalls the wing first, and the stall caps the load factor below the limit.

Now, what to do after a suspected overstress, or a heavy landing. Overstress may leave no visible sign from the cockpit. Even on the ground, the damage may be hidden inside the wing, or behind fairings.

There are signs worth looking for. Wrinkled or buckled skin, especially near the wing roots, the tail and the firewall. Popped or loose rivets. Cracked paint at joints. Doors or panels that no longer fit. Fuel or oil leaks. And after a heavy landing, a bent or splayed undercarriage leg, a damaged tyre or wheel, or a scraped propeller tip or tail. But not seeing any of these does not mean the aeroplane is undamaged.

So the rule is simple. If you think you may have exceeded the limit load, or landed heavily enough to wonder, say so. Enter it as a defect in the maintenance release. And do not fly the aeroplane again until a licensed engineer has inspected it and signed it off. The next pilot will not know what the aeroplane went through unless you tell them.

[[diagram:defect-endorsement-flow]]
Picture a short flow chart, running top to bottom. A defect is found, on the ground or in flight. It is written up in the defect section of the maintenance release, Part 2, with a description, the date, the time in service, and your signature and licence number. And then the aeroplane cannot fly until a licensed engineer fixes it and signs it off, or endorses it as a permissible unserviceability, with any conditions.

## What damage does to the stall

The wing's maximum lift depends on the air following its upper surface as far as possible before it separates. And that depends, above all, on a smooth, correctly shaped leading edge. The leading edge is where the airflow speeds up hardest, around a tight curve. So a small change there has a large effect. The air has to follow the curve, and any dent, tear or lump trips it into turbulence.

Picture a bird strike that has pushed in a section of the left leading edge. Three things follow.

First, the stall speed increases. Behind the dent, the airflow separates earlier, at a lower angle of attack. The maximum lift coefficient falls, and the critical angle itself is reduced. So the wing stalls at a higher speed than the flight manual figure. Possibly by a large margin, because there is no way of knowing how much the damage has cost. Damage also adds drag. So the aeroplane needs more power to hold a given speed, and it climbs less well.

[[diagram:damaged-leading-edge-flow]]
Picture two wing sections side by side, at the same angle of attack. Over the clean one, the airflow follows the upper surface almost to the trailing edge. Over the dented one, the flow is tripped into turbulence and separates early, well forward on the wing. Beside them, two lift curves. The damaged wing's curve peaks lower, and at a smaller angle. So it stalls at a higher speed than the book says, and that wing stalls first and drops.

Second, the stall becomes asymmetric. If only one wing is damaged, that wing reaches its reduced critical angle first. At the stall it drops sharply, while the other wing is still flying. That is a pronounced wing drop, and a strong tendency to spin, at a speed you would have thought was safe. The same is true at higher load factors. A damaged wing in a turn stalls first, and earlier than normal.

Third, the warnings may fail. The stall warning vane or reed is set for an undamaged wing. It triggers when the airflow at one point on the leading edge reaches a certain angle. If the damaged area stalls first, the warning may come late, or not at all. The buffet, which comes from the separated wake striking the tail, may also come later, or feel different. The warnings you learned to trust in the lesson on stalling can no longer be relied on.

The conclusion for the pilot: the published stall speeds and the stall warning are no longer valid. Fly faster than normal. Avoid high angles of attack. And avoid steep turns, which raise the stall speed further.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. It may offer: the stall speed is unchanged, because the critical angle is a property of the aerofoil. But the point is that damage changes the aerofoil. A damaged leading edge separates the flow earlier, reduces the maximum lift and the critical angle, and raises the stall speed, usually on one side only.

Question. A bird has dented the right wing's leading edge. Describe the likely stall.
[[pause 4]]
The answer: it will come at a higher speed than the book figure. The right wing will stall first and drop, possibly with a tendency to spin. And the stall warning may sound late, or not at all. Keep the speed well up, and avoid steep turns.

## What damage does to controllability

The second half of the element asks how damage changes your ability to control the aeroplane. Think of the controls as surfaces that need clean airflow, intact hinges, and free cables or pushrods to work. And think of the aeroplane as something that flies straight only because it is symmetrical. There are five kinds of damage to know.

The first is damage to a control surface or its linkage. A dented aileron, elevator, rudder or trim tab, a bent hinge, or a damaged cable can reduce control authority. It can make a control jam. Or it can let the surface vibrate. A loose or damaged surface can flutter. That is an oscillation that grows rapidly with speed, and it can tear the surface off. Any new vibration after damage is a reason to slow down.

The second is asymmetric damage. A damaged wing makes less lift and more drag than the other. So the aeroplane wants to roll and yaw towards the damaged side, and you must hold aileron and rudder against it.

How much you need depends on speed, in two competing ways. At low speed, the controls themselves become weak, because their power depends on the airflow over them. Meanwhile the lift loss on the damaged wing gets relatively worse as the angle of attack rises. So there comes a speed at which full control deflection can no longer hold the wings level. At high speed, the controls are powerful, but the asymmetric forces, and the risk of flutter and further damage, grow. That is why the answer is a moderate speed, found by test. Not as slow as possible, and not as fast as possible.

[[diagram:control-effectiveness-vs-airspeed]]
Picture a grid rating the elevator, the rudder and the ailerons in three cases. At low airspeed with the power off, all three are weak and sloppy. At low airspeed with high power, the slipstream over the tail makes the elevator and rudder work, but the ailerons, outside the slipstream, stay weak. At high airspeed, all three are effective, and heavier.

The third is damaged flaps. A flap damaged by a bird may jam. Or it may extend on one side only, or further on one side than the other. Asymmetric flap makes more lift on the side with more flap, so the aeroplane rolls towards the side with less. It is worst at low speed, when the ailerons are weakest, and it can be more than the ailerons can hold. So if the flaps are damaged, or you are unsure, a flapless approach is generally safer. Leave them where they are.

The fourth is a damaged windscreen or propeller. A damaged windscreen can let the bird in. It can injure the pilot and reduce visibility. And it can add noise and wind blast that make talking and concentrating hard. A damaged propeller causes severe vibration, because a blade out of balance shakes the whole engine on its mounts. Reduce power at once. If the vibration is severe, the engine may have to be shut down before it tears itself from its mounting.

And the fifth is structural deformation, from overstress or a strike. It can change the aeroplane's rigging. So the trim changes, and the aeroplane handles differently from what you expect.

Question. Why might an aeroplane with a damaged wing be controllable at 80 kt, but not at 60 kt?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the ailerons and rudder lose authority as the speed falls. Meanwhile the damaged wing loses lift relative to the good one as the angle of attack rises. At some speed, full control can no longer balance the difference. That speed sets the minimum for the approach.

## Flying a damaged aeroplane home

The sequence follows from everything so far. Keep flying. Find out what you have. Then plan an approach that keeps well clear of the new, unknown limits. There are seven steps.

One. Fly the aeroplane. Keep control, and a safe airspeed. Do not slow down.

Two. Assess the vibration, the engine indications, the control response, and what you can see of the damage.

Three. If controllability is in doubt and you have the height, do a controllability check at a safe height. Set the aeroplane up as you intend to land. Then slow down gradually, until control starts to become difficult, or the speed approaches the stall. Difficult means, for example, that the aeroplane cannot be held level even with a lot of aileron. That speed is the minimum safe speed for the damaged aeroplane. Plan the approach with a good margin above it.

Four. Avoid changing the configuration, particularly the flap, unless you must. If the flap is damaged, a flapless approach may be safer.

Five. Fly a flatter, wider, faster approach, with gentle bank angles, to a long runway if there is one, and land as soon as practicable.

Six. Declare an emergency, a MAYDAY or a PAN PAN, as appropriate.

And seven. After landing, report the damage, and have the aircraft inspected before it flies again.

Let's work one. After a bird strike on climb-out, the left wing's leading edge is dented. The normal approach speed is 65 kt. How do you choose the approach speed? Pause if you'd like to think it through.
[[pause 6]]
First, climb to a safe height, 3,000 ft, and lower the flap you intend to land with.

Second, slow down gradually, watching for the point where control becomes difficult. At 65 kt, you need full right aileron to stop a roll to the left. That is the minimum controllable speed today.

Third, add a margin above that minimum. Fly the approach at about 75 to 80 kt.

And fourth, plan for the consequences. The longest runway available. Gentle bank. No trust in the stall warning. And a longer landing roll, from the higher speed.

The normal approach speed would have put you exactly at the edge of control, with no margin for a gust or a turn. So the answer: about 75 to 80 kt.

[[diagram:controllability-check]]
Picture a speed scale. At 65 kt, a marker: full right aileron only just holds the wings level. That is the minimum. The normal approach speed, also 65 kt, sits right on top of it. And above it, a band at about 75 to 80 kt, where you fly the approach.

Last, reporting. A bird strike is a routine reportable matter. That means a written report to the ATSB, the Australian Transport Safety Bureau, within 72 hours. Check that against the current regulations. Serious damage or injury makes it an immediately reportable matter, reported by phone as soon as practicable. And separately, any damage or suspected overstress goes into the maintenance release as a defect. The aeroplane stays on the ground until an engineer has cleared it.

## Bringing it together

You depart Jandakot on runway {06|zero six} on a still morning, and pass through a flock of ibis at 300 ft. There is a thump on the left wing, and the aeroplane yaws left. You hold it with rudder, keep the climb speed, and keep climbing straight ahead.

At 2,000 ft over the training area, you can see a dent in the left leading edge, and the aeroplane needs right aileron to fly level. Think through what this lesson tells you. The left wing will stall first, at a speed above the book figure, without a reliable warning. And the aileron you are already holding will run out at some lower speed.

You make a PAN PAN call. Then you do the controllability check in the landing configuration, and you find the limit at 65 kt. So you plan 75 to 80 kt. A long, flat final to the longest runway. Gentle turns. No flap changes once set. And a longer landing roll.

On the ground, you report the strike to the ATSB, write it up in the maintenance release, and leave the aeroplane for the engineer.

## Key points

Let's recap.

The stall. Damage to a wing, especially its leading edge, separates the flow earlier. So the stall speed rises above the book figure. The damaged wing stalls first and drops, with a tendency to spin. And the stall warning may come late, or not at all.

Controllability. Damaged surfaces, hinges or cables reduce authority, jam, or flutter. Asymmetric damage must be held with aileron and rudder, and it becomes uncontrollable below some speed. Asymmetric flap can be more than the ailerons can hold. And a damaged propeller vibrates, and needs the power reduced.

The response. Fly the aeroplane and keep the speed up. Check controllability at a safe height, in the landing configuration. Approach well above that speed, with gentle turns and no configuration changes. And report it.

Overstress. Above Va, which is the stall speed times the square root of 3.8, a full pull or a gust can exceed the limit load. And Va falls as the weight falls. A suspected overstress or a heavy landing is a defect. Write it up, and have it inspected before the aeroplane flies again.

A final exam tip. The exam answer to structural damage is almost always this. The stall speed rises, and one wing may stall first. So keep the speed up, avoid steep turns and configuration changes, and find a safe approach speed with a controllability check at a safe height. If the flap is damaged, a flapless approach is generally safer. And if Va appears, remember that it decreases with weight.

That's the end of RBKA 3.8.
