This is RBKA 3.2, Flight controls. By the end you will know which axis each control works about, what each control does beyond its main job, how power and airspeed change the way the controls feel and the way the aeroplane trims, what trim is really for, and what flap does to lift, drag and attitude. There are a few questions along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (RBKA 3.2)

The exam wants four things from you. First, what the elevator, the rudder and the ailerons each do first, which is the primary effect, and what follows from it, which is the further effect, and about which axis. Second, how changes of power and airspeed alter the pitch trim, and how well each of the three controls works. Third, what the trim controls are for. And fourth, what lowering or raising flap does to lift, drag and attitude.

## Why this matters

Picture the first time you open the throttle for a go-around at Jandakot. The aeroplane is trimmed nose-up for a 65 kt approach, with full flap hanging out. The nose rears up and swings left. You need a firm push, and a bootful of right rudder, to keep it where you want it.

Nothing has gone wrong. The controls are doing exactly what this lesson describes. The aeroplane is reacting to power, airspeed and flap the way every single-engine trainer does. Understanding why turns that moment from a surprise into a sequence you expect, and fly calmly.

The exam asks which axis each control works about, what each control does beyond its main job, which controls the slipstream helps, why you trim, and what flap does to lift, drag and attitude.

## Three axes through one point

An aeroplane in the air is free to rotate in any direction. Every rotation happens about its centre of gravity, the point where its whole weight can be taken to act. To describe those rotations, we use three imaginary lines through the centre of gravity, each at right angles to the other two.

The first is the longitudinal axis. It runs from the nose to the tail. Rotation about it is roll: one wing goes up, and the other goes down.

The second is the lateral axis. It runs from wing tip to wing tip. Rotation about it is pitch: the nose goes up or down.

And the third is the normal axis, also called the vertical axis. It runs from top to bottom, at right angles to the other two. Rotation about it is yaw: the nose swings left or right.

[[widget:axes-and-controls]]
Picture the aeroplane with three skewers through it, all crossing at the centre of gravity. One runs nose to tail, one runs tip to tip, and one runs straight down through the cabin roof. Move the controls, and the aeroplane spins about one skewer at a time.

Each primary control works one axis. The elevator gives pitch, the nose up or down, about the lateral axis, the one from tip to tip. The ailerons give roll, one wing up and the other down, about the longitudinal axis, from nose to tail. And the rudder gives yaw, the nose left or right, about the normal, or vertical, axis.

All three work the same way. A control surface is a hinged rear section of an aerofoil: the tailplane, the fin, or the outer wing. Deflecting it changes the camber of that aerofoil. So the aerofoil makes more lift, or less, or lift in the opposite direction. That force acts some distance from the centre of gravity, and force times distance is a turning moment. The tail surfaces are a long way behind the centre of gravity, and the ailerons are out near the wing tips. So a small force produces a large rotation.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. The names of the axes do not match the names of the movements the way you might guess. The lateral axis is the one that runs sideways, from tip to tip, and rotation about it is pitch. Rotation about the longitudinal axis is roll. If in doubt, picture a skewer through the aeroplane along the named axis, and ask how the aeroplane could spin about it.

## The elevator: pitch, then speed

Move the control column back. The elevator, the hinged rear part of the tailplane, moves up. That increases the downward force the tailplane makes. The tail goes down, and the aeroplane pitches nose up, about the lateral axis. Forward on the column does the reverse: elevator down, tail up, nose down.

The further effect of the elevator is on the angle of attack and the airspeed. Raising the nose increases the wing's angle of attack, which makes more lift and more drag. Without a change of power, the extra drag slows the aeroplane. So raise the nose, and the airspeed decreases. Lower it, and the airspeed increases.

That is the foundation of how you fly. With the power set, the attitude you hold with the elevator sets the airspeed. So for the elevator: pitch first, then speed.

## The ailerons: roll, then yaw

Move the control column to the right. The right aileron goes up, and the left aileron goes down. The down-going aileron increases the camber of the left wing's outer section, so it makes more lift. The up-going aileron reduces the right wing's lift. The left wing rises, the right wing drops, and the aeroplane rolls right, about the longitudinal axis.

Ailerons have two further effects on yaw, and they happen in sequence.

The first is adverse yaw: the nose swings the wrong way first. The wing that is making more lift also makes more induced drag, the drag that comes with producing lift. In our example, that is the rising left wing. So while you are rolling right, the left wing is being held back harder than the right, and the nose yaws left, away from the direction of the roll. This is adverse aileron yaw. It is strongest at low speed and with large aileron deflections, when the down-going aileron is working the wing hardest.

[[diagram:adverse-yaw]]
Picture the aeroplane from above, flying from left to right across the page, and rolling right. In the top view, with aileron alone, the left wing's aileron is down. That wing makes more induced drag, and the nose swings left, away from the turn. In the bottom view, right rudder is applied with the right aileron. The rudder pushes the tail left and the nose right. Now the nose follows the turn, and the balance ball stays centred.

So the cure is the rudder, applied in the same direction as the aileron. Right aileron, right rudder. The rudder cancels the adverse yaw, so the nose follows the roll. That is what coordination means.

Designers help too, in two ways. Differential ailerons move the up-going aileron further than the down-going one. That adds a little drag on the descending wing, to balance the rising wing. And Frise ailerons are shaped so that the leading edge of the up-going aileron dips into the airflow, and adds drag on that side. Both reduce adverse yaw. Neither removes the need for rudder.

Then the yaw reverses, and the aeroplane slips into the turn. Once the aeroplane is banked, the lift is tilted, and the aeroplane begins to sideslip towards the lower wing. The air now comes slightly from the side, from the low-wing side. It strikes the large side area of the fin and the rear fuselage. Like a weathervane, the aeroplane swings its nose into that airflow. So the nose yaws towards the lower wing. Left alone, the nose drops, the bank steepens, the speed builds, and the aeroplane develops into a descending spiral.

So the full further effect of aileron is this. Adverse yaw away from the roll first. Then yaw towards the lower wing, as the aeroplane slips.

Question. You roll left with aileron alone, at low speed. Which way does the nose swing first, and why?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: to the right, away from the roll. The right aileron goes down to raise the right wing. So the right wing makes more lift and more induced drag, which holds it back and yaws the nose right. Left rudder with the left aileron cancels it.

## The rudder: yaw, then roll

Press the right rudder pedal. The rudder, the hinged rear of the fin, moves right. The fin now makes a sideways force to the left. The tail swings left, and the nose yaws right, about the normal axis.

The further effect of rudder is roll in the same direction. As the nose yaws right, the left wing, now on the outside, moves forward faster through the air. The right wing moves back. The faster wing makes more lift, so the left wing rises, and the aeroplane rolls right.

On an aeroplane with dihedral, the effect is stronger. Dihedral means the wings are angled up from root to tip. The yaw makes the aeroplane skid, so the air comes from the side. The wing on the leading side meets it at a larger angle of attack, and rises. Uncorrected, the roll steepens, the nose drops, and this too ends in a spiral descent.

So yaw and roll are linked both ways. Aileron produces yaw, and rudder produces roll. That coupling is why balanced flight needs both controls together.

The balance ball, or slip indicator, tells you whether the rudder and aileron are working together. With the ball in the centre, the aeroplane is in balance.

[[diagram:slip-and-skid-ball]]
Picture three balance balls side by side, each a small ball in a curved tube, and all three in a left turn. In the first, the aeroplane is slipping. The ball sits to the inside of the turn, on the left, the low wing side. There is too much bank, or too little rudder. In the middle, the aeroplane is balanced, and the ball is centred. In the third, the aeroplane is skidding. The ball sits to the outside, on the right, the high wing side, because there is too much rudder.

When the ball is displaced, step on the ball. That means apply rudder on the side the ball has gone to. Ball to the left, left rudder. Ball to the right, right rudder.

Question. What is the further effect of left rudder in level flight, and why does it happen?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: a roll to the left. The yaw moves the right wing forward faster than the left, so it makes more lift, and rises. Dihedral adds to it, as the aeroplane skids. Uncorrected, the roll and the dropping nose develop into a spiral descent.

## What airspeed and power do to the controls

Every control surface works by making a force from the air flowing over it. That force depends on dynamic pressure, which is half the air density times the speed squared. It is the same quantity that sets the lift on the wing. So the speed of the air over a control surface decides how much a given deflection achieves.

Start with airspeed. At high airspeed, the dynamic pressure is large. A small deflection makes a large force, so the controls are more effective. And they are heavier to move, because the air is pressing harder on them. At low airspeed, they are less effective. You move them further for less response, and they feel light and sloppy. That softness is one of the stall warnings in the lesson on stalling. And because the force goes with the square of the speed, halving the airspeed leaves only a quarter of the control force.

Now power, and the slipstream. The propeller throws a fast, spiralling column of air back over the fuselage. That is the slipstream. It flows over the tailplane and the fin. So whenever power is applied, it increases the airspeed over the elevator and the rudder, even when the aeroplane itself is flying slowly. The ailerons are out on the wings, mostly outside the slipstream, so power does little for them. With the power off, in a glide or the flare, the elevator and rudder lose that boost. They have only the aeroplane's own airspeed to work with.

[[diagram:control-effectiveness-vs-airspeed]]
Picture a small grid, with the three controls down one side and three situations across the top. In the first situation, low airspeed with the power off, all three controls are weak and sloppy. In the second, low airspeed with high power, the slipstream over the tail makes the elevator and rudder effective again. But the ailerons, outside the slipstream, stay weak. And in the third, high airspeed, all three are effective, and heavier.

That explains several things you will feel in the aeroplane. On the take-off roll, the rudder works early, because the slipstream is over it before the aeroplane is moving fast. In a glide approach, the elevator feels softer in the flare than in a powered approach, so you need a larger movement. And at low speed with high power, as in a go-around, the elevator and rudder are crisp, while the ailerons are still weak.

Power changes do more than help the tail. They also change the pitch trim. Here is what happens on most single-engine trainers.

Increasing power pitches the nose up. The extra slipstream increases the airflow over the tailplane, which on a typical trainer increases its downward force. And on many types, the position of the thrust line relative to the centre of gravity adds to the effect. The increased power also yaws the nose. On an aeroplane whose propeller turns clockwise seen from behind, it yaws to the left. That is because the spiralling slipstream strikes the left side of the fin, together with the propeller's other effects. You hold the nose with forward pressure and right rudder, then trim nose-down.

Reducing power does the opposite. The nose pitches down, and yaws the other way. You need nose-up trim.

Airspeed changes the trim too. An aeroplane trimmed for a given airspeed tends to return to it. Speed it up, by lowering the nose or adding power or both, and it tends to pitch up, back towards its trimmed speed. You need nose-down trim. Slow it down, and it tends to pitch down, and you need nose-up trim. So at every new airspeed, you hold a new pressure on the column until you re-trim.

Your aeroplane may differ in the size of these changes. The flight manual and your instructor will tell you what to expect for your type.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. More power makes all the controls more effective. No. Only the elevator and the rudder, which sit in the slipstream. The ailerons are outside it, and depend on airspeed alone. So when the exam asks which control is least helped by power at low speed, the answer is the ailerons.

Question. You are gliding at 65 kt and add a burst of power. What happens to the rudder's effectiveness, and to the ailerons' effectiveness?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the rudder becomes more effective at once, because the slipstream increases the airflow over the fin. The ailerons barely change, because they are outside the slipstream, and the aeroplane's airspeed has not yet changed.

## Trim: removing the pressure

You have seen that every change of power, airspeed or configuration changes the pressure you need on the controls to hold the attitude. Holding a steady push or pull for minutes on end is tiring. And a tired arm drifts, so the aeroplane wanders off height and speed. The trim controls exist to remove that sustained pressure.

So here is the purpose of trim. It relieves the pilot of a steady control force, so that the aeroplane holds the selected attitude, and therefore the airspeed, with no pressure on the controls. That reduces workload and fatigue. And it makes accurate flying easier, because the aeroplane now wants to stay where you put it.

Most light aeroplanes do it with a trim tab. That is a small hinged surface on the trailing edge of the elevator, moved by a wheel or a switch in the cockpit. The tab works backwards from what you might expect.

[[diagram:trim-tab]]
Picture the tailplane from the side, with the airflow coming from the left. Behind the tailplane hangs the elevator, and on the back of the elevator is the little tab. For nose-up trim, the elevator needs to be held up. So the tab is deflected down. The air pushes up on it. And that upward force, at the very back of the elevator, holds the elevator's trailing edge up, with no force from you.

The elevator stays in the nose-up position on its own. Other types use an adjustable tailplane, or a spring in the control circuit, but the purpose is the same.

The technique follows from the purpose. Attitude first, then trim. Select the attitude and power with the primary controls. Let the speed settle, and hold it. Then trim out the remaining pressure, until the aeroplane holds that attitude hands-off.

Trim is not a primary flight control. You do not fly the aeroplane through a manoeuvre with the trim wheel. Trim is slow, and it removes the feel of the controls that tells you what the aeroplane is doing.

Some aeroplanes also have rudder trim. It removes the steady rudder pressure needed to hold balance at a given power and speed. On many trainers it is a fixed tab, set on the ground.

Question. What is the trim for, and why should you not use it to raise the nose in a climb entry?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: trim removes a sustained control pressure, so the aeroplane holds the attitude you selected hands-off, reducing workload. You select the climb attitude with the elevator first, let the speed settle, then trim. Using the trim wheel to fly the manoeuvre is slow and imprecise.

## Flap: lift, drag and attitude

Flap changes the wing itself. So it changes all three things the syllabus asks about: lift, drag, and the attitude you fly at. The aerodynamics are in the lesson on lift and drag. Lowering flap increases the wing's camber. So at the same angle of attack it makes more lift and more drag. And its maximum lift is higher, so the stall speed is lower.

[[diagram:flap-effect-on-lift-curve]]
Picture a graph of lift against angle of attack, with two curves. The flap up curve is the lower one. The flap down curve sits above it at every angle, and rises to a higher peak, so the stall speed is lower. The same lift is reached at a smaller angle of attack, so the nose sits lower. The flap down peak also comes at a slightly smaller angle. And alongside, the drag has gone up.

What matters in the cockpit is what that does to the aeroplane at a constant airspeed. There are four things to know.

First, lift. Lowering flap at a given speed and angle of attack increases lift. If you change nothing else, the aeroplane rises. Raising flap reduces lift. Unless you raise the nose to compensate, the aeroplane sinks.

Second, drag. Lowering flap increases drag. So to hold the same speed level, you need more power. Without more power, the speed falls, or you must descend. Raising flap reduces drag.

Third, attitude. With flap down, the wing makes the same lift at a smaller angle of attack. So to hold the same speed in level flight, the nose must be lower. Raising flap means the wing needs a larger angle again, so the nose must come higher.

And fourth, the immediate pitch change when you move the flap. That depends on the type. Many high-wing trainers pitch nose-up, or balloon, when flap is lowered. That is because lowering flap moves the wing's centre of pressure, and changes the downwash over the tailplane. Raising flap usually brings a nose-down pitch and a sink. Your flight manual and your instructor will tell you what your aeroplane does.

So, side by side. Lowering flap: lift up, drag up, stall speed down, and the nose lower to hold the same indicated airspeed level. Many high-wing trainers balloon first. Raising flap: lift down, so a sink if you do not compensate. Drag down, stall speed up, and the nose higher to restore the angle of attack. And usually a nose-down pitch as it happens.

Flap also has a speed limit, because the extra lift it makes puts large loads on the flap and its mountings. The top of the white arc on the airspeed indicator is VFE, the maximum speed with flap extended. The flight manual gives the figure for your type.

[[diagram:flap-speeds-vfo-vfe]]
Picture the airspeed scale, with a white arc along it. The bottom of the white arc is VSO, the full-flap stall speed at maximum weight. The top is VFE, the maximum speed with flap extended. On some aeroplanes there is also VFO, the maximum speed for moving the flap, and it is lower than VFE. So you slow to VFO before selecting flap, and then VFE is the limit for flying with it out. Either way, slow down before you select flap.

Let's work one. You are flying level at 80 kt, below VFE, in a high-wing trainer. You select the first stage of flap. What happens, and what do you do? Pause if you'd like to think it through.
[[pause 6]]
First, lift increases at the same angle of attack. The aeroplane balloons upward, and the nose pitches up.

Second, to hold height, you lower the nose slightly. The flapped wing needs a smaller angle of attack for the same lift.

Third, drag has increased, so you add a little power to hold 80 kt.

And fourth, you hold the new attitude, let the speed settle, then re-trim.

The aeroplane is now flying level at the same speed, 80 kt, with a lower nose, a lower stall speed, and a higher power setting than before.

Now the reverse. You are going around from a low approach, with full flap down and full power set. What happens each time you raise a stage of flap, and what do you do?
[[pause 5]]
The answer: each stage raised reduces the lift at the same angle of attack, so the aeroplane sinks. And the stall speed rises, so your margin above the stall shrinks. Raise the flap in stages, at a safe speed and height, with climb power already set. As each stage comes up, raise the nose a little to restore the angle of attack, let the speed build, and re-trim.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Raising full flap at low speed close to the ground is dangerous. The lift drops, and the stall speed rises, at the same moment. Raise flap in stages, at a safe speed and height, after climb power is set.

## Bringing it together

Back to the go-around at Jandakot. You are on final at 65 kt, with full flap, trimmed nose-up, at low power. A vehicle moves onto the runway.

Full power. The slipstream floods the tail, and the elevator and rudder come alive. The nose pitches up hard, and yaws left. You hold the attitude with firm forward pressure, and keep straight with right rudder, because you expected exactly that. The ailerons are still soft. So you keep the wings level with larger movements than usual, and rudder to balance each one.

Positive climb. You raise the first stage of flap. The aeroplane sinks a little, and the stall speed rises. So you raise the nose slightly, and let the speed build. Then the rest of the flap, in stages. With the speed settled at the climb speed, you trim out the forward pressure. And the aeroplane climbs away, hands-off.

## Key points

Let's recap.

The controls and their axes. Elevator: pitch about the lateral axis, then the airspeed changes. Nose up, slower. Ailerons: roll about the longitudinal axis, then adverse yaw away from the roll, then yaw towards the lower wing as it slips. Rudder: yaw about the normal axis, then roll the same way. Uncorrected, both aileron and rudder lead to a spiral descent. Rudder with aileron gives balanced flight.

Airspeed and power. The controls are more effective and heavier at high airspeed, weaker and sloppy at low airspeed. Power puts slipstream over the elevator and rudder, but not the ailerons. More power typically pitches the nose up and yaws it left. Less power pitches it down. And a trimmed aeroplane tends to return to its trimmed speed.

Trim. It removes sustained control pressure, so the aeroplane holds the selected attitude hands-off, reducing workload and fatigue. Attitude first, then trim. Trim is not a primary control.

Flap. Lowering flap increases lift and drag, lowers the stall speed, and needs a lower nose attitude at the same indicated airspeed. Many high-wing trainers balloon first. Raising flap reduces lift and drag, raises the stall speed, causes a sink, and needs a higher nose attitude.

A final exam tip. Expect six kinds of question. An axis question: ailerons, longitudinal. Elevator, lateral. Rudder, normal. A further effect question: rudder gives roll the same way, and aileron gives adverse yaw away from the roll first. A slipstream question: the ailerons are least helped by power. A power change question: more power, nose up. A trim purpose question: to relieve sustained control pressure, not to fly the aeroplane. And a flap question: lowering flap, lift and drag increase, and the nose is lower at the same indicated airspeed. Raising flap, a sink, and the nose higher.
