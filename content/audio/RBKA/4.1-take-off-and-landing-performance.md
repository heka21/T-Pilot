This is RBKA 4.1, Take-off and landing performance. By the end you will be able to say whether runway slope, a wet surface or slush makes the take-off and the landing longer or shorter, what each does to the climb after take-off, and why. There are worked examples and questions along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (RBKA 4.1)

The exam wants one thing from you, for three conditions. For runway slope, for a wet runway surface, and for a slushy runway surface, say whether each one increases or decreases the take-off distance, the landing distance, and the climb after take-off.

## Why this matters

The performance charts in your flight manual are drawn for a level, dry, paved runway. Very few of the strips you will use once you leave the training circuit are all three.

Farm strips in the wheatbelt slope, sometimes noticeably, and many are gravel or grass. Winter rain soaks grass strips across the south-west for weeks. And a summer thunderstorm can leave standing water on a sealed runway for an hour. Each of these moves the take-off and landing distances, sometimes by more than the margin you allowed.

The lesson on take-off and landing performance in the basic aeronautical knowledge unit dealt with the air: density height and wind. This lesson deals with the ground. The exam asks a simple question for each factor, increase or decrease. But the answer is only reliable if you can see the force behind it.

## What decides the distances

Start with what happens on a take-off roll. The aeroplane starts at rest, and must reach its lift-off speed. What accelerates it is the net force along the runway. That is the propeller thrust, minus the aerodynamic drag, minus the rolling resistance of the wheels on the surface. The bigger the net force, the faster the aeroplane accelerates, and the shorter the run.

Here is the key point. On a light aeroplane, that net force is only a modest fraction of its weight. So anything that takes even a small bite out of it has a noticeable effect on the distance.

A landing roll is the same problem in reverse. The aeroplane arrives at its touchdown speed, and must be brought to rest. What slows it is aerodynamic drag, rolling resistance, and above all, the braking force from the tyres gripping the surface. The bigger the slowing force, the shorter the roll.

Slope and surface change these forces in different ways. That is why they can affect take-off and landing in opposite directions. Hold that picture, and every answer in this lesson becomes something you can work out, rather than memorise.

The third thing the syllabus names is the take-off climb. That is what happens after lift-off: the climb gradient from the end of the take-off distance, over whatever lies beyond the runway. Once the wheels leave the ground, the surface no longer acts on the aeroplane. So slope and surface affect the climb only indirectly. They change where it starts, and they come with whatever the ground ahead is doing.

## Runway slope

Imagine pushing a trolley up a ramp. Part of its weight now acts backwards along the ramp, and you have to push harder to get it moving. An aeroplane on an upsloping runway is the same. A part of its weight acts down the slope, against the thrust. For the gentle slopes of real runways, that part is very nearly the weight multiplied by the slope as a fraction. So on a 2 percent upslope, about 2 percent of the aeroplane's weight is pulling it backwards.

Two percent of the weight sounds small. But remember that the net accelerating force is itself only a fraction of the weight. So losing 2 percent of the weight to the slope takes a real share of what was accelerating you. As a rule of thumb, a 2 percent upslope can add around 10 percent to the take-off distance. On a downslope, the same part of the weight acts forwards, and helps the thrust.

[[diagram:slope-force-on-runway]]
Picture an aeroplane on an upsloping runway, with the slope drawn steeper than it really is. Its weight splits into two arrows. One presses into the runway. The other, much smaller, points back down the slope: about 2 percent of the weight on a 2 percent slope. Beside it, picture the force budget on the take-off roll. Thrust, minus drag and rolling resistance, leaves a net accelerating force that is only a modest fraction of the weight. Take the slope's 2 percent away from that, and you lose a real share. The result: about 10 percent more take-off distance.

So, the take-off. Uphill, the take-off distance increases. The slope force opposes the thrust throughout the run, so the aeroplane accelerates more slowly, and needs more runway to reach lift-off speed. Downhill, the take-off distance decreases. Gravity adds to the thrust, so the aeroplane accelerates faster, and lifts off sooner.

Now the landing, where it works the other way. Uphill, the landing distance decreases. Now the slope force acts against the aeroplane's motion, adding to the brakes and the drag. The aeroplane slows faster, and the landing roll is shorter. Downhill, the landing distance increases. The slope force pushes the aeroplane along the runway, working against the brakes. Downhill landings also tend to float, because the runway falls away beneath the aeroplane in the flare. Combine a downslope with a tailwind, and you have the classic overrun.

[[diagram:runway-slope-take-off-and-landing]]
Picture two side views of the same sloping runway. In the first, an aeroplane takes off uphill. Part of its thrust goes into climbing the slope, so the run is longer, around 10 percent longer for a 2 percent upslope. In the second, an aeroplane lands uphill, and the slope helps it slow down, so the landing roll is shorter. So with a light wind: land uphill, and take off downhill.

Now the take-off climb. Once airborne, the aeroplane climbs at the same rate, and the same gradient through the air, whether the runway sloped or not. Slope has no effect on the aeroplane's own climb. What changes is the ground.

Taking off uphill, the aeroplane lifts off later, and the terrain beyond the runway usually keeps rising. So the climb gradient relative to the rising ground ahead is reduced, and obstacle clearance suffers. Taking off downhill, the aeroplane lifts off earlier, and the ground beyond usually falls away. That helps obstacle clearance.

Slope or wind? On a sloping strip with a light wind, the usual choice is to land uphill and take off downhill. Each one uses the slope to help. When the wind is stronger, the two compete, and the wind usually wins, unless the slope is steep. The CASA take-off and landing charts include a slope correction, and the flight manual may too. Use them to compare the two directions with the actual wind, rather than guessing.

Let's work one. The chart gives a take-off distance of 520 m, for today's weight, density height and wind, on a level, dry runway. The farm strip has a 2 percent upslope in the direction you must use, and 600 m is available. Do you go? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
Step one, the correction. The rule of thumb for a 2 percent upslope is plus 10 percent.

Step two, the corrected distance. 520 times 1.10 is 572 m.

Step three, the margin. 600 minus 572 is 28 m. That is about 5 percent of the runway.

That is too thin to accept. Wait for a cooler part of the day, reduce the weight, or use the other direction if the wind allows. And where the chart or the flight manual gives its own slope correction, use that figure instead of the rule of thumb. So the answer: 572 m needed, a margin of only 28 m, and you don't go.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Slope works opposite ways for take-off and landing. The exam pairs them, to catch candidates who learn that uphill is bad. Uphill lengthens the take-off, but shortens the landing. Downhill shortens the take-off, but lengthens the landing.

Question. Does a downhill take-off change the aeroplane's rate of climb, once it is airborne?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: no. Once airborne, the slope no longer acts on the aeroplane, so its rate and gradient of climb through the air are unchanged. What improves is the clearance over the ground, because the aeroplane lifts off sooner, and the terrain beyond usually falls away.

## A wet runway surface

Water changes two things. First, how hard the wheels can grip the surface, which governs braking and steering. Second, how hard the wheels have to push to roll through it, which governs acceleration. On a wet sealed runway the first one matters most. On wet grass, both do.

Take a wet sealed runway first. The landing distance increases. A film of water between the tyre and the bitumen acts as a lubricant, and reduces the friction available for braking, often by a lot. The brakes can only stop the aeroplane as hard as the tyres can grip. So the landing roll grows, often by 15 percent or more.

Brake harder than the grip allows, and the wheels lock and skid. That stops you even less well, and costs you directional control. Crosswind control on the ground is reduced too, because the same lack of grip makes it harder for the tyres to resist the sideways push of the wind.

If there is standing water, the tyre may ride up on a wedge of water and lose contact with the runway altogether. That is aquaplaning, also called hydroplaning. An aquaplaning tyre has almost no braking and no steering, and the aeroplane can slide off the side of the runway in a crosswind.

What about the take-off on a wet sealed runway? There is little change. A thin film of water adds little rolling resistance on a sealed surface, so the take-off run hardly changes, and braking does not matter on a normal take-off. A rejected take-off is another story, though. The stopping distance is longer.

[[widget:crosswind-component]]
Picture splitting the wind into its part across the runway and its part along it. On a wet runway there is less grip to hold the aeroplane straight. So leave more margin below the flight manual's demonstrated crosswind than you would on a dry one.

Now wet grass. Here the take-off distance increases. Grass already has more rolling resistance than bitumen. When it is wet and the soil underneath softens, the wheels sink in and have to push through the surface. So the rolling resistance rises further, the net accelerating force falls, and the take-off run gets longer.

On wet grass the landing distance increases too. Wet grass is slippery, the way a wet lawn is under your shoes, so braking is poor, and the landing roll is longer. The extra rolling resistance helps a little, but not enough to make up for brakes that barely grip.

[[workbook:fig5]]
Picture the take-off chart in the workbook, figure 5, printed sideways on the page. It is a stack of panels, and you work down through them. The fourth panel from the top is for the surface. It has lines for hard sealed, short dry grass, short wet or long dry grass, and long wet grass. The panel above it applies the runway slope, in the same way.

And the take-off climb? Water has no direct effect on the climb once the aeroplane is airborne. But on wet grass, the aeroplane lifts off further down the runway. So the climb begins later, with less distance to the obstacles beyond, and obstacle clearance is reduced.

Let's work one. The chart gives a landing distance of 450 m on a dry sealed runway, for today's conditions. A shower has just passed, and the runway is wet. The runway is 550 m long. Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
Step one, the correction. The rule of thumb for a wet runway is at least plus 15 percent.

Step two, the corrected landing distance. 450 times 1.15 is {517.5|five hundred and seventeen point five}. Call it 518 m.

Step three, the margin. 550 minus 518 is 32 m.

A margin that small leaves nothing for a fast approach, or a long float. Wait for the runway to dry, or divert to a longer one. Where the flight manual gives a wet-runway factor, use it in place of the rule of thumb. So the answer: about 518 m needed, with only 32 m to spare.

Here is one for you to try. With no surface correction, the take-off distance from a grass strip is 480 m. After heavy winter rain the strip is soft and wet. Which way does the take-off distance go? And what happens to the landing distance on the same strip?
[[pause 5]]
Both increase. The take-off run gets longer, because soft, wet grass adds rolling resistance and the aeroplane accelerates more slowly. So it will need more than 480 m. The landing roll gets longer, because the brakes barely grip on wet grass. The flight manual's grass and wet-surface notes give the factors to apply. And if it gives none for a soft, waterlogged strip, treat that as a reason not to use it.

Question. Why does a wet sealed runway lengthen the landing, but barely change the take-off?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: landing depends on braking, and water lubricates the contact between the tyre and the surface, so the brakes can stop the aeroplane less hard. Take-off depends on acceleration, and a thin film of water adds little rolling resistance on a sealed surface.

## A slushy runway surface

Slush is a mixture of water and snow or ice. You will not meet it often in Western Australia. But the same physics applies to any deep, soft contaminant on the runway. Standing water after a thunderstorm. Deep mud on a waterlogged strip. The exam treats slush as the most severe surface of all, and the reasons show why.

The take-off first. A wheel rolling through slush has to push it out of the way. That is a drag called displacement drag. And the spray it throws up strikes the underside of the aeroplane and the flaps. That is called impingement drag.

Unlike ordinary rolling resistance, both of these grow with speed. As the aeroplane accelerates, the slush drag climbs, while the propeller's thrust falls away. So the net accelerating force shrinks just when it matters most. The take-off distance increases greatly. And the aeroplane may stop accelerating short of lift-off speed, and be unable to take off at all.

[[diagram:slush-drag-vs-thrust]]
Picture a graph of force against groundspeed on the take-off roll. The thrust line starts high and slopes down as the speed rises. On a dry runway, the drag and rolling resistance stay well below it, so the aeroplane keeps accelerating, all the way to lift-off speed. In slush, the drag line climbs steeply with speed, and it meets the falling thrust line short of lift-off speed. At that point the net force is zero. The aeroplane stops accelerating, however much runway is left.

Now the landing. On landing, slush and standing water bring the worst of both worlds. The tyres may aquaplane and lose contact with the runway, which takes away braking and steering together. And when they do grip, the grip is very poor. The landing distance increases greatly, and directional control is uncertain. The slush thrown up by the wheels can also damage the flaps and the underside of the aeroplane.

And the take-off climb. Once airborne, the aeroplane's own climb is unchanged. But it starts much further along the runway, if it starts at all, so obstacle clearance is reduced. Slush packed into the wheel fairings and on the flaps adds weight and drag too.

Most light-aeroplane flight manuals give no figures for slush or standing water. That absence is itself the answer. The manufacturer has not shown that the aeroplane can operate from such a surface, and you should not try.

Question. Why can slush stop an aeroplane reaching lift-off speed, when a long dry runway never would?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: slush drag grows with speed, while the propeller thrust falls with speed. At some speed below lift-off, the two can balance. Then the aeroplane stops accelerating, however much runway is left.

## The whole picture

Now the summary the exam is built on. Every answer comes from the forces you have just met, so you should be able to explain each one. Let's take it in three groups.

Slope first, because it is the one that works both ways. Uphill: the take-off distance increases, the landing distance decreases, and the climb is reduced relative to the rising ground. Downhill: the take-off distance decreases, the landing distance increases, and the climb is unchanged, with the terrain falling away.

Then the wet surfaces. On wet sealed, there is little change to the take-off, but the landing distance increases. On wet grass, both increase, and the climb is unchanged but starts later.

And slush, or a waterlogged strip: both distances increase greatly, and the climb is unchanged but starts later.

[[diagram:runway-slope-and-surface-effects]]
Picture all of that as one table, with the conditions down the side, and take-off, landing and climb across the top. The slope rows are the only ones where the arrows point opposite ways. Every surface row lengthens the landing, and every row except wet sealed lengthens the take-off as well.

These factors add to the ones from the air: density height and wind. A hot afternoon at a high, sloping, wet grass strip stacks the density height, slope and surface penalties one on top of another. And the corrections multiply.

[[diagram:takeoff-landing-factors]]
Picture a table of arrows, one row for each factor. A headwind shortens both distances. A tailwind lengthens both, markedly. Higher temperature, lower QNH and higher elevation lengthen both, through lower air density. Uphill slope lengthens the take-off, and downhill slope lengthens the landing. Grass, gravel, wet surfaces and higher weight lengthen both. And frost on the wing lengthens the take-off, and may prevent it.

## Bringing it together

It is July, and you plan a trip to a friend's farm strip near York. It is 700 m of grass, with a 2 percent slope down towards the east. A westerly of 5 kt is forecast, after a week of rain.

For landing, the light wind and the slope agree. You land uphill towards the west, into wind, and let the slope help the brakes on the wet grass.

For the departure, the light wind and the slope disagree. Taking off downhill to the east, the slope helps the acceleration, and the ground falls away beyond the strip. But you accept a 5 kt tailwind. Taking off uphill to the west, you have the headwind. But the upslope adds about 10 percent, and the hill beyond reduces your climb margin over the ground.

You work both through the flight manual charts, with the wet-grass correction applied, and take the direction with the bigger margin. You also walk the strip first. If it is soft and waterlogged, with the wheels sinking in, no chart figure applies, and the right decision is to wait for it to dry.

## Key points

Let's recap.

Slope. Uphill lengthens the take-off, and shortens the landing. Downhill does the reverse. As a rule of thumb, a 2 percent upslope adds about 10 percent to the take-off distance. The climb through the air is unchanged. But clearance over rising ground is reduced, and over falling ground it is improved.

Wet. A wet sealed runway lengthens the landing, often by 15 percent or more, with little effect on the take-off. Wet grass lengthens both. The climb is unchanged, but it may start later.

Slush or standing water greatly lengthens both the take-off and the landing. It may prevent lift-off altogether, and it risks aquaplaning and flap damage. No flight manual figures means do not operate.

A final exam tip. The exam asks increase or decrease, for each factor. Slope works opposite ways for take-off and landing: uphill is bad for the take-off, and good for the landing. Wet and slushy surfaces are bad for both, and slush is the worst. And if an option says a surface condition changes the aeroplane's rate of climb once it is airborne, it is wrong. The climb starts later, but the aeroplane climbs the same.

That's the end of RBKA 4.1.
