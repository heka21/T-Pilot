This is RBKA 3.4, Descents. By the end you will know what power and flap do to the rate of descent, the angle of descent and the nose attitude, what the wind does to a glide, and why gliding at any speed other than the recommended one gets you less far. There are worked examples and a few questions along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (RBKA 3.4)

The exam wants three things from you. First, what happens to the rate of descent, the angle of descent and the nose attitude when you change the power, or the flap, while holding the indicated airspeed constant. Second, what a headwind or a tailwind does to the glide path, and to the glide distance over the ground. And third, why gliding at any indicated airspeed other than the recommended glide speed reduces the distance you can cover in still air.

## Why this matters

Every circuit ends in a descent. And on every approach you are solving the same problem: arrive at the runway threshold at the right speed and the right height. The tools are the throttle, the flap lever and the attitude, and this lesson is about what each one does.

The other descent you train for is the one with no engine. Over the wheatbelt east of the scarp, with a field ahead and a wind blowing, the questions are which field you can reach, and how to fly the glide to get there. The instinct to raise the nose to stretch the glide is strong, and wrong, and the exam tests it.

Expect increase, decrease or no change questions on power, flap and wind. And a calculation or two on glide distance.

## The forces in a glide

Start with the simplest descent. The engine is at idle, and the aeroplane is gliding down a straight path at a steady speed. There is no thrust. Three forces act. Lift, at right angles to the flight path. Drag, back along the flight path. And weight, straight down.

With no thrust, something must keep the aeroplane moving forward against the drag. That something is weight. Because the path is tilted downwards, part of the weight acts forwards along the path. It is exactly the way part of your weight carries a bicycle down a hill without pedalling. In a steady glide, that forward part of the weight balances the drag. The rest of the weight, across the path, is balanced by lift.

[[diagram:forces-in-a-glide]]
Picture the aeroplane gliding down a straight slope from left to right. Lift points out of the top, at right angles to the slope. Drag points back up the slope. Weight points straight down. Now add the lift and drag arrows together. Their resultant points straight up, and it is exactly equal and opposite to the weight. The angle between the slope and the horizon is the glide angle.

Now the arithmetic, in words. Along the path, the weight times the sine of the glide angle equals the drag. Across it, the weight times the cosine of the glide angle equals the lift. Divide one by the other, and the weight cancels. What is left says this: the tangent of the glide angle equals the drag divided by the lift.

That is the most important line in this lesson. The steepness of the glide depends only on the ratio of drag to lift. Less drag for the lift you are making gives a flatter glide. The flattest glide of all comes when the drag is smallest compared with the lift. That is at the best lift to drag ratio, from the lesson on lift and drag. An aeroplane with a lift to drag ratio of 9 glides 9 ft forward for every foot it loses.

Notice what is not in that line: weight. That fact answers a favourite exam question, and you will meet it again later in this lesson.

## A descent with power: the same picture, plus thrust

Now add some power, as you do on a normal approach. Thrust acts forward along the path. So it shares the job of balancing the drag with the forward part of the weight. Less of the weight is needed for that job, so the path can be less steep. In words: the weight times the sine of the glide angle now equals the drag minus the thrust.

More thrust means a smaller angle, and a shallower path. Add enough thrust to equal the drag, and the angle is zero. You are flying level. Add more, and the path tilts up. You are climbing. So a powered descent, level flight and a climb are the same balance of forces, with different amounts of thrust.

That gives you the first half of the power and flap question. Hold the indicated airspeed constant, and change the power.

Power increased. The extra thrust means the path can be shallower for the same speed. So the angle of descent decreases. A shallower path at the same speed means the aeroplane loses fewer feet each minute. So the rate of descent decreases. And to stop the extra thrust simply accelerating the aeroplane, you must raise the nose to hold the speed. So the attitude is higher.

Power reduced. The reverse. With less thrust, the aeroplane needs a steeper path to keep its speed up. So the angle and the rate of descent increase. And you lower the nose to hold the indicated airspeed. So the attitude is lower.

So for power, at a constant indicated airspeed: more power, less rate, less angle, nose higher. Less power, more rate, more angle, nose lower.

This is the reason for the technique you are taught on approach. Attitude controls the airspeed. Power controls the rate of descent. With the attitude held for the approach speed, the throttle sets how steeply you come down. And so it sets where on the runway the path ends: your aim point.

If you are getting low, the answer is power, with the attitude adjusted to hold the speed. If you are getting high, take power off. Raising the nose instead of adding power, when low, slows the aeroplane. And as you will hear shortly, that steepens the path.

Question. On final at a constant 70 kt, you see that you are getting low. What do you do with the power and the attitude, and what happens to the rate of descent?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: add power, and raise the nose slightly to hold 70 kt. The extra thrust lets the path be shallower at the same speed. So the rate of descent decreases, and the path flattens towards the aim point. Raising the nose without power would only cost speed.

## Flap at a constant IAS

Now hold the power and the indicated airspeed constant, and lower flap.

Flap adds drag. And in the glide equation, more drag means a larger drag to lift ratio, and so a steeper path. The best lift to drag ratio with flap down is lower than with flap up, so the glide angle steepens. At the same airspeed down a steeper path, the aeroplane loses more height each minute. So the rate of descent increases.

The attitude change is the more interesting part, because two things add together. First, flap increases the wing's camber, so it makes the lift it needs at a smaller angle of attack. The nose comes down relative to the flight path. Second, the flight path itself is now steeper. Both lower the nose. So with flap down, the nose attitude is noticeably lower. That lower attitude is useful. It gives you a much better view of the runway over the nose on a flapped approach.

So for flap, at a constant indicated airspeed: more flap, more rate, more angle, nose lower. Less flap does the reverse: less rate, a flatter angle, and the nose higher.

[[diagram:powered-descent-vs-glide]]
Picture three descent paths starting from the same point, all at the same indicated airspeed. The glide with the power off and no flap is in the middle. Above it, with power added, the path is shallower, the rate of descent is lower, and the nose is higher. Below it, with flap lowered, the path is steeper, the rate of descent is higher, and the nose is noticeably lower. Power lifts the path and the nose. Flap drops them both.

On a real approach, you use both together. Flap sets a steep, controllable approach path, with a good view. Then small power changes fine-tune where that path meets the runway.

Let's work one. You are on a glide approach, with the power at idle, at 65 kt. Halfway down final, the aim point is moving down the windscreen. You are too high. What do you do, and what happens? Pause if you'd like to think it through.
[[pause 6]]
First, you lower a further stage of flap, while holding the attitude for 65 kt.

Second, the extra drag steepens the path. The angle of descent increases.

Third, to hold 65 kt, you lower the nose. The attitude is lower.

And fourth, on the VSI, the rate of descent increases, and the aim point stops moving down the windscreen.

Flap was the right tool for too high, because the power was already at idle. Had you instead been too low, the answer would have been power, not back pressure, while holding 65 kt.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Lowering flap at a constant indicated airspeed raises the nose. It does not. More flap at the same speed needs a lower nose. The cambered wing makes the lift at a smaller angle of attack, and the path is steeper. Many high-wing trainers do pitch up for a moment when the flap first goes down. But the attitude you then hold for the same indicated airspeed is lower.

Question. You lower full flap at a constant 65 kt, with the power unchanged. Rate of descent, angle of descent, attitude: up or down?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the rate of descent goes up. The angle of descent goes up, so the path is steeper. And the attitude goes down. The extra drag lowers the lift to drag ratio, so the path steepens. And the cambered wing needs a smaller angle of attack. So the nose comes down on both counts.

## Gliding at the right speed

Now the engine has stopped, and the only question is how far you can go. In still air, the answer comes straight from the glide angle. If the aeroplane glides a certain number of feet forward for every foot it descends, that number is the lift to drag ratio. So the still-air glide distance is the height, times the lift to drag ratio.

The lift to drag ratio is not a fixed property of the aeroplane. It depends on the angle of attack you fly the wing at. And it is greatest at one angle, about 4 degrees on a basic low-speed wing. At a given weight, that angle corresponds to one indicated airspeed. That is the recommended glide speed, or best glide speed, in your flight manual. Fly that speed, and you get the best lift to drag ratio, the flattest path, and the greatest still-air distance.

Let's work one. The best lift to drag ratio is {9:1|nine to one}. The engine fails at 3,000 ft above ground level, and you glide at the recommended speed in still air. How far can you go? And how far if you mishandle it, at a speed that gives only {7:1|seven to one}? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 8]]
First, the distance in feet. 3,000 times 9 is 27,000 ft.

Second, convert to nautical miles. There are 6,076 ft in a nautical mile. 27,000 divided by 6,076 is about 4.4 NM.

Third, the mishandled glide, at {7:1|seven to one}. 3,000 times 7 is 21,000 ft. 21,000 divided by 6,076 is about 3.5 NM.

So flying the wrong speed has cost almost a nautical mile: 4.4 NM at the best glide speed, against 3.5 NM. That can be the difference between the good field, and the one with the fence across it.

Here is one for you to try. An aeroplane with a best lift to drag ratio of {8:1|eight to one} has an engine failure at 2,500 ft above ground level. How far can it glide in still air?
[[pause 6]]
The distance in feet is 2,500 times 8, which is 20,000 ft. Divide by 6,076, and that is 3.3 NM. That is the best case, at the recommended glide speed. Any other speed gives a lower lift to drag ratio, and a shorter glide.

So why does any other speed glide less far? Recall the drag curve. Total drag is the sum of two kinds, which behave in opposite ways. Parasite drag, the drag of pushing the aeroplane's shape through the air, rises with the square of the speed. Induced drag, the price of making lift, is greatest at low speed and a high angle of attack, and it falls as the speed rises.

[[diagram:drag-curves-vs-airspeed]]
Picture the two drag curves against airspeed, with speed increasing to the right. Parasite drag climbs steeply to the right. Induced drag starts high on the left and falls away to the right. Their sum, the total drag, is a U shape, with its lowest point where the two curves cross. That lowest point is the best lift to drag ratio, and it is the best glide speed. Slower than that is the back side of the curve.

Now move away from that point, in either direction.

Faster than the best glide speed, you lower the nose. The angle of attack is smaller, so the induced drag falls. But the parasite drag rises much faster. The total drag is larger for the same lift. So the lift to drag ratio falls, and the glide steepens. You arrive at the ground sooner, and nearer.

Slower than the best glide speed, you raise the nose. The parasite drag falls, but the larger angle of attack brings much more induced drag. Again the total drag is larger, the lift to drag ratio falls, and the glide steepens. You are also closer to the stall. And you are in exactly the situation, low and with no engine, where a stall is least survivable.

Either way, the drag is greater for the lift produced. So the aeroplane comes down more steeply, and covers less distance in still air. The best glide speed is the bottom of the drag curve, and there is no speed on either side of it that does better.

[[diagram:glide-speed-off-best]]
Picture two graphs, one above the other. In the top one, the lift to drag ratio is plotted against airspeed. It peaks at {9 to 1|nine to one} at the best glide speed, and falls on either side, to {7 to 1|seven to one}, somewhat slower because of induced drag, or somewhat faster because of parasite drag. The bottom graph shows the result of an engine failure at 3,000 ft. At the best glide speed, the aeroplane reaches 27,000 ft, about 4.4 NM. At a speed giving only {7 to 1|seven to one}, it reaches 21,000 ft, about 3.5 NM. Almost a mile lost.

Now the instinct. Why does raising the nose not stretch a glide? The field is a little further than you hoped, and every instinct says pull the nose up to make it. Here is what actually happens.

For a few seconds, the aeroplane does rise, or at least stops sinking. You are trading airspeed for height. You are converting kinetic energy, the energy of speed, into potential energy, the energy of height. It is just like a ball thrown upwards, slowing as it rises.

[[diagram:energy-trade-zoom]]
Picture an aeroplane at 100 knots. It raises the nose without adding power, and zooms up to 60 knots. Ignoring drag, the kinetic energy it loses becomes potential energy: about 280 ft of height. Beside it, picture two energy bars, one for speed and one for height. The total stays the same. Only the share between speed and height changes.

But that trade is a one-off, and it is not free. You have used up speed you had no way of replacing. Once the speed has decayed below the best glide speed, the aeroplane settles into a steady glide at the new, slower speed, with its higher induced drag and its lower lift to drag ratio. The path is now steeper than before. The few feet you gained in the zoom are soon lost. And from then on, you are losing distance with every foot of height.

The only way to get the greatest distance is to hold the best glide speed from the start, by attitude, and accept the path it gives you. If the field is out of reach at the best glide speed, it is out of reach. Choose another one, early.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Raise the nose to stretch the glide is the classic distractor. Slower than the best glide speed, induced drag rises, the lift to drag ratio falls, and the glide steepens. You also move closer to the stall. Hold the best glide speed.

Finally, weight. Weight changes the speed, not the angle. Go back to the key line: the tangent of the glide angle equals the drag divided by the lift. Weight is not in it. The best lift to drag ratio occurs at the same angle of attack whatever the weight. So the best glide angle, and the still-air glide distance, do not change with weight.

What changes is the speed at which that angle of attack is reached. A heavier aeroplane needs more lift, so it must fly faster to reach the best lift to drag angle. So the best glide speed is higher when heavy, and lower when light, by the square root of the weight ratio. The heavy aeroplane goes down the same slope, only faster. So it has a higher rate of descent, and it reaches the ground sooner, at the same spot. The flight manual glide speed is usually for maximum weight. When you are light, the right speed is a little lower.

[[diagram:best-ld-and-critical-angle-speeds]]
Picture a graph of the angle of attack needed for level flight against airspeed, with one curve for maximum weight and one for a lighter weight. A horizontal line at about 4 degrees, the best lift to drag angle, crosses the heavy curve at 70 kt, and the light curve at about 63 kt. Another line, at the critical angle of about 16 degrees, gives the stall speed, which is also lower when light. The angles stay. Only the speeds move.

Question. Two identical aeroplanes, one heavy and one light, glide from the same height at their own best glide speeds in still air. Which goes further, and which arrives first?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: they go the same distance, because the best lift to drag ratio, and so the glide angle, does not depend on weight. The heavy one flies a faster best glide speed down the same slope. So it has a higher rate of descent, and it arrives first.

## Wind and the glide

The aeroplane glides relative to the air. Its airspeed, its rate of descent, and its glide angle through the air are set by the forces you have just heard about. A steady wind does not change any of them. What a wind does is move the whole body of air, with the aeroplane in it, across the ground.

So the time to reach the ground from a given height is the same in any wind, because the rate of descent is the same. The distance over the ground is that time, multiplied by the groundspeed. And the groundspeed is what the wind changes.

Into a headwind, the groundspeed is lower. So in the same time, the aeroplane covers less ground. The glide path over the ground is steeper, and the glide distance over the ground is reduced. With a tailwind, the groundspeed is higher. The path over the ground is flatter, and the distance is increased. In both cases, the rate of descent is unchanged.

To calculate it, work out the time to the ground from the height and the rate of descent. Then multiply by the groundspeed.

Let's work one. You are gliding at 65 kt true airspeed from 2,000 ft above ground level, with a rate of descent of about 670 fpm. How far do you go in still air, into a 20 kt headwind, and with a 20 kt tailwind? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 8]]
First, the time to the ground. 2,000 divided by 670 is about 3 minutes.

In still air, 3 minutes at 65 kt. 3 times 65, divided by 60, is 3.25 NM.

Into the 20 kt headwind, the groundspeed is 45 kt. 3 times 45, divided by 60, is 2.25 NM.

With the 20 kt tailwind, the groundspeed is 85 kt. 3 times 85, divided by 60, is 4.25 NM.

So the same aeroplane, from the same height, can reach a field 4 NM downwind, but not one 3 NM upwind. And the VSI reads about 670 fpm in every case.

[[widget:glide-range-vs-wind]]
Now imagine you can set the height, the glide ratio and the wind yourself, and watch the reachable distance change. A headwind shortens the distance over the ground, and a tailwind stretches it. The rate of descent, and the still-air distance, do not change.

Here is one for you to try. An aeroplane glides at 70 kt true airspeed, with a rate of descent of 700 fpm, from 2,800 ft above ground level. How far does it glide over the ground in still air, into a 15 kt headwind, and with a 15 kt tailwind?
[[pause 10]]
First, the time to the ground. 2,800 divided by 700 is 4 minutes, in every case.

In still air, the groundspeed is 70 kt. 4 times 70, divided by 60, is 4.67 NM.

Into the 15 kt headwind, the groundspeed is 70 minus 15, which is 55 kt. 4 times 55, divided by 60, is 3.67 NM.

With the 15 kt tailwind, the groundspeed is 70 plus 15, which is 85 kt. 4 times 85, divided by 60, is 5.67 NM.

Notice the pattern. Each 15 kt of wind, for 4 minutes, moves the landing point by exactly 1 NM. 15 times 4, divided by 60, is 1. The wind shifts the whole circle of reachable ground downwind. So the answers again: 4.67 NM in still air, 3.67 NM into the wind, and 5.67 NM with it.

That last idea is a useful way to picture it. In still air, the ground you can reach is a circle around you. A wind slides the whole circle downwind, by the wind speed times the glide time.

[[diagram:glide-circle-and-wind]]
Picture the view from above. The aeroplane is gliding from 2,800 ft, at 70 knots and 700 feet per minute, so it has 4 minutes. In still air, it can reach anywhere within 4.67 nautical miles: a circle around it. Now a 15 knot wind blows. It drifts the aeroplane 1 nautical mile in those 4 minutes, so the whole circle slides 1 mile downwind. The circle stays the same size. Downwind, 5.67 miles are now reachable. Upwind, only 3.67. A field 4 miles upwind is out of reach. One 5.5 miles downwind is not.

So a field downwind is reachable from further away, though you will land with a high groundspeed. A field upwind must be closer. Wherever you can, land into wind. The low groundspeed at touchdown matters more in a forced landing than anywhere else.

A refinement you will find in some textbooks. In a strong headwind, flying slightly faster than the best glide speed improves the distance over the ground, because it shortens the time the headwind has to push you back. In a tailwind, slightly slower lets the wind carry you further. The exam focus is the basic effect, and for practical flying, the best glide speed in the flight manual is the speed to fly.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. A headwind increases the rate of descent. It does not. The rate of descent is set by the aeroplane's glide through the air, and a steady wind does not change it. A headwind reduces the groundspeed. That steepens the path over the ground, and reduces the distance.

Question. Gliding at the best glide speed, you turn from a tailwind into a headwind. What happens to the rate of descent, and to the glide distance over the ground?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the rate of descent is unchanged, because the glide through the air has not changed. The groundspeed falls. So the path over the ground steepens, and the distance you can cover over the ground is reduced.

## Bringing it together

You are cruising at 3,000 ft above ground level, over farmland east of York, when the engine stops. You hold the attitude for the best glide speed, and trim. No other speed will give you more distance, and raising the nose would only steepen the path.

The wind is 15 kt from the west. You have about four minutes in the air, whatever you do. Westward, into the wind, the reachable distance shrinks. Eastward it grows, but you would land fast, downwind. So you choose a large paddock a little to the south-west, well inside the into-wind range, so that you arrive high rather than low.

On base, you are clearly high. So you lower flap in stages. Each stage steepens the path, lowers the nose, and increases the rate of descent, all at the same indicated airspeed. Had you been low, there would have been no power to add, and no amount of back pressure would have helped. That is why you chose a field within easy reach. And it is why the extra height was something to manage with flap, rather than a problem.

## Key points

Let's recap.

Power and flap, at a constant indicated airspeed. More power reduces the rate and the angle of descent, and raises the nose. Less power increases them, and lowers the nose. More flap increases the rate and the angle of descent, and lowers the nose. Less flap does the reverse. On approach, attitude holds the speed, and power sets the rate of descent.

Wind. The aeroplane glides through the air, so a steady wind does not change the rate of descent, or the time to the ground. A headwind lowers the groundspeed: a steeper path over the ground, and less distance. A tailwind raises it: a flatter path, and more distance. The distance equals the time to the ground, times the groundspeed.

The glide speed. In a glide, the tangent of the glide angle equals the drag divided by the lift. So the flattest glide is at the best lift to drag ratio. That occurs at one angle of attack, and so at one indicated airspeed, the recommended glide speed. Faster brings more parasite drag. Slower brings more induced drag. Either way, the lift to drag ratio falls, the glide steepens, and the still-air distance, which is the height times the lift to drag ratio, shrinks. Raising the nose does not stretch a glide. And weight changes the best glide speed, but not the angle, or the still-air distance.

A final exam tip. For power and flap at a constant indicated airspeed, think: power flattens and raises the nose, flap steepens and lowers it. For wind, the rate of descent is unchanged, and only the distance over the ground changes. For glide distance, multiply the height by the lift to drag ratio, and divide by 6,076 for nautical miles. Or multiply the time to the ground by the groundspeed. And any option that stretches a glide by raising the nose, or says weight changes the glide distance, is wrong.
