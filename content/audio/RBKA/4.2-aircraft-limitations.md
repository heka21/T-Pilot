This is RBKA 4.2, Aircraft limitations, the flap speeds. By the end you will be able to explain the flap operating speed and the flap extended speed, say what to do when they differ, and find them on the airspeed indicator. There are a few questions along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (RBKA 4.2)

The exam wants one thing from you. Explain the flap operating speed, VFO, and the flap extended speed, VFE.

## Why this matters

Flap is the one part of a light aeroplane's structure you move in flight, and it is built to work only at low speed. Every circuit at Jandakot has you selecting it while you are slowing down and talking to the tower. And the easy mistake is to select it a few knots too fast. Do that often enough, and the tracks wear, the flap distorts, or one side jams.

The exam asks for the two flap speeds, what to do when they differ, and where they sit on the airspeed indicator.

## Why speed limits exist

Every force the air puts on the aeroplane grows with dynamic pressure. That is lift on the wing, drag on the airframe, and the load on a control surface. Dynamic pressure is the pressure of the air brought to rest against the aeroplane. It equals a half, times the air density, times the speed squared.

The speed is squared. So 10 percent faster, and the air pushes about 21 percent harder. Twice as fast, and it pushes four times as hard.

Strength costs weight. So the designer decides the highest speed at which each part has to work, builds it strong enough for that speed with a margin, and no stronger. That speed becomes a limitation.

Flaps are the clearest case. They are only needed at low speed, for take-off and landing. Building them and their attachments strong enough for cruise would waste weight. So they are designed for a low speed, and that speed is the flap limit.

## The flap extended speed, V<sub>FE</sub>

The flap extended speed, VFE, is the maximum speed at which the aeroplane may be flown with the flaps extended. With flap out, the flap, its tracks, its hinges, its operating mechanism, and the rear part of the wing that carries them, are all designed for the loads up to VFE. Above it, those loads go past the design limits.

Many aeroplanes have more than one VFE. A higher one for the first stage of flap, and a lower one for full flap. The reason is that a fully extended flap sticks further into the airflow, at a steeper angle, so it takes more load at any given speed. The flight manual lists each figure.

Let's work one. Take a made-up aeroplane with a full-flap VFE of 95 kt. In a rushed approach, you select full flap at 112 kt. How much extra load does the flap take? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
Step one, the speed ratio. 112 divided by 95 is 1.18.

Step two, the load goes with the square of the speed. 1.18 squared is 1.39.

So the flap and its attachments carry about 39 percent more load than the most they were designed to carry in normal service. Seventeen knots does not feel like much on the dial. To the flap structure, it is more than a third more load. The answer again: about 39 percent extra.

[[diagram:flap-load-vs-speed]]
Picture a curve of the load on the flap, climbing more and more steeply as the airspeed rises. At the 95 kt VFE, the load is at 100 percent of the design figure. At 112 kt, which is 1.18 times VFE, the curve has climbed to 139 percent.

So what happens above VFE? The flap surface can bend or buckle. The tracks, rollers and hinges can deform or crack. The operating mechanism, whether it is a cable, a pushrod, a torque tube or an electric motor, is loaded beyond its rating. And the rear spar and the ribs that carry the flap loads into the wing take more than intended.

There is an aerodynamic side too. Extended flap moves the wing's centre of pressure rearwards, and makes the wing twist and pitch nose-down more strongly. That twisting load also grows with the square of the speed. Many flight manuals give a lower limit load factor with flap extended than with flap up. Check the flight manual for your type. So a high-speed pull-up with flap down combines two overloads.

The damage may not be symmetrical. If one flap distorts, or one side of the mechanism gives way, the two flaps can end up at different angles. That is asymmetric flap. It rolls the aeroplane towards the side with less flap, and at low speed it may be more than the ailerons can hold. In the worst case, a flap can come off the aeroplane.

An overspeed with flap extended, even one that seems to have done no harm, is a defect. Write it up in the maintenance release, so that an engineer can inspect the flap system before the next flight.

Question. Why does full flap often have a lower VFE than the first stage of flap?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: a fully extended flap sticks further into the airflow, at a steeper angle, so at any given speed it carries more load. To keep the load within what the structure was designed for, the speed must be lower.

## The flap operating speed, V<sub>FO</sub>

The flap operating speed, VFO, is the maximum speed at which the flaps may be moved, either extended or retracted. It is about the flap in motion, not the flap in position.

Why would moving the flap need a lower speed than holding it out? While the flap travels, the mechanism has to drive it against the air load. On some designs, the motor or the linkage is rated for less force than the locked structure can carry. So on those aeroplanes, the speed for operating the flap is set lower than the speed for flying with it set.

On many light aeroplanes, VFO and VFE are the same figure, and the flight manual quotes only VFE. Where they differ, VFO is the lower one. And the order of operations follows from the definitions. There are three steps.

First, slow to VFO or below before you select flap.

Second, once the flap is extended, VFE is the limit for flying with it out.

And third, to retract the flap, you must again be at VFO or below while it moves.

[[diagram:flap-speeds-vfo-vfe]]
Picture a speed scale with the white arc on it. The bottom of the arc is VSO, the full-flap stall speed at maximum weight. The top is VFE, the most you may fly with flap out. And on some aeroplanes, a mark partway down the arc, below VFE: that is VFO. Slow to VFO before selecting flap. Then VFE is the limit for flying with it out.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. It gives you a VFO lower than VFE, and a speed between the two, and asks what you may do. At that speed you may fly with the flap already extended, because you are below VFE. But you may not move it, to extend it or to retract it, until you are at or below VFO. The wrong answers either forbid flying with flap at that speed, or allow selecting it.

Here is one for you to try. The same made-up flight manual gives a VFO of 85 kt. VFE is 105 kt for the first stage of flap, and 95 kt for full flap. You are at 100 kt, with the first stage of flap extended. Are you within limits? And may you select full flap?
[[pause 8]]
You are within limits. 100 kt is below the first-stage VFE of 105 kt, so you may fly with that flap out.

But you may not select full flap yet, and there are two reasons. First, 100 kt is above VFO, 85 kt, so the flap may not be moved at all. Second, 100 kt is above the full-flap VFE of 95 kt, so you could not fly with full flap even once it was out.

So slow to 85 kt or below, which satisfies both, and then select full flap. Once it is out, you may fly with it up to 95 kt. But you must be back at 85 kt or below to retract it.

## Finding the flap speeds on the airspeed indicator

You will not have the flight manual open on short final. So the key limits are painted on the airspeed indicator, as coloured arcs and a line, and each end is a defined speed. There are three arcs and one line.

[[diagram:asi-colour-arcs]]
Picture the airspeed indicator dial, with the needle sweeping clockwise from slow to fast. Innermost, starting lowest, is a white arc. Outside it, starting a little higher and running further round, is a green arc. Beyond the green comes a yellow arc. And at the end of the yellow, a single red line.

The white arc is the flap operating range. Its bottom is VSO, the stall speed in the landing configuration, which means full flap and power off, at maximum weight. Its top is VFE, the maximum flap extended speed. The white arc is where flap may be used. Where a type has more than one VFE, the top of the white arc is normally the full-flap figure. The others are in the flight manual, and often on a placard. VFO, where it is different, is generally not marked on the dial.

The green arc is the normal operating range. Its bottom is Vs1, the stall speed in a specified configuration, normally flap up, at maximum weight. Its top is VNO, the maximum structural cruising speed. Up to VNO, the structure is designed to take the gusts of rough air. So the green arc is where you may fly in any air.

The yellow arc is the caution range. It runs from VNO to VNE. You may fly in it only in smooth air, and then with caution, because a strong gust at those speeds can overstress the structure.

The red line is VNE, the never-exceed speed. Beyond it, the margins for flutter and structural strength are not assured.

And what is not on the dial? Va, the manoeuvring speed, is not marked, because it changes with weight. You find it in the flight manual. VFO, where it is separate, is also only in the flight manual.

[[diagram:asi-colour-markings]]
Picture the same dial again, now with every end labelled. White, from VSO to VFE, for flap. Green, from Vs1 to VNO, for normal operation. Yellow, from VNO to VNE, for smooth air only. Red line at VNE. And no mark at all for Va.

Notice where the white arc sits against the green. It starts below the green, because flap lowers the stall speed, so VSO is below Vs1. And it ends below the top of the green, because the flap is designed for a lower speed than the airframe, so VFE is below VNO. In the overlap, you may fly either clean or with flap.

Question. What speeds mark the two ends of the white arc, and what does each mean?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the bottom is VSO, the stall speed in the landing configuration at maximum weight. The top is VFE, the maximum speed at which the aeroplane may be flown with flap extended.

## How the POH presents limitations

Section 2 of the flight manual, Limitations, holds the figures. And they are limits, not advice.

Its airspeed limitations table lists VNE, VNO, Va, often at more than one weight, and VFE for each flap stage, with VFO if it is separate. These are usually given in both indicated and calibrated airspeed. There is also an airspeed indicator markings table, which gives the speed at each end of each arc. The section also gives the load factor limits, flap up and flap down, and the cockpit placards, which may repeat the flap speeds beside the flap lever.

Use the indicated airspeed figures, since those are what the airspeed indicator shows. And use the flight manual for your aeroplane. It wins over any textbook figure, this lesson included.

## Using flap within its limits

On the approach, get the speed into the white arc, and below VFO where it is separate, before you reach for the flap lever. The common error is selecting flap in the green or yellow arc during a rushed descent. Slow down first, then flap, then trim. If you select the first stage at its VFE and then want more flap, check the speed again, because the next stage may have a lower limit.

Now the go-around. You are low, slow and fully configured. If you raise all the flap at once, the wing loses the extra lift that flap was giving it. Its stall speed jumps up towards the clean figure, Vs1, while you may still be near the full-flap approach speed. The aeroplane sinks, close to the ground, and may approach the stall.

[[diagram:flap-effect-on-lift-curve]]
Picture two lift curves, lift against angle of attack. The flap-down curve sits above the flap-up curve at every angle, and peaks higher. So with flap down, the wing stalls at a lower speed. Take the flap away, and you drop onto the lower curve, and the stall speed rises.

So the go-around is flown in steps, in the order and at the speeds the flight manual gives. There are three.

First, full power and the climb attitude, together. The power stops the descent, and the attitude stops the speed rising.

Second, once the aeroplane is climbing, retract the first stage of flap. That is usually the high-drag stage, from full flap to an intermediate setting. It removes most of the drag, while keeping most of the extra lift.

And third, accelerate. Once you are climbing positively, at a safe speed above the stall speed for the next configuration, retract the rest of the flap in stages.

[[diagram:go-around-flap-retraction]]
Picture a go-around from a full-flap approach, seen from the side, in two versions. In the first, all the flap comes up at once. The stall speed jumps while the aeroplane is still slow, and it sinks towards the ground. In the second, power and attitude come first, then the high-drag stage of flap, and then the rest, a stage at a time, keeping below VFE until the flap is up.

Watch the other limit as well. At full power with the flap still down, the aeroplane can accelerate quickly past VFE if the attitude is too low. Hold the climb attitude, and keep the speed in the white arc until the flap is up.

Question. Why is flap raised in stages on a go-around, rather than all at once?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: raising all the flap at once removes its extra lift. The stall speed jumps up towards the clean figure while the aeroplane is still slow and close to the ground, and it sinks. Raising it in stages, the high-drag stage first, keeps most of the lift while the aeroplane accelerates to a safe speed for the next configuration.

## Bringing it together

You join downwind at Jandakot at 100 kt, in the made-up aeroplane from earlier. VFO is 85 kt. VFE is 105 kt for the first stage, and 95 kt for full flap.

You reduce power, and wait for the needle to pass below 85 kt before you touch the flap, because VFO governs every movement.

On short final, the tower sends you around. Full power and climb attitude first. Then, because the flap may only move below 85 kt, you hold the attitude firmly, and raise the first stage as soon as you are climbing, and the rest at the flight manual speed. Let the nose drop and the speed run to 100 kt, and with the first stage still out, you would be inside its VFE of 105 kt, but unable to retract the flap without slowing down again.

## Key points

Let's recap.

VFE is the maximum speed with flap extended. It is the top of the white arc. Above it, the air loads, growing with the square of the speed, exceed what the flap, its tracks and the wing structure were designed for. A type may have a VFE for each flap stage.

VFO is the maximum speed at which the flap may be moved, extended or retracted. Often it equals VFE. Where it differs, it is lower. Slow to VFO to move the flap. Then VFE is the limit for flying with it out.

On the dial: the white arc runs from VSO to VFE. The green, from Vs1 to VNO. The yellow, from VNO to VNE. And the red line is VNE. Va and VFO are in section 2 of the flight manual, not on the dial. And on a go-around, raise the flap in stages.

A final exam tip. If a question gives a VFO lower than VFE, you must be at or below VFO to move the flap. Then you may fly up to VFE with it extended. The top of the white arc is VFE, and the bottom is VSO. The top of the green arc is VNO, not VFE.
