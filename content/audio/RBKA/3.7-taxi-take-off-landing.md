This is RBKA 3.7, Taxi, take-off, landing. By the end you will know why an aeroplane wheelbarrows, how to handle a crosswind, why we take off and land into wind, how a flapless approach differs, and what wind shear and ground effect do near the ground. There are worked examples and questions along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (RBKA 3.7)

The exam wants five things from you. First, the situations that can make an aeroplane wheelbarrow, and what the pilot should do. Second, what a crosswind does to high-wing and low-wing aeroplanes when taxiing, taking off and landing. Third, the advantages of taking off and landing into wind. Fourth, how a flapless approach compares with a flap approach: the attitude, the approach path, the threshold and touchdown speeds, and the landing roll. And fifth, what wind shear, or wind gradient, and ground effect do to the way the aeroplane flies, and how to recognise them.

## Why this matters

Most training accidents happen in the few minutes near the ground, and few of them are dramatic. A wing tip lifted by a gust while taxiing at Jandakot. A nosewheel collapsed by a fast, flat landing. A swing off the runway at Northam in a gusty crosswind. An aeroplane that sinks short as the headwind dies in the last 200 ft.

Each one is physics the pilot did not anticipate. Perth's afternoon sea breeze guarantees a crosswind or a gusty headwind on many training days. The exam asks you to describe each effect and the right response, so this lesson builds each one from the forces involved.

## Wheelbarrowing

A tricycle-undercarriage aeroplane is designed to roll on its main wheels, which sit just behind the centre of gravity. That arrangement is directionally stable on the ground. If the aeroplane starts to swing, the weight ahead of the main wheels tends to keep it rolling straight, the way a shopping trolley pushed from behind runs true.

Now imagine the aeroplane rolling fast with the nose held down. The wing is still lifting, so the main wheels carry very little weight, and the elevator presses the nose onto the runway. The aeroplane is now balanced on its nosewheel, which is ahead of the centre of gravity. That is wheelbarrowing.

Like a wheelbarrow, it pivots on a front wheel ahead of the weight, and that is unstable. The aeroplane becomes hard to steer. Any crosswind or brake input can swing it violently round, which is a ground loop. And the nose gear, which is not built to carry that load at that speed, can be damaged or collapse.

[[diagram:wheelbarrowing-pivot]]
Picture two aeroplanes seen from above. In the first, the main wheels, just behind the centre of gravity, carry the weight. When it swings, it is pulled straight again, like the trolley. In the second, it has landed fast and flat with the nose held down. The main wheels have gone light, and the nosewheel, out ahead of the centre of gravity, has become the pivot. Now any swing grows, into a ground loop.

There are three situations that cause it. The first is landing too fast and flying or pushing the aeroplane onto the runway in a flat or nose-down attitude, nosewheel first, or all three wheels together. The excess speed means the wing is still lifting strongly after touchdown. The second is holding forward pressure on the control column at high speed, during the take-off roll or the landing run. For example, to hold it down for a better lift-off. And the third is a crosswind take-off or landing with forward elevator held and excess speed. That adds a sideways force to an already unstable aeroplane.

The recommended action follows from the cause: get the weight back onto the main wheels.

On landing, close the throttle if any power is on, and ease the control column back to raise the nose. That takes load off the nosewheel and lets the main wheels take the weight. If the speed is high, or the runway remaining is short, go around, rather than trying to force the aeroplane to stay down.

On take-off, relax the forward pressure and hold the elevator about neutral, so the main wheels carry the weight until rotation.

Prevention is better than either. Arrive at the threshold at the correct speed, and land main wheels first in a nose-up attitude, holding the nosewheel off until it lowers by itself.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Pushing forward to stick the aeroplane to the runway after a fast landing is what causes wheelbarrowing. It is not the cure. The exam's correct option always involves back pressure, or a go-around. Never forward pressure, and never harder braking.

Question. Why does a tricycle aeroplane become directionally unstable when it wheelbarrows?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the weight is being carried on the nosewheel, which is ahead of the centre of gravity, instead of on the main wheels behind it. A pivot ahead of the weight is unstable, like a wheelbarrow. Any swing grows, instead of correcting itself.

## What a crosswind does to the aeroplane

A crosswind does two things to any aeroplane, and every crosswind technique is a response to one of them.

The first is weathercocking. The fin and the side of the fuselage behind the main wheels act like the tail of a weathervane. Wind from the side pushes on that area and swings the tail downwind, so the nose yaws into wind. On the ground the main wheels are the pivot, and most of the side area is behind them. So the tendency is strong at low speed, when the rudder has little airflow to fight back with.

The second is the into-wind wing lifting. Wind from one side tends to raise the upwind wing, and roll the aeroplane away from the wind. There are three reasons. One: dihedral, the upward tilt of the wings. Because of it, the sideways airflow meets the upwind wing at a higher effective angle of attack, so that wing makes more lift. Two: the fuselage partly shields the downwind wing from the wind. And three: on a high-wing aeroplane, the wind gets underneath the exposed upwind wing and pushes it up.

[[diagram:crosswind-takeoff-and-landing]]
Picture an aeroplane on its take-off roll, seen from behind, with the wind coming from the left. The wind tries to lift the left wing, and the fin swings the nose to the left, into wind. So you hold full aileron into wind at the start, reduce it as the speed builds, and keep straight with rudder. Now picture the same aeroplane on approach. It can either crab, heading into wind and tracking down the centreline, or fly wing-down, with the upwind wing lowered and opposite rudder holding the nose straight.

Let's take taxiing in a strong wind first. Taxiing slowly, the wind dominates the airflow over the controls, so you set them where the wind itself holds the aeroplane down. There are two cases.

Wind from ahead, from anywhere in the front quadrants. Hold the aileron into the wind, which means the control column towards the side the wind is coming from. That raises the upwind aileron, which spoils some lift on the upwind wing and lets the wind press down on it. In a tricycle aeroplane, hold the elevator neutral.

Wind from behind, from the rear quadrants. Hold the aileron away from the wind, which lowers the upwind aileron. And hold the elevator forward. With the wind coming from behind, it strikes the top of the down-going aileron and the down-going elevator, and presses them down. That holds the upwind wing and the tail on the ground. The memory aid is: dive away from the wind.

[[diagram:crosswind-taxi-control-positions]]
Picture an aeroplane from above, with four wind quadrants around it. The two in front: stick into the wind, elevator neutral. The two behind: stick away from the wind, and forward.

A high-wing aeroplane is the more vulnerable when taxiing. Its wing is high and exposed, and the wind gets under the upwind wing easily. A strong gust from the rear quarter can lift it, and tip the aeroplane onto the downwind wing tip. So taxi slowly, hold the controls firmly in position, and turn with care when the wind will be from behind. A low-wing aeroplane's wing is low and partly shielded by the ground, so it is less prone to lifting. But its wing tips are closer to the ground, and to taxiway lights, signs and other aeroplanes.

Now taking off in a crosswind. At the start of the roll the aeroplane is slow, the controls are weak, and both crosswind effects are at their strongest. Begin with full aileron into wind, to hold the upwind wing down, and keep straight with rudder against the weathercocking yaw. As the speed builds, the ailerons become more effective, and the same deflection would roll the aeroplane towards the wind. So you reduce the aileron progressively, to keep the wings level. At rotation, lift off cleanly and positively, so the aeroplane does not skip sideways on the runway as it becomes light. Then turn into wind just enough to crab, heading slightly into wind, so that the aeroplane tracks along the extended centreline. The rolling tendency is stronger in a high-wing aeroplane, whose wing is more exposed.

Now landing in a crosswind. On final, the aeroplane is flying in the air mass, and the air mass is moving sideways across the runway. So you must point slightly into wind to track down the centreline. There are two ways to deal with that at touchdown, and many pilots combine them.

The first is the crab method. Fly the approach heading into wind, with the wings level, tracking the centreline. Just before touchdown, use rudder to yaw the nose into line with the runway, and aileron to stop the drift that starts the moment you do. Too early, and the aeroplane drifts sideways. Too late, and it lands sideways, loading the undercarriage sideways, and possibly swinging.

The second is the wing-down method, which is a sideslip. Lower the upwind wing just enough to stop the drift, and hold opposite rudder to keep the nose lined up with the runway. The aeroplane is then slipping into wind at exactly the rate the wind is pushing it away. It touches down on the upwind main wheel first, then the other main wheel, then the nosewheel. After touchdown, keep increasing the aileron into wind as the aeroplane slows, so the wind cannot lift the upwind wing.

Here the difference between high and low wings works the other way. A low-wing aeroplane has much less wing-tip and flap clearance when the upwind wing is lowered. So an over-steep wing-down attitude, or a touchdown with bank, risks a tip or flap strike. A high-wing aeroplane has plenty of clearance.

Every flight manual states a maximum demonstrated crosswind. That is the largest crosswind component flown during certification, by a test pilot. It is not a hard limit, but treat it as one until you have the experience.

To compare the wind with that figure, you need two parts of the wind. The crosswind component is the part blowing straight across the runway. The headwind component is the part blowing straight down it. Take the angle between the wind direction and the runway direction. The crosswind is the wind speed times the sine of that angle. The headwind is the wind speed times the cosine of that angle.

[[widget:crosswind-component]]
Picture the wind swinging away from the runway direction. The crosswind part grows fast at first. Just 30 degrees off the runway already puts half the wind across it. That is the figure to compare with the flight manual's demonstrated crosswind.

Let's work one. Runway {24|two four}, and the wind is {280|two eight zero} degrees at 20 kt, both magnetic. Find the crosswind and the headwind. Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
Step one, the angle between the wind and the runway. 280 minus 240 is 40 degrees.

Step two, the crosswind. 20 times the sine of 40 degrees, which is 0.643, gives 12.9 kt.

Step three, the headwind. 20 times the cosine of 40 degrees, which is 0.766, gives 15.3 kt.

So about 13 kt across, and about 15 kt down the runway. Compare that 13 kt with your flight manual's demonstrated crosswind before you accept the runway. And expect the wind from the right, so the into-wind wing to hold down is the right one.

For a check in your head, there are rules of thumb. A wind 30 degrees off the runway puts half of it across. At 45 degrees, about seven-tenths. At 60 degrees, about nine-tenths.

Now try one with a twist. The tower at Jandakot is using runway {24|two four}, and the wind is {100|one zero zero} degrees at 12 kt. Work out the components for runway {24|two four}, and then for runway {06|zero six}.
[[pause 8]]
Runway {24|two four} first. The angle between 240 and 100 is 140 degrees. That is more than 90, so the wind has a part from behind. The crosswind is 12 times the sine of 140 degrees, 12 times 0.643, which is 7.7 kt. The part along the runway is 12 times the cosine of 140 degrees. That cosine is minus 0.766, so the answer is minus 9.2 kt. In other words, a tailwind of about 9 kt.

Now runway {06|zero six}. The angle is 100 minus 60, which is 40 degrees. So the crosswind is the same 7.7 kt, but now there is a headwind of 9.2 kt. The crosswind is identical. The choice of runway end decides whether that 9 kt helps you or hurts you. And that is the next chapter.

Question. You are taxiing a high-wing aeroplane, with a strong wind from the right rear quarter. Where do you hold the controls?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: aileron away from the wind, and elevator forward. That means the control column to the left, so the right aileron, on the upwind side, is down. Wind from behind presses on the top of the lowered aileron and elevator, holding the upwind wing and the tail down.

## Why take off and land into wind

The aeroplane lifts off and touches down at a fixed airspeed. Into a headwind, part of that airspeed is supplied by the wind before you have moved. So the groundspeed at lift-off and touchdown is lower. Everything good about taking off and landing into wind follows from that one fact. There are five advantages.

First, shorter take-off and landing distances. The energy the aeroplane must build up on take-off, or get rid of on landing, depends on the square of the groundspeed. So even a modest headwind gives a large saving.

Second, steeper climb-out and approach paths over the ground. The rate of climb or descent in feet per minute is the same. But you cover less ground each minute, so obstacles at the end of the runway are cleared more easily, and the approach can come over them more steeply.

Third, better directional control, with no drift and no weathercocking to fight.

Fourth, the controls work earlier in the take-off roll. The airspeed, and with it the authority of the rudder and ailerons, builds up before the groundspeed does.

And fifth, less wear on tyres and brakes. And if something goes wrong, there is less energy to absorb, so less damage and injury in an overrun or a collision.

Let's work one. The approach speed is 65 kt, and the wind is 15 kt straight down the runway. Compare landing into it with landing downwind. How much more energy do the brakes have to absorb? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
Into wind, the groundspeed is 65 minus 15, which is 50 kt. Downwind, it is 65 plus 15, which is 80 kt. Energy goes with the square of the speed, so the ratio is 80 over 50, squared. 80 over 50 is 1.6, and 1.6 squared is 2.56.

So landing downwind in a 15 kt wind, the brakes must absorb more than two and a half times the energy, 2.56 times. And the aeroplane reaches the runway at 30 kt more groundspeed. That is how tailwind landings become overruns.

[[diagram:kinetic-energy-speed-squared]]
Picture a curve of energy against speed, bending upwards more and more steeply. 10 percent faster carries 21 percent more energy, because 1.1 squared is 1.21. Double the speed carries four times the energy. A little extra groundspeed costs a lot.

On a sloping country strip, the wind is not the only factor. An upslope shortens the landing and lengthens the take-off. A wet or grass surface lengthens both. You'll find those in the lesson on take-off and landing performance. With a light wind the slope may decide the direction, but with any real wind, into wind usually wins.

Question. Does a headwind change the rate of climb after take-off?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: no. The rate of climb in feet per minute depends on the aeroplane's power and drag in the air mass. A headwind lowers the groundspeed, so the same rate of climb gives a steeper path over the ground, and better obstacle clearance.

## Flapless and flap approaches compared

Flap can fail, so you will practise flapless landings. The comparison starts with what flap does to the wing. Lowering flap increases the wing's camber. So the wing makes more lift at every angle of attack, and it has a higher maximum lift coefficient. Flap also adds a lot of drag.

[[diagram:flap-effect-on-lift-curve]]
Picture two lift curves on one graph, lift against angle of attack. The flap-down curve sits above the flap-up curve at every angle, and its peak is higher. So with flap, the wing makes the lift it needs at a smaller angle of attack, and it stalls at a lower speed.

The syllabus compares the two approaches in four ways: the attitude, the path, the speeds and the landing roll.

First, the attitude in the descent. With flap, the wing makes the lift it needs at a smaller angle of attack, so the nose sits lower, and you see the runway clearly over it. Without flap, the wing needs a higher angle of attack for the same lift. So the nose attitude is higher, and the view ahead is poorer.

Second, the approach path angle. Flap's drag lets the aeroplane descend steeply without gaining speed. The drag is doing the work the throttle would otherwise do. Without flap, the aeroplane is slippery, with a better lift to drag ratio. So it descends on a flatter, shallower path at a given speed and power setting. To fly a normal path it needs less power, and the approach starts further out, on a longer final.

Third, the threshold and touchdown speeds. Approach speeds are set as a margin above the stall speed in the landing configuration, typically about 1.3 times it. Without flap the stall speed is higher, so the threshold and touchdown speeds are higher. Use the flight manual's flapless approach speed.

And fourth, the landing roll. A higher touchdown speed means more energy to get rid of. And because energy goes with the square of the speed, a few knots make a large difference. The clean wing also floats further in ground effect before it settles. So the landing roll is longer, and the whole landing distance is longer still.

So, to sum up the flapless approach: the nose is higher and the view poorer, the path is flatter, the speeds are higher, and the roll is longer. With flap, it is the reverse: a lower nose, a steeper path, lower speeds and a shorter roll.

The flapless landing has one more trap. The attitude at touchdown is already high, so flaring too high or too hard can strike the tail on the runway. Plan a longer final, use the flapless speed, and fly the aeroplane onto the runway with less flare.

Question. Why is the nose attitude higher on a flapless approach, at the correct approach speed?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: without flap, the wing makes less lift at each angle of attack. So it must fly at a higher angle of attack to support the weight. And a higher angle of attack on the same approach path means a higher nose attitude.

## Wind shear, wind gradient and gusts

Wind shear is a change in wind speed or direction over a short distance, either vertically or horizontally. The aeroplane flies relative to the air, but it has inertia. When the wind changes suddenly, the aeroplane keeps its groundspeed for a moment. So its airspeed changes, by the amount the wind changed.

The most common form near the ground is the wind gradient. Friction with the surface slows the wind close to the ground. So on a windy day the wind may be 25 kt at 500 ft, and 10 kt at the runway. Trees, hangars and buildings upwind make it worse, and more abrupt.

[[diagram:wind-gradient-on-approach]]
Picture two panels. On the left, the wind gradient: a set of arrows, longest a few hundred feet up, and getting shorter towards the ground. On the right, an aeroplane on final into that strong headwind. As it descends, the headwind weakens, the airspeed decays, and the rate of descent increases. The aeroplane sinks below its intended path and undershoots, unless the pilot adds power.

Follow that approach through. As you descend, the headwind falls away. Inertia keeps the groundspeed, so the indicated airspeed drops, and lift falls. The aeroplane sinks below the approach path, with no change in attitude or power. If you do nothing, you undershoot. And if the speed was already low, the drop can take you to the stall.

The opposite shear raises the airspeed. That is a headwind that increases, or a tailwind that dies away. Then the aeroplane balloons above the path, then floats, and overshoots.

So there are two patterns. A headwind decreasing, or a tailwind increasing: the indicated airspeed falls, the aeroplane sinks, and the nose drops. The result is an undershoot, with a risk of the stall. A headwind increasing, or a tailwind decreasing: the indicated airspeed rises, and the aeroplane balloons. The result is an overshoot, and a float.

[[diagram:wind-shear-on-approach]]
Picture the approach from the side as the headwind dies near the ground. The groundspeed holds, the indicated airspeed falls, and the aeroplane drops below the path. The cure: a speed margin, and power ready.

On take-off, the same physics catches you in the climb. Climbing out of a strong headwind into a weaker one, or through a sea-breeze front into a tailwind, drops the airspeed just when you are low and slow. Hold the climb speed and be ready to lower the nose. And delay departure near a thunderstorm, whose outflow produces the most violent shear of all.

How do you identify wind shear? By its signature. The airspeed and the rate of descent change suddenly, with no change of attitude or power. Expect it in strong or gusty winds, behind buildings and trees, along a sea-breeze front, and near showers and thunderstorms.

Gusts are rapid, random changes in wind speed, and each one is a small shear. The usual technique in gusty conditions is to add half the gust factor to the normal approach speed. The gust factor is the difference between the steady wind and the gusts. Check the flight manual, and your instructor's guidance, for your type.

Let's work one. The normal approach speed is 65 kt. The Jandakot ATIS gives the wind as 15 kt gusting 25 kt, close to the runway direction. What approach speed do you fly? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
The gust factor is 25 minus 15, which is 10 kt. Half the gust factor is 5 kt. So the approach speed is 65 plus 5, which is 70 kt.

Those extra 5 kt mean that when a gust dies away on short final, the airspeed drops back to about the normal figure, instead of towards the stall. The price is a slightly longer landing roll. So, 70 kt.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Loss of headwind on approach means loss of airspeed, and an undershoot. Gain of headwind means gain of airspeed, and a float. The exam often swaps them round in the wrong answers. Remember that the aeroplane's inertia keeps the groundspeed, so the airspeed changes by the change in the wind.

## Ground effect

As the aeroplane comes within about one wingspan of the surface, the ground starts to interfere with the airflow around the wing. The effect is strongest within about half a span.

A wing making lift pushes air downwards behind it. That is downwash. It also sheds a vortex from each wing tip. Both are the source of induced drag. Near the ground, there is no room for the downwash to go down, and the tip vortices are squashed. The result: induced drag falls, and the wing makes more lift at a given angle of attack. Parasite drag is not affected.

[[diagram:ground-effect]]
Picture the same wing seen from behind, twice. On the left, it is more than a wingspan up. It pushes a deep downwash behind it and sheds full-sized tip vortices. On the right, it is within about half a span of the runway. The ground blocks the downwash and squashes the vortices, so the induced drag falls, and the wing makes a little more lift at the same angle.

That changes how the aeroplane flies in the last few feet, in three ways.

First, on landing, the aeroplane floats, especially if it arrives too fast. It is suddenly more efficient, so it loses speed slowly. A fast flapless approach floats the longest. Floating uses up runway, and the temptation to push the aeroplane onto the runway leads straight to wheelbarrowing.

Second, on take-off, the aeroplane may lift off below its normal speed, and fly happily at a few feet. Then, as it climbs out of ground effect and the induced drag returns, it sinks back or climbs poorly. If it was pulled off too early, it may be unable to climb over obstacles. So accelerate in ground effect to the proper climb speed before climbing. The soft-field technique uses ground effect deliberately, to get the wheels out of the mud as early as possible.

And third, the pitch and airspeed indications can change slightly, as the downwash at the tailplane changes.

How do you identify ground effect? By a floating, cushioned feel in the flare, and a reluctance to settle. Or by an aeroplane that leaves the ground early, but will not climb away.

Question. An aeroplane lifts off early on a soft strip, but will not climb past about 10 ft. What is happening, and what should the pilot do?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: it is flying in ground effect, where induced drag is reduced, at a speed too low to climb once the ground effect is lost. Hold it level in ground effect, and let it accelerate to the climb speed before climbing.

## Bringing it together

It is a summer afternoon at Jandakot. The sea breeze is in at 18 kt gusting 26, from about 30 degrees off runway {24|two four}, on the right.

Before taxiing, you work out the components. About half the wind, 9 kt, is across the runway, comfortably inside the flight manual's demonstrated figure. And the headwind is about 16 kt.

Taxiing out to the threshold with the wind behind you, you hold the stick away from the wind, and forward. On the roll you start with full right aileron, reduce it as the speed builds, and lift off cleanly before setting up a crab.

Back in the circuit, the gust factor is 8 kt, so you fly final 4 kt faster than normal. Passing 300 ft the headwind dies and the airspeed sags. You add power at once, rather than raising the nose.

Over the threshold you lower the right wing to stop the drift, hold left rudder to keep straight, and touch down on the right main wheel. The aeroplane floats a little in ground effect. You hold it off with back pressure rather than pushing it down, keeping the weight off the nosewheel, and adding right aileron as it slows.

## Key points

Let's recap.

Wheelbarrowing is weight on the nosewheel while the wing is still lifting, from a fast flat landing or from forward pressure. Close the throttle and ease the control column back, or go around. Prevent it with the right speed and a main-wheels-first touchdown.

A crosswind weathercocks the nose into wind, and lifts the upwind wing. High-wing aeroplanes are more exposed when taxiing and on the roll. Low-wing aeroplanes have less tip and flap clearance in a wing-down landing. When taxiing with the wind from ahead, stick into wind. With the wind from behind, stick away from the wind, and forward.

Into wind gives a lower groundspeed, shorter distances, steeper paths over obstacles, better directional control, and less energy to absorb.

A flapless approach has a higher nose attitude, a flatter path, higher threshold and touchdown speeds, a longer landing roll, and more float.

A decreasing headwind on approach drops the indicated airspeed and causes an undershoot. An increasing one causes a float. In gusts, add half the gust factor. Ground effect reduces induced drag within about a wingspan, causing a float on landing, and an early lift-off on take-off that the aeroplane cannot sustain.

A final exam tip. Expect questions on the taxi control positions, where wind from behind means stick away from the wind and forward. On the low-wing aeroplane's tip clearance in a wing-down landing. On the advantages of into wind, where you pick the one that is not an advantage. On the flapless approach: higher attitude, flatter path, higher speeds, longer roll. And on wind gradient or ground effect. Remember, ground effect reduces induced drag, not parasite drag.
