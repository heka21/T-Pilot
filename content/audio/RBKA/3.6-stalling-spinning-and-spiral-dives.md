This is RBKA 3.6, Stalling, spinning and spiral dives. By the end you will know what a stall really is, why the stall speed moves, how to recover, and how to tell a spin from a spiral dive. You will also be able to work out the stall speed in a turn or at a new weight in your head. There are questions along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (RBKA 3.6)

The exam wants six things from you. First, the symptoms as you approach the stall, and what the stall itself is like. Second, what aileron does near and in the stall, and why an aeroplane stalls at different speeds. Third, whether each of seven factors raises, lowers or leaves alone the level-flight stall speed: power, flap, gusts and wind shear, manoeuvres, weight, frost and ice, and altitude. Fourth, the aerodynamic principle of stall recovery. Fifth, the manoeuvres in which the aeroplane stalls at an attitude that looks nothing like the normal stalling attitude. And sixth, how to tell a spin from a spiral dive, and the standard recovery from each.

## Why this matters

Most fatal accidents in light aeroplanes that begin in the air are loss-of-control accidents. And most of those are a stall the pilot did not see coming, usually low and slow near an aerodrome.

The classic one is the turn from base onto final at Jandakot or Murrayfield on a gusty afternoon. A little overshoot. A little rudder to tighten the turn. A little back pressure to hold the nose up. And the wing lets go at 600 ft, with the nose near the horizon.

Understanding the stall breaks that chain. Once you see that a wing stalls at an angle, not at a speed and not at an attitude, every one of the exam's questions in this subtopic answers itself. The exam tests it with recall, like the symptoms in order and the spin recovery. And it tests it with numbers, like the stall speed in a turn or at a different weight.

## What a stall actually is

Start with the wing in cruise. Air meets it at a small angle, flows smoothly over the top, and leaves at the trailing edge. The chord line is the straight line from the wing's leading edge to its trailing edge. The relative airflow is the air coming at the wing, and it is always exactly opposite to the wing's own path through the air. The angle between those two is the angle of attack. Lift grows with angle of attack. Tilt the wing more, and it produces more lift at the same speed.

That works up to a point. Around 15 to 18 degrees for a typical light aeroplane wing, the air can no longer follow the curve of the upper surface. It separates, first near the trailing edge and then further forward, leaving a turbulent wake over the top of the wing. Lift falls sharply, and drag rises sharply. That angle is the critical angle of attack, and the wing is stalled.

[[diagram:cl-vs-angle-of-attack]]
Picture a graph, with the angle of attack along the bottom and the lift coefficient going up the side. The line climbs straight and steady from left to right. At the critical angle, about 16 degrees, it reaches a peak. Past that peak the line turns over and falls away, because the airflow has separated. That peak is the stall.

Two things follow, and the exam tests both again and again.

The first: the stall happens at the critical angle of attack, every time. Weight, speed, attitude and power change when you reach that angle. They never change the angle itself.

The second: the relative airflow comes from wherever the aeroplane is going, not from the horizon. So the wing can be past the critical angle with the nose above the horizon, on it, or below it. The stall is not an attitude.

[[widget:attitude-and-angle-of-attack]]
Picture the wing with little streamers along its top. Nose up, level or down, once the angle between the chord line and the relative airflow passes about 16 degrees, the streamers break away.

Now, what you feel on the way in. In straight and level flight at a fixed weight, the only way to reach the critical angle is to slow down. At a lower speed the wing must fly at a higher angle to make the same lift. So a level-flight stall arrives with four symptoms in a predictable order, and the exam expects you to know it.

First, low and decreasing airspeed, with a higher and higher nose attitude as you hold height.

Second, sloppy controls. The controls move further for less effect, because less air is flowing over them. And the airflow noise drops.

Third, the stall warning. That is a horn or a light, driven by a small vane on the leading edge. As the point where the airflow divides moves back under the nose of the wing, the vane lifts. It is set to trigger about 5 to 10 kt above the stall.

And fourth, buffet. That is a shake through the airframe and the control column, because the turbulent air separating from the wing is striking the tailplane.

[[diagram:stall-sequence]]
Picture three wing sections in a row, each at a higher angle of attack than the last. On the first, the airflow is attached over the whole upper surface. On the second, it is starting to separate near the trailing edge. On the third, past the critical angle, the flow over the top is separated and turbulent. Underneath, the four warnings run in order, ending in the stall itself.

So what is the stall itself like? At the stall, the nose drops. The centre of pressure moves, and the tail can no longer hold the nose up against the collapsing lift.

If one wing stalls before the other, that wing drops. That is a wing drop, and it is common. Wings are never perfectly matched, and any yaw makes one wing fly faster than the other. The rate of descent is high. The aeroplane may tend to roll further. The controls still work, but weakly. And the aeroplane will not climb or hold height however hard you pull. Lift is now less than weight, and more back pressure makes the angle of attack larger and the stall deeper.

Question. What single thing determines whether a wing is stalled?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the angle of attack. The wing stalls when the angle between the chord line and the relative airflow goes past the critical angle, about 15 to 18 degrees on a light aeroplane. Airspeed, attitude, weight and power only change the conditions under which that angle is reached.

Question. In what order do the symptoms arrive in a level-flight stall?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: low and decreasing airspeed with a rising nose attitude. Then sloppy controls and a quieter airflow. Then the stall warning, about 5 to 10 kt above the stall. Then buffet. Then the nose or a wing drops.

## Why the stall speed is not one number

Your flight manual quotes a stall speed. Say 50 kt with flaps up. That number is true in exactly one condition: level flight, maximum weight, power off, flaps up. Change anything, and it moves.

To see why, go back to the lift formula from your basic aerodynamics. Lift equals a half, times the air density, times the speed squared, times the wing area, times the lift coefficient. The lift coefficient rises with angle of attack, up to its maximum at the critical angle.

In level flight, lift must equal weight. The stall speed is simply the slowest speed at which the wing can still make that much lift. And that is the speed at which the lift coefficient has to be at its maximum. Turn the formula round, and the stall speed is the square root of twice the weight, divided by the air density times the wing area times the maximum lift coefficient.

Read that as a recipe. More lift needed, from more weight or a load factor above 1, pushes the stall speed up. More lift available at a given angle, from flap or slipstream, pushes it down. And the airspeed indicator responds to the same half times density times speed squared that the wing does. So the indicated stall speed does not change with density at all.

Now the exam's favourite calculation: load factor. In a level turn the lift is tilted, so its vertical part must still equal the weight, which means the total lift must be bigger than the weight. The ratio of lift to weight is the load factor. For a level turn, the load factor equals one over the cosine of the bank angle.

The lift needed has gone up by the load factor. Speed appears squared in the lift formula, so the stall speed goes up by the square root of the load factor. The stall speed in a turn equals the level-flight stall speed times the square root of the load factor.

[[diagram:stall-speed-vs-bank]]
Picture a curve of stall speed climbing as the bank angle increases. It starts almost flat, and beyond 45 degrees it steepens fast. There are three bank angles the exam loves. At 30 degrees, the load factor is 1.15, its square root is 1.07, and the stall speed rises by about 7 percent. At 45 degrees, the load factor is 1.41, its square root is 1.19, and the stall speed rises by about 19 percent. At 60 degrees, the load factor is 2, its square root is 1.41, and the stall speed rises by about 41 percent. Out at 75 degrees, the load factor is 3.86, and the stall speed has risen by about 97 percent, very nearly double.

[[widget:bank-angle]]
Picture rolling the bank steadily towards 60 degrees. For the first 30 degrees, almost nothing happens. Then the load factor and the stall speed take off together.

Let's work one. The level-flight stall speed is 55 kt, and the bank is 45 degrees. What is the stall speed in the turn? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
Step one, the load factor. One over the cosine of 45 degrees is one over 0.707, which is 1.41.

Step two, the square root of 1.41, which is 1.19.

Step three, the stall speed in the turn. 55 times 1.19 is 65.4. Call it 65 kt.

So a 45 degree turn has moved the stall 10 kt closer. If you were flying the turn at 70 kt, you had 15 kt in hand. Now you have 5. And a gust, or a little extra back pressure, takes the rest. The answer again: 65 kt.

Weight works the same way. Weight is the lift the wing must make in level flight, so the same square root rule applies. The new stall speed equals the old stall speed times the square root of the new weight divided by the old weight.

Let's work one. The flight manual stall speed is 55 kt at the maximum weight of 1,100 kg. You are flying solo with half tanks, at 900 kg. What is your stall speed today? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
Step one, the weight ratio. 900 divided by 1,100 is 0.818.

Step two, the square root of 0.818, which is 0.905.

Step three, 55 times 0.905 is 49.7. Call it 50 kt.

Lighter means a lower stall speed. That is why a solo student finds the aeroplane floats further in the flare than with the instructor on board: the flight manual approach speed was set for the heavy case. So, about 50 kt.

Here is one for you to try. An aeroplane stalls at 46 kt at 750 kg. What is its stall speed at 900 kg?
[[pause 8]]
The weight ratio is 900 over 750, which is 1.20. The square root of 1.20 is 1.095. And 46 times 1.095 is 50.4. So the answer is about 50 kt. Notice what happened. Twenty percent more weight added only about 10 percent to the stall speed. The square root is why the percentage is roughly halved.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. It asks for the indicated stall speed. Density height changes the true airspeed at which the wing stalls, but not the indicated airspeed, because the airspeed indicator measures the same dynamic pressure the wing feels. So in the list of factors, altitude has nil effect on the indicated stall speed. The trap answer is increases.

Question. Why does the stall speed rise by only about 41 percent when the load factor doubles?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because lift is proportional to the square of the speed. Doubling the lift needed only requires the speed to rise by the square root of 2, which is 1.41. The rule is always the stall speed times the square root of the load factor, or of the weight ratio.

## Ailerons near the stall

Picture the wing dropping at the stall. Instinct says roll it back up with aileron. Say the left wing has dropped. You move the control column to the right. That moves the left aileron down.

A down-going aileron increases the camber of that part of the wing, and with it the local angle of attack. On a wing already at or past the critical angle, more angle of attack means a deeper stall, less lift and more drag, on exactly the wing you were trying to lift. So the dropped wing drops further. The extra drag yaws the aeroplane towards it. And that combination, a stalled wing plus yaw, is the recipe for a spin.

[[diagram:wing-drop-and-recovery]]
Picture two panels side by side. On the left, the wrong way: aileron pushes the dropped wing further past the critical angle, and the aeroplane yaws towards a spin. On the right, the right way: rudder, then the control column forward, then aileron.

So near the stall, you keep the wings level with rudder, and you leave the ailerons roughly neutral. Rudder yaws the aeroplane, so the dropping wing speeds up a little and the rising wing slows. Once the wing is unstalled, the ailerons work normally again, and you roll level with them. The order matters, and the exam tests it. Unstall, then roll.

Question. A wing drops at the point of stall. What do you do with the ailerons, and why?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: keep them neutral. Use rudder to stop further yaw and wing drop, then reduce the angle of attack. Aileron to pick the wing up would lower the aileron on the dropped wing. That raises its angle of attack, deepens the stall on that side, and adds the yaw that can start a spin.

## The seven factors, one by one

The syllabus names seven factors, and for each wants to know whether the level-flight indicated stall speed increases, decreases, or doesn't change. See each one as a change either to the lift the wing must make, or to the lift it can make at the critical angle. Two of the seven lower the stall speed, four raise it, and one has no effect.

First, power. Power on decreases the stall speed. Two things help. The propeller slipstream flows over the inner wing faster than the free airstream, so that part of the wing makes more lift at a given indicated speed. And with the nose high at the stall, a share of the thrust points upwards and carries part of the weight, so the wing needs to make less lift. A power-on stall happens at a lower indicated speed, with a higher nose attitude, and often with a sharper wing drop.

Second, flap. Flap down decreases the stall speed. Lowering flap increases the camber of the wing. That raises the lift coefficient at every angle of attack, and raises the maximum lift coefficient. More lift available at the critical angle means the stall arrives at a lower speed. That is the whole point of flap for landing. The critical angle with flap down is a little lower, but the stall speed is lower too, and the exam asks about speed.

[[diagram:flap-effect-on-lift-curve]]
Picture two lift curves on the same graph, one for flap up and one for flap down. The flap-down curve sits higher at every angle, and its peak is higher. So the wing makes the lift it needs at a lower speed, and the stall speed falls. The same lift also comes at a smaller angle of attack, so the nose sits lower. The flap-down peak comes at a slightly smaller angle. And flap adds drag.

Third, vertical gusts and wind shear. They increase the stall speed. An up-gust changes the direction of the relative airflow, so the angle of attack jumps, with no change in attitude or in the airspeed reading. If you were already near the critical angle, the gust takes you past it. Wind shear works through speed instead. A sudden loss of headwind leaves the aeroplane, with its inertia, moving through the air more slowly. So the indicated airspeed drops towards the stall, with the attitude unchanged. Either way, the stall arrives at a higher indicated speed than the book figure. That is why you add a gust margin to the approach speed.

[[diagram:wind-shear-on-approach]]
Picture a side view of an approach into a strong headwind that dies away near the ground. Inertia keeps the groundspeed, so the indicated airspeed drops, and the aeroplane sinks below the path and undershoots, with no change in attitude or power. So add a speed margin in strong winds, and be ready with power.

Fourth, manoeuvres. They increase the stall speed. Any turn, pull-up or pull-out of a dive raises the load factor. The wing has to make more than the weight, and the stall speed rises by the square root of the load factor, as you worked out earlier.

Fifth, weight. More weight increases the stall speed. It is more lift to make, and the stall speed rises by the square root of the weight ratio.

Sixth, frost and ice. They increase the stall speed. Even a thin layer of frost, like the one on a wing left out overnight at Northam or Cunderdin in July, roughens the surface and changes the shape of the leading edge. The airflow separates earlier. So the maximum lift coefficient falls, and the critical angle itself is reduced. The wing stalls at a higher speed, and with less warning. And ice adds weight on top. Every trace of frost comes off before flight.

And seventh, altitude. Altitude has no effect, nil. In thinner air the wing must fly faster to make the same lift, so the true airspeed at the stall is higher. But the airspeed indicator measures dynamic pressure, exactly what the wing feels. So it reads the same at the stall, whatever the density. The indicated stall speed does not change.

So, two factors lower the indicated stall speed: power on and flap down. Four raise it: gusts and wind shear, manoeuvres, weight, and frost and ice. And one has no effect: altitude.

Question. Power on and flap down both lower the stall speed, but for different reasons. What are they?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: with power on, the slipstream speeds up the airflow over the inner wing, and the upward share of the thrust carries some weight, so the wing needs to make less lift. With flap down, the higher camber raises the lift coefficient at every angle, so the wing makes the lift it needs at a lower speed.

## Recovering: the only cure

Stall recovery follows from one principle. The wing unstalls when the angle of attack is reduced below the critical angle, and nothing else will do it. Power does not unstall a wing. Levelling the wings does not unstall a wing. Only moving the control column forward does, because that lowers the nose relative to the flight path and reduces the angle of attack.

The standard recovery has four steps, in the order the exam wants.

First, reduce the angle of attack. Ease the control column forward until the buffet stops and the controls firm up. You are trading height for angle. The height loss is the price of the recovery, and the reason you recover promptly.

Second, apply full power, to accelerate and to keep the height loss to a minimum. Keep straight with rudder against the yaw from the power increase.

Third, level the wings with aileron, now that the wing is unstalled and the ailerons work properly.

And fourth, recover to the climb attitude, and climb away with the minimum height loss.

The forward movement is often small. At the first buffet, relaxing the back pressure is usually enough. A fully developed stall with a wing drop needs a definite push, but a steep nose-down attitude wastes height.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. It offers apply full power, and level the wings, as the first action. Both are wrong. The first action in any stall recovery is to reduce the angle of attack. Power reduces the height loss, and aileron comes after the wing is flying again.

## Stalls that do not look like stalls

Because the stall is an angle and not an attitude, the wing can reach the critical angle with the nose nowhere near the stalling attitude you practised at height. The syllabus wants you to recognise four such manoeuvres.

The first is steep turns and pull-ups, where the load factor and so the stall speed are high. In a 60 degree turn, an aeroplane with a 50 kt book figure stalls at 71 kt. And the nose may be only a little above the horizon when it happens. The buffet is the warning. The attitude tells you nothing.

The second is pulling out of a dive, or out of a spiral dive, too hard. At high speed, a sharp pull makes a large load factor in an instant. The wing can reach the critical angle with the nose well below the horizon. That is an accelerated stall, and it comes with little warning, because the speed looked so healthy.

The third is the skidding turn onto final. You have overshot the centreline, turning base to final. Steepening the bank low down feels wrong, so instead you add rudder to hurry the nose round. The aeroplane skids. The inner wing is now moving more slowly through the air, and it is partly blanketed by the fuselage. And you are holding a little back pressure to keep the nose up in the turn. The inner wing reaches the critical angle first, and it drops, towards the ground, from a few hundred feet, with the nose near the horizon. This is the classic stall-spin accident. It is the reason instructors are so insistent about balance in the circuit.

The fourth is wind shear on approach. On a gusty day, the headwind falls away in the last few hundred feet. The indicated airspeed drops, the angle of attack rises as the aeroplane sinks, and the wing can stall at a perfectly normal approach attitude.

The common thread: attitude is not a stall cue. The cues are the airspeed, judged against the configuration and the load factor, the stall warning, and the buffet.

Question. Why is a skidding turn onto final so dangerous?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the pilot is low, slow and holding back pressure in a turn, so the stall speed is already raised by load factor. The skid slows the inner wing, which reaches the critical angle first and drops towards the ground. The nose is near the horizon, so the attitude gives no warning. And there is no height to recover.

## Spin or spiral dive

Both are steep, rotating descents that can follow a mishandled stall or a loss of control in cloud, and both end badly with the wrong recovery. The exam wants you to tell them apart, and to give the recovery for each.

A spin is autorotation. Both wings are stalled, but one more deeply than the other. The more stalled wing makes less lift and more drag, so it drops and drags back. The other wing, less stalled, makes more lift and rolls the aeroplane further. And the rolling raises the angle of attack of the down-going wing even more, which keeps it stalled. So the aeroplane rolls and yaws continuously, about a path that is close to vertical. The airspeed is low and roughly steady, because a stalled wing makes so much drag that the aeroplane cannot accelerate. A spin needs two ingredients: a stall, and yaw. Remove the yaw and the stall, and it stops.

A spiral dive is not a stall at all. The wings are flying. The aeroplane is in a steep descending turn with the nose low. Because the wing is not stalled, the aeroplane accelerates. The airspeed is high and increasing fast, and the load factor builds as the pilot pulls. The danger is overspeed and structural failure, not loss of lift.

[[diagram:spin-vs-spiral-dive]]
Picture the two side by side. On the left, the spin: wings stalled, airspeed low and steady, rate of descent high. On the right, the spiral dive: wings flying, airspeed high and rising, rate of descent very high. Look at the airspeed indicator, and you know which is which.

In a spin the load factor is low. In a spiral dive it is high and building. A spin kills by hitting the ground still spinning. A spiral dive kills by overspeed and overstress.

Now the standard spin recovery. The flight manual procedure for your type overrides any general one. There are five steps.

First, throttle closed. Power flattens the spin and delays the recovery.

Second, ailerons neutral. Aileron in a spin can deepen it.

Third, full opposite rudder, against the direction of rotation, to stop the yaw.

Fourth, control column centrally forward until the rotation stops. This unstalls the wings.

And fifth, centralise the rudder and ease out of the dive, without pulling hard enough to stall again.

The standard spiral dive recovery has four steps.

First, throttle closed, to stop the speed building.

Second, roll the wings level, with aileron and coordinated rudder.

Third, ease out of the dive gently, keeping the load factor down.

And fourth, restore power once the speed is under control.

Notice the recoveries are nearly opposite. In a spin you push, to unstall. In a spiral dive the wing is not stalled, so pushing just steepens the dive and adds speed. In a spiral dive you roll level first and then pull. In a spin, pulling keeps the wing stalled. The one instrument that tells you which you are in is the airspeed indicator.

Question. The aeroplane is rotating and descending steeply. The airspeed indicator shows a high and rising speed. Spin or spiral dive, and what do you do first?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: a spiral dive. A spinning aeroplane's airspeed stays low, because the stalled wings make so much drag. Close the throttle, roll the wings level with aileron and rudder, then ease out of the dive gently. Do not push, because that is the spin recovery. And do not pull before the wings are level, because that tightens the spiral.

## Bringing it together

It is a gusty afternoon at Jandakot, with the wind 15 kt straight down the runway, gusting 25. You are turning base onto final for runway {24|two four} at 70 kt, in a 1,000 kg aeroplane whose book stall speed is 50 kt.

The bank is 30 degrees, so the load factor is 1.15, and the stall speed has already risen 7 percent, to about 54 kt. The sea breeze has drifted you through the centreline, and your hand wants a little more rudder and a little more back pressure.

The rudder would skid the turn and slow the inner wing. The back pressure would raise the load factor and the angle of attack. Then a lull in the gusts drops the indicated airspeed by 10 kt in a second. Now you are at 60 kt indicated, with a stall speed somewhere in the high fifties, 600 ft above the highway.

The right answers come from this lesson. Keep the turn balanced. Hold the attitude for 70 kt plus a gust margin, and accept the overshoot. Roll out and correct on final, or go around. And if the wing does let go, the first movement is the control column forward. Not the throttle, and not the ailerons.

## Key points

Let's recap.

A wing stalls past the critical angle of attack, about 15 to 18 degrees, at any speed and any attitude. On the way in: low and decreasing airspeed, sloppy controls, the stall warning 5 to 10 kt early, buffet, then the nose or a wing drops.

Aileron to lift a dropped wing deepens its stall. Use rudder, unstall, then roll. The stall speed moves because the lift needed, or the lift available at the critical angle, moves. It goes with the square root of the load factor, and of the weight ratio.

The seven factors and the indicated stall speed. Power on, down. Flap down, down. Gusts and wind shear, up. Manoeuvres, up. Weight, up. Frost and ice, up. Altitude, nil.

Recovery: reduce the angle of attack first. Then full power. Then level the wings. Then climb away with the minimum height loss.

Steep turns, hard pull-outs, the skidding final turn and wind shear stall the wing at unfamiliar attitudes. Attitude is not a stall cue.

A spin is stalled, with a low, steady airspeed. Recover with throttle closed, ailerons neutral, full opposite rudder, and the stick forward. A spiral dive is not stalled, with a high, rising airspeed. Recover with throttle closed, roll level, and ease out. The airspeed indicator tells them apart.

A final exam tip. Expect one calculation, the stall speed in a turn or at a new weight: multiply by the square root. One question on which factor has no effect: altitude, for the indicated stall speed. One on the order of recovery: angle of attack first. And one that tells a spin from a spiral dive by the airspeed. If a question describes a wing drop at the stall, the answer that uses aileron is the wrong one.
