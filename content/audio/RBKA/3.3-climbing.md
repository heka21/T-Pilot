This is RBKA 3.3, Climbing. By the end you will be able to take any factor the syllabus names, weight, power, airspeed, flap, wind, bank or altitude, and say whether it raises or lowers the rate of climb and the angle of climb, and why. There are a few questions and worked examples along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (RBKA 3.3)

The exam wants one thing from you, with seven parts. Say whether the rate of climb and the angle of climb go up or down when each of these changes. The weight. The power. The airspeed, when you fly away from the recommended speed. The flap. A headwind or tailwind, and wind shear. The bank angle. And the altitude, and density altitude.

## Why this matters

On a cool morning at Jandakot, with only you on board, the aeroplane leaps off, and the VSI, the vertical speed indicator, shows a healthy climb. Now take the same aeroplane out of Northam at {2 pm|two in the afternoon} in February. Three people on board, full tanks, and a climbing turn to avoid the town. The VSI may show half that. And the trees at the end of the runway look much closer than they did.

Nothing is wrong with the engine. Every item on the syllabus list has moved against you at once: weight, density height, and bank angle.

This lesson builds on the earlier one that taught rate and angle of climb, Vx and Vy, and converting between them. Here you apply that to each factor the syllabus names, so that you can predict the answer, instead of memorising seven rows of a table. The exam asks, factor by factor, increase or decrease, with a calculation or two on wind.

## The forces in a steady climb

Start with the aeroplane climbing steadily along a straight path, tilted up from the horizon by the climb angle. Four forces act. Lift acts at right angles to the flight path. Drag acts back along the flight path. Thrust acts forward along it. And weight acts straight down, towards the centre of the earth, whatever the flight path is doing.

Because the path is tilted, the weight is no longer at right angles to it. So split the weight into two parts. One part acts across the path, and lift balances it. That part is the weight times the cosine of the climb angle. The other part acts backwards along the path, in the same direction as drag. That part is the weight times the sine of the climb angle.

[[diagram:forces-in-a-climb]]
Picture the aeroplane climbing up a slope from left to right. Thrust points forward up the slope, and drag points back down it. Lift points out of the top of the aeroplane, at right angles to the slope. Weight points straight down, and it is split into two arrows. A big one pressing into the slope, which lift balances. And a small one pulling back down the slope, alongside the drag.

Two results follow, and the first surprises most students.

First: in a steady climb, lift is slightly less than weight. Lift only has to balance the part of the weight across the path. And since the cosine of the climb angle is less than 1, that part is less than the weight. For the shallow climb of a light aeroplane, a few degrees, the difference is tiny. But the direction matters. The wing is not what lifts the aeroplane up the hill.

Second: thrust does the climbing. Along the path, thrust must overcome the drag and the backward part of the weight. Rearrange that, and you get this. The sine of the climb angle equals the thrust minus the drag, divided by the weight.

That surplus of thrust over drag is called the excess thrust. It is what holds the aeroplane on its climbing path. The more excess thrust for each kilogram of aeroplane, the steeper the climb. When there is no excess thrust, thrust only balances drag, and the aeroplane flies level.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. In a climb, lift is greater than weight. It is not. In a steady climb, lift is slightly less than weight, because part of the weight is carried along the path by thrust. Lift exceeds weight only for a moment, while you are pitching up into the climb.

## Excess thrust and excess power: angle and rate

The angle of climb is set by the excess thrust. The rate of climb, how many feet you gain each minute, is the speed along the path, multiplied by the sine of the climb angle. So the rate of climb equals the speed, times the thrust minus the drag, all divided by the weight.

Thrust times speed is power. So the speed, times the thrust minus the drag, is the power available from the engine and propeller, minus the power needed just to fly level at that speed. That is the excess power.

So here is the key idea of this lesson. The angle is set by excess thrust. The rate is set by excess power. Each is divided by the weight.

Both depend on airspeed, and both have a best speed. For a propeller aeroplane, the excess thrust peaks at a fairly low speed, a little above the stall. That is Vx, the best angle of climb speed. The excess power multiplies that surplus by the speed, so it keeps rising for a while beyond Vx, and peaks higher. That is Vy, the best rate of climb speed. Vx is always lower than Vy.

[[diagram:excess-thrust-and-power-vs-airspeed]]
Picture two graphs, one above the other, both with airspeed running left to right. In the top one, the thrust available falls as the speed rises, and the drag is a U-shaped curve. The gap between them, the excess thrust, is widest at a low speed, on the left. That is Vx. In the bottom one, the curves are power available and power required. Their gap, the excess power, is widest further to the right. That is Vy. Multiplying by the speed moves the peak to the right.

[[diagram:climb-rate-vs-airspeed]]
Now picture a single hump: the rate of climb against airspeed, for an example trainer. The top of the hump is Vy, the best rate, at about 75 knots. Now draw a straight line from the bottom left corner, the origin, so that it just touches the hump. Where it touches is Vx, the best angle, at about 60 knots. Vx is always below Vy.

The rest of this lesson uses one simple test for every factor on the list. Does it change the excess thrust, the excess power, or the weight they have to lift? Anything that reduces thrust or power, or adds drag, or adds weight, reduces both the rate and the angle. Wind is the one exception, and it gets its own section.

Question. What is the difference between excess thrust and excess power, and which one sets the rate of climb?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: excess thrust is the thrust minus the drag. Excess power is that surplus multiplied by the airspeed. Excess power, divided by the weight, sets the rate of climb. Excess thrust, divided by the weight, sets the angle.

## Weight

Add weight, say a passenger and full tanks. Two things happen, and both work against the climb.

First, the bigger weight is divided into the same surplus. The climb angle depends on the excess thrust divided by the weight. A larger weight underneath gives a smaller angle, and the same applies to the rate.

Second, the surplus itself shrinks. A heavier aeroplane needs more lift. At a given speed that means a larger angle of attack, which means more induced drag. Drag goes up, so the excess thrust goes down.

So more weight decreases both the rate and the angle of climb. Less weight increases both. That is why the climb you see solo is not the climb you will get with passengers. And it is why the flight manual climb chart has a weight scale.

Let's work one. The flight manual gives a sea-level climb rate of 700 fpm at 900 kg, and 560 fpm at the maximum weight of 1,100 kg. Today you have three people and full fuel, close to maximum weight. What rate should you plan on, and how much have you lost? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 5]]
Your weight today is close to 1,100 kg. So the rate to plan on at sea level is 560 fpm, not the 700 fpm you see when flying light. The loss is 700 minus 560, which is 140 fpm. That is a fifth of the climb.

So plan on about 560 fpm. Then reduce it further for a hot day or a high aerodrome, as you will hear in the section on density altitude.

## Power

Opening the throttle increases the thrust, and with it the excess thrust and the excess power. So more power increases both the rate and the angle of climb. Less power reduces both.

For a normal climb in a fixed-pitch trainer you use full power, or the climb power your flight manual specifies, because the climb is limited by the surplus available. With the throttle partly closed, the surplus is smaller. And below a certain power there is none. The aeroplane will only fly level, or descend.

## Airspeed away from the recommended speed

The flight manual gives you two climb speeds, because each is the top of its own curve. At Vy, the excess power is greatest, so the rate is greatest. At Vx, the excess thrust is greatest, so the angle is greatest. Fly any other speed, and you are below the top of the curve you care about. So flying away from the recommended speed decreases the rate and the angle.

Look at the two sides separately.

Faster than Vy, more of the power goes into overcoming parasite drag, which rises with the square of the speed. Less is left over for climbing, so the rate falls. And because you are also covering more ground, the angle falls further.

Slower than Vx, you are on the back of the drag curve, where induced drag is large, and rising as you slow. The excess thrust shrinks, so the angle falls, and the rate falls too. You are also close to the stall, with the engine working hard at an airspeed too low to cool it properly. There is nothing to gain by being slower than Vx.

Between Vx and Vy, you trade one for the other. Vx gives the best angle, with a slightly lower rate. Vy gives the best rate, with a slightly flatter angle. Which you use depends on whether the problem is distance, such as an obstacle, or time, such as reaching height.

[[diagram:vx-vs-vy]]
Picture two climbs starting from the same point on the runway. The Vx path is steeper. It gains the most height for the distance covered, so it clears a nearby obstacle. The Vy path is flatter, but the aeroplane is going faster, so it gains the most height per minute. Mark where each aeroplane is after one minute, and after two minutes. At each mark, the Vy aeroplane is higher.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Raising the nose and flying slower will steepen the climb. Only down to Vx. Below Vx, the angle gets worse as well as the rate, and the stall gets closer. The steepest climb is at Vx, not at the slowest speed you can fly.

## Flap

Flap increases lift at a given angle of attack. But it increases drag too, and in the climb it is the drag that matters. More drag means less excess thrust and less excess power. So extending flap generally decreases both the rate and the angle of climb.

Some flight manuals specify a small flap setting for take-off from a short or soft strip. That is not because the climb is better with flap. It is because the flapped wing makes enough lift to fly at a lower speed. So the ground roll is shorter, and the aeroplane is airborne sooner. Once you are airborne with a small take-off flap setting, the flight manual tells you the speed and the point at which to raise it.

Full flap, used for landing, adds a lot of drag and seriously reduces the climb. That is why it comes up in stages, early in a go-around.

Question. The flight manual allows a small flap setting for a short-field take-off. Does that mean flap improves the climb?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: no. The small flap setting lets the wing lift off at a lower speed, shortening the ground roll. But the extra drag of the flap still reduces the excess thrust and power. So the rate and angle of climb are generally lower than with the flap up.

## Bank angle

To turn while climbing, you bank, and the lift tilts with the wings. Only the vertical part of the lift now supports the aeroplane. So to keep climbing, the total lift must be larger. The ratio of lift to weight is the load factor. It is about 1.15 in a 30 degree bank, and 1.41 at 45 degrees.

[[widget:bank-angle]]
Picture the aeroplane from behind, with its lift as an arrow out of the top of the wings. Bank it, and the arrow tilts. The vertical part of that arrow must still hold up the weight, so the whole arrow has to grow. 1.15 times as long at 30 degrees, and 1.41 times at 45.

More lift at the same speed means a larger angle of attack, and more induced drag. Drag rises, so the excess thrust and the excess power fall. And some of the lift you are making is now turning the aeroplane, rather than supporting it. So increasing the bank angle decreases both the rate and the angle of climb.

A gently banked climbing turn costs little. A steep climbing turn can use up almost all of the surplus. That is why climbing turns after take-off are kept shallow. And it is why a climbing turn away from rising ground is a poor way to escape it.

## Altitude and density altitude

As you climb, the air gets thinner. A normally aspirated engine is one without a supercharger or turbocharger. It breathes less air with each stroke, and makes less power: roughly 3 percent less for every 1,000 ft of density height. The propeller, biting thinner air, makes less thrust. So the thrust and power available fall, while the drag at a given indicated airspeed stays about the same.

[[diagram:power-loss-with-altitude]]
Picture a line sloping down to the right: the power available at full throttle, as a percentage of sea-level power, against altitude. It falls by roughly 3 percent per 1,000 ft. By about 7,000 to 8,000 ft, a typical trainer can only produce about 75 percent power.

So increasing altitude, or density altitude, decreases both the rate and the angle of climb. It does so gradually as you climb. And it does so at the aerodrome on a hot day. The aeroplane performs according to density altitude. That is the height in the standard atmosphere at which the air is as thin as it is here today. An inland aerodrome a few hundred feet above sea level can have a density altitude of 3,500 ft or more on a 40 degree day. And the climb will be what the flight manual gives for that height, not for the aerodrome's elevation.

The two climb speeds move as the surplus shrinks. As altitude increases, Vx, as an indicated airspeed, increases slightly, and Vy decreases. They keep closing until they meet at the absolute ceiling. There the rate of climb is zero. There is no surplus at any speed, and the aeroplane can only just fly level. Because the climb rate dwindles towards zero near the absolute ceiling, a more practical limit is defined. The service ceiling is the height where the rate of climb has fallen to 100 fpm.

Question. Why does a hot day reduce the rate of climb, even at a sea-level aerodrome?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: hot air is less dense, so the density altitude is above the elevation. The engine makes less power, and the propeller less thrust, in thinner air. So the excess power and the excess thrust are smaller, and both the rate and the angle fall.

## Wind: the angle over the ground, not the rate

Every factor so far changed the excess thrust, the excess power, or the weight. Wind changes none of them. The aeroplane climbs through the air mass, and the whole air mass is moving over the ground. Relative to the air, the climb is the same in any steady wind. The rate of climb is unchanged, and so is the angle through the air. The VSI shows the same reading.

What changes is the path over the ground, which is the one that matters for trees and hills. A headwind reduces the groundspeed, so each minute of climbing covers less ground. The path over the ground is steeper. A tailwind increases the groundspeed, and flattens it.

To put numbers on it, convert the rate into a gradient using the groundspeed. The gradient, in feet per nautical mile, equals the rate of climb in feet per minute, times 60, divided by the groundspeed in knots.

Let's work one. The rate of climb is 500 fpm, at 70 kt true airspeed. Compare still air, a 15 kt headwind, and a 15 kt tailwind. Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 8]]
Still air first. The groundspeed is 70 kt. 500 times 60, divided by 70, is about 429 ft per nautical mile.

With the 15 kt headwind, the groundspeed is 70 minus 15, which is 55 kt. 500 times 60, divided by 55, is about 545 ft per nautical mile.

With the 15 kt tailwind, the groundspeed is 70 plus 15, which is 85 kt. 500 times 60, divided by 85, is about 353 ft per nautical mile.

The VSI shows 500 fpm in all three. But into the headwind, you are about 190 ft higher after each nautical mile than with the tailwind. That is why you take off into wind.

[[diagram:climb-gradient-and-wind]]
Picture three climb paths fanning out from the same starting point, height against distance over the ground. The steepest is into the 15 kt headwind, at 545 ft per nautical mile. The middle one is still air, at 429. And the flattest is with the 15 kt tailwind, at 353. Beside each one, the VSI reads the same 500 feet per minute.

Here is one for you to try, with a stronger wind. An aeroplane climbs at 650 fpm, at 75 kt true airspeed. Work out the gradient over the ground in still air, with a 20 kt headwind, and with a 20 kt tailwind. And say what the VSI shows in each.
[[pause 10]]
650 times 60 is 39,000 in every case.

In still air, the groundspeed is 75 kt. 39,000 divided by 75 is 520 ft per nautical mile.

With the 20 kt headwind, the groundspeed is 75 minus 20, which is 55 kt. 39,000 divided by 55 is 709 ft per nautical mile.

With the 20 kt tailwind, the groundspeed is 75 plus 20, which is 95 kt. 39,000 divided by 95 is 411 ft per nautical mile.

The VSI reads 650 fpm in all three. The headwind adds almost 190 ft per nautical mile, and the tailwind takes away more than 100. The stronger the wind compared with the airspeed, the bigger the effect. So again: 520 in still air, 709 into the headwind, and 411 with the tailwind.

Now, wind shear: when the wind changes suddenly. A steady wind does not change the rate. A wind that changes suddenly does, for as long as the change lasts, because of inertia. That sudden change is wind shear.

Suppose you are climbing out at Vy into a 20 kt headwind, and the headwind suddenly drops to 5 kt. That can happen below a gust front, or when you climb out of the shelter of trees into a different wind layer. The aeroplane's mass keeps it moving over the ground at the same speed, for a moment. With 15 kt less headwind, the air is now flowing past it 15 kt more slowly. So the airspeed drops.

With less airspeed, you have less lift, and the aeroplane is off its best climb speed. So the rate and the angle of climb decrease, until it has re-accelerated. To get back to Vy, you must lower the nose and give up some of the climb. Close to the ground on climb-out, that can be dangerous. A sudden increase in tailwind does the same.

[[diagram:wind-shear-on-climb-out]]
Picture the climb-out from the side. Low down, the aeroplane is climbing at Vy into a 20 kt headwind. Higher up, the headwind drops to 5 kt. As the aeroplane crosses the boundary, inertia holds its groundspeed, so its airspeed falls by the 15 kt of headwind it has lost. The path sags below the planned climb line, flattening out, until the pilot lowers the nose and the aeroplane gets back to Vy.

The opposite shear is a headwind that suddenly increases, or a tailwind that suddenly decreases. That raises the airspeed for a moment, and gives a temporary bonus. The rate and angle increase briefly, until the aeroplane settles back to its trimmed speed.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. It asks what a headwind does to the rate of climb. The answer is no change. The rate is measured relative to the air. A headwind increases the angle over the ground. Only a change in the wind, wind shear, alters the rate, and only for as long as the aeroplane takes to re-accelerate.

Question. Climbing out at Vy, you notice the indicated airspeed suddenly drop by 10 kt, with the attitude unchanged and the power at full. What has probably happened, and what does it do to the climb?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: a sudden loss of headwind, or a gain of tailwind. That is wind shear. Inertia kept the groundspeed, so the airspeed dropped. The rate and angle of climb fall until the aeroplane re-accelerates. And you must lower the nose to recover Vy.

## Bringing it together

Back to Northam in February. It is 40 degrees on the ground. There are three people on board and full tanks, close to maximum weight. And the wind is light and variable, swinging the sock, so the usual runway may have a light tailwind. Run the list.

Weight, near maximum: both rate and angle down. Use the flight manual figure for the heavy aeroplane, not the one you remember from solo flights.

Density altitude, thousands of feet above the elevation: less power and thrust, so both down again.

Wind: if it is a tailwind on climb-out, the rate is unchanged, but the angle over the trees is flatter. So take the other runway, or wait.

Flap: the flight manual take-off setting shortens the roll. But clean it up as the flight manual directs, once you are airborne.

Airspeed: Vx until the trees are below you, then Vy. Never slower.

Bank: keep the turn after take-off shallow, because every degree of bank comes out of a surplus you no longer have.

Done properly, the departure is uneventful. Done the way you would do it on a cool morning solo, it is not.

## Key points

Let's recap.

In a steady climb, lift is slightly less than weight, and thrust does the climbing. The angle is set by excess thrust, and the rate by excess power, each divided by the weight. Then, factor by factor.

Weight up: the rate and the angle both decrease. There is more induced drag, and more weight to lift.

Power up: the rate and the angle both increase.

Airspeed away from Vy or Vx: the rate and the angle both decrease. Slower than Vx is worse in every way.

Flap extended: the rate and the angle generally decrease, because of the extra drag. Take-off flap shortens the ground roll, not the climb.

Wind. A headwind leaves the rate unchanged, and increases the angle over the ground. A tailwind leaves the rate unchanged, and decreases the angle over the ground. Wind shear with a loss of headwind decreases the rate and the angle temporarily. A gain of headwind gives a temporary increase.

Bank increased: the rate and the angle both decrease, because of the load factor and the extra induced drag.

Altitude or density altitude up: the rate and the angle both decrease, because there is less power and thrust. Vx rises and Vy falls, until they meet at the absolute ceiling. The service ceiling is where the climb rate is 100 fpm.

A final exam tip. One rule answers six of the seven factors. Anything that adds weight or drag, or takes away power, reduces both the rate and the angle. That covers the load, flap, bank, height, heat, and a speed away from Vx or Vy. Wind is the odd one out. A steady headwind or tailwind leaves the rate unchanged, and changes only the angle over the ground. And expect one calculation converting feet per minute and groundspeed into feet per nautical mile. Multiply the rate by 60, and divide by the groundspeed.
