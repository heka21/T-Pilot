This is RMTC 2.1, Knowledge of local weather. By the end you will know when and where thunderstorms, low cloud, poor visibility and turbulence are likely over the south-west of Western Australia, and how each one threatens a flight. There are a few questions along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (RMTC 2.1)

The exam wants a working knowledge of the weather where you fly. When and where thunderstorms, low cloud, poor visibility and turbulence are likely. And how each one threatens the safety of a flight.

## Why this matters

The recreational licence keeps you close to home, so the weather that matters is over the Swan coastal plain, the Darling Scarp and the wheatbelt. It follows a pattern you can learn.

A summer morning at Jandakot starts with a gusty easterly tumbling off the hills. The afternoon brings the Fremantle Doctor, and thermals strong enough to throw your head against the canopy. And a February evening at Northam can end under a dry thunderstorm, with a dust-laden gust front.

A winter morning is different. It starts with fog in the Avon valley. It ends with a cold front, low cloud on the Scarp, and showers sweeping in from the west.

The exam asks about these by recall. When are they likely, and what do they do to the aeroplane? The right answer is nearly always the one that avoids, delays or turns back early. This lesson builds each phenomenon from its cause, so that you can predict it rather than memorise it.

## The weather machine over the south-west

Before the individual hazards, you need the big picture. Every one of them follows from where the high pressure systems sit.

Air flows from high pressure towards low pressure. But the Earth turns underneath it. The effect of that rotation is called the Coriolis effect, and it deflects moving air to the left in the Southern Hemisphere. So air spilling out of a high is turned left, and ends up circling it anticlockwise. Air flowing into a low is also turned left, and circles it clockwise. Northern Hemisphere textbooks show the reverse. There is more detail in the lesson on winds.

[[diagram:southern-hemisphere-highs-and-lows]]
Picture two maps side by side, with north at the top. On the left is a high, marked H. Air spirals out of it anticlockwise, so on its northern side, along the top of the circle, the wind blows from east to west. On the right is a low, marked L, with air spiralling into it clockwise. The point to take away: in the Southern Hemisphere, anticlockwise round a high, clockwise round a low, and the northern side of a high has an easterly.

Now put a high, an anticyclone, over the Great Australian Bight. On its northern side, over southern Western Australia, the wind blows from east to west. That is the summer pattern. From about November to March the highs track across the far south, and the wind over Perth is a dry, hot easterly off the interior.

As the land bakes, a trough of low pressure forms along the coast. This is the west coast trough. Its position each day decides whether Perth gets a scorching easterly, or an early sea breeze.

In winter the belt of highs moves north, over the continent. The south-west now lies in the westerlies beneath them. And cold fronts sweep in from the Indian Ocean every few days. Ahead of each front, the wind is a freshening northerly and the cloud lowers. With the front comes a change to the west or south-west, rain, and sometimes thunderstorms. Behind it come cold, showery south-westerlies.

[[diagram:wa-summer-winter-pressure-pattern]]
Picture two simple maps of Australia. In the summer map, a high sits in the Bight, the west coast trough lies along the coast, and Perth is on the high's northern side, in the easterly. In the winter map, the highs have moved north over the continent, and cold fronts sweep in from the Indian Ocean.

Hold those two pictures: summer easterlies, and winter fronts. With them, the timing of every hazard in this lesson falls into place. Fronts are covered in full in the lesson on air masses and fronts.

Question. A high is centred south of Esperance. What wind direction would you expect over Perth, and why?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: easterly. In the Southern Hemisphere, air circulates anticlockwise around a high. So on the high's northern side, where Perth lies, the air flows from east to west.

## Thunderstorms

A thunderstorm needs three things. First, moisture, so that rising air makes cloud. Second, instability, meaning air that, once lifted, stays warmer than its surroundings and keeps rising on its own. And third, a trigger that gives it the first lift. The trigger can be surface heating, a front, a trough, hills that force air upwards, or the line where a sea breeze meets the inland air.

Instability is about buoyancy. Rising air expands and cools. If it stays warmer than the air around it, it is lighter, and it keeps going, like a hot-air balloon. When it reaches its dew point, the vapour condenses into cloud and releases heat. That heat drives it higher still. In unstable air, that feedback can carry the cloud to 40,000 ft.

A single storm cell lives through three stages, and the hazards change with each.

[[diagram:thunderstorm-life-cycle]]
Picture three side views of the same storm cell, left to right, as time passes. On the left is the cumulus stage: a growing tower with updraughts throughout. There is no rain at the ground yet, but the updraughts are strong enough to carry a light aeroplane up thousands of feet.

In the middle is the mature stage. Rain and hail grow too heavy to be held up, and they fall, dragging air with them. Rain evaporating into drier air chills it. So the falling air is heavier than its surroundings, and it accelerates. That is a downdraught, right beside the updraught. Rain at the surface, and lightning, mark this stage. The mature stage begins when rain reaches the ground, and it is the most dangerous one.

On the right is the dissipating stage. The downdraught takes over the whole cell, and chokes off its warm inflow. The rain eases, and the top spreads into an anvil of ice cloud.

A single cell lasts about an hour. But new cells grow on its flanks, so a storm area can last all afternoon.

Now, what a storm does to an aeroplane. When the downdraught hits the ground, it spreads out in every direction, like water poured on a floor. The leading edge of that cold outflow is the gust front. It can run many kilometres ahead of the storm. It arrives as a sudden wind shift, a squall, and often a wall of dust. An intense, concentrated downdraught is a microburst. It produces wind shear that can push an aeroplane on approach into the ground. The encounter itself is covered in the lesson on the significance of observations.

[[diagram:thunderstorm-hazards]]
Picture a side view of a mature storm, a tall cloud with a flat anvil spreading from its top. Beneath it, a microburst strikes the ground and spreads out ahead of the storm as a gust front. The point: the danger reaches well outside the cloud. Here is the full list of hazards. Severe turbulence, inside and near the cell, enough to break an aeroplane. Hail, which can fall from the anvil well outside the visible rain. Lightning. Heavy rain, which removes the visibility in seconds. Icing in the cloud, above the freezing level. And the microburst and gust front, with their wind shear on take-off and landing.

For a VFR pilot, the only safe response is to stay well clear. Stay at least 10 nm from a mature cell, and more from a severe one. Never fly under the anvil. And never try to fly through a line of storms. If storms are forecast at your destination for your arrival time, plan to be on the ground early, or go somewhere else.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. The danger is not confined to the cloud you can see. The gust front can reach the aerodrome before the rain. Hail falls from the anvil into clear air. And the clear air under the anvil is where the downdraughts are. So "stay clear of the cloud" is not enough. Stay 10 nm from the cell.

Now, when and where in Western Australia. Each storm type has its own trigger, and the trigger tells you the time of day and the time of year. There are three types.

The first is summer afternoon storms, inland and over the Scarp. The trigger is surface heating. Moist air drawn down from the tropics in a trough meets the afternoon heat over the wheatbelt and the goldfields. Storms build from about midday, peak in the late afternoon, and die away after dark, when the heating stops.

These storms have a twist. The inland air is dry near the ground, so the dew point is far below the temperature, and the cloud base is high, often 8,000 to 10,000 ft. Rain falling from that high base evaporates before it lands. That is why these storms are often dry: lightning, gust fronts and dust, with little rain. That same evaporation chills the downdraught. So dry storms produce some of the strongest microbursts. And their lightning starts many of the State's bushfires.

The second type is frontal storms in the south-west. The trigger is the lifting along a cold front, or along the trough ahead of it. These come mostly in late spring and autumn, and with the more vigorous winter cold fronts along the coast. They do not care about the time of day. A front can bring a line of storms through Perth at dawn.

The third type is tropical storms in the north. In the wet season, October to April, the Kimberley and the Pilbara get heavy afternoon and evening storms, often severe. Tropical cyclones come in the same months.

Question. Why are summer storms over the wheatbelt often dry, and why does that make them more dangerous at the surface, not less?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the air beneath the cloud is dry, so the cloud base is high, and the rain evaporates on its way down. Evaporation cools the falling air, making it colder and heavier. So the downdraught accelerates, and strikes the ground as a strong microburst and gust front, often carrying dust. And there is little rain to warn you.

## Low cloud

Air always holds some water vapour. The dew point is the temperature to which the air would have to be cooled, with no change in its moisture, for that vapour to start condensing. The gap between the air temperature and the dew point is the dew point spread. It tells you how close the air is to making cloud. A spread of 15 degrees is dry air. A spread of 1 degree is air on the edge of cloud or fog.

Cloud forms when something cools the air to its dew point. Low cloud forms when that cooling happens close to the ground. There are four ways it happens near Perth.

The first way is lifting over hills. Moist air pushed up a slope expands and cools. Behind a winter front, the moist south-westerly rides up the Darling Scarp. It can reach its dew point a few hundred feet above the hills, capping them in what is called orographic cloud, while the plain stays clear. That is exactly where a flight from Jandakot to Northam has to cross.

[[diagram:orographic-cloud-on-the-scarp]]
Picture a cross-section, with the coast on the left and the wheatbelt on the right. A moist south-westerly crosses the flat coastal plain from the left. Then it meets the face of the Darling Scarp, roughly 1,000 ft high, and is forced up it. Rising, the air expands and cools. A few hundred feet above the hills it reaches its dew point. So cloud sits on the ridges, while the plain behind stays clear.

The second way is ahead of a front. Warm, moist air is lifted over the colder air, and the base lowers through the day, from high cloud to low cloud and rain.

The third way is sea air moving inland. The sea breeze carries moist ocean air over the land. There it can cool further in the evening, and form low stratus along the coast that spreads inland overnight.

And the fourth way is the ground cooling overnight under clear skies. That gives fog, or very low stratus, and it has its own section next.

Low stratus is common on the south-west coast on winter mornings, and in the humid north during the wet season.

Let's work one: estimating the cumulus base from the temperature and the dew point. A mid-morning observation at Northam shows a temperature of 18 degrees and a dew point of 10 degrees. Where would you expect the base of the cumulus? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
Step one, the dew point spread. 18 minus 10 is 8 degrees.

Step two, the rule of thumb. Rising air loses about 1 degree of spread for every 400 ft. So 8 times 400 is 3,200 ft above the ground.

So when the heating sets off thermals, expect the cumulus base at roughly 3,200 ft above Northam. The rule only works for cumulus, cloud formed by air rising from the surface, not for stratus lowered by a front. And it is an estimate. The answer again: about 3,200 ft.

Now, why low cloud kills VFR pilots. For a VFR pilot, low cloud attacks two rules at once. You must stay a set distance from cloud, or at least clear of it, to remain in visual meteorological conditions. Those minima are in the lesson on conditions of flight. And you must stay above the minimum height over the ground. As the base lowers, the space between those two limits shrinks.

Over rising ground, that squeeze is lethal, because the terrain comes up to meet the cloud. Picture the winter flight home from Northam after a front. Ahead, the Scarp is capped, with the base a few hundred feet above the ridges. Each mile you press on, the ground rises and the cloud does not. By the time you admit there is no gap, there may be no room to turn either.

[[diagram:low-cloud-and-rising-terrain]]
Picture a side view. A small aeroplane flies from left to right, inland. Above it, the cloud base slopes down ahead. Below it, the ground slopes up towards hills. The gap between cloud and terrain narrows like a funnel, until there is no room to stay clear of cloud and above the minimum height. The lesson of the picture: turn while there is still room to turn.

So the response is to turn back early, while there is still room. Do not press on. Scud running, flying low under low cloud into rising ground, is one of the main causes of fatal VFR accidents. And many of those pilots were within a few minutes of clear air behind them.

Question. A moist south-westerly is blowing behind a winter front. Where are you most likely to find low cloud between Perth and York, and why there?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: on the Darling Scarp, and the hills just east of it. The wind forces the moist air up the slope. It expands and cools as it rises, and reaches its dew point a few hundred feet above the hills. Meanwhile the coastal plain can stay clear.

## Poor visibility

Start with radiation fog, and how it forms. On a still, clear winter night, the ground radiates its heat into space. With no cloud to send that heat back, the ground cools quickly, and it cools the thin layer of air resting on it. If that air is moist enough, meaning a small dew point spread at sunset, it reaches its dew point before dawn. The vapour condenses into droplets so small they stay suspended. That is radiation fog.

It needs four conditions together. First, clear skies, so the ground loses heat freely. Second, moist air, so only a little cooling brings it to the dew point. A day of rain the day before helps. Third, a light wind, about 2 to 5 kt. A light breeze stirs the cooled air through a layer a few hundred feet deep. Dead calm gives dew or frost on the grass, but only a shallow ground fog. And a wind much over 10 kt mixes the chilled air with warmer air above, and stops the fog forming at all, sometimes giving low stratus instead. And fourth, long nights, so there is time to cool. Winter, May to September in the south-west, is fog season.

[[diagram:temperature-dew-point-overnight]]
Picture a graph across the top, running from sunset on the left to mid-morning on the right. The temperature line falls steadily all night. The dew point line stays almost level. So the gap narrows, and around dawn the two lines meet, and fog forms. Underneath are three small panels for the wind: calm, light, and strong. Only the light wind, about 2 to 5 kt, gives a fog layer.

Cold air is heavier than warm air. So on a sloping landscape, the chilled air drains downhill overnight and pools in the lowest ground. That is why fog lies in the river valleys: the Avon valley around Northam and York, the Swan valley, and low-lying airfields along the coast. A hilltop farm strip can be clear while the valley aerodrome ten miles away is in fog.

That night-time drainage down a slope is a katabatic wind. Its daytime opposite, warmed air creeping up a sunlit slope, is an anabatic wind. Both are in the lesson on winds.

[[diagram:radiation-fog-overnight]]
Picture four panels in a row, from evening to mid-morning. Evening: a clear sky, calm, humid air. Overnight: the ground radiates its heat away, and the air touching it cools to its dew point. Dawn: fog lies in the river valleys and along the coast. Mid-morning: the sun heats the ground and the fog clears.

After sunrise, the sun heats the ground through the fog, and the droplets evaporate from below. The fog usually clears by mid-morning, often lifting first into low stratus. Because the coldest time is just after sunrise, it often thickens briefly after first light, before it improves.

Question. The evening forecast for Northam is clear skies and a 20 kt south-westerly overnight, with high humidity. Why is radiation fog unlikely, despite the humidity?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the wind is too strong. It mixes the air cooled at the ground with warmer air above. So no layer stays in contact with the cold ground long enough to reach its dew point. Radiation fog needs a light wind of a few knots, not 20 kt.

Visibility goes in four other ways. Smoke, from bushfires and controlled burns in late summer and autumn, spreads over hundreds of kilometres, and it is worst looking into the sun. Dust is raised by strong winds over the dry interior and the wheatbelt, ahead of fronts and in thunderstorm gust fronts. It can take the visibility to a few hundred metres in minutes. Rain and showers with fronts can reduce visibility below the visual minima quickly. And post-frontal showers come in bands that can close behind you. Finally, haze in the warm, humid north takes the edge off the horizon over long distances.

Why is poor visibility dangerous? It hides terrain, obstacles and other aircraft until late. Worse, it can take away the horizon. And the inner ear cannot tell a gentle descending turn from level flight. That is how a pilot in haze, over the featureless wheatbelt, enters a spiral without knowing it. You'll find more in the lesson on vision, spatial disorientation and illusions.

The visual minimum of 5,000 m flight visibility is a legal floor. It is not a comfortable working figure for a new pilot. A horizon that is merging into haze, or a visibility that is falling as you fly, is a sign to stay on the ground, or turn back.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. A forecast of clear skies is not a forecast of good visibility. The clear night is the one that produces fog. And a cloudless February sky can be full of smoke. Read the visibility and the weather in a forecast, not just the cloud.

## Turbulence

Turbulence is air moving irregularly, up, down and sideways, jolting the aeroplane about. Each kind has a different cause, and the cause tells you where and when to expect it.

The first kind is thermal turbulence. Bare paddocks, bitumen and dark soil heat faster than crops, bush or water. The air over a hot patch warms, becomes lighter, and breaks away as a rising bubble, a thermal. The air between thermals sinks to replace it. Fly through that pattern and you are lifted, dropped, and lifted again. That is thermal, or convective, turbulence.

It follows the sun. It starts mid-morning, is worst in the early to mid afternoon, and dies away in the evening. And it is worst at low level over bare ground. The circuit at Northam or Cunderdin on a January afternoon is hard work. If the air is moist enough, each thermal is capped by a flat-based cumulus, and the turbulence reaches up to that base.

The second kind is mechanical turbulence, and its big brother, the rotor off the Scarp. Wind blowing over an obstruction goes over and around it, and tumbles into eddies on the far side. That is mechanical turbulence. It is stronger the stronger the wind, and the bigger the obstacle. A 20 kt crosswind across a row of hangars or a tree line sends eddies over the runway exactly where you flare.

On the larger scale, the obstacle is the Darling Scarp. On a summer morning, with the high in the Bight, a strong easterly crosses the plateau. It pours down the face of the Scarp, roughly 1,000 ft. And on the lee side, the Perth side, it rolls into a rotor. A rotor is a horizontal eddy of tumbling air, with gusts and sudden sink close to the ground. The easterly is often strongest overnight and early in the morning. So a dawn departure from Serpentine or Jandakot towards the hills can be rough below 2,000 ft.

[[diagram:rotor-in-the-lee-of-the-scarp]]
Picture a cross-section near Perth, with west on the left. A strong easterly blows from the right, across the plateau, over the edge of the Scarp, and down onto the coastal plain. There it rolls into a rotor of tumbling air, on the lee side. The point: the rough air is on the downwind side, and the same happens behind hangars, buildings and trees.

The third kind is frontal turbulence. It comes at the wind change as a front passes. Two air masses moving in different directions meet, and the boundary between them is rough.

The fourth kind is wind shear turbulence. It occurs wherever the wind changes sharply over a short distance. One place is the top of a nocturnal inversion, where calm, chilled air at the surface lies under wind that keeps blowing a few hundred feet up. Another is the sea breeze front.

Clear air turbulence, at high levels near jet streams, is not usually a concern for the recreational licence. Wake turbulence has its own lesson.

Now the Fremantle Doctor. On a sunny morning, land and sea get the same sunshine, but the land heats far faster. The sea spreads its heat through a deep, mixing layer, and spends some of it on evaporation. The land concentrates it in the top few centimetres. So by late morning, the air over the coastal plain is several degrees warmer than the air over the ocean.

The warm column over the land expands upwards. So aloft, the pressure over the land is higher than over the sea, and air flows out to sea up there. That leaves less air above the land. So the surface pressure over the land falls below the pressure over the sea. And the surface air flows from sea to land. That is the sea breeze. The returning air sinks over the sea, completing a circulation a few thousand feet deep.

In Perth the sea breeze is the Fremantle Doctor, a cool south-westerly. It typically reaches the coast around noon to early afternoon, and pushes inland through the afternoon. A strong easterly gradient wind holds it offshore. Then the Doctor arrives late, or not at all, and those are the hottest days.

[[diagram:sea-breeze-front]]
Picture a cross-section of the west coast, with the sea on the left. Warm air rises over the land, and cool sea air flows onshore underneath it, in a loop that returns seaward high up. Where the cool air meets the warm inland air, it pushes in as a shallow wedge. That wedge is the sea breeze front.

As the front passes, the wind swings from easterly to south-westerly within minutes. The temperature drops, and the humidity rises. At the front there is shear and turbulence, and in moist air a line of cumulus. For you, that means a midday runway change at Jandakot. It means a crosswind or a tailwind if you are caught on the old runway. And it means a rough patch as you fly through the front.

At night the pattern reverses. The land cools below the sea, and a weaker offshore land breeze, about 5 to 10 kt, blows from land to sea. It is strongest around dawn.

What does turbulence do? It makes the aeroplane hard to control at low level, upsets the instruments, and causes airsickness. Severe turbulence can overstress the airframe. And a sharp gust can stall a wing flying near the critical angle.

In rough air, slow to the manoeuvring speed, or the turbulence speed in the POH, so that a gust stalls the wing before it can overstress it. Hold the attitude, rather than chasing height and airspeed. Keep the seat belts tight. And avoid the lee of hills in strong winds. Better still, fly before the thermals start, or after they die.

Question. On a summer morning the wind at Jandakot is a strong easterly. Where would you expect the roughest air on a departure towards the hills, and why?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: low down on the coastal plain, close to the foot of the Scarp. The easterly pours down the Scarp, and rolls into rotor and gusts on its lee side, the western side. That is the side Jandakot and Serpentine are on. So the worst turbulence is below about 2,000 ft, near the hills.

## Bringing it together

A Saturday in late January: Jandakot to Northam and back. The forecast shows a high in the Bight, a trough inland, and storms possible east of the Scarp in the afternoon.

At {0730|oh seven thirty}, the easterly at Jandakot is 18 kt. The climb-out towards the hills is bumpy below 2,000 ft. That is rotor, in the lee of the Scarp.

By {1100|eleven hundred}, the Northam paddocks are hot. Small cumulus are popping up, and the circuit is rough with thermals.

At {1230|twelve thirty}, high-based towers are building to the north-east, with rain shafts that do not reach the ground. Those are the dry storms. You leave now, rather than at {1500|fifteen hundred}, because a gust front can arrive before the storm does.

Approaching the Scarp westbound, a line of small cumulus marks the sea breeze front. The air is rough as you descend through it. And Jandakot has changed to runway {24|two four}, in a fresh south-westerly. Every phenomenon arrived on the schedule its cause predicted.

Question. Why do you leave Northam early, rather than waiting for the storms to pass?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the afternoon storms are driven by heating. So they will build further, not pass, until evening. Their gust fronts and microbursts can reach well ahead of the cells. So the safe choice is to be on the ground at home before they mature, not to try to time a gap.

## Key points

Let's recap.

Thunderstorms need moisture, instability and a trigger. In Western Australia there are three kinds. Summer afternoon storms inland and on the Scarp are often dry and high-based, with strong microbursts. Frontal storms come in the south-west. And tropical storms come in the north, from October to April. The hazards are turbulence, hail, lightning, heavy rain, icing, and the microburst and gust front. Stay at least 10 nm from a mature cell, and never fly under the anvil.

Low cloud forms when moist air is cooled to its dew point near the ground: over the Scarp, ahead of fronts, as sea air moves inland, and overnight. Between a lowering base and rising ground, turn back early.

Poor visibility: radiation fog forms on clear, humid winter nights with a light wind. It pools in valleys like the Avon, and clears by mid-morning. Smoke, dust, rain and haze take visibility too. Losing the horizon brings disorientation. And 5,000 m is a floor, not a target.

Turbulence: thermal turbulence on hot afternoons over bare ground. Mechanical turbulence and rotor downwind of obstacles, which means the Perth side of the Scarp in strong easterlies. Frontal turbulence at the wind change. And shear at inversions and at the sea breeze front. Slow to the turbulence speed, hold the attitude, and avoid the lee of hills.

And the big picture. In the Southern Hemisphere, air goes anticlockwise round highs and clockwise round lows. Summer brings easterlies and the Fremantle Doctor. Winter brings westerlies and cold fronts.

A final exam tip. Think "when and where" for each phenomenon. Thunderstorms come with heating and fronts. Fog comes on clear, calm, humid mornings. Low cloud comes when moist air is cooled or lifted. Thermal turbulence comes on hot afternoons, and mechanical turbulence downwind of hills. The safety response for all of them is to avoid, delay or turn back early. Have 10 nm from a mature thunderstorm by heart. And remember that in the Southern Hemisphere, the wind goes anticlockwise round a high.
