This is RMTC 2.3, Significance of observations. By the end you will be able to read the signs of turbulence, thermals, dust devils, wind gradient and wind shear, in the forecast and in the sky, and work out what each one does to the way the aeroplane flies. There are a few questions and worked examples along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (RMTC 2.3)

The exam wants you to do two things. First, recognise the signs of turbulence, thermals and dust devils, and of wind gradient and wind shear. Those signs come from the forecast, and from what you and other pilots see. And second, describe what each one does to the way the aeroplane flies.

## Why this matters

The forecast tells you what the air is likely to do. The sky, the ground and the aeroplane tell you what it is doing.

Think of a line of cumulus over the Scarp. A column of dust spinning across a paddock at Cunderdin. Smoke from a burn-off lying flat, and then torn apart. The whitecaps on the Swan as you turn final at Jandakot. Each one is a measurement, if you know how to read it.

Reading them matters most in the last few hundred feet of an approach, and the first few hundred feet after take-off. There the aeroplane is slow, close to the stall and close to the ground. A sudden loss of airspeed has nowhere to go.

The exam tests the signs by recall, and the effects by reasoning. CASA names "the effect of wind shear on aircraft performance" as one of the weak areas in the RPLA exam. So this lesson builds both from the physics. That way you can work out the effect of any shear, rather than memorise a list.

## Two kinds of evidence

Every sign in this lesson comes from one of two places.

The first is the forecast and the reports. They give you warning before you leave the ground. You learnt to decode them in the lesson on forecasts and reports. Here you learn which groups point to rough air and shear.

The second is your own observations, and those of other pilots. They tell you what is happening now. Clouds, dust, smoke, birds, water and the wind sock are all instruments. They show you the motion of air you cannot otherwise see. And a report from a preceding aircraft, such as "wind shear on final, plus or minus 10 kt", is the most direct evidence of all.

A good pilot uses the forecast to know what to look for, and the view out of the window to confirm it.

## Signs of turbulence

Start with the forecast. Several products point to turbulence before you have seen a cloud. There are five to look for.

First, turbulence groups in the GAF, giving moderate or severe turbulence, and the heights it extends between.

Second, SIGMETs for severe turbulence.

Third, strong surface winds in the TAF, and especially a gust group of 10 kt or more above the mean wind. Gusts are the turbulence of the surface wind itself. A wind of 15 kt gusting 27 kt means the air near the ground is tumbling over obstacles hard enough to change speed by 12 kt in seconds.

Fourth, forecasts of thunderstorms or a front.

And fifth, a large difference between the surface wind and the wind at 2,000 ft or 5,000 ft in the GPWT, the grid point wind and temperature chart. A big change in wind over a short height is shear, and shear makes turbulence.

Now thermals, and the clouds they build. You met the cause of thermals in the lesson on local weather. Uneven heating of the ground sends bubbles of warm air upwards, with sinking air between them. You cannot see a dry thermal, but you can see what it does.

The clearest sign is cumulus cloud. A thermal rises, and cools as it expands. At the height where it reaches its dew point, the vapour condenses. All the thermals in an area start with roughly the same temperature and moisture. So they reach their dew point at roughly the same height. That is why a field of cumulus has flat bases, all at one level.

Each cloud is the visible top of a column of rising air. And the blue gaps between them are where the air is sinking.

The growing tops show how vigorously the air is still rising. Small, flat cumulus, with tops close to the base, mean weak thermals. Tall, bubbling cumulus, with ragged, rapidly growing tops, mean strong thermals, and a rough ride under and inside them. Cumulus is the picture of an unstable atmosphere. And towering cumulus is a thunderstorm in its first stage.

[[diagram:visible-signs-of-thermals]]
Picture a hot afternoon over bare ground. Bubbles of warm air rise as thermals, and each one builds a cumulus cloud on top, all with flat bases at the same level and growing tops. Between the thermals, the air sinks. Down on the bare ground, a dust devil spins. Elsewhere, smoke rises steadily and then breaks up, and birds soar in circles. Every one of those marks rising air, with sinking air in between.

There are three other visible signs of thermals. The first is smoke, from a chimney or a burn-off, that rises steadily, then breaks up and spreads at a certain height. The rise shows the air is unstable. The break-up shows the turbulence at the top of the rising layer.

The second is birds soaring in circles without flapping, eagles and kites over the wheatbelt in particular. They are riding a thermal, and they mark it for you.

And the third is gliders, doing the same thing. On a summer day near the gliding fields at Narrogin or Cunderdin, circling gliders tell you exactly where the lift is. And they tell you there is sink around it.

Now dust devils, which are thermals you can see. On a hot afternoon with little wind, the ground over a bare paddock or a dry runway strip can heat the air touching it far above the temperature of the air just above. That layer is extremely unstable. It is lighter than the air on top of it, and it is waiting for a reason to rise.

When it finally breaks away at one point, air rushes in along the ground from all sides to replace it. Any slight rotation in that inflow, from a gust or from the shape of the ground, is concentrated as the air converges. It is the same way a figure skater spins faster when she pulls her arms in. The result is a narrow, fast-spinning column of rising air that picks up dust. That is a dust devil.

[[diagram:dust-devil-formation]]
Picture three stages over a bare paddock, left to right. In the first, the ground heats a thin layer of air far above the air just above it. In the second, that layer breaks away at one point, and air rushes in along the ground from all sides. In the third, the converging inflow spins up into a narrow, fast-spinning column of rising air, carrying dust, with sharp changes of wind around it.

So a dust devil is a visible thermal, and an unusually strong and narrow one. It marks a strong updraught in a column only a few metres to tens of metres across. Around it there are sharp changes in wind speed and direction, because the air is spinning. For a light aeroplane at low level, flying through one means a sudden gust from one side, and then from the other. That is enough to roll the aeroplane, or swing it on the ground.

Dust devils are common inland and on dry aerodromes in summer. They need hot ground and light wind. A fresh wind mixes the hot surface layer away before it can build up. Avoid them on take-off and landing. And treat one as a sign that the air over that area is very unstable.

Question. Why do dust devils appear on hot afternoons with light wind, but rarely when the wind is fresh?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: they need a very hot, very unstable layer of air sitting on the ground until it breaks away. A light wind lets that layer build up. A fresh wind mixes it with the cooler air above, before it can become unstable enough to form a spinning column.

Some signs mark mechanical turbulence rather than thermals. Start with lenticular clouds, which are lens-shaped. They sit smooth and stationary downwind of hills and ranges, even in a strong wind. They form in the crests of mountain waves. Those are the standing waves that a strong wind makes as it flows over a ridge and oscillates up and down on the lee side. Beneath them, close to the ground, ragged rotor clouds mark rolling eddies of very rough air.

[[diagram:mountain-wave-lenticular-rotor]]
Picture a side view, with a strong wind blowing from left to right across a ridge. A cap cloud sits on the crest. Downwind, on the right, the air sinks steeply down the lee slope, then rises and sinks again in a standing wave. Smooth, lens-shaped lenticular clouds sit still at the wave crests. Under each crest, at about ridge height, a rotor of severe turbulence turns, with ragged roll cloud. The sink in the wave can exceed a light aeroplane's climb. The advice drawn with it: cross ridges 2,000 ft or more above them, at 45 degrees, and stay upwind of the rotor.

Even without cloud, a strong wind across a ridge produces turbulence in its lee. In Western Australia, that means the Perth side of the Darling Scarp in a strong easterly.

[[diagram:rotor-in-the-lee-of-the-scarp]]
Picture a cross-section near Perth, with west on the left. A strong easterly blows from the right, across the plateau and over the edge of the Scarp, and down onto the coastal plain. There it rolls into a rotor of tumbling air, on the lee side, the western side. The same happens, on a smaller scale, behind hangars, buildings and trees.

Next, the surface. Ragged, fast-moving low cloud, and whitecaps on water, show a strong, gusty surface wind. With it comes mechanical turbulence close to the ground. And a wind sock standing straight out and swinging back and forth is the aerodrome's own gust indicator.

Last, the signs of downdraughts. Virga is rain falling from a cloud and evaporating before it reaches the ground. Virga, and the dark base of a cumulonimbus, show downdraughts and gust fronts. Evaporating rain cools the air. The cooled air sinks, hits the ground, and spreads out. Virga under a high-based inland storm is the warning of a dry microburst.

So what does turbulence do to the aeroplane? It causes sudden changes in altitude, attitude and airspeed, and it makes it hard to hold a heading and a height. Each up-gust changes the angle of attack, and therefore the load factor. So turbulence can stall the wing if you are slow, or overstress the airframe if you are fast. That trade-off is why the POH gives a turbulence penetration speed. It is covered in the lesson on stalling, spinning and spiral dives. On approach, turbulence means a rough ride, and a risk of a hard or long landing.

Thermals have a particular effect on the approach. They produce a gain of height over the updraught, and a loss in the sinking air around it. Final approach over a hot bare paddock can be sinking air followed by lift over the runway, or the reverse. The aeroplane balloons over the threshold when you thought you were on the path, or it sinks short of it. Expect it, correct early with power and attitude, and be ready to go around.

Question. Two fields of cumulus have the same base. In one, the tops are flat and a few hundred feet deep. In the other, they are tall, with ragged, rapidly growing tops. Which promises the rougher ride, and why?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the tall, ragged cumulus. The depth and growth of the tops show how strongly the air is still rising. So they mark stronger thermals, and more vigorous updraughts and downdraughts beneath and inside them.

## Wind gradient

First, why the wind slows near the ground. Air moving over the ground is dragged back by friction, against the surface, the trees and the buildings. The air closest to the ground is slowed the most. The air a little higher is slowed less. And above a few hundred to a couple of thousand feet, the wind blows at its full gradient speed. That steady change of wind speed with height, caused by friction, is the wind gradient.

Friction also lets the surface wind turn across the isobars. So the wind a few hundred feet up is stronger, and often from a slightly different direction, than the wind at the surface. Over rough ground and in strong winds, the gradient is steeper. Over smooth water it is gentler.

Now, why a change of wind changes the airspeed. To see what the gradient does to an aeroplane, you need one idea that applies to all wind shear. Here it is. The aeroplane's inertia keeps its speed over the ground for a moment, and the airspeed changes instead.

[[diagram:wind-shear-inertia]]
Picture two moments on approach, with the aeroplane flying from left to right. In the first, the airspeed is 70 kt, into a 25 kt headwind. So the groundspeed is 70 minus 25, which is 45 kt. Now the headwind suddenly falls to 10 kt. The aeroplane is a tonne of metal with momentum, so it cannot instantly change its groundspeed. In the second moment it is still doing 45 kt over the ground. But now only 10 kt of wind is coming the other way. So its airspeed is 45 plus 10, which is 55 kt. The wing feels 55 kt, not 70 kt, and makes lift accordingly. 15 kt has gone in a moment.

So there are two cases. A decreasing headwind, or an increasing tailwind, makes the airspeed fall. The aeroplane loses lift, sinks, and drops below the path. That is performance-decreasing shear. An increasing headwind, or a decreasing tailwind, makes the airspeed rise. The aeroplane gains lift, floats, and rises above the path. That is performance-increasing shear.

Given time, the aeroplane settles back to its trimmed airspeed. But that takes seconds, and close to the ground you may not have them.

Now the gradient on approach and after take-off. On approach into a strong headwind, you descend through the gradient. So the headwind decreases as you go down. That is a steady, performance-decreasing shear. The airspeed tends to decay, the rate of descent increases, and the aeroplane undershoots unless you add power.

[[diagram:wind-gradient-on-approach]]
Picture two halves. On the left, the wind gradient: the headwind is strongest a few hundred feet up and weakest at the surface, because friction slows the wind near the ground. On the right, an aeroplane on final into that strong headwind. As it descends, the headwind decreases, the airspeed decays, and the aeroplane sinks below its intended path. It undershoots, unless the pilot adds power.

Let's work one: the airspeed lost in a wind gradient on final. It is a windy winter afternoon. You are on final at 70 kt, into a headwind of 25 kt at 500 ft. The surface wind is 10 kt, straight down the runway. The flap-down stall speed is 48 kt. If inertia held your groundspeed, what would the airspeed be near the ground, and what margin would be left above the stall? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
Step one, the groundspeed at 500 ft. 70 minus 25 is 45 kt.

Step two, the loss of headwind from 500 ft to the surface. 25 minus 10 is 15 kt.

Step three. If inertia held the 45 kt groundspeed through the descent, the airspeed near the ground would be 45 plus 10, which is 55 kt.

Step four, the margin above the stall. At 500 ft, it is 70 minus 48, which is 22 kt. Near the ground, it is 55 minus 48, which is only 7 kt.

In practice, the gradient is gradual, and the aeroplane partly recovers its speed as it goes. But the trend is real. In a strong wind, the airspeed bleeds away in the last few hundred feet. That is why you carry extra speed, and expect to need power in the flare. So the answer again: about 55 kt near the ground, with a margin of only 7 kt.

After take-off into a headwind, you climb through the gradient the other way. The headwind increases as you climb. That gives a brief airspeed gain, and a better climb angle. A tailwind gradient does the opposite. The tailwind increases with height, the airspeed falls as you climb, and the climb gradient is reduced. That is one more reason never to take off downwind towards obstacles.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. "Loss of headwind means loss of airspeed" is true only because of inertia, and only for a moment. Do not reason from groundspeed. The trap answer says that a decreasing headwind on approach increases the groundspeed, and so the aeroplane floats. But the wing flies on airspeed. The airspeed falls, and the aeroplane sinks.

## Wind shear

Wind shear is any sudden change in wind speed or direction over a short distance, horizontally or vertically. The wind gradient is a gentle, steady version of it. The dangerous versions are sudden. There are five places to expect it.

The first is low-level inversions and nocturnal jets. On a clear night, the ground cools the air touching it. A layer of cold, dense air forms near the surface, under warmer air. That is an inversion. The cold layer is cut off from the wind above. So at the surface it is calm, while a few hundred feet up the wind may be 20 or 30 kt. Sometimes it is even stronger than the gradient wind, as a nocturnal low-level jet. Climbing or descending through the top of the inversion gives a sharp change. Summer dawns on the coastal plain, with a strong easterly above a calm surface, are the classic case.

[[diagram:nocturnal-inversion-low-level-jet]]
Picture a cross-section of the lowest few hundred feet, at dawn on the coastal plain. Along the ground lies a layer of cold, calm air. At its top, a few hundred feet up, the wind jumps to 20 or 30 kt: the low-level jet, here a strong easterly. An aeroplane climbing out to the east passes through the top of the inversion, and meets a sudden headwind. Off to one side, a small temperature line shows the air getting warmer with height through the inversion, and then cooler above it.

The second place is sea breeze fronts, where the cool sea air meets the inland air. Flying through the front, or approaching an aerodrome as it passes, you can go from an easterly to a south-westerly in a few hundred feet, or a few hundred metres. And there is turbulence at the boundary.

[[diagram:sea-breeze-front]]
Picture a cross-section of the west coast, with the sea on the left. Warm air rises over the land, and cool sea air flows onshore underneath it, in a loop that returns seaward high up. Where the cool air meets the warm inland air, it pushes in as a shallow wedge. The leading edge of that wedge is the sea breeze front, and that is where the shear and turbulence are.

The third place is fronts, at the wind change as the front passes. A winter cold front can swing the wind from a north-westerly to a strong south-westerly within minutes.

The fourth is terrain and buildings. The wind curls and shears around hills, hangars and tree lines beside the runway. So the last 50 ft of an approach in a strong crosswind can bring sudden changes of both speed and direction.

And the fifth is thunderstorms. The gust front and the microburst beneath a storm are the most dangerous shear of all. They combine a large change in horizontal wind with a strong downdraught, close to the ground.

Now the microburst. A microburst is an intense downdraught, typically less than a few kilometres across. It hits the ground and spreads out in all directions.

[[diagram:microburst-encounter]]
Picture a side view of a storm, with a strong downdraught falling from its base, hitting the ground, and spreading out to both sides. An aeroplane on approach flies into it. Follow the aeroplane through, in three stages.

Entering the outflow, it meets a headwind. That is performance-increasing shear, so the airspeed and lift rise, and it floats above the path. A pilot who reacts by reducing power has made the next part worse.

In the centre, it meets the downdraught, which pushes it down.

On the far side, the outflow is now a tailwind. That is performance-decreasing shear, so the airspeed and lift fall. And that happens just as the downdraught has pushed the aeroplane low, and the ground is close. The total change in wind across the microburst can exceed what a light aeroplane can out-climb. So remember the order: headwind, downdraught, tailwind.

The only defence is avoidance. Do not take off or land through a thunderstorm gust front, or beneath a storm, or under virga from a high-based inland storm.

So what does shear do to the aeroplane? There is an abrupt change in indicated airspeed, in lift, and in rate of descent. And there is a change in drift, if the wind direction changes. A sudden loss of headwind on final can drop the airspeed towards the stall, and the aeroplane below the glidepath. A sudden tailwind on take-off lengthens the ground run, and reduces the climb. Because the aeroplane's inertia keeps its groundspeed roughly constant for a moment, the airspeed change is immediate. You must respond with power and attitude at once.

Question. Climbing out at dawn, you pass through the top of an inversion into a 25 kt wind from directly ahead. What happens to the airspeed and the climb, and why?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the airspeed rises, and the aeroplane climbs more steeply for a moment. The headwind has increased suddenly, but inertia keeps the groundspeed. So the airspeed jumps, by up to the change in wind. It is performance-increasing shear. The same layer met on descent, or with the wind from behind, would do the opposite.

## Responding to the signs

When shear or turbulence is forecast or suspected, the response is the same set of tools, whatever the cause. There are five.

First, add a margin to the approach speed. Half the gust factor is the usual rule. Add half the difference between the gust and the mean wind to your normal approach speed. Your POH or your instructor may set a limit on how much to add.

Second, use less flap, if the POH allows. Then the aeroplane is less affected by gusts, and easier to accelerate.

Third, be ready to go around early. Go around at the first sign that the speed or the path is getting away from you.

Fourth, do not take off or land through a thunderstorm gust front, at all.

And fifth, listen to other pilots. On a windy day, a report of "wind shear on final" from a preceding aircraft is a reason to add speed and be ready for a go-around. And passing on your own report helps the next pilot.

Let's work one: approach speed with a gust margin. The ATIS gives the wind as {250|two five zero} degrees at 14 kt, gusting 24 kt, close to straight down the runway. Your normal approach speed is 65 kt. What approach speed should you fly? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
Step one, the gust factor. 24 minus 14 is 10 kt.

Step two, the margin, which is half the gust factor. 10 divided by 2 is 5 kt.

Step three, the approach speed. 65 plus 5 is 70 kt.

The extra 5 kt is there to be lost in a lull without bringing you near the stall. Expect a longer float if the gust is still blowing when you flare. The answer again: 70 kt.

Now try one yourself. The TAF for your arrival gives the wind group {22012G30KT|two two zero one two, gust three zero, K T}. That is a mean wind of 12 kt, gusting 30 kt. Your normal approach speed is 62 kt. What approach speed does the rule give, and what else does that wind tell you?
[[pause 6]]
The answer: the gust factor is 30 minus 12, which is 18 kt. Half of that is 9 kt. And 62 plus 9 is 71 kt.

A gust of 18 kt above the mean is well past the 10 kt that signals turbulence in a forecast. So expect a rough approach, and strong mechanical turbulence near the ground. Check the crosswind component against your limit. And decide before you leave whether this is a day for a student pilot to be landing there at all. The approach speed again: 71 kt.

## Bringing it together

It is a February afternoon. You are returning to Northam from a training area to the north, at {1500|fifteen hundred} WST. The TAF gave a gusty north-easterly. The GPWT showed the wind at 2,000 ft at 30 kt. And the GAF carried moderate turbulence below 5,000 ft.

On the way back, you see three things. Cumulus with ragged, growing tops is building to the east. One dark-based cloud is trailing virga. And two dust devils are wandering across the bare paddocks south of the aerodrome.

You read them in turn. The ragged cumulus means strong thermals, so a rough ride at circuit height. The virga cloud means a downdraught and a possible gust front, so you watch it and keep your distance. And the dust devils mean very unstable air near the surface around the aerodrome, so you expect sharp gusts on short final.

The wind sock is swinging between 15 and 25 kt. So you add half the gust factor, 5 kt, to the approach speed. On late final you descend through the gradient. The airspeed sags, and you add power. Over the hot runway, the aeroplane balloons in a thermal. You hold the attitude and let it settle.

Had the virga cloud moved over the aerodrome, the answer would have been different. You would hold off well clear, or divert. You would not try to land underneath it.

Question. In that scenario, which single observation would most change your plan, and why?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the dark-based cloud trailing virga, moving towards the aerodrome. Virga under a high-based storm marks a cooling downdraught. That can reach the ground as a microburst and gust front, the most dangerous shear there is. The thermals, the dust devils and the gusts are managed with speed and attention. But a microburst is only managed by staying away.

## Key points

Let's recap, in two parts.

First, turbulence. It is forecast by turbulence groups in the GAF, by severe turbulence SIGMETs, by TAF gusts of 10 kt or more above the mean wind, by thunderstorms and fronts, and by big wind changes with height in the GPWT.

Thermals show as flat-based cumulus, and taller and more ragged means stronger. They also show as smoke that rises and breaks up, and as soaring birds and gliders. Dust devils are visible, strong, narrow thermals on hot, light-wind days. Avoid them near the ground.

Lenticular and rotor clouds mark mountain wave and lee turbulence. Whitecaps and ragged low cloud mark a gusty wind. Virga and dark cumulonimbus bases mark downdraughts. The effects are sudden changes in height, attitude and airspeed, a risk of stall or overstress, and sink and lift on final.

Second, wind gradient and shear. The wind gradient is friction slowing the wind near the ground. Because inertia holds the groundspeed, a loss of headwind, or a gain of tailwind, costs airspeed and lift, and the aeroplane sinks. A gain of headwind adds them, and the aeroplane floats. Descending into a strong headwind, the airspeed decays, so add power.

Expect shear at inversions, at sea breeze fronts, at fronts, around buildings, and worst of all in thunderstorm microbursts, which go headwind, downdraught, tailwind. Respond with half the gust factor on the approach speed, less flap if the POH allows, an early go-around, and no take-off or landing through a gust front.

A final exam tip. Loss of headwind means loss of airspeed. That is the whole point of wind shear questions. Descending through a wind gradient into a strong headwind, the airspeed decays and the aeroplane sinks, so expect to add power. Dust devils are visible thermals, and should be avoided on take-off and landing. Lenticular and rotor clouds point to lee turbulence. A microburst gives headwind, then downdraught, then tailwind. And for gusts, add half the gust factor to the approach speed.

That's the end of RMTC 2.3.
