This is RMTC 2.2, Knowledge of forecasts and reports. By the end you will know what each weather product is for, how to decode a TAF and a METAR group by group, and how to use them all to decide whether a VFR flight can go. There are decoding exercises along the way, with a pause to try each one before the answer.

## What the exam expects (RMTC 2.2)

The exam wants one thing from you, in three parts. You need to understand the forecasts, reports and broadcasts that cover the area you fly in. That means knowing what each product is for, how to decode it, and how to use it to decide whether a VFR flight can go.

## Why this matters

Before every flight beyond the circuit, you will open NAIPS or the Bureau's aviation site, and be handed a page of coded text. There will be a GAF for the area, a TAF for Jandakot, a METAR from Northam, and perhaps a SIGMET. Then, taxiing out, you will listen to the ATIS.

Each of these answers a different question, in a different time frame. Sometimes they use a different datum for the same cloud base. And the coded ones are written in a compact language designed for teleprinters.

CASA lists "interpretation of meteorological forecasts and requirements" as a weak area in the RPLA exam. In the air, the same weakness shows up as a pilot who read the main body of a TAF, and missed the TEMPO group that put showers over the destination at arrival time. So this lesson teaches the products first by purpose, then the code group by group, then how to put them together into a go or no-go.

## Three kinds of weather information

Every product you will meet is one of three things.

The first is a forecast. It tells you what the Bureau expects to happen. It is the only thing that can tell you about your arrival time two hours from now. But it is an expectation, and it carries the forecaster's uncertainty in its change and probability groups.

The second is an observation, also called a report. It tells you what was actually measured, at a place and a time. It is fact. But it is already old when you read it, and it says nothing about the future.

The third is a broadcast. It delivers an observation by radio, so that you get it in the cockpit at the moment you need it.

A good briefing uses all three. The forecast to plan. The latest observation to check that the forecast is coming true. And the broadcast to confirm, on the day, at the aerodrome.

The products also differ in scale. Some describe a whole area, so that you know what to expect en route. Others describe a single aerodrome, and the few kilometres around it. And a third group are warnings, issued only when something hazardous is happening.

## The area products: GAF, GPWT and area QNH

Start with the area products, the ones that cover your route.

The GAF, the Graphical Area Forecast, is the en route forecast. It is a chart of a forecast area. Western Australia is covered by two areas, {WA-N|W A N} and {WA-S|W A S}. Each area is divided into subdivisions. For each subdivision there is a table giving the cloud, visibility, weather, icing and turbulence expected, and the freezing level. The Bureau of Meteorology issues it four times a day, and each one is valid for 6 hours. Verify the current issue times and validity in the AIP. For a flight from Jandakot to Northam, you find the subdivisions your track crosses, and read what each one says for the time you are in it.

One fact about the GAF catches more students than any other. Its cloud heights are above mean sea level, written AMSL. That makes sense for an area product. A GAF subdivision may cover a coastal plain at sea level and a plateau at 1,000 ft. The only datum common to the whole area is the sea. An aerodrome forecast, by contrast, measures cloud from the aerodrome itself. That is because the question it answers is how high the cloud is above the runway.

[[diagram:cloud-height-datum]]
Picture one layer of cloud, with an aerodrome on raised ground beneath it, and the sea off to one side. The GAF measures the cloud base up from sea level. The TAF and the METAR measure it up from the aerodrome. So at an aerodrome above sea level, the TAF figure is smaller than the GAF figure for the same cloud, by the aerodrome's elevation.

Let's work one. The GAF for the subdivision covering an inland aerodrome gives a cloud base of 3,500 ft. The aerodrome's elevation is 650 ft. How high is the cloud above the aerodrome? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
Step one: the GAF base is above mean sea level, so it is 3,500 ft above the sea. Step two: subtract the elevation. 3,500 minus 650 is 2,850 ft.

So a TAF for that aerodrome, describing the same cloud, would show it at about 2,850 ft, not 3,500. And over hills higher than the aerodrome, the gap between the ground and the cloud is smaller still. Always subtract the height of the ground you will actually fly over. The answer again: 2,850 ft above the aerodrome.

The next area product is the GPWT, the Grid Point Wind and Temperature chart. It gives forecast wind and temperature at set heights, for example 2,000, 5,000, 7,000 and 10,000 ft, on a grid of points. It is the product you use for planning headings, groundspeeds and true airspeed, TAS. Its temperatures feed your performance and density height calculations. It is also a turbulence clue. A big jump in wind between 2,000 and 5,000 ft is shear. That is in the lesson on the significance of observations.

The last area product is the area QNH. It is a forecast QNH for each area, valid for 3-hour periods, given in the GAF and in the NAIPS briefing. You set it on the altimeter when you have no local QNH from a station within 100 nm. The rule is in the lesson on conditions of flight.

Question. Why does an area forecast give cloud heights above sea level, when an aerodrome forecast gives them above the aerodrome?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: an area forecast covers ground at many different heights, so the only datum common to all of it is mean sea level. An aerodrome forecast describes one place. What the pilot needs there is the height of the cloud above the runway.

## The aerodrome forecast: the TAF

The TAF, the aerodrome forecast, is a forecast of wind, visibility, weather and cloud at one aerodrome. It has change groups that say when and how the conditions will change. A TAF is valid for 12, 18 or 24 hours, depending on the aerodrome. There is also a variant, the {TAF3|taff three}, which is updated every 3 hours. Again, verify these against the current AIP.

Before the code itself, time. All aviation weather products use Coordinated Universal Time, UTC, marked with a Z, for Zulu. Western Australia has no daylight saving, and WST, Western Standard Time, is UTC plus 8. So add 8 hours to the UTC time to get Perth local time. And watch for the date ticking over when the sum passes {2400|twenty-four hundred}.

The date and time groups are packed as day, hours and minutes. So {030400Z|zero three zero four zero zero Zulu} is the 3rd at {0400|oh four hundred} UTC.

Let's work one. A TAF has the validity group {0306/0318|zero three zero six, stroke, zero three one eight}. What does that cover in local time? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
The start is the 3rd at {0600|oh six hundred} UTC. {0600|Oh six hundred} plus 8 is {1400|fourteen hundred} WST on the 3rd.

The end is the 3rd at {1800|eighteen hundred} UTC. {1800|Eighteen hundred} plus 8 is {2600|twenty-six hundred}. That passes midnight, so it is {0200|oh two hundred} WST on the 4th.

So the forecast covers your afternoon and evening on the 3rd, and runs past midnight local time. And a flight planned for {1100|eleven hundred} WST on the 3rd, which is {0300|oh three hundred} UTC, is not covered by it at all.

Now try one yourself. A change group reads {FM031000|F M zero three one zero zero zero}. At what local time does the change happen?
[[pause 6]]
The answer: that is from the 3rd at {1000|ten hundred} UTC. Add 8 hours, and it is {1800|eighteen hundred} WST on the 3rd. Early evening in Perth.

Now the language of the code. Each group says one thing, always in the same order.

First, wind. Wind is five digits and a unit. Three digits give the direction the wind blows from, in degrees true. Two give the speed. So {12012KT|one two zero one two K T} is {120|one two zero} degrees true at 12 kt. A G adds gusts, and V R B means variable.

Notice that forecasts and reports give true wind. They are meteorological products, tied to the map. The tower and the ATIS give the wind in magnetic, because it is meant to be compared with runway headings, which are magnetic. Near Perth the difference is small, but the exam asks about it.

Second, visibility. Visibility is four digits, in metres. {4000|Four zero zero zero} is 4,000 m. And {9999|nine nine nine nine} means 10 km or more.

Third, weather. Weather is a set of letter codes, and the common ones fall into three families. For precipitation: RA is rain, DZ is drizzle, SH is showers, and GR is hail. For storms: TS is thunderstorm, TSRA is thunderstorm with rain, SQ is squall, and FC is funnel cloud. And for things that obscure the view: FG is fog, BR is mist, HZ is haze, FU is smoke, DU is dust, and DS is dust storm.

A minus sign in front means light, and a plus sign means heavy. VC means in the vicinity. So SHRA is showers of rain. And {+TSRA|plus T S R A} is a heavy thunderstorm with rain.

Fourth, cloud. Cloud is an amount and a height. The amount is measured in oktas, which are eighths of the sky.

[[diagram:cloud-amounts-in-oktas]]
Picture the four cloud codes side by side, from the least cloud on the left to the most on the right. FEW is 1 to 2 oktas. SCT, scattered, is 3 to 4. BKN, broken, is 5 to 7. And OVC, overcast, is 8. Only the last two, broken and overcast, make a ceiling.

The height is three digits, in hundreds of feet above the aerodrome. So 045 is 4,500 ft. A broken or overcast layer is a ceiling, because at that amount the sky is more cloud than gap. You cannot count on climbing or descending through it visually. So, FEW 1 to 2, scattered 3 to 4, broken 5 to 7, overcast 8.

Last, CAVOK, said "cav okay". It means ceiling and visibility OK. It replaces the visibility, weather and cloud groups when all four of these are true. Visibility is 10 km or more. There is no cloud below 5,000 ft, or below the highest minimum sector altitude. There is no cumulonimbus. And there is no significant weather.

Now the change groups, which say when the conditions apply. A TAF's opening conditions are only the starting point. The change groups say how they change, and they are where most decoding errors happen. There are five to know.

First, FM, meaning from. It marks a permanent change at a set time. {FM031000|F M zero three one zero zero zero} means that from the 3rd at {1000|ten hundred} UTC, everything that follows replaces what came before.

Second, BECMG, meaning becoming. It is a gradual change over the period given. By the end of the period, the new conditions apply.

Third, TEMPO, meaning temporary. It describes temporary fluctuations, each lasting 30 to 60 minutes at a time, within the period given. Check that against the current AIP.

Fourth, INTER, meaning intermittent. It describes intermittent fluctuations, each lasting less than 30 minutes, within the period given.

And fifth, {PROB30|prob thirty} or {PROB40|prob forty}. These give the probability, 30 or 40 percent, of the condition that follows.

[[diagram:taf-change-groups]]
Picture a timeline from {0600|oh six hundred} to {1800|eighteen hundred} UTC, running left to right. The main conditions start at the left edge. At {1000|ten hundred} UTC, an FM group cuts in and replaces them for good, with wind {090|zero nine zero} at 8 kt and CAVOK. Over the first part of the line, from {0600|oh six hundred} to {0900|oh nine hundred}, a TEMPO bar adds spells of 4,000 m in showers with broken cloud at 2,000 ft, each spell lasting 30 to 60 minutes. Underneath, the other groups are listed: BECMG, a gradual change; INTER, spells under 30 minutes; and PROB, a probability.

The key to TEMPO and INTER is this. The main conditions still apply most of the time. But at any moment within the period, the worse conditions may be there. For planning you must allow for them, because you cannot choose to arrive during a gap.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Reading only the first line of a TAF. The opening line may be CAVOK, while a TEMPO or INTER group puts showers and a 1,500 ft ceiling over the aerodrome for the very hours you plan to arrive. Read every change group against your arrival time.

Question. What is the practical difference for a pilot between FM and TEMPO?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: FM is a permanent change. After the time given, the old conditions no longer apply. TEMPO describes spells of 30 to 60 minutes each within its period, with the main conditions applying in between. You must still plan for the worse conditions, because you cannot be sure of arriving between spells.

Now let's decode a whole TAF from start to finish.

## The observations: METAR and SPECI

A METAR is a routine observation of the actual conditions at an aerodrome. It is issued every half hour or hour. It uses the same groups as a TAF for the observed values, and adds two of its own. The first is the temperature and dew point, written with a stroke between them. The second is the QNH. It ends with any recent weather, and a remarks section.

A SPECI is the same message, but a special one. It is issued when conditions change significantly, or are below set criteria. So the word SPECI in place of METAR is itself a warning that something has changed.

And AUTO means the report came from an automatic station, with no human observer. So the cloud and weather groups may be incomplete. An automatic sensor sees cloud only directly above it, and it can miss a storm two kilometres away.

[[diagram:metar-anatomy]]
Picture a single line of code for Jandakot, each group labelled, much like the TAF but shorter. Let's decode it. Pause if you'd like to try each group first.

It reads {METAR YPJT 030430Z 12012KT 9999 FEW030 24/18 Q1016|mee-tar, Y P J T, zero three zero four three zero Zulu, one two zero one two K T, nine nine nine nine, few zero three zero, two four stroke one eight, Q one zero one six}.

The visibility is 10 km or more. There is few cloud at 3,000 ft above the aerodrome. The temperature is 24 degrees and the dew point 18 degrees, a spread of 6 degrees. And the QNH is {1016|ten sixteen} hectopascals.

Now set it beside the TAF decoded earlier, which was issued half an hour before this observation. The conditions the TAF opens with are already in place: the same wind, the same visibility, the lower cloud layer. Checking the forecast against what is actually happening is what an observation is for.

[[pause 6]]
The answer: it is a special observation at Jandakot, on the 3rd at {0715|oh seven fifteen} UTC, which is {1515|fifteen fifteen} WST. The wind is {120|one two zero} degrees true at 14 kt. The visibility is 4,000 m in showers of rain. There is broken cloud at 2,000 ft above the aerodrome. The temperature is 18 and the dew point 16. The QNH is {1015|ten fifteen} hectopascals.

The TAF's TEMPO conditions have arrived, exactly the spell it warned of. And the word SPECI tells you it was the change that triggered the report. The temperature has dropped in the rain-cooled air, and the spread has narrowed to 2 degrees.

Question. A METAR for a country aerodrome is marked AUTO, and shows no cloud. Can you take that to mean the sky is clear?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: not with confidence. AUTO means an automatic station with no observer. Its sensor sees only the cloud directly above it, and it may not report all the weather. Check the TAF, the GAF and any other nearby reports before relying on it.

## The broadcasts: ATIS and AWIS

The ATIS, the automatic terminal information service, is a recorded broadcast at a controlled aerodrome, such as Jandakot or Perth, on a published frequency. It gives the runway in use, the wind, the QNH, the cloud, the visibility and the temperature. Each version is identified by a letter, such as "information Bravo", and it is updated when conditions change.

You listen to it before you call for taxi or inbound. Then you tell the tower which letter you have, so that the controller knows you have the current information. The wind on the ATIS is magnetic, ready to compare with the runway.

The AWIS, the automatic weather information service, is found at some non-towered aerodromes. It gives the current wind, QNH, temperature, cloud and visibility, by radio or by telephone. It is an observation, so it tells you what is happening now. That is exactly what you want before descending into a country aerodrome, or before leaving a farm strip for it.

## The warnings: SIGMET and AIRMET

A SIGMET is a warning of significant en route weather. That means thunderstorms, severe turbulence, severe icing, volcanic ash and tropical cyclones. It is issued as needed, and is valid for up to 4 hours.

An AIRMET is a warning for low-level flights. It covers weather hazardous to light aircraft below 10,000 ft that the GAF does not already show.

Neither appears unless there is a hazard. So their absence from your briefing is good news. And their presence on your route is usually a no-go for a light aeroplane.

Now the products side by side, as a recap, grouped by what they share.

The forecasts come first. The GAF is the area chart, from the Bureau, issued four times a day and valid for 6 hours, with cloud above sea level. The GPWT gives forecast wind and temperature at set heights, for each forecast period. The area QNH is valid for 3-hour periods, and it is in the GAF and the NAIPS briefing. And the TAF is valid for 12, 18 or 24 hours, depending on the aerodrome, with change groups and a probability group.

The observations are the METAR, routine, every half hour or hour, and the SPECI, special, when conditions change significantly. Neither is a forecast.

The broadcasts are the ATIS, at a controlled aerodrome, identified by a letter, and the AWIS, at some non-towered aerodromes, by radio or phone.

And the warnings appear only as needed: the SIGMET, valid for up to 4 hours, and the AIRMET, for low-level flights below 10,000 ft.

These validity periods and issue times are from the AIP and the Bureau's services guide as they stood when the lesson was written. Verify them against the current AIP.

## Using the forecasts for a VFR flight

The products fit together in the order you use them. There are seven steps.

First, the GAF, for every subdivision you fly through. Read the cloud base and amount, the visibility, the weather, and whether thunderstorms or turbulence are forecast. Remember that its cloud is above mean sea level.

Second, the GPWT, or the GAF winds, for headings and groundspeeds, and the forecast temperature for performance.

Third, the TAF, for the departure, the destination and any alternate. Read it against the VMC minima for the time of your flight, including the TEMPO and INTER groups. An alternate, or extra fuel, is required when the forecast conditions at the destination are below the alternate minima for your arrival period. The detail is in the AIP and the Part 91 Manual of Standards.

Fourth, the latest METAR or SPECI, to see whether the forecast is coming true.

Fifth, any SIGMETs and AIRMETs on the route.

Sixth, the area QNH for the altimeter.

And seventh, on the day, the ATIS or the AWIS, to confirm what is actually happening.

## Bringing it together

You are planning a solo flight from Jandakot to Northam. You will depart at {1300|thirteen hundred} WST, which is {0500|oh five hundred} UTC, and arrive at about {1340|thirteen forty} WST.

The GAF for the subdivision east of the Scarp shows scattered cumulus based at 4,000 ft, with isolated showers. The Northam TAF has a main body of 10 km or more, with scattered cloud at 3,500 ft. But a TEMPO group covering {0500|oh five hundred} to {0800|oh eight hundred} UTC gives 3,000 m in showers, with broken cloud at 1,500 ft.

The Scarp lies between the two aerodromes, rising to roughly 1,000 ft above the plain. Now put the numbers together. A GAF base of 4,000 ft above sea level, over hills of about 1,000 ft, leaves roughly 3,000 ft of clearance en route. That is workable.

But the TEMPO at Northam covers your arrival time. During the spells, it puts the visibility below 5,000 m, and a ceiling at 1,500 ft above the aerodrome. You cannot arrive between spells by choice. So the flight as planned is not a good one.

The options are to leave later, after {0800|oh eight hundred} UTC, which is {1600|sixteen hundred} WST, if the TAF for that period is better. Or to choose another destination. And on the day, you would still check the latest Northam METAR before starting. A SPECI showing showers already over the town would settle the question.

Question. In that scenario, why is the GAF the right product for the Scarp crossing, but the TAF the right one for the arrival?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the GAF covers the subdivision you fly across, and gives cloud above sea level, so you compare it with the terrain height. The TAF covers only the aerodrome and its immediate surroundings, with cloud above the aerodrome. That is what you need for the circuit and the landing.

## Key points

Let's recap.

Forecasts tell you what to expect: the GAF, the GPWT, the area QNH and the TAF. Observations tell you what is happening: the METAR and the SPECI. Broadcasts deliver it in the cockpit: the ATIS and the AWIS. And warnings appear only when there is a hazard: the SIGMET and the AIRMET.

Times are in UTC, Zulu, and WST is UTC plus 8. Forecast and report winds are true. ATIS and tower winds are magnetic.

Cloud: FEW is 1 to 2 oktas, scattered 3 to 4, broken 5 to 7, and overcast 8. Broken and overcast make a ceiling. GAF heights are above mean sea level. TAF and METAR heights are above the aerodrome.

Change groups: FM is permanent, and BECMG is gradual. TEMPO is spells of 30 to 60 minutes. INTER is spells under 30 minutes. And {PROB30|prob thirty} and {PROB40|prob forty} are probabilities. Plan for the worst group that covers your time.

A METAR adds the temperature, dew point and QNH. SPECI means a significant change. And AUTO means no observer, and possibly incomplete cloud and weather.

The GAF is valid for 6 hours and issued four times a day. The area QNH is valid for 3-hour periods. And a SIGMET is valid for up to 4 hours.

A final exam tip. CASA lists "interpretation of meteorological forecasts and requirements" as a weak area in the RPLA exam. So practise decoding TAFs and METARs until the cloud, visibility, change and probability groups are automatic. And remember three things. GAF cloud heights are above mean sea level. TAF and METAR cloud heights are above the aerodrome. And forecast winds are true. Expect a question that gives you a TEMPO or INTER group and asks what it means. And expect one that turns on the difference between a forecast and an observation.

That's the end of RMTC 2.2.
