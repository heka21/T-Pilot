This is PNVC 2.5, Pilot navigation. By the end you will be able to read and prepare a chart, and use the {1 in 60|one in sixty} rule and simple proportion to get back on track, revise an estimate, find a dead reckoning position and plan a diversion in your head. There are worked examples and questions along the way, so pause whenever you want a moment to think.

## What the exam expects (PNVC 2.5)

The exam wants three things from you. First, map reading: orienting the chart, map to ground and ground to map, which position lines give groundspeed, track error and a fix, and which features to use at each height and over each kind of country. Second, preparing a chart: tracks, track error lines, time and distance marks, and which charts to carry. And third, working from a planned track in flight: track made good and drift, altering heading to parallel, intercept and hold the track, revising estimates, a dead reckoning position, and all of it in your head, including a diversion.

## Why this matters

Out past Northam, the wheatbelt's paddocks, salt lakes and gravel roads all look alike from 4,500 ft. And the forecast wind is rarely the wind you get.

This subtopic teaches you to notice early that you have drifted from the plan, by how much, and to correct it in your head. CASA names time and distance work, and the revision of estimates, as weak areas in the PPLA exam. And the {1 in 60|one in sixty} rule is examined with numbers every time. It is all one idea, small angles and simple proportion, used over and over.

## Holding the chart and reading it (2.5.1 a and b)

You are tracking east from Northam, chart north-up on your knee. A road appears ahead and right. On the chart, ahead is towards the right of the page, and right is towards the bottom. So every feature must be rotated in your head, and under workload, that is where mistakes creep in.

So turn the chart. Orienting the chart means holding it track-up, so the track line points the way the aeroplane is going over the ground. Now a feature ahead and right of the aeroplane is ahead and right of your position on the chart. Rotate the chart with you at each turn. On a southbound leg the printing is upside down. That does not matter. The shapes do.

There are two ways to read the chart. The first is map to ground, and it starts on the chart. You know where you are, so you pick the next feature the chart says you should see. You decide when, from groundspeed and distance. You decide where, left, right or ahead. And you decide what it should look like. Then you look outside for it.

Looking for one thing, in one place, at one time, is fast and reliable. So this is the normal method: after every fix, whenever you are confident of your position, and approaching a turning point or the destination.

The second method is ground to map, and it starts outside. You see a feature, and search the chart for it. This is slower and error-prone, because one dam, bend or silo looks very like the next. Use it only when you need to. First, when you are uncertain of your position, or lost. Second, when map to ground has failed and the expected feature has not appeared. Third, after a period of distraction, a diversion around weather, or a turn you did not time. And fourth, when an unexpectedly prominent feature appears and you want to identify it.

Even then, start from your dead reckoning position, your DR position, which is your best estimate from heading, groundspeed and time. Search around it, and confirm any match with a second feature.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. The classic error is making the ground fit the map. You expect a town with a railway. A town with a railway appears, and you accept it, without checking the shape of the lake beside it or the angle of the road. The way out: confirm every fix with at least two independent features. And be most suspicious when a fix is exactly what you hoped to see.

Question. You were busy with a radio call and a fuel tank change for 10 minutes, and now nothing outside looks familiar. Which method of map reading do you use, and where on the chart do you start?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: ground to map, because you are no longer sure of your position. Start from your DR position. That is your last fix, plus the groundspeed times the time elapsed, along the track you were making good. Search outward from there, and confirm any match with a second feature.

## Position lines: groundspeed, track error and a fix (2.5.1 c)

A position line is any line on which you know you are, even if you do not yet know where along it: the railway you are crossing, a line through a homestead off the wingtip, the coast directly below. The art is choosing the right line for the question.

For groundspeed, you want a line across the track. Groundspeed is distance along track divided by time, so you need the exact moment you reached two points a known distance apart. A road, railway or river crossing the track at about 90 degrees gives a sharp moment of crossing, which does not move if you are a little left or right. A line crossing at a shallow angle is poor. A small error left or right moves the crossing point a long way along track.

Let's work one. You cross a railway at right angles to track at {1012|ten twelve}, and a highway, also across track, at {1024|ten twenty-four}. The chart shows them 21 nm apart along the track. Pause if you'd like to find the groundspeed.
[[pause 6]]
The time between them is {1024|ten twenty-four} minus {1012|ten twelve}, which is 12 minutes. 21 nm in 12 minutes, times 60 to turn minutes into hours, is 105 kt.

So the groundspeed is 105 kt. If you planned 115 kt, you now know the wind is stronger on the nose than forecast, and every estimate further down the leg will be late.

For track error, you want a line along the track. A railway, a long straight road, or a coastline roughly parallel to the track shows at a glance how far left or right you are, and whether that is growing. It says nothing about groundspeed, because you could be anywhere along it.

For a fix, you want lines that cross. A fix is a definite position: two position lines crossing near 90 degrees, so a small error in either moves the fix only a little. Lines crossing at 20 degrees give a long, thin region of uncertainty instead of a point. Three lines rarely meet exactly. They form a small triangle, the cocked hat, and you take its centre. The quickest fix is a single unmistakable point feature: a distinctive town, a lake with an island, a road and railway junction.

Question. A railway runs 5 degrees off your track for 30 nm. What can it tell you, and what can it not?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: being almost parallel to track, it is an excellent track error line. You can see how far off track you are, and whether the error is growing. It cannot give a groundspeed, because the moment of reaching a line you are flying alongside is not defined.

## Choosing features for height and terrain (2.5.1 d)

Two things change with height: how far you can see, and how much detail survives.

At low level, 500 ft above ground level, the view is close and fast. At 100 kt, a feature 2 nm ahead arrives in just over a minute, and a town cannot be seen whole. So choose small, close features before flight, at short intervals: roads, creeks, fence lines, homesteads and single hills. And minimum height rules apply. You'll find those in the lesson on conditions of flight.

Between 2,000 and 10,000 ft, you see far but lose small detail in haze. So large features work best: towns, lakes, coastline, major roads and railways, ranges, and in WA, salt lakes, whose shapes are a signature.

Terrain and population change the picture again, and the exam asks for the reasons.

In mountains, use valleys, river systems, prominent peaks, passes and the shape of ridges, not individual hills. Peaks look alike from different angles, but the pattern of valleys and rivers does not change. And the apparent height of terrain is misleading.

Along the coast, use its shape, river mouths, headlands, islands, lighthouses and coastal towns, and stay in sight of it. The shoreline is a ready-made track error line, which is why a route such as Jandakot to Lancelin keeps it in view.

Over densely populated country, use major highways, railways, large water bodies and airports, not suburbs and minor roads. Over Perth's suburbs, minor detail merges into one grey sheet, and only the river, the freeway, the railways and the airports stand out.

And over sparsely populated country, the WA interior, use salt lakes, creek lines, the few roads, the vermin fence, mines and ranges. The chart shows few features, and some on the ground, such as new tracks and bores, are not charted at all. So avoid those, and lean on DR and the clock.

Contours, lakes, railways and towns are the ones you will match most often. And intermittent rivers are drawn dashed, because they are often dry.

Question. Why do salt lakes make better checkpoints than homesteads when you are at 5,500 ft over the eastern wheatbelt?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: at that height, small features such as homesteads are lost in detail and haze, and many look alike. Salt lakes are large, distinctive in outline and in their relationship to each other, and they do not change. So their shape identifies them uniquely.

## Preparing the chart (2.5.2)

A good chart answers two questions at a glance. How far off track am I? And how far have I come?

First, the track. Draw each leg as a bold straight line between turning points, with the magnetic track and distance written beside it, clear of features.

Second, the track error lines. From the departure point, and each turning point, draw two thin lines at 10 degrees either side of track. Draw two more at 10 degrees into the destination, and into the next turning point. These fan lines are a built-in protractor. A fix's place between the departure lines shows the track error by eye. Halfway out to the line is about 5 degrees. And its place between the destination lines shows the closing angle. Both are explained shortly.

Third, distance and time marks. Mark every 10 nm from departure, and the halfway point. Some pilots add time marks every 6 minutes, a tenth of an hour.

Fourth, climb, descent and safety. Mark the top of climb and top of descent. Mark the leg's lowest safe altitude, the highest terrain or obstacle plus a margin. And mark nearby controlled airspace steps, the CTA steps, and the prohibited, restricted and danger areas, the PRD areas.

Then select the charts. Each chart in the AIP visual series answers a different question.

The WAC, the World Aeronautical Chart, at 1 to 1,000,000, is the main cross-country chart, with topography, towns, roads, railways and aerodromes. The WAC index on the PCA, the Planning Chart Australia, shows which sheets the route crosses. Near a sheet edge you need both.

The VNC, the Visual Navigation Chart, at 1 to 500,000, adds controlled airspace, PRD areas and frequencies. You need it wherever the route passes near controlled airspace.

The VTC, the Visual Terminal Chart, at 1 to 250,000, covers terminal areas such as Perth, with VFR routes, approach points and CTA steps.

And ERSA goes with every chart, for every aerodrome on the route, and for the PRD areas.

Picture four chart panels side by side. The WAC has the topography but no airspace. The VNC and VTC add airspace at larger scales. And the {ERC-L|E R C low} has airspace and navigation aids, but no topography.

Jandakot to Merredin, for example, needs the Perth VTC for the departure, the VNC for the airspace east of Perth, the wheatbelt WAC sheet or sheets, and ERSA.

## Track made good, track error and drift (2.5.3 a and b)

You set a heading that, with the forecast wind, should have kept you on track. 20 minutes later, a fix shows you are not where the plan said. Three terms describe what happened.

The track made good is the line from your departure point, or last fix, to your present fix. It is the path you actually flew over the ground.

The track error is the angle between the planned track and the track made good.

The drift is the angle between the heading flown and the track made good. It is what the real wind did to you. A heading of {085|zero eight five} and a track made good of {084|zero eight four} means 1 degree of left drift.

[[diagram:heading-track-drift]]
Picture the aeroplane from above. The nose points along the heading, but the wind carries the aeroplane sideways. Its line over the ground is the track, and the angle between the two is the drift.

Keep them apart by what they compare. Track error compares plan with reality. Drift compares nose with ground.

## The 1 in 60 rule: why it works

You need the track error in degrees, in the air, without a protractor. The {1 in 60|one in sixty} rule does it. An error of 1 nm in 60 nm subtends an angle of 1 degree.

Picture a circle of radius 60 nm around your departure point. Its circumference is 2 pi times 60, about 377 nm, shared among 360 degrees. So each degree is about 1.05 nm.

So at 60 nm, a 1 degree difference puts you about 1 nm sideways. At 30 nm, half a nautical mile. At 120 nm, 2 nm.

Turn that around for the angle. Track error, in degrees, equals the distance off track, times 60, divided by the distance flown.

Looking forward, the angle between the planned track and the line from you to the destination is the closing angle. Closing angle, in degrees, equals the distance off track, times 60, divided by the distance to go.

It is a small-angle rule of thumb, treating a short arc as a straight line. It is accurate to a few percent for navigation angles, and rough beyond about 20 degrees.

Picture a fix you can slide off the track line. For the same offset, the track error grows as you move it closer to departure. And the closing angle grows as the destination gets closer.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Track error divides by the distance flown. Closing angle divides by the distance to go. Swapping them is the most common wrong answer, and the distractors include it. The way out: ask yourself which end of the leg the angle is measured at.

## Getting back on track (2.5.3 c)

Knowing the two angles, the syllabus names three choices.

First, parallel the track. To stay offset, alter heading towards track by the track error. The old heading was wrong by exactly that amount, so the new track made good runs parallel to the plan.

Second, intercept the track at a nominated point. Alter by the track error plus the closing angle. The track error part stops the drift. The closing angle part points you at the target. Use the distance to the nominated point, not to the destination, in the closing angle.

And third, maintain the track once intercepted. Back on track, take off the closing angle, and keep the track error correction, which is the real fix for the wind. Leaving the closing angle on would carry you across to the other side.

Let's work one, back to the destination. The planned track is {090|zero nine zero} degrees magnetic. You have flown a heading of {085|zero eight five} degrees magnetic for 30 nm. The fix shows you 3 nm left of track, with 45 nm to go. Pause if you'd like to find the heading to the destination, and the heading to hold track.
[[pause 6]]
The track error is 3 times 60, divided by the 30 nm flown, which is 6 degrees left of track. The closing angle is 3 times 60, divided by the 45 nm to go, which is 4 degrees. The alteration is 6 plus 4, which is 10 degrees right. So the new heading is {085|zero eight five} plus 10, which is {095|zero nine five} degrees magnetic. On regaining track, take off the closing angle. {095|Zero nine five} minus 4 is {091|zero nine one} degrees magnetic, to hold it.

So, {095|zero nine five}, then {091|zero nine one}. The track made good was {090|zero nine zero} minus 6, which is {084|zero eight four} degrees magnetic. So the actual drift was 1 degree left, where you had allowed 5 degrees. The wind had more southerly in it than forecast. And {091|zero nine one} is also the heading that would have paralleled the track in the first place.

Now suppose instead a restricted area lies just north of track, and you want to regain it at a town 15 nm ahead. It is the same fix, 3 nm left after 30 nm, with a track error of 6 degrees.
[[pause 6]]
The closing angle to the nominated point is 3 times 60, divided by 15, which is 12 degrees. The alteration is 6 plus 12, which is 18 degrees right. So the new heading is {085|zero eight five} plus 18, which is {103|one zero three} degrees magnetic. Over the town, take off the closing angle. {103|One zero three} minus 12 is {091|zero nine one} degrees magnetic.

The closer the nominated point, the bigger the closing angle and the steeper the intercept. But the holding heading at the end is {091|zero nine one} either way, because the wind has not changed.

Now try one with the error on the other side. The planned track is {240|two four zero} degrees magnetic, and your heading is {247|two four seven}, because you allowed 7 degrees of right drift correction. After 45 nm, the fix shows you 3 nm right of track, with 90 nm to go. Find the heading to the destination, and the heading to hold track once regained.
[[pause 8]]
The track error is 3 times 60, divided by 45, which is 4 degrees right. The closing angle is 3 times 60, divided by 90, which is 2 degrees. You are right of track, so turn left, by 4 plus 2, which is 6 degrees. {247|Two four seven} minus 6 is {241|two four one} degrees magnetic to the destination. On regaining track, turn right by the closing angle. {241|Two four one} plus 2 is {243|two four three} degrees magnetic, to hold it.

So, {241|two four one}, then {243|two four three}. The track made good was {244|two four four} against a heading of {247|two four seven}, so the actual drift was 3 degrees left, where you had allowed 7. The wind from the right was weaker than forecast.

A useful special case. At halfway, the closing angle equals the track error, so the correction to the destination is double the track error. That makes the halfway mark worth a careful fix.

Question. Why do you take off only the closing angle, and not the whole alteration, when you regain track?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the closing angle was added only to converge on the track. The track error correction compensates for the wind that the original heading did not allow for. Removing the closing angle stops you crossing the track. Keeping the track error correction keeps you on it.

## Revising the ETA (2.5.3 d)

Your plan's estimated times of arrival, the ETAs, rest on a forecast groundspeed. Two timed fixes give the actual groundspeed, and every later estimate should be revised, by either of two methods.

[[diagram:groundspeed-headwind-tailwind]]
Picture two bars, drawn to scale. A true airspeed, a TAS, of 105 kt into a 20 kt headwind gives a groundspeed of 85 kt. With a 20 kt tailwind, it gives 125 kt. Only the wind changes the groundspeed, and with it every ETA on the leg.

The first method is by groundspeed. Groundspeed equals the distance, divided by the time in minutes, times 60. The time to go, in minutes, equals the distance to go, divided by the groundspeed, times 60.

The second is by proportion. Skip the groundspeed. The remaining distance takes time in the same ratio. Time to go equals the distance to go, times the time taken, divided by the distance flown. It is often quicker in the air, because prepared legs have round numbers.

Let's work one. The first 30 nm of a 75 nm leg took 18 minutes. The plan said 16. The fix was at {0942|oh nine forty-two} WST, Western Standard Time. Pause if you'd like to find the revised ETA.
[[pause 6]]
By proportion, the remaining 45 nm will take 45 times 18 over 30, which is 27 minutes. The plan was 45 times 16 over 30, which is 24 minutes. Check it by groundspeed. 30 over 18, times 60, is 100 kt. And 45 over 100, times 60, is 27 minutes, the same answer. So the revised ETA is {0942|oh nine forty-two} plus 27, which is {1009|ten oh nine} WST, 3 minutes later than planned for this part of the leg.

So, {1009|ten oh nine} WST. Pass any significant change to ATS, air traffic services, if you are on a flight plan, and revise the fuel and the SARTIME along with it.

Now try the companion to the {240|two four zero} degree problem. The first 45 nm of that leg took 25 minutes, against a plan of 23. You fixed at {1043|ten forty-three} WST, with 90 nm to go. What is the revised ETA, and what is the groundspeed?
[[pause 8]]
By proportion, the remaining 90 nm will take 90 times 25 over 45, which is 50 minutes. The plan, 90 times 23 over 45, was 46 minutes. So the revised ETA is {1043|ten forty-three} plus 50, which is {1133|eleven thirty-three} WST, 4 minutes later than the plan for that stretch. The groundspeed is 45 over 25, times 60, which is 108 kt. And 90 over 108, times 60, is 50 minutes, which confirms it.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set: mixing hours and minutes. Groundspeed is in nautical miles per hour, so a distance divided by a time in minutes must be multiplied by 60. A groundspeed of 1.67, or an ETA 6,000 minutes away, is the sign you skipped that step. The way out: write the units next to every number.

## Dead reckoning: where am I now? (2.5.3 e)

Sometimes there is no fix, in haze over featureless country, or over ground that will not match the chart. You still know your last fix, the track made good since, and the groundspeed. Dead reckoning, DR, combines them. The DR position is the last fix, plus the groundspeed times the time since the fix, along the track made good. Use the latest track made good and groundspeed, not the planned ones.

Let's work one. Your last fix was at {1015|ten fifteen} WST, over a railway siding. The track made good since is {075|zero seven five} degrees magnetic, and the groundspeed is 105 kt. It is now {1029|ten twenty-nine} WST, and there is nothing recognisable below. Pause if you'd like to find your DR position.
[[pause 6]]
The time since the fix is {1029|ten twenty-nine} minus {1015|ten fifteen}, which is 14 minutes. The distance per minute is 105 over 60, which is 1.75 nm a minute. The distance flown is 14 times 1.75, which is 24.5 nm. So the DR position is 24.5 nm along {075|zero seven five} degrees magnetic from the siding. To plot it, apply the variation to get true.

Now read ground to map from that point. Search the features within a few miles of the DR position, rather than the whole sheet.

A DR position's error grows with time since the fix. DR tells you where to look. Map reading confirms it, and starts the next DR.

Question. Why do you use the latest track made good and groundspeed for a DR position, rather than the planned track and groundspeed?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the planned values assume the forecast wind. The measured values include the wind you are actually experiencing, so a DR position built from them is much more likely to be close to where you really are.

## Navigating in your head: mental DR and diversions (2.5.3 f)

In the air you rarely have room for the flight computer. The {1 in 60|one in sixty} rule and proportion are mental arithmetic already, and four habits make them quick.

First, per-minute speeds. 90 kt is 1.5 nm a minute, 105 kt is 1.75, and 120 kt is 2. Second, the 6 minute rule. 6 minutes is a tenth of an hour, so in 6 minutes you cover a tenth of your groundspeed: 10.5 nm at 105 kt. Third, the halfway check. At halfway, the correction to the destination is double the track error. And fourth, round freely. The next fix will show any error.

The biggest mental task is a diversion. Weather ahead, a sick passenger, a rough engine. It has four parts.

First, mark and measure. Mark the diversion point. Estimate its track with the protractor, or by eye against the graticule, the latitude and longitude grid. On a WAC, the meridians point to true north. Estimate the distance with the scale, or the latitude scale, because one minute of latitude is one nautical mile.

Second, the heading. Apply variation, and a drift re-estimated for the new track, because the same wind meets it at a different angle.

Third, time. Estimate the groundspeed on the new track, and from it the estimated time interval, the ETI.

And fourth, check and tell. Check fuel against the ETI plus reserve, terrain, airspace and daylight, and tell ATS or your SARTIME holder. Fly the heading, start the clock, and refine at the first fix.

Let's work a mental diversion. Your TAS is 105 kt. The wind from your last leg is about {270|two seven zero} degrees true at 20 kt, and the variation is 2 degrees west. The diversion aerodrome lies about {040|zero four zero} degrees true, 34 nm away. Pause if you'd like to find the magnetic heading and the time.
[[pause 10]]
First, the wind angle. The wind blows from {270|two seven zero}, which is 40 plus 90, or 130 degrees left of the new track. So it comes from behind on the left: a tailwind, with a left crosswind, and right drift.

Second, the crosswind. The angle from the tail is 50 degrees. 20 times the sine of 50 degrees is about 20 times 0.77, which is 15 kt.

Third, the drift, by {1 in 60|one in sixty}. 15 over 105, times 60, is about 9 degrees right. So steer 9 degrees left of track. {040|Zero four zero} minus 9 is {031|zero three one} degrees true.

Fourth, the magnetic heading. West variation is added. {031|Zero three one} plus 2 is {033|zero three three} degrees magnetic.

Fifth, the tailwind. 20 times the cosine of 50 degrees is about 20 times 0.64, which is 13 kt. So the groundspeed is about 105 plus 13, which is 118 kt, about 2 nm a minute.

And sixth, the ETI. 34 over 118, times 60, is about 17 minutes.

So turn onto {033|zero three three} degrees magnetic, note the time, and work out the fuel for 17 minutes plus reserve. If the first fix shows a track error, correct it with the {1 in 60|one in sixty} rule, just as on a planned leg.

Question. On a diversion, why is it not enough to use the same drift correction you were using on the original leg?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: drift depends on the angle between the wind and the track. The wind has not changed, but the new track meets it at a different angle. So both the drift and the groundspeed change, and must be re-estimated for the new track.

## Bringing it together

You depart Northam for Merredin, about 75 nm, planning 110 kt, with fan lines and 10 nm marks on the WAC.

You cross the Great Eastern Highway at right angles to track, then a railway 18 nm further on, 10 minutes later. Groundspeed, 108 kt.

Near halfway, a salt lake fix puts you 2 nm left of track. The track error is 2 times 60, divided by 37, about 3 degrees. So you turn right about 6 degrees, and take off 3 when the railway paralleling the last part of the leg slides under the nose. By proportion, the remaining 38 nm take about 21 minutes.

Then the Merredin AWIS, the automatic weather service, reports a thunderstorm overhead. The diversion procedure gives you a heading, a time and a fuel check for the alternate inside two minutes. Small angles, proportion and the clock.

## Key points

Let's recap.

Map reading. Chart track-up. Map to ground normally, and ground to map only when uncertain, from the DR position. A line across track gives groundspeed, a line along track gives track error, and two lines near 90 degrees give a fix. Small, close features low. Large features high.

Preparing the chart. A bold track, 10 degree fan lines from both ends, 10 nm and halfway marks, top of climb and descent, and lowest safe altitude. The WAC en route, the VNC near controlled airspace, the VTC for terminal areas, and ERSA always.

The arithmetic. Track error is the distance off track, times 60, divided by the distance flown. Closing angle is the distance off track, times 60, divided by the distance to go. To parallel, alter by the track error. To intercept, add the closing angle. Once regained, take the closing angle off. Revise the ETA by groundspeed or by proportion. DR is the last fix plus groundspeed times time, along the track made good. And a diversion is track, distance, drift, heading, ETI, fuel, and tell someone.

A final exam tip. The {1 in 60|one in sixty} rule is examined with numbers every time. The correction to the destination is the sum of track error and closing angle, and on regaining track you remove only the closing angle. For revised estimates, write the units down, and remember the factor of 60 between minutes and hours. And expect map reading questions on which position line gives groundspeed, which gives track error, and when to read ground to map.
