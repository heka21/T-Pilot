This is PNVC 2.1, Form of the earth, direction and bearings. By the end you will be able to describe the earth and its grid of latitude and longitude, measure distance and direction on the chart, turn a true track into a compass heading, and turn a landmark off the nose into a bearing you can plot. There are worked examples and questions along the way, so pause whenever you want a moment to think.

## What the exam expects (PNVC 2.1)

The exam wants six things from you. First, the shape of the earth and the way it rotates. Second, latitude and longitude, and how a position is written with them. Third, the difference between true north and magnetic north. Fourth, how distance and direction are measured on the chart and used in navigation. Fifth, magnetic variation and compass deviation, and how to apply them. And sixth, how magnetic heading, relative bearing and magnetic bearing relate to one another.

## Why this matters

Every line you draw on a WAC, the World Aeronautical Chart, rests on a few facts about the planet underneath it. The grid you measure your track against is a set of circles drawn on a spinning ball. The distance scale you lay your dividers on exists because a minute of latitude happens to be a nautical mile. And the compass you steer by points somewhere other than the grid's north.

For the RPL you learned to quote directions and to convert true to magnetic. You'll find that in the lesson on direction of flight. The PPL takes you off the local chart and across the state, from Jandakot to Kalgoorlie, or up the coast to Geraldton. That is where those facts start to show. Variation changes along the route. The longitude scale lies about distance. And a town off your wingtip has to be turned into a bearing you can plot.

The exam asks short descriptive questions on the earth and its grid, a true to magnetic conversion, and almost always one question on magnetic heading plus relative bearing. This lesson builds each of them from the globe outwards.

## The shape and rotation of the earth

Picture the earth from space. It looks like a perfect ball, and for almost all of your navigation you can treat it as one. In fact it is very slightly squashed. The spin flings the equator outwards, so the diameter across the equator is about 43 km greater than the diameter from pole to pole. A ball flattened at the poles in this way is called an oblate spheroid.

Forty-three kilometres out of nearly 13,000 is about a third of one percent. That is why the charts and the nautical mile can treat the earth as a sphere, without you ever noticing.

The earth spins about an axis through its centre. The two places where that axis comes out of the surface are the geographic poles, also called the true north and south poles. Everything on the chart's grid is measured from them. So they are the fixed reference for the whole of navigation.

The spin is from west to east, once a day. Stand in Perth, and the ground carries you eastward towards the sun each morning. So the sun appears to rise in the east and travel west across the sky.

The same rotation means places further east see the sun first. Sunrise reaches Kalgoorlie before it reaches Perth, and Sydney well before either. That is the root of time zones, and of the arc to time arithmetic in the lesson on time.

The earth turns 360 degrees in 24 hours. That is 15 degrees of longitude every hour. Hold on to that number: 15 degrees an hour.

Question. Which way does the earth rotate, and why does that make the sun rise earlier in Kalgoorlie than in Perth?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: from west to east. Kalgoorlie is east of Perth, so the rotation carries it round to face the sun first. Every place further east sees sunrise, noon and sunset earlier, by about 4 minutes for each degree of longitude.

## Latitude and longitude: a grid on a ball

To say where something is on a ball, you need two numbers, the way a street directory needs a letter and a number. The earth's grid uses two sets of circles, both tied to the spin axis. Let's take latitude first, then longitude, then how a position is written.

Halfway between the poles, a circle runs round the earth's middle. That is the equator. Latitude is how far north or south of the equator a place is. It is measured as an angle at the earth's centre, from 0 degrees at the equator to 90 degrees at each pole.

Every place at the same latitude lies on a circle parallel to the equator. So these circles are called parallels of latitude. They shrink as you go towards the poles, until the parallel of 90 degrees is a single point. Perth is at about 32 degrees south.

Now longitude. Draw half-circles from pole to pole, like the segments of a peeled orange. Each one is a meridian. One of them, through Greenwich in England, was chosen as the starting line. It is the prime meridian, longitude 0.

Longitude is the angle east or west of the prime meridian, from 0 to 180 degrees each way. 180 east and 180 west are the same meridian, on the far side of the world. Perth is at about 116 degrees east.

Every meridian runs exactly north and south. That is why you measure true direction against a meridian on the chart.

Now writing a position. Each degree is divided into 60 minutes, and minutes into decimals or seconds. A position is always written latitude first, then longitude, each with its hemisphere letter, N or S, E or W.

Perth Airport, for example, is 31 degrees 56.5 minutes south, 115 degrees {58.0|58} minutes east. ERSA, the En Route Supplement, gives every aerodrome's position in this form.

[[diagram:chart-legend-symbols]]
On the WAC you plot a position against the graticule. That is the printed grid of parallels and meridians. On the WAC it is marked every 30 minutes, with a tick for each minute. Picture a sheet of sample chart symbols, like a chart legend: contours and tints, rivers and lakes, towns, roads and railways, obstacles, aerodromes, dashed isogonals, and the latitude and longitude graticule. The point to notice is the graticule. The minute ticks along a meridian are a ready-made distance scale.

Question. A position is given as 30 degrees 47 minutes south, 121 degrees 28 minutes east. Which number is the latitude, and which way would you go from it to reach the equator?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the first, 30 degrees 47 minutes south, is the latitude, because latitude is always written first. It is south of the equator, so you would go north, about 30 degrees, to reach it.

Now two kinds of line on the globe: great circles and rhumb lines.

Slice an orange straight through its centre, and the cut traces the biggest circle the skin can carry. On the earth, any circle whose plane passes through the centre is a great circle. The equator is one. And every meridian, together with its partner on the far side, makes one. Parallels other than the equator are small circles, because their planes miss the centre.

The useful fact is this. The shortest distance between two points on the earth lies along the great circle through them. Stretch a string tight between two pins on a globe, and it lies along a great circle.

A great circle has an awkward property for a pilot steering a compass heading. Except along a meridian or the equator, it crosses each meridian at a slightly different angle. The meridians converge towards the pole. So the straight path keeps changing its direction measured from north.

The alternative is a rhumb line. That is a line that crosses every meridian at the same angle. Fly a constant true track, and you follow a rhumb line. It is easy to steer, but slightly longer than the great circle. And on a long east-west leg at high latitude, it bows towards the equator.

[[diagram:great-circle-vs-rhumb-line]]
Picture a globe seen from above the Southern Ocean, with the meridians converging on the South Pole in the middle. Two points sit on the same southern parallel, and they are joined two ways. The great circle bulges towards the pole, and cuts each meridian at a different angle. The rhumb line follows the parallel, crossing every meridian at the same angle, and it is a little longer. So the great circle is the shortest path, and the rhumb line is the constant direction.

For a VFR flight the difference is negligible. Your legs are short. And the WAC is drawn on a Lambert conformal conic projection, on which a straight line is very close to a great circle. So the straight line you rule on the WAC is, for practical purposes, the shortest path.

Because the meridians on that chart converge, the line crosses each one at a slightly different angle. That is why you measure its direction at the middle of the leg. More on that shortly.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. The shortest distance is the great circle, not the rhumb line. The rhumb line is the constant direction line. Exam distractors swap the two. Or they describe parallels of latitude as great circles. Only the equator is.

## Measuring distance

The unit of distance is the nautical mile, and the reason for it is on the chart's edge. One degree of latitude is 60 minutes, and the nautical mile was chosen as one minute of latitude. Today it is defined as 1,852 m, which matches a minute of latitude closely enough for any navigation you will do. Speed follows from it. A knot is one nautical mile per hour.

That makes the latitude scale a ruler. Open your dividers across the leg. Carry them to a meridian near the leg. And count the minutes of latitude. The count is the distance in nautical miles. It works on any chart at any scale, because a minute of latitude is the same length everywhere on the earth.

The longitude scale is not a ruler. Meridians meet at the poles, so the distance between two of them shrinks as you go towards the pole. At the equator, a minute of longitude is a nautical mile. At Perth's latitude it is only about 0.85 NM. And at 60 degrees it is half a nautical mile. So never measure distance against the longitude scale along a parallel.

Let's work one, to see why the longitude scale lies. Perth Airport is at 31 degrees 56.5 minutes south, 115 degrees {58.0|58} minutes east. Kalgoorlie is at 30 degrees 47 minutes south, 121 degrees 28 minutes east. How far apart are they? This one is easier to follow than to do in your head, so just listen.

First, the difference of latitude. 31 degrees 56.5 minutes, minus 30 degrees 47 minutes, is 1 degree {09.5|9.5} minutes. That is 69.5 minutes. So Kalgoorlie is 69.5 NM further north.

Second, the difference of longitude. 121 degrees 28 minutes, minus 115 degrees {58.0|58} minutes, is 5 degrees 30 minutes. That is 330 minutes.

Third, turn those minutes of longitude into miles. At about 31.4 degrees south, a minute of longitude is the cosine of 31.4 degrees, about 0.85 NM. So the east-west distance is 330 times 0.85, about 282 NM. Not 330.

Fourth, combine the two by Pythagoras. The square root of 69.5 squared plus 282 squared is about 290 NM.

So the leg is about 290 NM. Reading 330 minutes of longitude as 330 NM would have overstated the distance by about 40 NM. Measured on the chart against the latitude scale, the leg comes out at about 290 NM.

On a WAC you can also measure against the chart's scale bar, or by the scale itself. At {1:1,000,000|one to one million}, a centimetre is 10 km, which is about 5.4 NM. You'll find chart measurement worked through in the lesson on extracting information from documents.

Question. You measure a leg with dividers. Do you read the distance against the latitude scale or the longitude scale, and why?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the latitude scale, on a meridian near the leg. A minute of latitude is one nautical mile everywhere. But meridians converge towards the poles, so a minute of longitude is shorter than a nautical mile everywhere except the equator. At Perth it is about 0.85 NM.

## Measuring direction

A direction is an angle measured clockwise from north, from {001|zero zero one} to {360|three six zero}, written as a three-figure group. On the chart, the north you measure from is the meridian. So a direction measured on the chart is true.

Because the meridians converge on the WAC, a long straight line meets the meridian at its start at a slightly different angle from the meridian at its end. So align the protractor with a meridian near the middle of the track. That gives you the mean of the two, which is the track you will fly. And read the direction from the departure end, so that you do not end up with the reciprocal.

[[diagram:compass-rose-headings]]
Picture a compass rose, a circle with north at the top. Clockwise round it, north is {360|three six zero}, east is {090|zero nine zero}, south is {180|one eight zero}, south-west is {225|two two five}, and west is {270|two seven zero}. A runway is drawn across the circle. One end points 214 degrees magnetic and is runway 21. The other end points {034|zero three four} degrees and is runway 03. The point to notice: clockwise from north, always three figures, and the reciprocal is 180 degrees away.

Three directions matter once you are airborne. The track is the path over the ground, the line you drew. The heading is where the nose points. And the drift is the angle between them, caused by the wind carrying the aeroplane sideways. You plan the track, then work out the heading that will make it good in the day's wind. That arithmetic is in the lesson on computation techniques.

[[diagram:heading-track-drift]]
Picture the aeroplane from above. Its nose points along the heading. But a wind from the left side carries it sideways, so over the ground it moves along a different line, the track. The angle between the two lines is the drift. With no wind, heading and track are the same.

## True north and magnetic north

The chart's north is the geographic pole. The compass's north is somewhere else.

The earth behaves as if it had a huge bar magnet inside it. A freely suspended magnet lines up with that field. The field points towards magnetic north. That is a region in the Canadian Arctic, hundreds of kilometres from the geographic pole, and it wanders slowly from year to year.

So there are two norths. True north lies along the meridian, fixed by the spin axis. Magnetic north lies along the field, and the compass needle follows it. A direction measured from true north is written in degrees true. One measured from magnetic north is in degrees magnetic.

Let's set the two side by side. True north is the geographic pole, the end of the spin axis. You find it in the meridians on every chart. It does not move. And it is used for chart tracks and forecast winds.

Magnetic north is where the earth's magnetic field points. You find it with the compass needle. It moves slowly, year by year. And it is used for runways, for headings and winds from ATC, air traffic control, and for what you steer.

Question. Why can you not simply steer the true track you measured on the chart?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because the compass points to magnetic north, not true north, and the two differ by the local variation. The true track has to be converted to magnetic before it can be compared with the compass or the direction indicator.

## Variation and deviation

There are two corrections between the chart and the compass. Variation belongs to the place. Deviation belongs to the aeroplane. Let's take variation first.

Stand anywhere on the earth and look along the meridian to true north. Then look at the compass needle. The angle between the two is the variation at that place. It is named east if magnetic north lies east of true north, and west if it lies west. The chart joins places of equal variation with dashed lines called isogonal lines, or isogonals, each labelled with its value.

Variation depends on where you are, not on the aeroplane. It changes as you move across the country, and slowly over the years as magnetic north wanders. Around Perth it is small, about 1 to 2 degrees west. In Sydney it is about 12 degrees east. Verify both against the current chart, because they change.

So a trip from Perth to the eastern states passes through zero variation and into easterly values. A habit formed at Jandakot, where a forgotten variation costs you only a couple of degrees, can cost you a dozen there.

Now deviation. The compass in your aeroplane sits among steel, an engine, radios and wiring, each with its own magnetic field. They pull the needle away from magnetic north. That error is the deviation. And the direction the compass shows is a compass direction, in degrees compass.

Deviation depends on the aeroplane and its heading. Turn the aeroplane, and its own magnetism turns with it, sitting at a new angle to the earth's field. Each aeroplane's deviation is measured in a compass swing. It is written on the compass correction card near the compass, often in the form "for 240, steer 237".

[[diagram:true-magnetic-compass]]
Picture three lines fanning out from one point. The first is true north, along the meridian. The second is magnetic north, offset from it by the variation. The third is compass north, offset again by the deviation. The angles are exaggerated so you can see them. A track of 120 degrees true, with 3 degrees of east variation, becomes {117|one one seven} magnetic. With 2 degrees of west deviation, it becomes {119|one one nine} compass. Variation east is subtracted, and deviation west is added.

Now applying them. The chain runs from the chart to the cockpit: true, then magnetic, then compass. Variation turns true into magnetic. Deviation turns magnetic into compass. And one rule covers both steps.

Variation east, magnetic least. Magnetic equals true minus an easterly variation. Variation west, magnetic best. Magnetic equals true plus a westerly variation. And the same for deviation: deviation east, compass least. Deviation west, compass best.

Why subtract an easterly variation? If magnetic north lies east of true north, the compass's zero is already turned towards the east. So every direction measured from it is smaller by that amount. Going the other way, from compass back to true, reverse every sign.

Let's work one, here in WA. The track measured on the chart is {245|two four five} degrees true. The variation is 2 degrees west. The deviation on that heading is 3 degrees east, and the card says "for 240, steer 237". What is the compass heading, ignoring wind? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
First, magnetic. Variation west is best, so 245 plus 2 is {247|two four seven} degrees magnetic. Then compass. Deviation east is least, so 247 minus 3 is {244|two four four} degrees compass. The card's "for 240, steer 237" is the same 3 degrees east of deviation, written as an instruction. So the answer is {244|two four four} degrees compass.

Now the same idea in the east. A track of {038|zero three eight} degrees true near Sydney. Variation 12 degrees east. Deviation 2 degrees west on that heading. Try it.
[[pause 6]]
Magnetic first. East is least, so {038|zero three eight} minus 12 is {026|zero two six} degrees magnetic. Then compass. West is best, so {026|zero two six} plus 2 is {028|zero two eight} degrees compass. So the answer is {028|zero two eight} compass. Twelve degrees of variation is big enough that ignoring it would put you about 1 NM off track for every 5 NM flown.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Variation belongs to the place, and is read from the chart. Deviation belongs to the aeroplane and its heading, and is read from the card. And the memory aid works from true towards compass. Run it backwards without reversing the signs, and your answer is wrong by twice the correction.

Question. On the way from Perth to Kalgoorlie you hold one heading the whole way. Which changes, variation or deviation?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: variation, because it depends on where you are, and you cross isogonals as you go east. Deviation stays the same, because it depends on the aeroplane's heading, which has not changed.

## Magnetic heading, relative bearing and magnetic bearing

Cruising north-east over the Wheatbelt, you see a salt lake well out to the left. To use it as a position line, you need its direction from you, as a magnetic bearing you can plot. But the only thing you can measure directly is its angle from the nose.

That angle, measured clockwise from the nose, from 000 to 360, is the relative bearing, written RB. An object straight ahead is at 000 relative. One on the right wingtip is at {090|zero nine zero}. One behind is at {180|one eight zero}. And one on the left wingtip is at {270|two seven zero}. It is the clock code in degrees: each hour is 30 degrees.

[[diagram:clock-code-traffic]]
Picture an aeroplane heading {090|zero nine zero}, due east, with a clock face laid flat around it and 12 o'clock on the nose. Traffic at two o'clock is 60 degrees right of the nose, so it bears {150|one five zero}. Traffic at ten o'clock is 60 degrees left of the nose, so it bears {030|zero three zero}. The point to notice: an angle measured from the nose is added to the heading to give a bearing from north.

The nose points along the magnetic heading, written MH. That is the angle from magnetic north to the nose. The relative bearing is the angle from the nose to the object. Add the two angles, and you have the angle from magnetic north to the object. That is its magnetic bearing from the aircraft, written MB.

So the formula is: magnetic bearing equals magnetic heading plus relative bearing. And if the result is more than 360, subtract 360.

The bearing you have found is from you to the object. To plot a position line on the chart, you need the bearing from the object to you. That is its reciprocal: add or subtract 180. Draw that line out from the lake, and you are somewhere on it.

[[diagram:mh-rb-mb-bearings]]
Picture the aeroplane from above, with magnetic north straight up. It is heading {070|zero seven zero} degrees magnetic, up and to the right. A lake lies 30 degrees left of the nose. Measured clockwise from the nose, all the way round, that is a relative bearing of 330. The heading and the relative bearing join end to end, and together they span from magnetic north to the lake.

Let's work that one. Heading {070|zero seven zero} degrees magnetic. A lake lies 30 degrees left of the nose. Variation 2 degrees west. What bearing goes on the chart? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
First, the relative bearing. 30 degrees left of the nose is 360 minus 30, which is 330 relative. Second, the magnetic bearing to the lake. {070|zero seven zero} plus 330 is 400. Take off 360, and it is {040|zero four zero} degrees magnetic. Third, the true bearing to plot. Variation west, so true is magnetic minus 2. {040|zero four zero} minus 2 is {038|zero three eight} degrees true. Fourth, the bearing from the lake to you. {040|zero four zero} plus 180 is {220|two two zero} degrees magnetic. So on the chart you draw a line from the lake on {218|two one eight} degrees true, which is 220 magnetic converted to true. And you are on it.

The formula rearranges for the other questions the exam asks. Suppose you know the bearing of a landmark and your heading, and you want to know where to look. Then relative bearing equals magnetic bearing minus magnetic heading, adding 360 if the answer is negative. A landmark bearing {200|two zero zero} degrees magnetic, while you head {110|one one zero} magnetic, is at 200 minus 110, which is {090|zero nine zero} relative. That is on the right wingtip.

And if you know the bearing and where it appears, magnetic heading equals magnetic bearing minus relative bearing.

This is the arithmetic an ADF uses too. The ADF is the automatic direction finder. Its needle shows the relative bearing of a beacon, and heading plus relative bearing gives the magnetic bearing to it. You'll find that in the lesson on radio navigation aids.

Now try one yourself. You are heading {155|one five five} degrees magnetic, and a dam lies exactly off your left wingtip. What is its magnetic bearing from you, and what is your bearing from the dam? Pause and work it out.
[[pause 6]]
The left wingtip is {270|two seven zero} relative. So the magnetic bearing is 155 plus 270, which is 425. Take off 360, and it is {065|zero six five} degrees magnetic to the dam. The reciprocal, from the dam to you, is {065|zero six five} plus 180, which is {245|two four five} degrees magnetic. A sanity check. You are heading roughly south-south-east, so the left wingtip points roughly east-north-east, which is {065|zero six five}. So the dam bears {065|zero six five}, and you bear {245|two four five} from it.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Left of the nose is not a negative relative bearing to add. It is 360 minus the angle. And magnetic heading plus relative bearing gives the bearing to the object. If the question asks for the bearing from the object, or the radial you are on, add or subtract 180 at the end.

Question. Why does magnetic bearing equal magnetic heading plus relative bearing?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because both are angles measured clockwise, and they join end to end. The heading runs from magnetic north to the nose. The relative bearing runs from the nose to the object. So together they span from magnetic north to the object.

## Bringing it together

You are planning Jandakot to Kalgoorlie. ERSA gives you both positions in latitude and longitude, and you plot them on the WAC against the graticule.

The ruled line is close to a great circle, so it is the shortest route. You measure its true track against a meridian near the middle, because the meridians converge and the angle changes along the line. You walk the dividers along the latitude scale, not the longitude scale, and get the distance in nautical miles.

Along the line the isogonals change. So you note the variation for each leg, and convert each true track to magnetic, then to compass with the card.

Four hours later, the sun is in the west earlier than at home, because Kalgoorlie lies further east. You see a mine headframe 60 degrees right of the nose, while heading {085|zero eight five} degrees magnetic. {085|zero eight five} plus 60 is {145|one four five} degrees magnetic to the headframe. From it to you is {325|three two five} magnetic. And a position line goes on the chart.

## Key points

Let's recap.

The earth is very nearly a sphere, slightly flattened at the poles. It is an oblate spheroid, about 43 km wider across the equator. It spins west to east once a day, 15 degrees an hour, about the axis through the true poles.

Latitude runs from 0 to 90 degrees north or south of the equator, along parallels. Longitude runs from 0 to 180 degrees east or west of Greenwich, along meridians. Positions are written latitude first, in degrees and minutes. A great circle is the shortest path. A rhumb line keeps a constant direction.

True north is the geographic pole, and the meridians point to it. Magnetic north is where the compass points, and it wanders.

Distance is in nautical miles, one minute of latitude each, measured on the latitude scale. Direction is clockwise from north, measured on the chart against a meridian near the middle of the track, so it is true.

Variation belongs to the place, and is read from the isogonals. It turns true into magnetic. Deviation belongs to the aeroplane and its heading, and is read from the card. It turns magnetic into compass. East is least, west is best. Reverse the signs going back.

Magnetic bearing equals magnetic heading plus relative bearing, wrapping at 360. And the reciprocal, plus or minus 180, is the bearing from the object to you.

A final exam tip. Expect a descriptive question on the shape and rotation of the earth, or on what latitude and longitude are. Expect a true to magnetic conversion. And expect a question on magnetic heading plus relative bearing. Measure distance on the latitude scale, and direction at the mid-meridian. Apply variation east, magnetic least, and the same for deviation. And remember that heading plus relative bearing gives the bearing to the object. The question may want its reciprocal.

That's the end of PNVC 2.1.
