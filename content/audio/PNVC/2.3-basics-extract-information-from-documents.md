This is PNVC 2.3, Extracting information from charts and documents. By the end you will be able to read the main chart symbols, judge the terrain, measure a track and a distance, and hold the chart the right way in flight. And you will be able to pull runway and restricted area data out of ERSA. There are worked examples and questions along the way, so pause whenever you want a moment to think.

## What the exam expects (PNVC 2.3)

The exam wants three groups of things from you. First, the charts. Recognise the major features without the legend: roads, rivers and lakes, obstacles and spot heights, and the airspace, PRD areas and aerodrome data on the VTC or ERC. Decode everything else with the legend. Judge the height of the terrain, estimate a track and a distance, and orient the chart in flight. Second, use the legend to identify airspace boundaries on the visual charts. And third, extract runway data, and the data for prohibited, restricted and danger areas, from ERSA.

## Why this matters

In the RPL you met the charts and ERSA as documents. The PPL turns that into a working skill.

Picture planning Jandakot to Kalgoorlie. You draw a line across two WACs, measure it, and read the high ground along it. You pick out the railway and the Great Eastern Highway as features to follow. You check the Perth steps and the restricted areas around RAAF Pearce on the VNC. Then you go to ERSA for the runway at your destination, and for the hours of every restricted area your track touches. And in the air, you hold the chart so the country below matches it.

The exam gives you an extract of a chart or an ERSA page, and asks you to read it quickly and correctly. An obstacle's two figures. A distance on a stated scale. The meaning of a boundary line. Whether an area is active at your ETA, your estimated time of arrival.

## Charts at a glance

You already know the family, so here it is in brief. The WAC, the World Aeronautical Chart, is at {1:1,000,000|one to one million}. It shows the ground, and no airspace. The VNC, the Visual Navigation Chart, is at {1:500,000|one to five hundred thousand}. The VTC, the Visual Terminal Chart, is at {1:250,000|one to two hundred and fifty thousand}. Those two show the ground and the airspace together. And the ERC-L, the En Route Chart Low, shows airspace and air routes, but no topography.

Some symbols you must read at sight, because in the air you have no time to look up a railway. The rest you read with the legend, while planning.

Picture four chart panels side by side. The WAC, on the left, has topography and no airspace. The VNC and the VTC add the airspace, PRD areas and frequencies to the ground, the VTC in more detail. And the ERC-L, on the right, has airspace, air routes and navigation aids, with no topography at all.

## Features you should know without the legend

Picture the view from 3,500 ft east of the Darling Scarp. Wheat paddocks, a highway and a railway side by side, a string of salt lakes, and a town every 20 or 30 miles. Each has a symbol you should know at sight. Here is how they look on the WAC; verify each against your current chart's legend.

Roads are red for major sealed roads, and brown or dashed for minor and unsealed ones.

Railways are black lines with cross ticks, a single line for a single track. Railways and highways often run together out of Wheatbelt towns, and the ticks tell you which line is which.

Power lines are red lines carrying their own symbol.

Rivers are blue. Solid for perennial, and dashed for intermittent, which is most rivers in WA. A dry riverbed shows as a line of trees, not water.

Lakes have a blue outline. Salt lakes have a distinctive fill. They are often the best landmarks in the Wheatbelt: white, oddly shaped, and visible from a long way off.

The coastline is shown with the sea in blue. Towns are yellow shapes or circles, sized by the town. And in the outback, where they may be the only features for miles, homesteads, mines, tanks, windmills and vermin fences each have a symbol.

[[diagram:chart-legend-symbols]]
Picture a sheet of sample symbols, like a chart legend: tints and contours, a spot height, rivers, lakes, towns, roads, railways, power lines, obstacles, aerodromes and isogonals. The ones to fix in your mind are the railway's cross ticks, the dashed intermittent river, the salt lake's own fill, and the obstacle's two figures.

Learn the symbols so that your attention goes on the real question. Will this feature be unmistakable from the air? A line feature crossing your track, or a distinctive lake, beats a homestead that looks like every other homestead.

Now spot heights and obstacles. A spot height is a dot with a number beside it. The number is the surveyed elevation of that point, in feet above mean sea level. It is usually a hilltop.

An obstacle is a tall structure: a mast, a tower, a chimney or a wind turbine. Its symbol carries two figures, and they answer two different questions.

The larger figure, without brackets, is the elevation of the top above mean sea level, AMSL. That is what you compare with your altimeter set to QNH, because the altimeter reads height above sea level.

The figure in brackets is the height of the structure above the ground it stands on. It tells you how much the structure sticks up from the land around it, and so how visible it will be.

Lit obstacles carry a flash symbol, and a group of obstacles has its own symbol. Confirm which figure is which on your chart's legend. The convention given here is the one the exam uses.

Let's work one. An obstacle on the WAC is marked 1,250, with 400 in brackets. What do the two figures tell you, and how high is the ground at its base? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
Step one. The figure without brackets is the elevation of the top: 1,250 ft AMSL.

Step two. The bracketed figure is the height of the structure above the ground: 400 ft.

Step three, the ground at its base. 1,250 minus 400 is 850 ft AMSL.

So on a QNH altimeter, the top of the mast is at 1,250 ft. Flying at 1,500 ft, you would pass only 250 ft above it.

Now one for you. A mast near your track is marked 2,180, with 520 in brackets. What is the elevation of the ground it stands on? And what altimeter reading on QNH puts you 500 ft above its top?
[[pause 6]]
The ground first. 2,180 minus 520 is 1,660 ft AMSL. Then the top of the mast is at 2,180 ft. So 500 ft above it is 2,180 plus 500, which is 2,680 ft on QNH. The 520 ft only tells you how tall the mast looks from the ground. The altimeter cares about the 2,180.

So the ground is at 1,660 ft, and you need 2,680 ft on QNH.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. It offers the bracketed figure as the height to clear. It is not. The altimeter reads above sea level, so the figure to compare with it is the elevation of the top. Adding the two figures together is the other distractor. The elevation already includes the structure.

There is a third set of things to read without the legend. They are on the VTC, and en route on the ERC-L, and you will be looking at them in the busiest part of the flight. First, controlled airspace: the control zone around a towered aerodrome, and the CTA steps around it, each labelled with its lower and upper limits. CTA means controlled airspace. Second, PRD areas, the prohibited, restricted and danger areas, each with its identifier, the letter P, R or D and a number, and its vertical limits. Third, VFR routes and reporting points, the paths and named places you fly and report at in the terminal area. And fourth, aerodromes, with their elevation, runway alignment and frequency, and the navigation aids.

The VTC also prints a maximum elevation figure in each grid square. It is the highest terrain or obstacle in that square, rounded up with a margin. With it, you can pick a safe height for a whole area at a glance.

Question. An obstacle reads 940, with 380 in brackets. You are cruising at 1,200 ft on the area QNH. Which figure do you compare with your altimeter, and by how much do you clear the top?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the 940, because it is the elevation of the top above sea level, and that is what a QNH altimeter measures against. You clear it by 1,200 minus 940, which is 260 ft. The 380 is the structure's height above its own ground.

## Using the legend for everything else

No pilot remembers every symbol. The kinds of aerodrome, heliports and ALAs, the aeroplane landing areas. The navigation aid roses. Glider and parachute activity. For anything outside the core set, you decode it from the legend printed on the chart, and the exam allows you to use it. The skill is reading it carefully, rather than guessing.

Airspace is where the legend earns its keep, and the syllabus names it separately. Controlled airspace is layered, like an upside-down wedding cake. There is a control zone from the surface around the aerodrome. Then come steps whose lower limits rise as you move away from it, with Class G underneath each step. A chart is flat. So it has to show that three-dimensional shape with line styles, colours and labels.

[[diagram:airspace-cross-section]]
Picture a slice through the airspace, seen from the side. A Class C control zone starts at the surface at a major airport, with Class C steps around it whose bases rise with distance. A regional tower has a smaller Class D zone. Class E sits above the steps, Class A at high level, and Class G underneath, with a restricted area off to one side. The chart shows each step as a boundary line, with its limits printed beside it.

With the legend, you decode these on the VTC and the VNC. The line styles and colours for Class C, D and E boundaries, and for the control zone. The vertical limits, printed as a pair, the upper limit over the lower limit. SFC means the surface, and FL a flight level. Where they are used, LL means lower limit, and UL upper limit.

Then the hatching and identifiers of restricted and danger areas. VFR route lines, lanes of entry, and their altitude limits. Reporting points, as named triangles or circles, with the compulsory and on-request kinds drawn differently. The CTAF, the common traffic advisory frequency, and the ATC frequencies. And the aerodrome categories, the navigation aid symbols, such as the VOR rose, the NDB and the DME, and the obstacle, power line, and glider or parachute symbols.

Read a step label as a band. Say a step is labelled with an upper limit of FL125, over a lower limit of 2,500 ft. That means controlled airspace from 2,500 ft up to FL125. Below 2,500 ft you are in Class G, and you need no clearance. At 2,500 ft or above, you are inside the step. That is an illustration of the format. Read the real limits from the current chart.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. The bottom number of a step label is the base of the step. It is not a height you may climb to. You stay clear by staying below the base. And the WAC shows none of this airspace. A question that asks where to find a step base, or a restricted area's limits, has the VNC, the VTC or the ERC-L as its answer.

## Judging the height of the terrain

Before you choose a cruising altitude, you need to know how high the ground under the route is. The chart shows height in three ways, and each answers a different question.

Contour lines join points of equal elevation, drawn at set intervals. Where they crowd together, the ground is steep. Where they spread out, it is gentle.

Hypsometric tints are the colour bands between the contours. They run from green or white lowlands, through yellow and brown, to the higher ground. So you can see where the high country is without reading a number.

And spot heights give the exact height of particular points, usually the tops of hills. Those stand above the general level of their tint band.

To assess a route, follow the line and note the highest tint band it crosses. The top of that band is the general height of the terrain you must clear. Then look along the line for spot heights and obstacles that stand above the band. The tint shows the general level, not every hilltop.

A rule of thumb for a comfortable cruising height is to add about 1,000 ft to the highest ground. Then check that against the minimum height rules, which you'll find in the lesson on conditions of flight, and against the VFR cruising levels.

Let's work one. Along a leg, the highest tint band is the one whose upper limit is 2,000 ft. A spot height on a small hill within that area reads 2,140 ft. Tints show the general terrain, and isolated tops can stand above the band. There is also an obstacle a mile off track, marked 2,310, with 290 in brackets. What is your rule-of-thumb minimum cruising height? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
Step one, the general terrain. The band tops out at 2,000 ft.

Step two, the highest point near the line. The obstacle, at 2,310 ft AMSL, beats the spot height at 2,140 ft.

Step three, the rule-of-thumb minimum cruising height. 2,310 plus 1,000 is 3,310 ft.

And step four. Choose the next suitable VFR cruising level above that, for your track.

The tint told you the country is up to about 2,000 ft. The spot height and the obstacle told you what pokes above it. So the answer is 3,310 ft, and then the next suitable cruising level.

[[diagram:terrain-safe-altitude]]
Picture a side view along the leg. The terrain rises into the top tint band, at 2,000 ft, with a hilltop poking above it at 2,140 ft. Higher still stands the obstacle, its top at 2,310 ft. It is the highest point, so 1,000 ft above it gives 3,310 ft.

Question. Why is the highest tint band along a route not, on its own, enough to set a safe altitude?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because the tint shows the general level of the ground between two contours. Hilltops, which are the spot heights, and obstacles stand above that level. So you must scan the route for them, and add a margin above the highest one.

## Estimating track and distance

Measuring a track is the first job of every navigation plan, and the exam expects you to do it on a WAC extract. There are four steps.

First, draw the line. Join the departure and the destination with a straight line. On a WAC that line is close to a great circle, the shortest route. You'll find great circles in the lesson on the form of the earth.

Second, measure the true track. Lay the protractor's centre on a meridian near the middle of the track, with its north line along the meridian. The meridians converge towards the pole, so the angle your line makes with them changes slightly along its length. The mid-meridian gives the average. Read the direction from the departure end, clockwise from true north. The result is a true track, because the meridians point to true north.

Third, measure the distance. Use dividers, or a scale ruler, against the latitude scale on a meridian near the track. There, 1 minute of latitude is 1 nautical mile. Or use the chart's scale bar. Never use the longitude scale, because a minute of longitude shrinks towards the poles.

And fourth, convert to magnetic. Apply the variation from the isogonal nearest the track. Variation west, magnetic best. Variation east, magnetic least.

[[diagram:true-magnetic-compass]]
Picture three lines fanning out from one point, each called north. True north runs along the meridian, and that is what the protractor measures against. Magnetic north is offset from it by the variation, read from the nearest isogonal. Compass north is offset again, by the deviation. For example, a track of {120|one two zero} degrees true, with a variation of 3 degrees east, gives {117|one one seven} magnetic. The angles are exaggerated so you can see them.

Now the scale arithmetic. When a question gives you a length on the chart and its scale, the scale is the conversion.

{1:1,000,000|One to one million} means 1 mm on the chart is 1,000,000 mm on the ground. That is 1 km. So on a WAC, a length in millimetres is the ground distance in kilometres. And dividing by 1.852 turns kilometres into nautical miles. As a rule of thumb, 1 cm on a WAC is about 5.4 NM.

On a {1:500,000|one to five hundred thousand} VNC, 1 mm is half a kilometre. So 1 cm is 5 km, about 2.7 NM.

Let's work one, track and distance on a WAC. On a {1:1,000,000|one to one million} WAC, a leg eastward from Jandakot measures 251 mm. The protractor at the mid-meridian reads {067|zero six seven} degrees true. And the nearest isogonal shows 1.5 degrees west. What are the distance and the magnetic track? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
Step one, the ground distance. 251 mm times a million is 251 million mm, which is 251 km.

Step two, in nautical miles. 251 divided by 1.852 is {135.5|one hundred and thirty-five point five}, about 136 NM.

Step three, the rule-of-thumb check. 251 mm is 25.1 cm, and 25.1 times 5.4 is {135.5|one hundred and thirty-five point five} nautical miles. It agrees.

Step four, the magnetic track. Variation west, magnetic best. So {067|zero six seven} plus 1.5 is {068.5|zero six eight point five}, about {068|zero six eight} degrees magnetic.

So the leg is roughly 136 NM, on about {068|zero six eight} degrees magnetic. At 110 kt, that is about an hour and a quarter.

Now distance on a VNC. On a {1:500,000|one to five hundred thousand} VNC, a leg measures 120 mm. How far is it? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
Step one, the ground distance. At {1:500,000|one to five hundred thousand}, 1 mm is 0.5 km. So 120 times 0.5 is 60 km.

Step two, in nautical miles. 60 divided by 1.852 is 32.4, about 32 NM.

The same 120 mm on a WAC would be twice as far: 120 km, or 65 NM. So always check which chart, and which scale, before you convert. On the VNC, the answer is about 32 NM.

On the latitude scale, the arithmetic disappears. Open the dividers along the track. Then lay them on a meridian beside it. If they span 42 minutes of latitude, the leg is 42 NM.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Three slips are common. The first is measuring distance on the longitude scale, which overstates it everywhere except at the equator. The second is measuring the track at one end of a long line, instead of at the mid-meridian. And the third is forgetting that the protractor gives true. The track you fly is magnetic, after the variation.

Question. Why do you measure the track at a meridian near the middle of the line?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because the meridians converge, so the angle between a straight line and the meridians changes along the line. Measuring at the middle gives the average track for the whole leg.

## Orienting the chart in flight

On the ground you read a chart north-up. In the air, that becomes a trap. Fly south with the chart north-up, and a lake on your left appears on the right of the chart. Every feature has to be turned round in your head, and under workload that is the step that goes wrong.

The answer is to turn the chart so that the track line points in the direction of flight. That is called track up. Ground features then appear on the chart in the same relative positions as they appear outside.

A lake right of the track line on the chart is right of the nose. You can glance from the chart to the ground and back without any mental rotation, which is the whole point.

Two habits go with it. First, fold the chart to the area of the current leg. That keeps it manageable in the cockpit, with the part you need on top. Second, keep your thumb, or a mark, at the last fix. Then you can find your place instantly after a radio call or a look-out scan.

And read from the map to the ground. Pick the next feature on the chart, and look for it. Don't see something outside and then hunt for it on the chart. The lesson on pilot navigation builds the whole technique of map reading on this.

Question. You are flying a track of {200|two zero zero} degrees magnetic, with the chart held north-up. A railway on the chart lies to the right of your track line. Where is it outside?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: on your left. Flying roughly south with the chart north-up, the chart's right is your left. Turn the chart track-up, and the railway appears on the same side as it does outside. That is why you orient the chart that way.

## Reading ERSA for runways and restricted areas

The charts tell you where things are. ERSA, the En Route Supplement Australia, tells you how to use them. The RPL lesson on documentation walks through an entry field by field. At PPL level, the exam gives you a route. It expects you to pull out the runway data for the destination, and the PRD data for every area the track touches. And it expects you to combine the hours with your ETA.

Start with the runway data. The ERSA FAC entry for an aerodrome gives the runway designators. Then the length and width, in metres. Then the surface, the slope, the lighting and the pavement strength. Together with those, it gives the aerodrome elevation, in feet, its frequencies, and the local procedures.

Each piece goes somewhere in your plan. The length and surface go into the performance charts. The slope tells you which way is downhill. The designators are what you compare with the wind. The lighting tells you about use after dark. And the remarks tell you how to join.

[[diagram:ersa-entry-anatomy]]
Picture an ERSA entry for an invented aerodrome, laid out in blocks from top to bottom, with a label on each. At the top, the header, with the elevation, position, variation, time zone and operator. Below it, the runway table: designators, length and width in metres, surface, slope, strength and lighting. Then the communications and navigation aids. And at the bottom, the remarks and local traffic regulations. Notice that the runway length and width are in metres, while the elevation in the header is in feet.

Let's work one, at the invented aerodrome Example Creek. Its entry gives runway {06|zero six} and {24|two four}: 1,200 by 30, sealed, 0.5 percent up to the east, and lit. The elevation is 1,050 ft. And the remarks give right-hand circuits for runway {24|two four}. Your POH, the pilot's operating handbook, has a take-off chart that works in feet. What do you take from the entry? Pause if you'd like to think it through.
[[pause 6]]
Step one, the length in feet. 1,200 times 3.28 is 3,936 ft, to enter in the chart or compare with it.

Step two, the slope. The runway rises to the east. Runway {24|two four} points roughly {240|two four zero} degrees magnetic, west-south-west. So runway {24|two four} slopes downhill for take-off, and runway {06|zero six} uphill.

Step three, the elevation for pressure height and density height: 1,050 ft.

Step four, the lighting. Runway {06|zero six} and {24|two four} is lit. So it is usable for a night landing by a suitably rated pilot.

Step five, the circuit. Joining for runway {24|two four}, you fly right-hand circuits.

So one line of the table, and one remark, fed the performance chart, the runway choice and the join: 3,936 ft, sealed, downhill on {24|two four}, lit, and a right-hand circuit.

Now the PRD data. For each prohibited, restricted or danger area, the ERSA PRD section gives six things. Its identifier and name. Its lateral and vertical limits. Its hours of activity. Its controlling authority. And, for a restricted area, its conditional status. That status says whether a clearance is normally available, which is {RA1|R A one}. Or not to be planned on, which is {RA2|R A two}. Or not available at all, which is {RA3|R A three}.

Specified hours are in UTC. So for a WA flight, you add 8 hours, and compare the result with your ETA in WST. You'll find that conversion in the lessons on time.

An area shown as activated by NOTAM is inactive unless a NOTAM has activated it. And a NOTAM can change any area's hours or limits. So the check always ends with the NOTAMs.

[[diagram:utc-to-wst-activity]]
Picture two clock scales, one above the other: UTC on top, and WST underneath, lined up so each instant sits directly above itself. An area listed active from {2200|twenty-two hundred} to {0600|oh six hundred} UTC shows as a bar across the top scale. Directly below it, the same bar runs from {0600|oh six hundred} to {1400|fourteen hundred} WST. Add 8 hours: the same moments, read on a different clock.

Let's work one. Is the area active at your ETA? A restricted area beside your track is listed active {2200|twenty-two hundred} to {0600|oh six hundred} UTC, with status {RA2|R A two}. You plan to pass it at {1340|thirteen forty} WST. Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
Step one, the start of activity. {2200|Twenty-two hundred} plus 8 hours is {3000|thirty hundred}. Minus 24, that is {0600|oh six hundred} WST.

Step two, the end of activity. {0600|Oh six hundred} plus 8 hours is {1400|fourteen hundred} WST.

Step three, compare. {1340|Thirteen forty} WST is inside {0600|oh six hundred} to {1400|fourteen hundred}. So the area is active when you get there.

Step four, the status. {RA2|R A two} means do not plan through it. Either route around it, or plan to pass after {1400|fourteen hundred} WST. And check the NOTAMs either way.

So the area is active at your ETA. Twenty minutes either side decided the route.

[[diagram:airspace-active-check]]
Picture four steps from left to right. The chart, for the boundaries and limits. The ERSA PRD section, for the hours and the controlling authority. The NOTAMs, for changes. And, if in doubt, the controlling authority itself. Then the rule: an active restricted area needs a clearance, and without one, you stay out.

Question. The chart shows a restricted area across your track, and its limits. Why is that not enough to plan the flight?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the chart gives where the area is and how high it goes. But it does not say when it is active, who controls it, or whether a clearance is likely. Those come from the ERSA PRD section. And NOTAMs can change them on the day.

## Bringing it together

You are planning a flight from Jandakot to a Wheatbelt strip.

On the WAC, you draw the line. You measure the true track at the mid-meridian, and the distance on the latitude scale. Then you apply the variation.

Along the line, the scarp's tint band, a spot height and a mast's two figures set your cruising height. It is 1,000 ft above the highest of them.

You mark the features you will use. The highway and the ticked railway together. A salt lake right of track. And the town where the railway branches.

On the VNC, you decode the Perth step bases with the legend, and plan to stay under them. You spot a restricted area beside the track. ERSA gives its hours in UTC. Adding 8 shows it active when you will pass, so you route around it.

The FAC entry gives the strip's runway: its length in metres, gravel, its slope, and unlit.

And in the air, you hold the chart track-up, with your thumb on the last fix, and read from map to ground.

## Key points

Let's recap.

Know at sight: red roads, black ticked railways, blue rivers, dashed when intermittent, salt lakes, and yellow towns. Spot heights are in feet AMSL. An obstacle gives the elevation of its top AMSL, with its height above ground in brackets. On the VTC and ERC-L, know the CTA steps, PRD areas, VFR routes, aerodromes and navaids.

Everything else, decode from the chart legend. Knowing where to look beats guessing.

Tints show the general height, contours the steepness, and spot heights and obstacles what stands above. Add about 1,000 ft to the highest point for a comfortable height.

Measure the true track at the mid-meridian, and the distance on the latitude scale, where 1 minute is 1 NM. Then apply the variation. On a WAC, 1 mm is 1 km, and 1 cm is about 5.4 NM. On a VNC, 1 cm is 5 km.

Fly with the chart track-up, so features appear on the same side as outside. Fold it to the leg, and keep a thumb on the last fix.

Airspace boundaries are line styles and colours, with the limits printed as upper over lower. You stay clear of a step by staying below its base. And the WAC shows none of it.

ERSA FAC: the designators, length and width in metres, surface, slope, lighting, strength, elevation in feet, frequencies and remarks. ERSA PRD: the identifier, limits, hours in UTC, which you add 8 to for WST, the controlling authority and the conditional status. Always finish with the NOTAMs.

A final exam tip. Obstacle figures: the big number is the elevation AMSL, and the bracketed number is the height AGL, above ground level. Measure distance on the latitude scale, and the track at the mid-meridian. On a WAC, read millimetres as kilometres before dividing by 1.852. Orient the chart track-up, and read map to ground. And for any PRD question, convert the UTC hours to WST before comparing them with the ETA.

That's the end of PNVC 2.3.
