This is PNVC 2.6, Radio navigation aids. By the end you will be able to say what an NDB, a VOR and a DME each measure, turn an ADF needle into a position line, read a VOR display, find an aid's details in ERSA and on the en route chart, and give the rated coverage of a VOR at your altitude. There are worked examples and questions along the way, so pause whenever you want a moment to think.

## What the exam expects (PNVC 2.6)

The exam wants two things from you, and they sit in a single element. First, to find the details of a beacon or a VOR in ERSA or on the en route chart: its identifier, its frequency, its position, its hours, its coverage and its limitations. And second, to state the rated coverage of a VOR at the heights a private pilot flies.

## Why this matters

A VFR flight in WA is navigated by map and clock. But the radio aids are still there to cross-check you. A VOR with a DME tells you which line you are on and how far out you are. And an ADF needle points at an NDB.

Used well, they turn a doubtful fix over featureless wheatbelt into a certain one. Used carelessly, with the wrong station tuned, an aid beyond its coverage, or an NDB needle swinging towards a thunderstorm, they put you confidently in the wrong place.

The syllabus element is narrow: reading aid details from ERSA and the en route chart, and knowing VOR coverage. But you cannot read those entries sensibly without knowing what each aid measures. So this lesson teaches the aids from first principles, then where their details are published, then how far you can trust them.

## Two aids, two questions

Every radio aid answers one geometric question, and knowing which question is the whole key to using it.

First, an NDB, a non-directional beacon on the ground, with an ADF, an automatic direction finder in the aeroplane. Together they answer one question. Which way is the station from my nose?

Second, a VOR, a VHF omnidirectional range, answers: which magnetic bearing from the station am I on?

And third, a DME, distance measuring equipment, usually co-located with a VOR. It answers: how far am I from the station?

The first answer depends on which way the aeroplane is pointing. The second and third do not. That difference drives everything that follows.

## The NDB and the ADF needle

A non-directional beacon is a ground transmitter. It radiates the same medium-frequency signal in every direction, somewhere between 190 and 1,750 kHz. It carries no directional information at all.

The cleverness is in the aeroplane. The automatic direction finder uses its aerials to sense the direction the signal arrives from, and it drives a needle to point at the station.

On the basic instrument, the fixed-card ADF, the card has zero at the top, and the top is always the nose. So the needle shows the relative bearing, RB for short. That is the angle from the nose, measured clockwise, to the station. A needle on {000|zero zero zero} means the station is dead ahead. {090|zero nine zero} means it is off the right wingtip. {180|one eight zero} means behind. And {270|two seven zero} means off the left wingtip.

You already use this idea in the circuit. Traffic called at two o'clock is about 60 degrees right of the nose, wherever the nose happens to point. A relative bearing is the same thing in degrees.

[[diagram:clock-code-traffic]]
Picture an aeroplane heading {090|zero nine zero}, with a clock face laid flat around it and 12 o'clock on the nose. Traffic at two o'clock is 60 degrees right of the nose. Add that to the heading, and it bears {150|one five zero}. Traffic at ten o'clock is 60 degrees left, and bears {030|zero three zero}. The ADF needle works exactly the same way.

Because the needle is tied to the nose, turn the aeroplane and the needle moves, even though the station has not. That is why the ADF alone cannot tell you where you are. You need the heading as well.

## From relative bearing to magnetic bearing

The direction of the station that you can draw on a chart is its magnetic bearing, MB for short. That is the angle from magnetic north, clockwise, to the station.

You get it by adding two angles. The first is the angle from north to your nose, which is the magnetic heading. The second is the angle from your nose to the station, which is the relative bearing. So the magnetic bearing to the station equals the magnetic heading plus the relative bearing. If the sum is more than 360, subtract 360.

The bearing from the station is the line you actually draw outward from the NDB on the chart. It is the reciprocal, so add or subtract 180. In Q-code terms, the bearing to the station is the QDM, and the bearing from it is the QDR. You met the same addition in the lesson on the form of the earth.

[[diagram:compass-rose-headings]]
Picture a compass rose, with north at the top and directions running clockwise as three figures. Any bearing and its reciprocal sit on opposite sides of the circle, 180 degrees apart. So {160|one six zero}, a little east of south, has its reciprocal at {340|three four zero}, a little west of north.

Let's work one. The magnetic heading is {120|one two zero} degrees magnetic, and the ADF needle is on {040|zero four zero} relative. What is the bearing to the station, and which line from the station are you on? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
Step one, the bearing to the station. 120 plus 40 is {160|one six zero} degrees magnetic.

Step two, the bearing from the station, the QDR. 160 plus 180 is {340|three four zero} degrees magnetic.

So you are somewhere on the {340|three four zero} line drawn outward from the NDB, north-north-west of it. One NDB gives one position line. Cross it with a map feature or a second aid for a fix. And if you turned onto {160|one six zero}, with no wind the needle would sit on {000|zero zero zero}, on the nose.

Now try one where the sum passes 360. The heading is {300|three zero zero} degrees magnetic, and the needle is on {270|two seven zero} relative, off the left wingtip. What is the bearing to the station, and which position line from the station are you on?
[[pause 6]]
300 plus 270 is 570. Take away 360, and the bearing to the station is {210|two one zero} degrees magnetic. The reciprocal is 210 minus 180, which is {030|zero three zero}. So you are on the {030|zero three zero} line from the NDB, north-north-east of it.

Check it with a picture. You are heading north-west, and the station is off your left wing. That puts the station to your south-west, and {210|two one zero} is south-west. So: {210|two one zero} to the station, and the {030|zero three zero} line from it.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Reading the relative bearing as if it were a magnetic bearing. A needle on {040|zero four zero} says 40 degrees right of the nose, and nothing more. Without the heading, it says nothing about where you are. The exam's distractors include the bare needle reading, and the wrong reciprocal.

Question. You turn the aeroplane 30 degrees right. What happens to the relative bearing of the NDB, and to its magnetic bearing?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the relative bearing decreases by 30 degrees, because the nose has swung 30 degrees towards the station. The magnetic bearing is unchanged. The station and your position have not moved. The magnetic heading went up by exactly what the relative bearing went down.

## Why an NDB can mislead you

Medium-frequency signals do not travel only in straight lines. They follow the ground, they reflect off the ionosphere, and they bend at boundaries. Each of these produces a known error, and the exam lists four.

The first is night effect. After dark, the ionosphere reflects more of the signal back to earth. The ADF receives the direct ground wave and a sky wave from a different direction at once, and the needle wanders. It is worst around dusk and dawn, when the ionosphere is changing.

The second is coastal refraction. A ground wave crossing a coastline bends, because it travels slightly differently over sea and over land. A signal crossing the coast at a shallow angle is bent the most. So the needle points slightly away from the true direction of the station. Flying along the WA coast, treat bearings with suspicion from an NDB whose signal crosses the coastline at a shallow angle.

The third is thunderstorm effect, also called static effect. Lightning is a powerful medium-frequency radio transmitter. The needle can swing towards a thunderstorm, and point at it rather than at the station.

And the fourth is mountain effect. Reflections from high ground make the needle fluctuate, or point wrongly, near mountains.

So, four errors: night, coastal, thunderstorm and mountain.

The signal travels along the ground, and its strength depends on the transmitter. So an NDB's useful range is set by its power, not by your altitude. Each NDB therefore has its own rated coverage, published in ERSA. It is typically 30 to 100 nm, depending on power. Verify each station in the current ERSA.

And many Australian NDBs have been decommissioned, under the navigation aid rationalisation program. So an NDB on an old chart may simply not be there. Check the ERSA entry and the NOTAMs before you plan to use one.

Question. Late on a summer afternoon, a towering cumulus is building 20 nm to your left, and the ADF needle has swung towards it. Which error is this, and what do you do?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: thunderstorm effect, also called static effect. Lightning is a strong medium-frequency source, and the ADF points at it. Disregard the ADF until you are well clear, and navigate by the map and the clock.

## The VOR: a bearing that does not care about your heading

A VHF omnidirectional range works the other way round from an NDB. The information is built into the transmission itself.

The station sends two signals. One, the reference, is the same in every direction. The other, the variable signal, rotates, so that its timing relative to the reference differs with direction from the station. The receiver measures that difference, and from it works out the magnetic bearing from the station on which the aeroplane lies.

Each of those magnetic bearings from the station is a radial. Think of 360 spokes radiating from the VOR, numbered clockwise from magnetic north as three-figure groups, exactly like the compass rose. An aeroplane on the {090|zero nine zero} radial is due magnetic east of the station. On the {180|one eight zero} radial, it is due south.

The aeroplane's heading plays no part. Point the nose anywhere, and you are still on the same radial.

VORs operate between 108 and 118 MHz. Being VHF, their signal travels in straight lines. So it does not suffer the NDB's night, coastal and thunderstorm effects. A VOR bearing still carries an error of a degree or two, and that matters for area navigation, in the next lesson. The price of straight lines is that the signal stops at the horizon. That is where the coverage rules come from, later in this lesson.

## Reading the VOR display

The VOR indicator has three parts.

The first is the omni bearing selector, the OBS. It is a knob that sets a course on a rotating card.

The second is the course deviation indicator, the CDI. It is a vertical needle that shows whether the selected course lies left or right of you. Fly towards the needle to centre it. The deflection shows angular distance off the selected course, not miles. So a given deflection means fewer miles off track as you get closer to the station.

And the third is the TO FROM flag. It shows whether flying the selected course would take you towards the station, or away from it. A red OFF flag, or on some sets a NAV flag, means the signal is unusable.

The TO FROM flag depends on where you are, not on your heading. Imagine a line through the station, at right angles to the selected course. If flying the selected course from where you are would carry you across that line, you have not yet passed the station, and the flag reads TO. If you are already beyond it, the flag reads FROM.

With the needle centred, a FROM flag means you are on the radial you have selected. A TO flag means you are on its reciprocal.

Let's work one. The receiver shows the aeroplane on the {090|zero nine zero} radial of a VOR. Where are you, what is the course to the station, and what do you set? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
Step one, your position. A radial is the magnetic bearing from the station. So you are due magnetic east of the VOR.

Step two, the course to the station. That is the reciprocal of the radial. 90 plus 180 is {270|two seven zero} degrees magnetic.

Step three, set {270|two seven zero} on the OBS. The needle centres, and the flag reads TO. Flying {270|two seven zero} takes you to the station.

Step four, the alternative. Had you set {090|zero nine zero}, the needle would also centre, but with FROM. Flying {090|zero nine zero} takes you away along the radial.

To track to the station, set the inbound course with TO showing, and keep the needle centred, adjusting heading for wind.

[[diagram:vor-radials-to-from]]
Picture the VOR from above, with the aeroplane out to the right on the {090|zero nine zero} radial. A dashed line runs through the station at right angles to the course, dividing the TO side from the FROM side. Both {270|two seven zero} and {090|zero nine zero} centre the needle. Only {270|two seven zero} shows TO, the course that takes you to the station, and the aeroplane's heading plays no part.

Now try the reverse. With {030|zero three zero} set on the OBS, the needle is centred and the flag reads FROM. Where are you, and what do you set to track to the station?
[[pause 6]]
A centred needle with FROM means you are on the radial you selected. That is the {030|zero three zero} radial, north-north-east of the station. To track inbound, set the reciprocal. 30 plus 180 is {210|two one zero}, which centres the needle with TO. Then fly about {210|two one zero} degrees magnetic, corrected for drift. So: on the {030|zero three zero} radial, and {210|two one zero} to go in.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Flying inbound with the outbound radial set, so TO and FROM are the wrong way round. The needle then works backwards. That is called reverse sensing. You fly towards the needle, and it moves further away. If you want to go to the station, the flag must read TO.

Question. Two aeroplanes are on the {180|one eight zero} radial of the same VOR. One is heading north, and the other is heading east. Do their VOR indications agree?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: yes. With the same course set, both show the same needle position and the same flag, because a VOR shows position relative to the station, not heading. But the ADF needles of the same two aeroplanes, tuned to an NDB at that site, would point in different directions.

## DME: distance, measured on a slant

Distance measuring equipment is usually paired with a VOR. It works between 962 and 1,213 MHz. The aeroplane transmits a pulse, the ground station replies, and the receiver times the round trip to give a distance in nautical miles.

That distance is the straight line from the aeroplane to the station. It is called the slant range, and it is not the distance over the ground.

Let's work one. You cross directly over a DME at 6,000 ft. One nautical mile is about 6,076 ft. What does the DME read overhead, and what does it read 20 nm out at the same height? Pause if you'd like to think it through.
[[pause 6]]
Step one, the ground distance overhead is 0 nm.

Step two, the slant range overhead. It is your height, 6,000 ft, divided by 6,076 ft per nautical mile. That is about 1.0 nm.

Step three, 20 nm out at the same height. The slant range is the square root of 20 squared plus 1.0 squared. That comes to about 20.02 nm.

So close in and high, the DME reads more than the ground distance: about 1.0 nm with you directly overhead. A few miles out, the difference is negligible.

A VOR radial is a position line. A DME distance is a circle around the station. Together they give a fix, such as the {090|zero nine zero} radial at 20 DME. That pairing is the basis of VOR DME area navigation, which is the next lesson.

## How far can you trust a VOR

VHF travels in straight lines. So a VOR can only be received while the aeroplane is above the station's radio horizon. Climb, and the horizon moves further out. Descend behind a hill, and the signal is gone.

So a VOR's usable range depends on your altitude. The AIP publishes a rated coverage: the range, by altitude band, within which the signal is reliable. AIP GEN 1.5 gives it for VOR and DME. Verify it against the current AIP.

The pattern is simple. Each higher band of 5,000 ft adds 30 nm. Below 5,000 ft, the rated coverage is 60 nm. From 5,000 ft to below 10,000 ft, it is 90 nm. From 10,000 ft to below 15,000 ft, it is 120 nm. And from 15,000 ft to below 20,000 ft, it is 150 nm.

So up to 10,000 ft, the rated coverage of a VOR is 90 nm. And below 5,000 ft, it is 60 nm.

[[diagram:vor-rated-coverage]]
Picture a side view, with the VOR on the left and the coverage stepping outward to the right as the height increases: 60 nm, then 90, then 120, then 150. Now put an aeroplane 75 nm out. At 4,500 ft it is outside the coverage. At 6,500 ft it is inside.

Two things reduce the coverage. First, terrain between you and the station blocks the signal, whatever the table says. And second, the ERSA entry overrides the table, where a particular VOR has a restricted sector or a reduced range. NDB coverage, as you heard earlier, is listed per station in ERSA.

Let's work one. You plan to cruise at 4,500 ft, and you want to cross-check your position against a VOR 75 nm away. No ERSA restriction applies to the sector. Is the VOR usable on this leg? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 5]]
Step one. At 4,500 ft you are in the band below 5,000 ft. The rated coverage is 60 nm.

Step two. 75 nm is beyond 60 nm. So the station is outside rated coverage. You may receive it, but it is not reliable.

Step three. At 6,500 ft, which is a VFR level for a westbound track, you are in the band from 5,000 to 10,000 ft. The coverage is 90 nm, so 75 nm is within it, terrain permitting.

So the answer: at 4,500 ft the VOR is not usable for that cross-check, and at 6,500 ft it is. Plan the cross-check for the part of the leg within 60 nm, or at a level that brings the station within coverage. And check ERSA for any sector limitation.

Question. Why does an NDB have its rated coverage listed station by station in ERSA, while VOR coverage comes from a single altitude table?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: an NDB's ground wave follows the curve of the earth, so its range depends on the transmitter's power, which differs between stations. A VOR's line-of-sight signal is limited mainly by your height above the radio horizon. So one table by altitude covers them all, with ERSA noting the individual restrictions.

## Finding an aid in ERSA and on the ERC

The element asks you to extract an aid's details, and there are two places to find them: ERSA, and the en route chart.

In ERSA, the NAV COMM section lists every aid. For each one, it gives six things.

First, the identifier. That is two or three letters, transmitted in Morse code, so that you can confirm you have tuned the right station.

Second, the frequency. That is in kilohertz for an NDB, in megahertz for a VOR, and as a channel for a DME.

Third, the position, as latitude and longitude, and the elevation.

Fourth, the hours of operation.

Fifth, the rated coverage, or range.

And sixth, the limitations, such as sectors that are unusable, and scheduled maintenance.

So: identifier, frequency, position, hours, coverage and limitations.

The ERSA FAC entry for an aerodrome also lists the aids at or near it, in its communications and navigation aids block.

[[diagram:ersa-entry-anatomy]]
Picture an ERSA aerodrome entry as a stack of blocks. At the top is the header, with the elevation and position. Below it is the runway table. Below that sits the communications and navigation aids block, and last come the remarks. The aerodrome entry tells you which aids are there, but the full details of each aid are in the NAV COMM section.

The ERC-L, the en route chart, low, shows each aid by its symbol. A VOR is a compass rose. An NDB is a circle with a dot pattern. And a DME is a box. Beside each one is a frequency box, with its identifier, frequency and Morse code. The chart also shows the air routes the aids define. The VNC and the VTC show aids with their frequencies too.

[[diagram:chart-features-panel]]
Picture four chart panels side by side. The WAC carries terrain, towns and roads, but no airspace. The VNC and the VTC carry both terrain and airspace. And the ERC-L carries airspace, air routes, navigation aids and frequencies, but no terrain at all.

Let's work one, checking an aid before you rely on it. Your planned track to a wheatbelt aerodrome passes 40 nm from a VOR DME shown on the ERC-L, and you will cruise at 5,500 ft. What do you check, and in what order? Pause if you'd like to list them first.
[[pause 6]]
There are five steps.

First, the ERC-L. Read the identifier, the frequency and the Morse code from the frequency box. And note the radial your track crosses.

Second, ERSA NAV COMM. Confirm the frequency and identifier, the hours, and any unusable sector that contains your track.

Third, coverage. 5,500 ft is in the band from 5,000 to 10,000 ft, rated 90 nm. And 40 nm is within it.

Fourth, the NOTAMs. Check the aid is not withdrawn, or under maintenance.

And fifth, in flight. Tune it, identify it by the Morse ident, check the OFF flag has cleared, and then use it.

Only after all five steps is the indication worth comparing with your map fix. Chart, ERSA, coverage, NOTAMs, and identify in flight.

## Using the aids as a VFR pilot

For a private pilot flying VFR, the aids are a cross-check on visual navigation, not a replacement for it.

A VOR radial is a position line. A DME distance is an arc. An NDB bearing is a position line. Any two of them crossing at a good angle give a fix, exactly as two ground features do in the lesson on pilot navigation.

Tune the aid, identify it by listening for the Morse ident, and check the flags before trusting any indication. A needle that looks right on the wrong station is worse than no needle.

## Bringing it together

You are cruising at 3,500 ft, north-east from Jandakot towards a country strip. The haze has thickened, and the features on the WAC have gone grey.

On the ERC-L there is a VOR DME behind you, 35 nm away. ERSA lists no restriction in your sector. And 35 nm is within the 60 nm rated for your level.

You tune it, and hear the correct Morse ident. With the needle centred, the flag reads FROM on the {047|zero four seven} radial. That is a position line running away from the station on {047|zero four seven} degrees magnetic. The DME reads 35 nm.

The radial and the distance give a fix. It puts you 2 nm left of your planned track. The {1 in 60|one in sixty} rule from the lesson on pilot navigation turns that into a heading correction.

Meanwhile, the ADF is tuned to an old NDB at your destination, and it is swinging towards a storm to the south. That is thunderstorm effect, so you ignore it.

Question. In that scenario, why is the VOR DME fix trustworthy, while the ADF bearing is not?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the VOR DME was identified, within rated coverage and free of ERSA restrictions, and VHF is not bent towards storms. The ADF was showing a known medium-frequency error, thunderstorm effect. So its bearing was meaningless until the storm was well clear.

## Key points

Let's recap.

The ADF needle shows the relative bearing, measured clockwise from the nose. The magnetic bearing to the NDB equals the magnetic heading plus the relative bearing, and the position line from the station is its reciprocal.

NDBs suffer four errors: night effect, coastal refraction, thunderstorm effect and mountain effect. Each NDB has its own coverage, listed in ERSA.

A VOR radial is the magnetic bearing from the station, and it does not depend on your heading. To go to the station, centre the needle with TO showing.

DME gives the slant range, not the ground distance.

An aid's details, its ident, frequency, position, hours, coverage and limitations, are in ERSA NAV COMM and on the ERC-L.

VOR rated coverage is 60 nm below 5,000 ft, and 90 nm from 5,000 to 10,000 ft.

And always identify the aid before you use it.

A final exam tip. Have the VOR rated coverage by heart: 60 nm below 5,000 ft, and 90 nm from 5,000 to 10,000 ft. That comes from AIP GEN 1.5, so verify it. NDB coverage is per station, in ERSA. Expect questions that ask where an aid's frequency, hours or identifier are published. The answer is ERSA NAV COMM, and the ERC for the frequency box. Expect a question that a radial is a bearing from the station. And expect magnetic heading plus relative bearing arithmetic that passes 360. Identify every aid before using it.

That's the end of PNVC 2.6.
