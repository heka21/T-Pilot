This is PNVC 2.7, Area navigation systems. By the end you will be able to say what an area navigation system needs and what it gives you, explain how VOR DME and satellite navigation each fix a position and why each can be wrong, and say why and how a system's position is updated. There are worked examples and questions along the way, so pause whenever you want a moment to think.

## What the exam expects (PNVC 2.7)

The exam wants five things from you. First, to name the two kinds of area navigation system that use external sensors: VOR DME, and GNSS. Second, the general principles: the inputs a system needs, air data and other inputs, the outputs it produces, and what those outputs are used for. Third, VOR DME area navigation: how it works, its advantages and disadvantages, its limitations of errors, accuracy, reliability, coverage and range, and a typical control panel. Fourth, satellite navigation: how GNSS works and its elements, GPS, GLONASS and others, its advantages, disadvantages and limitations, a typical control panel, the approvals needed for IFR navigation, and the enhancements. And fifth, why an area navigation system's position must be updated, how it is updated, and what you see when it updates. One element number in this subtopic is reserved, so there are only five.

## Why this matters

On a flight from Jandakot to Kalbarri, there is not a ground beacon on the direct track for most of the way. Yet the GNSS on the panel shows you a straight line to the destination, your groundspeed, the wind, and the time to go. That is area navigation, and nearly every aeroplane you fly will carry some form of it.

The exam asks what such a system needs to work, and what it gives you. It asks how the older VOR DME version differs from GNSS, why each can be wrong, and what keeps its position honest. Knowing the answers is also what stops you following a confident display into the wrong valley.

This lesson builds on the radio aids of the previous lesson, and on the GNSS principles taught in full in the GNSS unit.

## What area navigation is (2.7.1)

Before area navigation, an instrument route ran from beacon to beacon. You tracked outbound from one VOR or NDB, then inbound to the next. The route went wherever the beacons were, not where you wanted to go. A dogleg of 20 nm to pass over a beacon was normal.

Area navigation, written RNAV, removes that constraint. An RNAV system works out the aeroplane's position continuously, as a latitude and longitude. So it can steer you along a straight track between any two points you choose.

Those points are waypoints. A waypoint is a position defined by coordinates, or by a bearing and distance from a known point. It need not have anything on the ground beneath it.

To know where it is, a system needs a source of position. The syllabus covers the two that use external sensors, meaning signals from outside the aeroplane. The first is VOR DME RNAV, which computes position from a VOR radial and a DME distance. The second is GNSS, the global navigation satellite system, which computes position from satellite signals.

There are also self-contained systems, such as inertial navigation. They measure the aeroplane's own accelerations, and need no outside signal. They appear in this lesson only because they show most clearly why position must be updated, which comes at the end.

Question. What is the essential difference between tracking from one VOR to the next, and flying an RNAV route?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: beacon-to-beacon navigation can only follow lines to and from the stations, so the route goes where the aids are. RNAV computes your position continuously. So it can fly a straight track between any two waypoints, whether or not there is an aid at either.

## What goes in and what comes out (2.7.2)

Think of an RNAV system as a navigator with a calculator who never tires. Like a human navigator, it needs information to start with. And what it produces is the same set of answers you would work out by hand in the lesson on pilot navigation, only continuously and more precisely.

Start with the inputs. There are two groups.

The first group is the air data inputs. These are true airspeed, from the air data computer, pressure altitude, and outside air temperature. They describe how the aeroplane is moving through the air.

The second group is the other inputs. There is heading, from the compass system. There is the sensor data, which is either a VOR radial and DME distance, or the satellite ranges, called pseudoranges. There is the navigation database, of waypoints, aids, aerodromes and airspace. And there is the flight plan the pilot enters.

Now the outputs. There are six.

First, present position, as latitude and longitude.

Second, track and groundspeed, worked out from successive positions.

Third, the bearing and distance to the active waypoint, the time to go, and the ETA.

Fourth, the cross-track error. That is how far left or right of the desired track you are, shown on a CDI or a map display.

Fifth, the wind. The system gets it by comparing what the aeroplane is doing through the air, its heading and TAS, with what it is doing over the ground, its track and groundspeed.

And sixth, fuel and range estimates, where fuel flow is an input.

The wind output shows why both groups of inputs are needed. Position alone gives track and groundspeed, but not the wind. For the wind, the system also needs the heading and the TAS. The difference between the two is the drift.

[[diagram:heading-track-drift]]
Picture an aeroplane from above. The nose points along the heading. But a wind from one side carries the aeroplane sideways, so over the ground it moves along a different line, the track. The angle between the heading and the track is the drift. Heading and TAS describe the motion through the air. Track and groundspeed describe the motion over the ground. The gap between them is the wind's doing, and the computer solves for it.

Let's work one, checking the wind the system shows. The heading is {090|zero nine zero} degrees magnetic, and the TAS is 110 kt. The RNAV shows a track of {080|zero eight zero} degrees magnetic, and a groundspeed of 110 kt. Roughly what is the wind? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
Step one, the drift. The track is 10 degrees left of the heading. So the drift is 10 degrees left, and the wind is from the right.

Step two, the crosswind, by the {1 in 60|one in sixty} rule. 10 times 110, divided by 60, is about 18 kt, from the right.

Step three, the head or tail component. The groundspeed equals the TAS, so that component is about zero.

So the wind is roughly from the south at about 18 kt. On a heading of {090|zero nine zero}, from the right means from about {180|one eight zero}. The system solves the same triangle exactly. Your rough check tells you whether its answer is sensible. So: about 18 kt, from about the south.

What are those outputs used for? Route navigation along the planned track. Situational awareness, relative to airspace and terrain. Diversion planning, with a direct track and a time to any aerodrome. And, on approved equipment, instrument approaches.

Question. A GNSS receiver with no heading input can still show track and groundspeed. Why can it not show the wind?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: track and groundspeed come from successive positions alone. But the wind is the difference between the motion through the air and the motion over the ground. So the system also needs the heading and TAS, the air data and heading inputs, to compute it.

## VOR/DME RNAV (2.7.3)

This chapter has four parts: the principle, the advantages, the disadvantages and limitations, and a typical control panel.

First, the principle. From the lesson on radio navigation aids, a VOR gives you the radial you are on, and a co-located DME gives your distance from the station. Radial plus distance is a fix. It is called a rho-theta fix: rho for range, and theta for angle.

If you know where you are relative to the station, and you know where a waypoint is relative to the same station, the rest is geometry.

So the RNAV computer lets you define a waypoint as a radial and a distance from the station. The {070|zero seven zero} radial at 25 nm, for example. That waypoint is often called a phantom station, because the computer then presents steering to it as if a VOR were sitting there.

The CDI shows your deviation from the track to the waypoint. And the distance readout shows the distance to the waypoint, not to the real station. As you fly, the computer keeps solving the triangle between you, the station and the waypoint.

[[diagram:compass-rose-headings]]
Picture the compass rose again, with the station at its centre. Radials are numbered clockwise from magnetic north, exactly like the rose. {090|zero nine zero} points east, {180|one eight zero} south, and {270|two seven zero} west. A waypoint on the {070|zero seven zero} radial is simply a direction from the station, a little north of east, plus a distance along it.

Now the advantages. There are three. Direct tracks between waypoints, off the airway structure. Simplicity in principle, with the display being the familiar CDI. And it uses existing ground stations, so no satellites are needed.

Now the disadvantages and limitations. There are four to know: coverage and range, errors and accuracy, slant range, and reliability.

Coverage and range first. The system only works within the rated coverage of the VOR DME in use. The signal is line of sight, so range increases with altitude, and it is lost behind terrain. The 60 nm and 90 nm figures from the lesson on radio navigation aids apply. A waypoint based on a station hidden behind high ground is unusable.

Then errors and accuracy. The VOR bearing itself has an error, typically plus or minus 1 to 2 degrees, and up to plus or minus 5 degrees. Verify that against your reference text. An angular error turns into a sideways error that grows with distance from the station. It follows exactly the {1 in 60|one in sixty} geometry from the lesson on pilot navigation. So accuracy falls off the further you are from the station.

Let's work one. How big is the position uncertainty for a waypoint 50 nm from the VOR, if the VOR bearing error is anywhere between plus or minus 1 and plus or minus 5 degrees? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
Step one, the sideways error for 1 degree at 50 nm. 1 times 50, divided by 60, is about 0.8 nm.

Step two, the sideways error for 5 degrees at 50 nm. 5 times 50, divided by 60, is about 4.2 nm.

So a waypoint 50 nm out may be uncertain by roughly 1 to 4 nm across the radial, and that is before any DME error is added. The same bearing error close to the station costs far less.

[[diagram:vor-dme-rnav-error]]
Picture the VOR DME on the left, and a waypoint 50 nm out to the right along a radial. From the station, a narrow wedge opens out towards the waypoint. With a 1 degree error, the edges of the wedge are about 0.8 nm either side of the waypoint. With 5 degrees, about 4.2 nm. The bearing error is an angle, so its sideways cost grows with distance.

Now try it nearer the station. With a VOR bearing error of plus or minus 2 degrees, how far across the radial could a waypoint 20 nm from the station be in error?
[[pause 6]]
2 times 20, divided by 60, is about 0.7 nm, either side. At 50 nm, the same 2 degree error would cost about 1.7 nm. At 20 nm it costs well under a mile. That is why VOR DME RNAV is most accurate near the station. So the answer is about 0.7 nm.

The third limitation is slant range. DME measures slant range, not ground distance. High and close to the station the difference matters. A few miles out, it does not.

And the fourth is reliability. It depends on the ground station, and on the receiver. A station off the air for maintenance, a sector listed as unusable in ERSA, or a receiver fault all remove the fix.

Few light aircraft now carry a dedicated VOR DME RNAV unit. The function lives on inside the flight management systems of larger aircraft.

Last, a typical control panel. A VOR DME RNAV panel has four things. First, a mode selector, with ordinary VOR operation, RNAV en route, and RNAV approach. Second, a frequency window for the VOR DME station. Third, a waypoint definition for each stored waypoint, entered as a radial and a distance from the tuned station. And fourth, the output, on the normal CDI or the HSI, the horizontal situation indicator, with a distance readout to the waypoint.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Assuming RNAV accuracy is the same everywhere. VOR DME RNAV is good near the station, and progressively worse away from it. And it fails completely outside rated coverage, or behind terrain. A distractor that says its accuracy is independent of distance is wrong.

Question. Why does a VOR DME RNAV waypoint 60 nm from the station carry a larger position error than one 15 nm away, when the equipment is the same?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the VOR's error is an angle. The same angle produces a sideways error in proportion to distance, by the {1 in 60|one in sixty} rule. So at four times the distance, the cross-radial error is about four times as large.

## Satellite navigation (2.7.5)

This chapter has six parts: the principle and the elements, the advantages, the disadvantages and limitations, a typical control panel, the approvals for IFR navigation, and the enhancements.

First, the principle. GNSS fixes position by measuring the distance to several satellites at once, from the time each signal takes to arrive. The GNSS unit teaches this in full. The essentials for this subtopic are two pairs of numbers.

Four satellites give a {3D|three D} position. Three of them are for latitude, longitude and height, and one more is to solve the receiver's clock error. Three satellites give only a {2D|two D} position.

Five satellites allow RAIM, receiver autonomous integrity monitoring, to detect a faulty satellite. And six allow it to be excluded.

So: four for {3D|three D}, five to detect a fault, six to exclude it.

A GNSS has three segments.

The first is the space segment, the satellite constellations. GPS, the United States system, has about 31 satellites in six orbital planes, at about 20,200 km. GLONASS is the Russian system. Galileo is the European one, and BeiDou the Chinese. Modern receivers use several.

The second is the control segment. These are ground stations that track the satellites, and upload corrections to their clocks and orbits.

And the third is the user segment. That is the receiver in the aeroplane, which measures each signal's travel time and computes the pseudoranges.

Now the advantages. There are five. First, worldwide coverage, independent of ground stations. Second, high accuracy, typically 5 to 15 m, the same over the Nullarbor as over Perth. Third, a continuous position, with track, groundspeed, distance and time to go. Fourth, cheap receivers and tablet applications, with moving-map situational awareness. And fifth, it is the basis of RNP and GNSS instrument approaches.

Compare the VOR DME worked example. A VOR DME waypoint 50 nm from the station may be uncertain by miles. A GNSS position is good to tens of metres wherever you are.

Now the disadvantages and limitations. There are four headings: errors, reliability, coverage and range, and the pilot.

Errors. Satellite clock and ephemeris errors, ionospheric and tropospheric delay, multipath reflections, receiver noise, and poor satellite geometry all degrade the fix. Poor geometry is called a high dilution of precision. The GNSS unit explains each one.

Reliability. The signals are weak, so GNSS is vulnerable to jamming, spoofing and interference. The antenna can lose satellites under the wing in a steep turn, or in a valley. And the database must be current, or the waypoints and airspace it shows may be wrong.

Coverage and range. Coverage is global, and range is unlimited. But availability depends on the satellites in view at that moment. RAIM may be unavailable for periods. That is why IFR receivers predict RAIM before flight.

And the pilot. The greatest hazard is over-reliance. Picture a VFR pilot who stops map reading because the screen is so convincing. When it fails, or when a mistyped waypoint leads them astray, they have no idea where they are.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. GNSS is accurate to 15 m, so I am where it says. But accuracy is not integrity. A faulty satellite, a jammed signal or a wrong waypoint gives a confident, precise-looking and wrong answer. RAIM exists to catch the first. Only your cross-check against the ground catches the last.

Next, a typical control panel. A panel-mounted GNSS unit typically has seven things. A direct-to key, to fly straight to any waypoint. Flight plan pages, to enter and check the route. A nearest function, listing aerodromes with bearing and distance. A CDI output with a selectable scale, which is how many nautical miles a full-scale deflection represents. An OBS mode, to fly a selected course to or from a waypoint. A map page. And message and RAIM annunciators. Hand-held and tablet applications offer the same functions, without the IFR approval.

The cross-track error on the CDI is in nautical miles, not degrees. To turn it into a heading correction, use the {1 in 60|one in sixty} rule, exactly as in the lesson on pilot navigation.

Let's work one. You are 30 nm along a leg. The GNSS shows a cross-track error of 1.5 nm right of track, with 45 nm to go. What heading correction gets you to the destination? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 8]]
Step one, the track error. 1.5 times 60, divided by the 30 nm flown, is 3 degrees right.

Step two, the closing angle. 1.5 times 60, divided by the 45 nm to go, is 2 degrees.

Step three, add them. Alter heading left by 3 plus 2, which is 5 degrees, to reach the destination. And on regaining track, turn back right by 2 degrees.

So the answer: 5 degrees left now, then 2 degrees back right on track. The GNSS gives you the offset precisely. The correction is still your arithmetic, and a glance at the map confirms the offset is real.

Then the approvals for IFR navigation. GNSS may be used as the sole means of navigation under IFR only when five things all hold. Verify the current requirements in AIP GEN 1.5.

First, the receiver is certified to the relevant standard. That is one of the TSO classes: {C129|C one two nine}, {C145|C one four five} and {C146|C one four six}, or {C196|C one nine six}.

Second, it is installed and approved, under the aircraft's flight manual supplement.

Third, the navigation database is current.

Fourth, integrity is provided, by RAIM or by SBAS.

And fifth, the pilot holds the GNSS endorsement, and has been trained on that unit.

For VFR, GNSS is an aid. Below 2,000 ft AGL you must navigate by visual reference. And above that, visual navigation remains the primary method, with GNSS used to confirm it. Check the current Part 91 MOS and VFRG wording. The GNSS unit covers the rules.

Last, the enhancements. Plain GNSS errors can be measured at a point whose position is known exactly. That is the idea behind every enhancement, and there are three.

The first is DGNSS, differential GNSS. A ground station at a surveyed position measures the error in each satellite's signal, and broadcasts corrections to nearby receivers.

The second is SBAS, satellite-based augmentation, such as WAAS in the United States and EGNOS in Europe. A network of ground stations computes corrections and integrity information, and broadcasts them from geostationary satellites. That enables approaches with vertical guidance. Australia's {SouthPAN|south pan} SBAS is being introduced. Check its current aviation status before relying on it.

And the third is GLS, the GBAS landing system. A ground-based augmentation station at the aerodrome provides corrections for precision approaches there.

Question. What do DGNSS, SBAS and GBAS have in common, and what is the main difference between them?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: all three measure GNSS errors at a known position, and broadcast corrections, along with integrity data. DGNSS and GBAS broadcast from a ground station to nearby users, and GBAS specifically for approaches at one aerodrome. SBAS broadcasts from geostationary satellites, over a whole region.

## Updating area navigation systems (2.7.6)

This chapter has three parts: why position must be updated, three ways to update, and what an update looks like.

First, why. Picture dead reckoning from the lesson on pilot navigation. Your DR position gets steadily worse the longer you go without a fix. A self-contained system, such as inertial navigation, does exactly the same. It integrates its measured accelerations, so small errors accumulate, and the position drifts with time.

And any RNAV system's position is only as good as its last sensor input. So the position has to be updated, from external references.

There are three ways to update.

The first is manual updating. The pilot enters a known position, for example when overhead a fix, or corrects the displayed position.

The second is automatic updating. The system continuously corrects itself, from VOR DME, from DME DME, or from GNSS.

And the third is inhibiting updating. The pilot stops automatic updates when the sensor data is known to be unreliable. That might be a DME behind terrain, an area of GNSS jamming, or a station known to be in error. A bad update would replace a good position with a wrong one. So it is better to keep the last good position and let it drift slowly, than to accept a sudden wrong jump.

So: manual, automatic, and inhibited.

Now, what does an update look like? When a system updates, you may see four things. A jump in the displayed position. A sudden change in the computed track, groundspeed or wind. An annunciator showing the sensor in use, such as GPS, DME DME or VOR DME, or a message such as position updated. And afterwards, a change in the ETA and in the cross-track error.

A jump is not always a fault. A system that had drifted, and has just been corrected, will jump back to the truth. That is why you judge an update by checking it against an independent source. A VFR pilot's equivalent is comparing the GNSS position with a visual fix after every leg.

Question. During an exercise that NOTAMs warn will jam GNSS, why might the pilot of an aeroplane with inertial and GNSS sensors inhibit GNSS updating?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: a jammed or spoofed GNSS could feed the system a wrong position. An automatic update would then replace a good inertial position with a bad one. Inhibiting updates keeps the last good position, which drifts only slowly, until reliable updates are available again.

## Bringing it together

You are flying VFR at 6,500 ft from Jandakot towards Kalbarri, on a direct GNSS track. The receiver shows a groundspeed of 118 kt, a wind of about 20 kt from the south-east, and a cross-track error of 0.4 nm. Your map shows the same offset from the highway, so you accept it.

You approach an area that NOTAMs warn of GNSS interference. A RAIM message appears, and the position jumps 3 nm. You do not follow it.

The map still agrees with your DR. And the aeroplane's VOR DME is tuned to a station 30 nm behind you, well inside the 90 nm coverage for your level. It gives a radial and distance that match the map too.

GNSS is now an untrusted sensor. So you navigate by map and clock, using the VOR DME as a cross-check that is accurate this close to the station. You carry on that way until RAIM is restored, and the GNSS position agrees with the ground again.

Question. In that scenario, which outputs, inputs and limitations of each system came into play?
[[pause 5]]
The answer: the GNSS gave position, groundspeed, wind and cross-track error. Those are outputs. It computed them from satellite ranges plus heading and air data. Those are inputs. Its limitations were integrity and interference, flagged by RAIM. The VOR DME was usable because it was within rated coverage, and close to the station, where its angular error costs little distance.

## Key points

Let's recap.

Area navigation flies straight tracks between waypoints, rather than beacon to beacon. The two external-sensor types are VOR DME and GNSS.

The inputs are air data, which is TAS, pressure altitude and temperature, and the others: heading, sensor data, the database, and the flight plan. The outputs are position, track, groundspeed, bearing and distance to the waypoint, time to go and ETA, cross-track error, wind, and fuel. The wind needs both the heading and TAS, and the track and groundspeed.

VOR DME RNAV is a rho-theta fix, with waypoints as phantom stations defined by a radial and a distance. It is simple and ground-based. But it is limited to rated coverage and line of sight, its accuracy falls with distance from the station, 1 to 4 nm at 50 nm, and DME slant range matters close in.

GNSS has space, control and user segments. Four satellites for {3D|three D}, five for RAIM, six to exclude a fault. It is accurate, 5 to 15 m, and global. But it is vulnerable to errors, to jamming, and to over-reliance. IFR use needs a certified, approved installation, a current database, integrity, and a trained pilot. The enhancements are DGNSS, SBAS, which includes WAAS and {SouthPAN|south pan}, and GLS.

Position drifts, and is only as good as its last sensor, so it is updated. Manually, automatically, or inhibited when the sensor is suspect. Updates show as position jumps, changes in track, groundspeed and wind, and sensor annunciations.

A final exam tip. Four satellites for a {3D|three D} fix, five for RAIM, six to exclude a faulty one. VOR DME RNAV accuracy falls off with distance from the station, and it is limited by line of sight and rated coverage. Expect questions that ask which is an output, and which is a limitation, so know the lists. GNSS for VFR is an aid, never a substitute for map reading. IFR use needs a certified installation, a current database and a trained pilot. And you inhibit updating when the sensor is suspect, not when it is working.

That's the end of PNVC 2.7.
