This is PNVC 2.4, Computation techniques. By the end you will be able to work every number on a navigation log, on the computer and in your head. There are plenty of worked examples, so pause whenever you want to try one first.

## What the exam expects (PNVC 2.4)

The exam wants ten things from you. Time intervals in your head. Endurance in your head, not counting the reserve. Applying variation. Converting units. Wind components, on the computer and from the ERSA tables. Calibrated and true airspeed. Heading, groundspeed and drift from the track. The reverse, the track from the heading. Climb and descent rates and gradients. And the top of climb and top of descent.

## Why this matters

Every number on your navigation log comes out of this lesson. The flight from Jandakot to Kalgoorlie starts as a line on a chart and a forecast wind. It becomes a heading, a groundspeed, a time for each leg, a fuel figure, and the points where the climb ends and the descent begins.

Some of it you do on the ground with the flight computer, and the rest in your head in the air, when the wind is not what was forecast. CASA names time and distance, and the revision of estimates, as a weak area in the PPLA exam. The exam allows the computer. But it rewards the pilot who checks every answer with a mental estimate, because that catches a factor of two, or a wrong sign, at once.

## Time and distance in your head

You are 36 NM from Northam at a groundspeed of 120 kt. How long until you arrive?

Time in minutes equals the distance in nautical miles, times 60, divided by the groundspeed in knots. But in the air you want the answer without a pencil. So think in nautical miles per minute: divide the groundspeed by 60.

Learn these by heart. 60 kt is 1 NM a minute. 90 kt is 1.5. 105 kt is 1.75. 120 kt is 2. 150 kt is 2.5. And 180 kt is 3. Multiply by ten for the distance every 10 minutes: 15 NM at 90 kt, 17.5 at 105, 20 at 120.

Then divide the distance by the miles per minute. Between round numbers, use the ten-minute figure, or scale from the nearest value.

Let's work two. First, 36 NM at 120 kt. Second, 42 NM at 105 kt. Pause if you'd like to try them.
[[pause 6]]
The first. 120 divided by 60 is 2 NM a minute. 36 divided by 2 is 18 minutes.

The second. 105 divided by 60 is 1.75 NM a minute. 42 divided by 1.75 is 24 minutes. Check it the other way. At 105 kt you cover 17.5 NM in 10 minutes, so 42 NM is a little under 2.5 lots of 10, which is 25 minutes. It agrees. The answers are 18 minutes and 24 minutes.

Now one for you. Your groundspeed is 96 kt, and the next town is 40 NM away. How long?
[[pause 6]]
96 divided by 60 is 1.6 NM a minute. 40 divided by 1.6 is 25 minutes. Check it. At 90 kt it would be about 27 minutes, and at 120 kt, 20 minutes. 96 kt sits between them, a little closer to 90, so 25 minutes is right.

## Endurance in your head

Endurance is how long the aeroplane can fly on its fuel. For planning, set aside the fixed reserve first. That is 30 minutes for day VFR, under the visual flight rules; verify the current figure in the Part 91 MOS and CASA's fuel guidance. Set aside any holding or alternate fuel too. What is left is the fuel available. Endurance in hours equals the fuel available in litres, divided by the fuel flow in litres per hour.

The reserve is a time, so turn it into a quantity at the fuel flow you will use. 30 minutes at 30 L/h is 15 L.

Let's work one. You have 120 L on board, a fuel flow of 30 L/h, and a reserve of 15 L. What is the endurance? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
Fuel available: 120 minus 15 is 105 L. Endurance: 105 divided by 30 is 3.5 hours. In minutes, 3.5 times 60 is 210 minutes. That is 3.5 hours, 210 minutes, before you touch the reserve.

Now work out the reserve yourself. 150 L on board, a fuel flow of 25 L/h, and a 30 minute fixed reserve.
[[pause 6]]
The reserve: half an hour at 25 L/h is 12.5 L. Fuel available: 150 minus 12.5 is {137.5 L|one hundred and thirty-seven point five litres}. Endurance: that divided by 25 is 5.5 hours, which is 330 minutes. A mental check: 25 L/h is 100 L every 4 hours, so the fuel lasts a little under 4 times 1.4, which is 5.6 hours. The answer is 5.5 hours, 330 minutes.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. One distractor is the endurance on all the fuel, forgetting the reserve. The other is an answer in hours read as hours and minutes. 3.5 hours is 3 hours 30 minutes, not 3 hours 50 minutes.

## Applying variation

You already know the rule. Variation east, magnetic least. Variation west, magnetic best.

With 3 degrees east, a track of {125|one two five} degrees true becomes 125 minus 3, which is {122|one two two} degrees magnetic. To plot a magnetic direction on the chart, reverse it. A heading of {300|three zero zero} degrees magnetic, with 2 degrees west, is 300 minus 2, which is {298|two nine eight} degrees true.

What this lesson adds is where the variation goes in a calculation. Forecast winds are true. So a triangle of velocities is solved entirely in true, and only the heading that comes out is converted to magnetic. Mixing a true wind with a magnetic track gives the wrong drift.

## Conversions

Aviation mixes units. Runways in metres and altitudes in feet. Pounds in an American POH, and kilograms on the load sheet. Fuel in US gallons on the gauge, litres from the bowser, and kilograms on the weight and balance.

One idea stops most errors: converting to a smaller unit gives a bigger number. A metre is longer than a foot, so 1,500 m is about 4,920 ft. A kilogram is heavier than a pound, so the weight in pounds is the bigger number. Decide which way the answer should move, and the direction of the factor follows.

Feet to metres, times 0.3048, because 1 m is 3.28 ft. So 1,000 ft is about 305 m. Nautical miles to kilometres, times 1.852, and 1 km is 0.54 NM. So 100 NM is about 185 km. Statute miles to nautical miles, times 0.87. Pounds to kilograms, times 0.4536, because 1 kg is 2.205 lb. So 2,200 lb is about 1,000 kg. US gallons to litres, times 3.785, so 37 US gallons is about 140 L. Imperial gallons to litres, times 4.546.

For avgas, litres to kilograms is times 0.72. So 100 L is 72 kg, and dividing by 0.72 takes 72 kg back to 100 L. US gallons to kilograms is times 2.72, from 6 lb per US gallon. So 10 US gallons is about 27 kg.

[[diagram:units-conversion-ladder]]
Picture three pairs of rulers, one for distance, one for speed and one for fuel, each read straight across. 1 NM lines up with 1.852 km, 110 kt with 204 km/h, and 100 L of avgas with about 72 kg.

Fuel needs more thought, because it changes from a volume to a weight. The bridge is the specific gravity, the SG: the weight in kilograms of a litre of fuel. It is about 0.72 for avgas, and the exam gives the figure to use.

Let's work one, two ways. You have 48 US gallons of avgas. Find its weight in kilograms, first with an SG of 0.72, then with 6 lb per US gallon and 2.205 lb to the kilogram. Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 8]]
Via litres. 48 times 3.785 is {181.7 L|one hundred and eighty-one point seven litres}. Times 0.72, that is {130.8 kg|one hundred and thirty point eight kilograms}.

Via pounds. 48 times 6 is 288 lb. Divided by 2.205, that is {130.6 kg|one hundred and thirty point six kilograms}.

Both routes give about 131 kg for the weight and balance. The small difference is rounding in the 6 lb per gallon figure.

Now the other way. The load sheet allows 95 kg of fuel, and a runway is 1,500 m long. How many litres, and how many feet?
[[pause 6]]
The fuel goes from weight to volume, so divide by the SG. 95 divided by 0.72 is {131.9 L|one hundred and thirty-one point nine litres}, so ask for about 132 L. The runway goes from metres to feet, a bigger number. 1,500 times 3.28 is 4,920 ft.

The common errors are multiplying when you should divide, and using the wrong gallon. The imperial gallon is a fifth larger than the US one.

Question. You have 60 kg of avgas. Is that more or less than 60 litres, and why?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: more, about 83 L. A litre of avgas weighs only 0.72 kg, so it takes more litres than kilograms to make the same amount of fuel. Divide the weight by 0.72.

## Wind components

You are lined up on runway 24, and the tower wind is {290|two nine zero} degrees at 20 kt. How much of that wind helps you get airborne, and how much pushes you sideways?

Any wind splits into two parts. The part along the runway, or your heading, is the headwind component, or the tailwind component if it blows from behind. The part across it is the crosswind component.

The split depends on the wind angle, between the wind direction and the runway direction, both in the same reference. Runways and tower winds are magnetic; forecast winds are true. The headwind component is the wind speed times the cosine of the wind angle. The crosswind component is the wind speed times the sine. When the angle is more than 90 degrees, the cosine is negative, and the headwind component has become a tailwind.

[[widget:crosswind-component]]
Imagine swinging the wind round from straight down the runway. Thirty degrees off already puts half the wind across it, and past 90 degrees the headwind turns into a tailwind.

You do not need a calculator for the sine. The clock rule treats the wind angle as minutes on a clock face, and the crosswind as that fraction of an hour. 15 degrees gives a quarter of the wind across, 0.25. 30 degrees gives a half, 0.5. 45 degrees about 0.7. 60 degrees about 0.9. And 90 degrees all of it. The headwind is the same table read from the other end. At 60 degrees off, half the wind is still along the runway.

Let's work one. Runway 24 points {240|two four zero} degrees magnetic, and the tower wind is {290|two nine zero} magnetic at 20 kt. Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
The wind angle: {290|two nine zero} minus {240|two four zero} is 50 degrees, from the right. The crosswind is 20 times the sine of 50 degrees, 20 times 0.77, which is 15 kt from the right. The headwind is 20 times the cosine of 50 degrees, 20 times 0.64, which is 13 kt.

The clock rule check: 50 degrees is between 45, at 0.7, and 60, at 0.9, so about 0.8. 20 times 0.8 is 16 kt across. Close enough. So, 15 kt of crosswind and 13 kt of headwind. If the aeroplane's maximum demonstrated crosswind is 15 kt, this is at the limit.

Another. Runway {06|zero six} points {060|zero six zero} degrees magnetic, and the tower wind is {100|one zero zero} magnetic at 18 kt.
[[pause 6]]
The wind angle is 40 degrees, from the right. The crosswind is 18 times the sine of 40 degrees, 18 times 0.64. That is 11.6 kt, about 12 kt. The headwind is 18 times 0.77, which is 13.8 kt, about 14 kt. So about 12 kt across and 14 kt along: comfortably inside a typical trainer's crosswind figure, with a useful headwind.

On the navigation computer's wind side, set the wind direction under the index and mark the wind speed up from the centre. Rotate the runway direction under the index. The dot's offset across the centre line is the crosswind, and above or below the centre, the headwind or tailwind.

With the ERSA GEN table, enter with the wind angle and wind speed, read both components, and interpolate between the printed values. Verify its layout in the current issue.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. The wind and the runway must be in the same reference. A forecast wind is true; a runway number is magnetic. Near Perth the difference is a degree or two, but the exam may set a larger variation. And past 90 degrees off, the headwind is really a tailwind. A wind from behind the wing shortens nothing.

Question. Why does the crosswind component grow so quickly at small wind angles, and then level off?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the crosswind is the wind speed times the sine of the angle. The sine climbs steeply from zero, already 0.5 at 30 degrees, then flattens towards 1 at 90 degrees.

## True airspeed from temperature and pressure height

Correct the indicated airspeed, the IAS, for instrument and position error, using the calibration table in section 5 of the POH, and you have the calibrated airspeed, the CAS. The two are usually within a few knots. True airspeed, TAS, is CAS corrected for air density, which depends on pressure height and temperature. Thinner air means a higher TAS for the same CAS.

[[diagram:airspeed-chain-ictg]]
Picture four boxes in a row, joined by arrows: indicated, calibrated, true, and groundspeed. The arrows correct, in turn, for instrument and position error, for density, and for wind. Always I, C, T, G.

On the computer, the airspeed window does the density correction. Set the pressure height against the outside air temperature, the OAT, and read the TAS on the outer scale, opposite the CAS on the inner scale.

By rule of thumb, TAS exceeds CAS by about 2 percent per 1,000 ft of density height. The ISA temperature, for the International Standard Atmosphere, is 15 degrees, minus 2 for every thousand feet of pressure height. And each degree warmer than ISA adds about 120 ft.

Let's work one, a warm day. Pressure height 8,000 ft, OAT 5 degrees, CAS 100 kt. What is the TAS by the rule of thumb? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 8]]
Step one, ISA at 8,000 ft. 15 minus 2 times 8 is minus 1 degree.

Step two, the deviation. From minus 1 up to 5 is 6 degrees warmer than ISA.

Step three, the density height. 8,000 plus 120 times 6 is 8,720, about 8,700 ft.

Step four, the rule of thumb. 8.7 times 2 percent is about 17 percent. So the TAS is about 100 times 1.17, which is 117 kt.

The computer works the density out exactly, and gives a little less, about 114 kt. The 2 percent rule overestimates slightly at these heights. In the exam, use the method the question names. So, 117 kt by the rule, 114 kt on the computer.

Now a hot day. Pressure height 6,000 ft, OAT 15 degrees, CAS 105 kt.
[[pause 8]]
ISA at 6,000 ft is 15 minus 2 times 6, which is 3 degrees. The deviation is 15 minus 3, plus 12 degrees. The density height is 6,000 plus 120 times 12, which is 7,440 ft. Then 7.44 times 2 percent is 14.9 percent. So the TAS is about 105 times 1.149, which is 121 kt. The computer gives about 117 kt.

[[widget:density-height]]
Imagine dialling in any pressure height and temperature. Heat is the big one. Every degree above ISA adds about 120 ft of density height, and with it about a quarter of a percent of TAS.

Question. You hold the same CAS while climbing. Does the TAS go up or down, and why?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: up. The air thins as you climb. So to produce the same dynamic pressure, which is what the airspeed indicator measures, the aeroplane must move faster through it.

## The triangle of velocities

You want to fly a track of {070|zero seven zero} degrees true at 110 kt TAS, with the wind from {020|zero two zero} degrees at 25 kt. Point the nose along {070|zero seven zero}, and the wind carries you sideways. How far do you point into the wind? And how fast will you cover the ground?

Three motions add, each a vector, with a direction and a speed. The air vector, heading and TAS, is the aeroplane through the air. The wind vector is the air over the ground. And the ground vector, track and groundspeed, is the aeroplane over the ground. Put the air and wind vectors end to end, and the ground vector closes the triangle.

The angle between the heading and the track is the drift. A wind from the left carries you right: right drift, with the track to the right of the heading. So to fly a given track, point the nose into the wind, left of the track by the drift angle.

[[diagram:heading-track-drift]]
Picture the aeroplane from above. The nose points along the heading, a wind from the left carries it sideways along the track, and the angle between the two is the drift.

The first problem is the planning one: heading, groundspeed and drift, from the track, the TAS and the wind.

On the computer's wind side, set and mark the wind as before. Set the true track under the index, slide the TAS arc under the wind dot, and read the drift from the dot's offset and the groundspeed under the centre. The heading is the track corrected by the drift, towards the wind; expect 5 to 10 degrees in typical winds. Convert it to magnetic at the end.

In your head, split the wind along and across the track, as for a runway, and use two rules. The drift in degrees is about the crosswind component times 60, divided by the TAS. That is the {1 in 60|one in sixty} rule. And the groundspeed is about the TAS minus the headwind component, or plus the tailwind.

Let's work the opening problem. Track {070|zero seven zero} true, TAS 110 kt, wind {020|zero two zero} true at 25 kt, variation 2 degrees west. Find the magnetic heading and the groundspeed. Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 8]]
The wind is from {070|zero seven zero} minus {020|zero two zero}, which is 50 degrees left of the track, ahead of the wing. The crosswind is 25 times the sine of 50 degrees, 25 times 0.77, about 19 kt from the left.

The drift is 19 times 60, divided by 110, about 10 degrees. It is right drift, because the wind is from the left.

The heading points into the wind, left of the track. {070|zero seven zero} minus 10 is {060|zero six zero} true. With 2 degrees west, {060|zero six zero} plus 2 is {062|zero six two} magnetic.

The headwind is 25 times the cosine of 50 degrees, 25 times 0.64, about 16 kt. 110 minus 16 is 94 kt. The computer gives about 92 kt, because the quick method ignores drift. So steer {062|zero six two} magnetic, and expect a groundspeed in the low nineties.

[[widget:wind-triangle]]
Imagine the triangle redrawn for any track and wind. The heading always points upwind of the track. Solve in true, then apply the variation.

Now a wind from the right. Track {160|one six zero} true, TAS 100 kt, wind {230|two three zero} true at 20 kt.
[[pause 8]]
The wind is 70 degrees right of the track. The crosswind is 20 times the sine of 70 degrees, 20 times 0.94, which is 18.8 kt from the right.

The drift is the angle whose sine is 18.8 divided by 100, about 11 degrees. It is left drift, because the wind is from the right. The {1 in 60|one in sixty} rule gives 18.8 times 60, divided by 100, which is 11.3 degrees.

The heading points into the wind, right of the track. {160|one six zero} plus 11 is {171|one seven one} true.

The headwind is 20 times the cosine of 70 degrees, 20 times 0.34, which is 6.8 kt. With a large drift, the aeroplane's own speed along the track is a little less than its TAS: 100 times the cosine of 11 degrees, which is 98 kt. 98.2 minus 6.8 is about 91 kt. So, heading {171|one seven one} true, groundspeed about 91 kt.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Get the direction of the heading correction from the picture, not from memory. The nose always points into the wind. Wind from the left gives right drift and a heading left of the track. Wind from the right gives left drift and a heading right of the track. If your heading is on the downwind side of the track, the sign is wrong.

Now the reverse problem: the track, from the heading, the TAS and the wind. You have flown a heading for a while, and want the track made good and the groundspeed, to plot a dead-reckoning position. On the computer, set the heading, not the track, under the index. The track made good is the heading corrected by the drift, downwind.

Let's work one. Heading {300|three zero zero} true, TAS 95 kt, wind {250|two five zero} true at 15 kt. What track and groundspeed are you making good?
[[pause 8]]
The wind is 50 degrees left of the nose. The crosswind is 15 times the sine of 50 degrees, 11.5 kt from the left, so right drift. The drift is 11.5 times 60, divided by 95, about 7 to 8 degrees.

The track is downwind of the heading. {300|three zero zero} plus 8 is about {308|three zero eight} true. The headwind is 15 times the cosine of 50 degrees, which is 9.6 kt, and 95 minus 9.6 is about 86 kt.

So you have been drifting 8 degrees right of where the nose points, along {308|three zero eight} true, at 86 kt over the ground.

Question. The wind is exactly on the nose. What are the drift and the groundspeed?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: no drift, because there is no crosswind component, so heading and track are the same. The groundspeed is the TAS minus the full wind speed.

## Climb and descent rates and gradients

The VSI, the vertical speed indicator, shows feet per minute. But whether you clear the trees depends on feet per nautical mile over the ground, the gradient. Groundspeed links them. The gradient in feet per nautical mile is the rate in feet per minute, divided by the groundspeed in knots over 60.

As a percentage, it is the height gained per hundred feet travelled. A nautical mile is 6,076 ft, so a knot is about {101.3|one hundred and one point three} ft per minute. So the gradient in percent is the rate in feet per minute, divided by the groundspeed in knots times 1.013.

Let's work two. A climb at 500 ft/min at a groundspeed of 80 kt. Then 600 ft/min at 90 kt. Pause if you'd like to try them.
[[pause 8]]
The first. 80 divided by 60 is 1.33 NM a minute. 500 divided by 1.33 is 375 ft per nautical mile. As a percentage, 80 times 1.013 is 81, and 500 divided by 81 is 6.2 percent.

The second. 90 divided by 60 is 1.5 NM a minute. 600 divided by 1.5 is 400 ft per nautical mile, about 6.6 percent.

So, 375 and 400 ft per nautical mile. The higher rate at the higher groundspeed has given only a slightly steeper path.

[[diagram:climb-gradient-wind]]
Picture the same aeroplane climbing at 600 ft/min, three times over. In still air, at 80 kt over the ground, it gains 450 ft per nautical mile. Into a 20 kt headwind the path steepens to 600, and with a 20 kt tailwind it flattens to 360.

Wind changes the gradient, but not the rate. A headwind lowers the groundspeed and steepens the path.

For descent, the same arithmetic runs backwards. A standard 3 degree approach path is about 300 ft per nautical mile. That gives a handy rule: the rate of descent in feet per minute is about the groundspeed times 5. At 100 kt, that is about 500 ft/min.

Question. You climb at a steady 500 ft/min. Does a headwind make the gradient steeper or shallower, and why?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: steeper. The rate is the same, but the headwind lowers the groundspeed. So you cover fewer nautical miles in each minute of climb, and gain more height per mile.

## Top of climb and top of descent

The top of climb, TOPC, is the point along the track where you reach cruising level. The top of descent, TOPD, is where you must start down to arrive at circuit height. Both go on the chart, because only between them do the cruise groundspeed and fuel flow apply.

The method is the same for both. First, the height to change: cruising level minus the starting height, or minus circuit height for a descent. Second, the time: that height divided by the rate. Third, the groundspeed for the phase: its average TAS, corrected for the wind. And fourth, the distance: time multiplied by groundspeed in nautical miles a minute.

Let's work one, a Wheatbelt flight. Departure elevation 100 ft, cruise at 5,500 ft. You climb at 500 ft/min at a groundspeed of 75 kt. You descend to a circuit height of 1,100 ft at 500 ft/min and 110 kt groundspeed. Where are the top of climb and the top of descent? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 8]]
The climb. 5,500 minus 100 is 5,400 ft. 5,400 divided by 500 is 10.8 minutes. 75 divided by 60 is 1.25 NM a minute. And 10.8 times 1.25 is 13.5 NM, from departure to the top of climb.

The descent. 5,500 minus 1,100 is 4,400 ft. 4,400 divided by 500 is 8.8 minutes. 110 divided by 60 is 1.83 NM a minute. And 8.8 times 1.83 is 16 NM before the destination.

So mark the top of climb 13.5 NM along the track from departure, and the top of descent 16 NM back from the destination.

Now with the wind. Climb from 1,500 ft to 7,500 ft at 600 ft/min, with a climb TAS of 80 kt into a 10 kt headwind component. Descend from 8,500 ft to 1,500 ft at 500 ft/min, with a descent TAS of 135 kt into a 15 kt headwind component.
[[pause 8]]
The climb. 7,500 minus 1,500 is 6,000 ft, and 6,000 divided by 600 is 10 minutes. The groundspeed is 80 minus 10, which is 70 kt, or 1.17 NM a minute. So the top of climb is 10 times 1.17, which is 11.7 NM out.

The descent. 8,500 minus 1,500 is 7,000 ft, and 7,000 divided by 500 is 14 minutes. The groundspeed is 135 minus 15, which is 120 kt, or 2 NM a minute. So the top of descent is 14 times 2, which is 28 NM before the destination.

So, 11.7 NM out and 28 NM back. A headwind pulls the top of climb closer to departure. A tailwind would push the top of descent further out.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Use the groundspeed for the phase: not the cruise TAS, and not the still-air climb speed. And subtract the starting height. A climb from a 1,500 ft aerodrome to 7,500 ft is 6,000 ft of climbing, not 7,500.

## Bringing it together

You are planning Jandakot to a Wheatbelt strip, 96 NM on a track of {070|zero seven zero} true, at 6,500 ft. The forecast wind is {020|zero two zero} true at 25 kt, and the temperature is 10 degrees.

ISA there is 2 degrees. So the density height is about 7,500 ft, and your 100 kt CAS is about 115 kt TAS. The wind gives right drift of about 10 degrees, and a groundspeed of about 97 kt. So you steer {060|zero six zero} true, {062|zero six two} magnetic.

The 6,400 ft climb at 500 ft/min takes about 13 minutes. At 70 kt over the ground, it ends 15 NM out. A 5,000 ft descent at 500 ft/min and 90 kt starts 15 NM before the strip. That leaves a 66 NM cruise, at about 1.6 NM a minute, which takes roughly 40 minutes.

On arrival, the ERSA table gives you the crosswind. And the fuel gauge's US gallons become kilograms on the load sheet.

## Key points

Let's recap.

Time is the distance divided by the groundspeed over 60: 60 kt is 1 NM a minute, 90 kt 1.5, 120 kt 2, 150 kt 2.5. Endurance is the fuel on board minus the reserve, divided by the fuel flow; convert hours to minutes carefully.

Variation east, magnetic least; west, magnetic best. Solve wind triangles in true, and convert the heading at the end.

To a smaller unit, a bigger number. 1 m is 3.28 ft, 1 NM is 1.852 km, 1 kg is 2.205 lb, 1 US gallon is 3.785 L, and avgas is 0.72 kg per litre.

Headwind is the wind times the cosine of the wind angle, crosswind the wind times the sine, with both in the same reference. The clock rule: 15, 30, 45 and 60 degrees give a quarter, a half, 0.7 and 0.9. Use the computer or the ERSA table.

CAS comes from the POH table. TAS comes from the computer, or at about 2 percent per 1,000 ft of density height.

Drift is about the crosswind times 60, divided by the TAS, and groundspeed about the TAS minus the headwind. The nose points into the wind: wind from the left, right drift, heading left of track. The track made good is the heading corrected by the drift, downwind.

The gradient in feet per nautical mile is the rate divided by the groundspeed over 60. A 3 degree descent needs about the groundspeed times 5, in feet per minute.

For the top of climb and descent, height to change over rate is the time, and time times the phase groundspeed is the distance.

A final exam tip. Practise the per-minute speeds until they are automatic. Keep every wind problem in one reference: true for forecast winds, magnetic for the tower. Show the arithmetic step by step on the scribble paper. And for every computer answer, run the mental check: the drift from the {1 in 60|one in sixty} rule, the groundspeed from the headwind component, and a heading that points into the wind.

That's the end of PNVC 2.4.
