This is PNVC 2.2, Time. By the end you will be able to tell apart the four kinds of time a pilot meets, turn a longitude into time, carry a reading from the AIP daylight graphs through UTC to your watch, and name what can end useful daylight early. There are worked examples and questions along the way, so pause whenever you want a moment to think.

## What the exam expects (PNVC 2.2)

The exam wants four things from you. First, to explain UTC, local mean time, local standard time and local summer time, and how they differ. Second, to read the beginning and the end of civil twilight from the AIP daylight and darkness graphs, to the precision the syllabus asks for. Third, to convert between local mean time, UTC, local standard time and local summer time. And fourth, to list the conditions that can make useful daylight end before the time the darkness graph gives.

## Why this matters

In the RPL you learned to turn Western Standard Time into UTC and back. The PPL takes you far enough across the state for a second kind of time to matter. That is time by the sun.

Leave Jandakot for Kalgoorlie at three in the afternoon in June, and you are flying towards an earlier sunset. Kalgoorlie sits more than 5 degrees of longitude east of Perth. And last light at your destination, not at your departure point, decides when you must be on the ground.

The AIP gives first and last light as graphs in local mean time. So every reading has to be converted before it means anything on your watch. And a flight to Melbourne or Adelaide in summer adds daylight saving on top.

The exam supplies a graph, or a reading from one, and asks you to carry it through to WST without losing a date. And it asks what can steal daylight before the graph says it is gone.

## Four clocks for one moment

Picture yourself on the apron at Jandakot, at the instant the sun is highest in the sky. Four different clocks could describe that moment. Each exists for a reason, so let's take them in turn.

The first clock is UTC, one clock for the whole world. UTC, Coordinated Universal Time, is the time at the Greenwich meridian, longitude 0. It is kept by atomic clocks, and it is never moved for summer. It is the single time used in aviation. Flight notifications, forecasts, NOTAMs, SARTIMEs and ATC logs are all in UTC. It is written as a four-figure group, often with a Z, for Zulu. So {0330Z|oh three thirty zulu} is {0330|oh three thirty} UTC. The RPL lesson on time covers it in full. In this lesson, UTC is the hub. Every conversion passes through it.

The second clock is local mean time: time by the sun. Before railways and telegraphs, every town kept its own time from the sun. Noon was the moment the sun crossed the town's meridian. So a town a little east of yours had its noon a little earlier.

That is local mean time, LMT. It is time based on the sun's mean position relative to your own meridian. Noon LMT is the moment the mean sun crosses it. It is called mean because the real sun runs a few minutes fast or slow through the year. LMT uses an average sun that keeps perfect time.

LMT is tied to longitude, and that is exactly why it matters to you. Sunrise and sunset sweep across the country from east to west with the earth's rotation. The earth turns 360 degrees in 24 hours. So the sun appears to move 15 degrees of longitude every hour. You'll find more on that in the lesson on the form of the earth.

Every place has its own LMT. Two places one degree of longitude apart differ by 4 minutes. The daylight and darkness graphs give their times in LMT because that lets one graph serve every longitude. The sun rises at much the same LMT at Perth and at Kalgoorlie, on the same latitude and date, even though the clock times are different.

The third clock is local standard time: one clock per zone. A country cannot run trains and phone calls on a different time in every town. So it divides itself into zones. Each zone keeps one legal clock time, a whole or half number of hours from UTC. That clock is local standard time.

The zone covering Western Australia is set on the meridian of 120 degrees east. 120 divided by 15 is 8. So Western Standard Time, WST, is UTC plus 8. Central Standard Time, CST, is UTC plus 9 and a half. And Eastern Standard Time, EST, is UTC plus 10. The AIP uses those abbreviations: WST, CST and EST.

Perth lies a little west of 120 degrees east. So the sun crosses Perth's meridian about a quarter of an hour after noon WST. Standard time is a compromise that keeps a whole zone on one clock. LMT is the sun's own time at your spot.

[[diagram:utc-and-wst-strip]]
Picture two time strips, one above the other, lined up so the same moment sits in the same column. UTC is on top, and WST underneath, always 8 hours ahead. Because of that offset, the WST date changes at {1600|sixteen hundred} UTC. Two examples sit on the strips. {1530|Fifteen thirty} WST is {0730|oh seven thirty} UTC, the same day. And {0545|oh five forty-five} WST on Tuesday 7 October is {2145|twenty-one forty-five} UTC on Monday 6 October.

The fourth clock is local summer time, an extra hour in some states. Summer time, or daylight saving, moves the clocks one hour ahead of standard time in summer, so that daylight falls later in the evening. Eastern Daylight Time, EDT, is UTC plus 11. Central Daylight Time, CDT, is UTC plus 10 and a half.

Summer time runs from the first Sunday in October to the first Sunday in April. It applies in New South Wales, Victoria, the ACT, Tasmania and South Australia. Western Australia does not observe daylight saving. Neither do Queensland and the Northern Territory. So in summer the difference between Perth and Sydney is 3 hours, not 2. Verify the current arrangements in AIP GEN 2.1.

So, the four clocks side by side. UTC is based on the Greenwich meridian and atomic clocks, and its offset from UTC is zero. LMT is based on the mean sun crossing your own meridian, and its offset is your longitude converted to time. Standard time is based on the zone's meridian: WST plus 8, CST plus 9 and a half, and EST plus 10. Summer time is standard time plus one hour: CDT plus 10 and a half, and EDT plus 11. And there is none in Western Australia, Queensland or the Northern Territory.

Question. Why do the daylight graphs give their times in LMT, instead of in WST?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because sunrise and sunset follow the sun's position relative to your own meridian, and that is exactly what LMT measures. In LMT the times depend only on the date and the latitude, so one graph serves every longitude. Converting to a zone time afterwards brings in the longitude.

## Arc to time: turning longitude into hours and minutes

The bridge between LMT and UTC is the arc-to-time conversion. It is how far the earth turns, measured in time, between Greenwich and your meridian. And it comes straight from the 15 degrees an hour.

There are three steps on the ladder. 15 degrees of longitude is 1 hour. 1 degree is 60 divided by 15, which is 4 minutes. And 1 minute of arc is again 60 divided by 15, which is 4 seconds.

So the method goes like this. Divide the degrees by 15 for the whole hours. Multiply the degrees left over by 4, for minutes. Then multiply the minutes of arc by 4, for seconds. And round to the nearest minute at the end.

Let's work one. Perth Airport is at longitude 115 degrees 58 minutes east. What is its arc to time? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
Step one, the hours. 115 divided by 15 is 7, with 115 minus 105, which is 10 degrees, left over. So 7 hours.

Step two, the remaining degrees. 10 times 4 is 40 minutes.

Step three, the minutes of arc. 58 times 4 is 232 seconds, which is 3 minutes 52 seconds.

Step four, the total. 7 hours 40 minutes, plus 3 minutes 52 seconds, is 7 hours 43 minutes 52 seconds. That is about 7 hours 44 minutes. So Perth's LMT is 7 hours 44 minutes ahead of UTC.

Now Kalgoorlie, at 30 degrees 47 minutes south, 121 degrees 28 minutes east. Only the longitude matters for arc to time. Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
The hours: 121 divided by 15 is 8, with 121 minus 120, which is 1 degree, left over. So 8 hours. The remaining degree: 1 times 4 is 4 minutes. The minutes of arc: 28 times 4 is 112 seconds, which is 1 minute 52 seconds. The total is 8 hours 5 minutes 52 seconds, about 8 hours 6 minutes.

So Kalgoorlie's LMT is 8 hours 6 minutes ahead of UTC. That is 22 minutes further ahead than Perth's. Its sun does everything 22 minutes earlier by the clock.

Now try two for yourself. Bunbury, at 115 degrees 40 minutes east. And Geraldton, at 114 degrees 42 minutes east.
[[pause 8]]
Bunbury first. 115 divided by 15 is 7 hours, with 10 degrees left over. 10 times 4 is 40 minutes. Then 40 minutes of arc times 4 is 160 seconds, which is 2 minutes 40 seconds. The total is 7 hours 42 minutes 40 seconds, about 7 hours 43 minutes.

Geraldton. 114 divided by 15 is 7 hours, with 114 minus 105, which is 9 degrees, left over. 9 times 4 is 36 minutes. Then 42 minutes of arc times 4 is 168 seconds, which is 2 minutes 48 seconds. The total is 7 hours 38 minutes 48 seconds, about 7 hours 39 minutes.

So Bunbury is about 7 hours 43 minutes, and Geraldton about 7 hours 39 minutes.

Now the direction, and it follows from the rotation. The earth spins west to east. So places east of Greenwich see the sun earlier, and their LMT is ahead of UTC. For an east longitude, LMT equals UTC plus the arc to time. So UTC equals LMT minus the arc to time. For a west longitude it is the other way round. But in Australia every longitude is east.

There is also a shortcut from LMT straight to WST. WST is the LMT of the 120 degrees east meridian. So the gap between your LMT and WST is just your longitude's distance from 120 degrees, at 4 minutes a degree.

Perth Airport is 120 degrees minus 115 degrees 58 minutes, which is 4 degrees 2 minutes, west of that meridian. 4 times 4 is 16 minutes, plus 2 times 4, which is 8 seconds. So Perth LMT runs about 16 minutes behind WST. Kalgoorlie, 1 degree 28 minutes east of 120, runs about 6 minutes ahead.

West of 120, add the difference to LMT to get WST. East of it, subtract. Use this as a cross-check on the full conversion through UTC. The full conversion is the method the exam expects you to show.

[[diagram:lmt-vs-wst-longitude]]
Picture a strip of longitude running from 114 degrees east on the left to 122 degrees east on the right. The 120 degree meridian is marked as WST, UTC plus 8, and each degree along the strip is 4 minutes. Geraldton, at 114 degrees 42 minutes, sits far left, about 21 minutes behind WST. Bunbury is about 17 minutes behind, and Perth Airport about 16. Kalgoorlie, right of the 120 line, is about 6 minutes ahead. Underneath, a worked line for Perth: {1815|eighteen fifteen} LMT minus 7 hours 44 is {1031|ten thirty-one} UTC, and plus 8 hours is {1831|eighteen thirty-one} WST. That is the same as {1815|eighteen fifteen} plus 16 minutes.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Two slips cost marks. The first is the remainder. After dividing by 15, the leftover degrees are multiplied by 4 to give minutes. The minutes of arc are multiplied by 4 to give seconds, not minutes. The second slip is the sign. East of Greenwich, LMT is ahead of UTC, so you subtract the arc to time to get UTC. Adding it puts you 15 to 16 hours out, and the date usually gives that away.

Question. Two places are 3 degrees of longitude apart on the same parallel. How far apart are their LMTs, and which is ahead?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: 12 minutes, because each degree is 4 minutes of the earth's turn. And the eastern one is ahead. The rotation carries it to face the sun first.

## Reading the daylight and darkness graphs

The AIP tells you when daylight begins and ends with a set of graphs in GEN 2.7. Before you can read them, you need to know what they mean by daylight.

At sunset the sun's upper edge dips below the horizon. But the sky stays bright for a while, because the atmosphere scatters sunlight down from above. That glow fades steadily.

Civil twilight is the period while the centre of the sun is between the horizon and 6 degrees below it. At the end of it, there is still enough light to see the ground and the horizon in clear weather. Shortly after it, there is not.

The AIP calls the start of morning civil twilight the beginning of daylight, or first light. And it calls the end of evening civil twilight the end of daylight, or last light. They are not sunrise and sunset. First light is before sunrise, and last light is after sunset.

Now, how the graphs are built. There are separate graphs for the beginning and for the end of daylight, and for different latitude bands. Each is entered with the date along one axis. On it sits a family of curves, one for each printed latitude. You read off the time on the other axis, and that time is in LMT. Tables of first and last light for specific aerodromes are also published. Verify the current layout in AIP GEN 2.7, because the format has changed over the years.

The procedure has five steps.

First, pick the right graph: beginning or end of daylight, and the band that covers your latitude.

Second, find the date on the date axis, and draw a line up, or across, from it.

Third, find where that line meets the curve for your latitude. Your latitude will usually fall between two printed curves. So estimate the point between them in proportion. A place at 31 degrees 30 minutes south sits halfway between the curves for 31 and 32 degrees.

Fourth, read the time off the time axis, to the nearest 5 minutes. That is the precision the syllabus asks for, and the best the graph can give.

And fifth, convert the LMT to UTC with your longitude, then to WST, or whatever zone you need.

The exam supplies the graph, or a reading from it. The skill it tests is interpolating honestly between the curves, and then not mangling the conversion.

Question. Is last light before or after sunset, and why?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: after. Last light is the end of evening civil twilight, when the centre of the sun is 6 degrees below the horizon. The sky keeps scattering light down for some time after the sun itself has set.

## Carrying a graph reading through to your watch

Every conversion in this subtopic follows one chain, and it never skips a link. Start with LMT. Subtract the arc to time, and you have UTC. Then add the zone offset, and you have local time.

Write the UTC line down every time. Mixing LMT, zone time and summer time in one step is where the marks go.

Let's work one: the end of daylight at Perth. The end-of-daylight graph gives {1815|eighteen fifteen} LMT at Perth on the day of your flight. The arc to time is 7 hours 44 minutes. What is last light in WST, and when must you be on the ground? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
Step one, LMT to UTC. {1815|Eighteen fifteen} minus 7 hours 44 minutes is {1031|ten thirty-one} UTC.

Step two, UTC to WST. {1031|Ten thirty-one} plus 8 hours is {1831|eighteen thirty-one} WST.

Step three, the cross-check with the shortcut. {1815|Eighteen fifteen} plus 16 minutes is {1831|eighteen thirty-one} WST. It agrees.

Step four, the landing time. A day VFR flight must be on the ground 10 minutes before the end of daylight. {1831|Eighteen thirty-one} minus 10 minutes is {1821|eighteen twenty-one} WST. You'll find that rule in the lesson on conditions of flight. Verify it in the current Part 91 MOS.

So last light is {1831|eighteen thirty-one} WST, and {1821|eighteen twenty-one} WST is the latest landing time you can plan on at Perth that evening.

Now one in the morning, with midnight in the way: the beginning of daylight at Kalgoorlie. The beginning-of-daylight graph gives {0535|oh five thirty-five} LMT at Kalgoorlie. The arc to time is 8 hours 6 minutes. What is first light in UTC and in WST? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
Step one, LMT to UTC. {0535|Oh five thirty-five} minus 8 hours 6 minutes is negative. So you have gone back past midnight. Add 24 hours, which makes it {2935|twenty-nine thirty-five}, and subtract. {2935|Twenty-nine thirty-five} minus 8 hours 6 minutes is {2129|twenty-one twenty-nine} UTC, on the previous day.

Step two, UTC to WST. {2129|Twenty-one twenty-nine} plus 8 hours is {2929|twenty-nine twenty-nine}. That is past {2400|twenty-four hundred}, so subtract 24. The answer is {0529|oh five twenty-nine} WST. And the date moves forward again, back to the day you started on.

Step three, the cross-check. Kalgoorlie is 6 minutes east of the zone meridian. So {0535|oh five thirty-five} minus 6 minutes is {0529|oh five twenty-nine} WST. It agrees.

So first light at Kalgoorlie is {0529|oh five twenty-nine} WST. In UTC it falls on the evening before, and that matters for anything you file.

Now try two for yourself. First: the end-of-daylight graph gives {1820|eighteen twenty} LMT at Bunbury, where the arc to time is 7 hours 43 minutes. By when must a day VFR flight be on the ground there, in WST? Second: the beginning-of-daylight graph gives {0550|oh five fifty} LMT at Geraldton, where the arc to time is 7 hours 39 minutes. What is first light in UTC and in WST?
[[pause 8]]
Bunbury first. {1820|Eighteen twenty} minus 7 hours 43 minutes is {1037|ten thirty-seven} UTC. Plus 8 hours is {1837|eighteen thirty-seven} WST. On the ground 10 minutes before that, by {1827|eighteen twenty-seven} WST. The shortcut check: Bunbury is 4 degrees 20 minutes west of 120 degrees, about 17 minutes. {1820|Eighteen twenty} plus 17 minutes is {1837|eighteen thirty-seven}.

Now Geraldton. {0550|Oh five fifty} minus 7 hours 39 minutes is negative. So add 24 hours, making {2950|twenty-nine fifty}, and subtract. That gives {2211|twenty-two eleven} UTC, on the previous day. Plus 8 hours is {3011|thirty eleven}. Minus 24 gives {0611|oh six eleven} WST, back on the day you started. The shortcut check: Geraldton is 5 degrees 18 minutes west of 120 degrees, about 21 minutes. {0550|Oh five fifty} plus 21 minutes is {0611|oh six eleven}.

So, on the ground at Bunbury by {1827|eighteen twenty-seven} WST. And first light at Geraldton is {2211|twenty-two eleven} UTC the previous day, which is {0611|oh six eleven} WST.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Last light at your destination governs, not at the aerodrome you leave. Flying east, the destination's LMT is further ahead of UTC. So its last light comes earlier by the clock. On the same evening, Kalgoorlie's end of daylight is roughly 20 minutes before Perth's, before any difference in latitude is counted. And the 10-minute margin comes off the end of daylight, not off sunset.

## Summer time and the other zones

Summer time only adds one more link to the chain. Convert to UTC first. Then apply the offset for the zone and the season you need. UTC itself never changes for summer, and that is why it makes such a reliable middle step.

Let's work one: Melbourne to Perth in summer. You depart Melbourne at {0900|oh nine hundred} Eastern Daylight Time, which is UTC plus 11. The flight time to Perth is 4 hours 30 minutes. What time do you land, in WST? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
Step one, the departure in UTC. {0900|Oh nine hundred} minus 11 hours is negative. So add 24 hours, making {3300|thirty-three hundred}, and subtract. That gives {2200|twenty-two hundred} UTC, on the previous day.

Step two, the arrival in UTC. {2200|Twenty-two hundred} plus 4 hours 30 minutes is {2630|twenty-six thirty}. Minus 24 gives {0230|oh two thirty} UTC, back on the departure date.

Step three, the arrival in WST. {0230|Oh two thirty} plus 8 hours is {1030|ten thirty} WST.

So you leave Melbourne at 9 in the morning, and arrive in Perth at half past 10 the same morning, {1030|ten thirty} WST. That is after four and a half hours in the air. The 3-hour summer difference has eaten most of the flight.

Now Perth to Adelaide in summer. You depart Perth at {1400|fourteen hundred} WST in January. The flight time is 3 hours 10 minutes. Adelaide is on Central Daylight Time, UTC plus 10 and a half. What do the clocks in Adelaide say when you land? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
Step one, the departure in UTC. {1400|Fourteen hundred} minus 8 hours is {0600|oh six hundred} UTC.

Step two, the arrival in UTC. {0600|Oh six hundred} plus 3 hours 10 minutes is {0910|oh nine ten} UTC.

Step three, the arrival in Central Daylight Time. {0910|Oh nine ten} plus 10 hours 30 minutes is {1940|nineteen forty} CDT, the same date.

So the clocks in Adelaide say {1940|nineteen forty} when you land. That is five and a half hours after you left Perth at {1400|fourteen hundred}. Three hours ten in the air, and two and a half hours of time zone.

The same chain converts between two LMTs. Say you want the LMT at Kalgoorlie at the moment it is {1200|twelve hundred} LMT at Perth. Go through UTC. {1200|Twelve hundred} minus 7 hours 44 minutes is {0416|oh four sixteen} UTC. Then {0416|oh four sixteen} plus 8 hours 6 minutes is {1222|twelve twenty-two} LMT at Kalgoorlie.

Or take the difference in longitude directly. 121 degrees 28 minutes, minus 115 degrees 58 minutes, is 5 degrees 30 minutes. 5 degrees times 4 is 20 minutes, and 30 minutes of arc times 4 is 120 seconds, which is 2 minutes. That is 22 minutes. Kalgoorlie is east, so it is ahead. {1222|Twelve twenty-two} again.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. The exam may give a summer-time departure and ask for a WST arrival, or the reverse. Never subtract "Perth is 2 hours behind Sydney" from a summer time. In summer the gap is 3 hours. Go through UTC, with the offset for the season and the zone. And WA's time is UTC plus 8 all year.

Question. It is January. What is the time difference between Perth and Adelaide? And why is it not the 1 and a half hours shown on a standard-time map?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: 2 and a half hours. In summer Adelaide is on Central Daylight Time, UTC plus 10 and a half. Perth stays on UTC plus 8 all year, because WA has no daylight saving.

## When daylight ends before the graph says

The darkness graph answers a narrow question. When does civil twilight end at sea level, with a clear sky and an unobstructed, flat horizon? Real evenings rarely match that. The light that matters to you is the light to see the ground, the horizon and the runway. And several things take it away early. There are five.

First, high terrain to the west. The sun sets behind a ridge or range before it sets behind the true horizon. Shadow fills the valleys well before the published last light. Fly east of the Darling Scarp late in the day, and the eastern side is in shadow while the coastal plain is still bright.

Second, cloud. A heavy overcast, especially a thick layer to the west, cuts out the scattered light that makes twilight. Under it, dusk arrives early and fast.

Third, poor visibility. Haze, smoke from autumn burns in the South West, dust in the wheatbelt, or rain all absorb light and blur the horizon. The light level falls sooner. And the horizon you need for attitude goes first.

Fourth, low level or a valley. The graph applies at the surface with an open horizon. Flying low, or in a valley, your horizon is higher. So the useful light for map reading ends sooner than the twilight the graph gives.

And fifth, a destination lower or further east. A destination at a lower elevation is in shadow sooner. And one further east than the place you read the graph for has an earlier last light by the clock.

For these reasons, CASA's advice is to plan to land with a margin on top of the 10-minute rule. Treat last light as a hard deadline, not a target. A pilot without a night rating who arrives in deep twilight is landing in conditions the rating exists for.

Question. The graph says last light at your destination is {1840|eighteen forty} WST, and the evening is clear. But a range of hills lies just west of the airstrip. Why should you plan to be on the ground well before {1830|eighteen thirty}?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the graph assumes a flat horizon at sea level. The hills hide the sun, and fill the strip's surroundings with shadow before the published time. So useful daylight there ends earlier than {1840|eighteen forty}. And the 10-minute rule is only the legal minimum.

## Bringing it together

It is a hazy autumn Friday, and you want to fly Jandakot to Bunbury after work. The flight is about 40 minutes.

The end-of-daylight graph for Bunbury gives {1820|eighteen twenty} LMT for the date. The arc to time for 115 degrees 40 minutes east is 7 hours 43 minutes. So last light is {1820|eighteen twenty} minus 7 hours 43 minutes, which is {1037|ten thirty-seven} UTC, or {1837|eighteen thirty-seven} WST. The 10-minute rule puts you on the ground by {1827|eighteen twenty-seven}.

Suppose a NOTAM lists a temporary restricted area on your track near Mandurah, until {0930|oh nine thirty} UTC. That is {1730|seventeen thirty} WST. You will be clear of it if you leave after then.

Now the factors. There is smoke from a burn-off south of Pinjarra, and the forecast mentions haze. Both steal light and horizon. So you add a margin, and plan to land by {1815|eighteen fifteen}.

Work back from {1815|eighteen fifteen}. 40 minutes of flight, and 15 minutes of start-up and taxi, put the latest sensible engine start at {1720|seventeen twenty}. That is ten minutes before the restriction lifts. So the answer is to plan a track around it, or wait for another evening. Four kinds of time, one decision.

## Key points

Let's recap.

UTC is Greenwich time, the hub of every conversion. LMT is time by the mean sun on your own meridian. Standard time is the zone's legal clock: WST is UTC plus 8, CST plus 9 and a half, and EST plus 10. Summer time adds an hour, making CDT plus 10 and a half, and EDT plus 11. It applies in the south-eastern states from the first Sunday in October to the first Sunday in April. WA, Queensland and the Northern Territory do not use it.

The AIP daylight graphs give the beginning and end of civil twilight, with the sun 6 degrees below the horizon, in LMT. You enter them with the date and your latitude. Interpolate between the latitude curves, and read to the nearest 5 minutes.

Arc to time: 15 degrees is 1 hour, 1 degree is 4 minutes, and 1 minute of arc is 4 seconds. Perth is 7 hours 44 minutes. For an east longitude, UTC equals LMT minus the arc to time. Then add the zone offset. Always write the UTC line, and watch midnight.

Daylight ends early with high ground to the west. With cloud, haze, smoke, dust or rain. At low level or in a valley. And at a destination that is lower, or further east. Treat last light as a deadline, with a margin.

A final exam tip. Longitude divided by 15 gives the hours. The remainder times 4 gives the minutes. And the minutes of arc times 4 give the seconds. East longitude means LMT is ahead of UTC, so subtract. Convert graph times from LMT to UTC first, then to WST, which is UTC plus 8 with no daylight saving. Check the answer with the shortcut: Perth LMT is about 16 minutes behind WST. And the 10-minute rule applies to the end of daylight, not to sunset.

That's the end of PNVC 2.2.
