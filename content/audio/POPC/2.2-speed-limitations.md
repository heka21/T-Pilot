This is POPC 2.2, Speed limitations, for the private licence. By the end you will be able to explain each speed and load limit, work out the manoeuvring speed for your weight, say why it falls as the aeroplane gets lighter, and recognise the situations in private flying that break the limits. There are worked examples and questions along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (POPC 2.2)

The exam wants two things from you. First, explain the normal operating speed VNO, the never exceed speed VNE, the manoeuvring speed VA, the turbulence penetration speed VB, the limit and design load factors, and the flap speeds VFO and VFE. And second, describe the situations that can take an aeroplane past its speed and load factor limits.

## Why this matters

You have met every one of these speeds already, in the recreational lessons on speed limitations and on flaps. The PPL syllabus repeats them because the flying changes.

As a private pilot you cruise higher and further. You descend from 8,500 ft into a terminal area with a schedule to keep. You cross the Darling Scarp in an easterly, with mountain waves rolling off it. And you share circuits at Perth or Jandakot with aircraft whose wake can roll you. Those are the situations in which limits get broken, not a calm circuit at a training aerodrome.

The PPLA exam asks you to define each limit. But it also asks for the numbers behind VA, and which way it moves with weight. And it gives you a scenario, and asks what you should have done. So this lesson recaps each limit briefly, then spends its time on the reasoning a PPL candidate needs.

## The speed scale in one picture

Start where you start in the aeroplane: the airspeed indicator. Its colours carry most of the limits. And the ones that are not on the dial are the ones the exam likes to ask about.

[[diagram:asi-colour-markings]]
Picture the face of the airspeed indicator, with its markings running clockwise as the speed rises. A white arc, the flap range. A green arc, from VS1, the clean stall speed, up to VNO. A yellow arc. And a red line. Notice what is missing: VA has no mark at all. Now read it from the top down.

The red line is the never exceed speed, VNE. Beyond it, the designers have not shown the aeroplane to be free of flutter. Flutter is a self-feeding oscillation of a control surface or wing, and it can destroy the aeroplane in seconds. Beyond VNE the structure and controls may fail. It is a limit in any air, smooth or rough.

The yellow arc runs from VNO to VNE. It is the caution range: legal, but for smooth air only.

The top of the green arc is the normal operating speed, VNO, also called the maximum structural cruising speed. Up to it, the structure is designed to take the gusts the certification standard assumes you will meet in turbulence. So do not exceed VNO except in smooth air.

The white arc is the flap range. Its top is the flap extended speed, VFE, the maximum speed with flap out. Its bottom is VSO, the full-flap stall speed at maximum weight.

Four things are not on the dial. VA, VB, VFO where it differs from VFE, and the load factors. They live in section 2 of the POH, the pilot's operating handbook, and on placards. The reason VA is missing is the most examinable fact in this subtopic. It changes with weight, and a fixed mark on the dial could only be right at one weight.

Question. The needle is just above the top of the green arc, on a smooth, clear evening descent. Are you breaking a limit?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: no. The yellow arc, from VNO to VNE, is permitted in smooth air. But you are now relying on the air staying smooth. The moment it is not, you slow back into the green.

## Load factors: limit and design

Every speed limit in this lesson protects one of two things. Either the structure against load factor, or the airframe against something that grows with speed.

Load factor is lift divided by weight, measured in g. It is 1 g in level flight. In a level turn it is one over the cosine of the bank angle, which makes it 2 g at 60 degrees. The recreational lesson on speed limitations builds that from first principles.

The limit load factor is the most the structure is designed to carry in service without permanent deformation. Up to it, the airframe flexes and springs back. It depends on the certification category, and each step up is stronger. The normal category has a limit of plus 3.8 g and minus 1.52 g. The utility category has plus 4.4 g and minus 1.76 g. And the aerobatic category has plus 6.0 g and minus 3.0 g.

The design load factor, also called the ultimate load factor, is the limit multiplied by a safety factor of 1.5. So the positive design loads are 5.7 g for normal, 6.6 g for utility, and 9.0 g for aerobatic. Between the limit and the ultimate load, the structure is not expected to break, but it may bend permanently. Beyond the ultimate load, it may fail.

[[diagram:category-load-limits]]
Picture three bars side by side, one for each category, with load factor running up the page. Each bar has a mark at its limit: 3.8 g, 4.4 g and 6 g. Above each limit, a band runs up to the ultimate load, at 1.5 times the limit, where the structure may bend permanently. Now put two turns against them. A 60 degree level turn sits at 2 g. A 75 degree turn sits at about 3.86 g, already past the normal category limit.

Two PPL-level points follow. First, the 1.5 margin is not yours to use. It absorbs scatter in materials and manufacture, corrosion, fatigue, and the over-stresses of previous pilots you know nothing about. An aeroplane that has been taken to 4.5 g and landed safely needs an inspection. It has not proved that it is strong.

Second, many four-seat trainers carry two categories at once. Utility at a reduced weight, and normal at full weight. The loading systems in the PPL lesson on loading have both envelopes. The structure is the same. What changes is the weight the limit load factor applies to. Lift is what the spar carries. So 4.4 g at the utility weight and 3.8 g at the higher normal weight put roughly comparable loads on the wing. Load the aeroplane above the utility weight, and the lower normal category limit applies to every manoeuvre you fly that day.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. The limit load, not the ultimate load, is the one you must not exceed. Suppose someone says it didn't break at 5 g, in a normal category aeroplane. That means it went past 3.8 g, into the range where the structure may now be permanently bent. Report it.

Question. A training aeroplane is certified utility up to one weight, and normal above it. With four people on board, which limit load factor applies, and why does the number go down?
[[pause 5]]
The answer: the normal category limit, plus 3.8 g, because the aeroplane is above the utility weight. The wing structure carries lift. At a higher weight, each g needs more lift. So a lower load factor keeps the wing within the same design load.

## The manoeuvring speed, V<sub>A</sub>

First, the idea from the recreational lesson. The most load factor the wing can make before it stalls grows with the square of the airspeed. The maximum load factor equals the airspeed divided by the stall speed, squared.

At low speed, a full pull stalls the wing before it can overload anything. At high speed, the wing can make more lift than the structure can carry. The speed where the two meet is VA. It is the highest speed at which a full, abrupt control deflection will stall the wing before the limit load is reached.

[[diagram:v-n-diagram]]
Picture a graph of load factor up the side, against airspeed along the bottom, for a normal category aeroplane with a 52 kt stall speed. From the stall speed on the left, a curve sweeps upward, rising with the square of the speed. A flat line runs across the top at plus 3.8 g. Where the curve meets that line is VA, at about 101 kt. To the left of VA, a full control movement stalls the wing before the limit load. To the right of it, a pull or a gust can reach the limit load before the wing stalls.

At PPL level you are expected to produce the number. Set the maximum load factor equal to the limit load, and solve for the speed. VA equals the stall speed, times the square root of the limit load factor. For a normal category aeroplane, the square root of 3.8 is 1.95. So VA is just under twice the clean stall speed at the same weight.

Let's work one: VA at maximum weight, and at 80 percent of it. The clean stall speed is 55 kt at maximum weight. The aeroplane is normal category, with a limit of plus 3.8 g. Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 8]]
Step one, the square root of the limit. The square root of 3.8 is 1.949.

Step two, VA at maximum weight. 55 times 1.949 is 107 kt.

Step three, the weight. At 80 percent of maximum weight, the stall speed falls with the square root of the weight ratio. The square root of 0.8 is 0.894.

Step four, VA at 80 percent weight. 107 times 0.894 is 96 kt.

So the answers are 107 kt at maximum weight, and 96 kt at 80 percent of it. Solo with part fuel, the speed below which a full control input is safe is 11 kt lower than the placard figure for maximum weight.

Why lower, and not higher? A lighter aeroplane needs less lift for each g. So at any given speed, its wing can pull more g before it stalls. It therefore reaches the limit load at a lower speed. The structure has not got weaker. The stall has stopped protecting it sooner. Many POHs give VA at two or three weights for this reason. Use the figure for your weight, or the lower one.

Here is one for you to try. An aeroplane stalls clean at 60 kt at maximum weight, and is limited to plus 3.8 g. What is VA at 75 percent of maximum weight?
[[pause 8]]
VA at maximum weight is 60 times 1.949, which is {116.9|one hundred and sixteen point nine} kt. The weight factor is the square root of 0.75, which is 0.866. And {116.9|one hundred and sixteen point nine} times 0.866 is {101.3|one hundred and one point three}. So VA at 75 percent weight is about 101 kt.

Working through the stall speed gives the same answer. 60 times 0.866 is 52.0 kt, and 52.0 times 1.949 is {101.3|one hundred and one point three} kt. Either way, about 101 kt.

VA promises less than it seems, in two ways. First, it covers one full deflection in one axis. Full aileron and full elevator together, or a full deflection followed by a rapid reversal, can overload parts of the structure even below VA. And second, it says nothing about the flap. With flap down, the structure is protected by VFE and a lower flap-down load limit, not by VA.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. VA decreases as weight decreases. The distractor says it rises, because a light aeroplane is under less stress. But the structure is the same. The wing reaches the limit load at a lower speed, because it needs less lift for each g.

## Gusts: V<sub>NO</sub> and the turbulence penetration speed V<sub>B</sub>

A gust loads the wing without any input from you. Fly into a rising current, and the relative airflow briefly comes from below as well as ahead. The angle of attack jumps, and with it the lift. The extra angle depends on the gust's speed compared with your airspeed. The lift that angle produces depends on the square of your airspeed. Put the two together, and the extra load factor from a given gust grows in proportion to your airspeed.

Let's work one: the same gust at two speeds. A hypothetical aeroplane meets a sharp up-gust that adds 1.0 g at 100 kt. What does the same gust do at 130 kt? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
First, the speed ratio. 130 divided by 100 is 1.3. Second, the extra load factor at 130 kt. 1.0 times 1.3 is 1.3 g. Third, the total at 130 kt. 1 plus 1.3 is 2.3 g, against 2.0 g at 100 kt.

So the same gust gives 2.3 g at 130 kt, and 2.0 g at 100 kt. Every knot you take off before the rough air reduces the hit. The relation is a rule of thumb for a given aeroplane and weight, not a figure from any POH. The point is the proportion.

[[diagram:gust-load-vs-airspeed]]
Picture the same kind of graph, load factor against airspeed. Gust lines fan out upward from 1 g at zero speed, and the faster you go, the higher each line climbs. The strongest design gust stays inside the plus 3.8 g limit up to VNO, and crosses it just beyond. At low speed, the same gust line runs into the stall curve first. So the wing stalls momentarily, instead of overloading the structure.

That proportion is what the two gust speeds are built on. VNO is the highest speed at which the structure has been shown to take the design gust. Above it, you are in smooth-air-only territory.

VB, strictly the design speed for maximum gust intensity, is lower again. It is slow enough that the strongest design gust stalls the wing momentarily, rather than overloading it. And it is fast enough to keep a margin above the stall, so the aeroplane stays controllable while the airspeed jumps around. Where the POH publishes VB, it is the turbulence penetration speed. Many light aeroplane POHs do not. They give a rough-air or turbulence penetration speed near VA instead, and that is the speed you fly.

Why not simply fly as slowly as possible? Because the gust works both ways. A strong up-gust at a speed only a little above the stall will stall the wing. And a down-gust at that speed can leave you with too little margin to recover cleanly. The penetration speed is the compromise, between overstress at high speed and loss of control at low speed. Hold the attitude, not the airspeed, and accept that the needle will swing.

Question. VB is not published for your aeroplane. What speed do you fly in severe turbulence, and why not the bottom of the green arc?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the POH's turbulence penetration speed, which for light aeroplanes is near VA for the weight. The bottom of the green arc is too close to the stall. A strong gust, or the airspeed swings in turbulence, could stall the wing and leave too little margin to recover.

## The flap speeds, V<sub>FO</sub> and V<sub>FE</sub>

The flap, its tracks, its hinges and its operating mechanism, and the rear spar that carries them, are built for the loads at approach speeds. And air load grows with the square of speed. Hence there are two flap limits. The lesson on flaps builds them in full.

VFE, the flap extended speed, is the most you may fly with the flap out. It is the top of the white arc. A type may have a separate VFE for each flap stage, lower for the larger settings.

VFO, the flap operating speed, is the most you may be doing while the flap is moving, extending or retracting. On many light aeroplanes it equals VFE, and is not quoted separately. Where it differs, it is lower, and it is not on the dial.

[[diagram:flap-speeds-vfo-vfe]]
Picture the white arc as a speed scale, from VSO at the bottom up to VFE at the top, with VFO, on some aeroplanes, marked below VFE. Slow to VFO before the flap moves, in either direction. Once it is out, VFE is the limit for flying with it.

The PPL point is the go-around, and the busy arrival. On a missed approach at full power, the aeroplane accelerates quickly, and the flap is still out. If the nose is allowed to drop, the speed can pass the full-flap VFE before you have the flap up. And if VFO is separate, you cannot retract it either until you slow again. So hold the climb attitude, and raise the flap in the stages the POH gives.

The other trap is a fast arrival at a controlled aerodrome. You are asked to keep the speed up on final, and then you select flap with the needle still in the green arc.

Question. The POH gives VFO lower than VFE. You are between the two, with flap already out. What may you do?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: you may keep flying with the flap extended, because you are below VFE. But you may not move it, in or out, until you are at or below VFO, because VFO is the limit for flap in motion.

## How private flying breaks the limits

The recreational list still holds: spiral dives and nose-low upsets, harsh pull-outs, steep turns and accelerated stalls at speed, turbulence above VA, the yellow arc in rough air, flap above its limits, power-on descents, and over-controlling. The PPL syllabus asks you to see them in the flying you will now do. Here are five situations.

The first is the long descent with cruise power on. At the end of a cross-country, you lower the nose from 8,500 ft towards Jandakot, and leave the power where it was. The engine's thrust and the height you are trading both go into speed. Over a 15 minute descent, the needle drifts through VNO without any single moment you notice. That is fine in smooth air. Then the descent takes you into the low-level turbulence of a hot afternoon, and you are taking every bump at a yellow-arc speed. The defence is planning. Reduce power at the top of descent to hold a speed in the green, and slow further before the rough air, not after.

The second is mountain waves over the scarp. In a strong easterly, the Darling Scarp can set up waves and rotor on its lee side, over the coastal plain. The air can be smooth until it suddenly is not, and the vertical gusts are large. A cruise speed set in the smooth air ahead of the wave puts you above VA when the rotor arrives. So slow to the penetration speed when the forecast, or the first bumps, warn you.

[[diagram:rotor-in-the-lee-of-the-scarp]]
Picture a cross-section from west to east near Perth, with a strong easterly blowing from right to left. The wind flows across the plateau on the right, over the edge of the scarp, and down onto the coastal plain on the left. There it rolls into a rotor of tumbling, turbulent air, on the lee side. That rotor is where a cruise speed above VA meets its biggest gusts.

The third is wake turbulence at a controlled aerodrome. Following a heavier aircraft at Perth, a vortex encounter can roll the aeroplane abruptly. The instinct is full opposite aileron and rudder, perhaps with a pull. That is exactly the full, multi-axis deflection that VA does not protect against. And it may come at an approach speed above VFE for the flap stage you are about to select. Avoiding the wake by your flight path is the real defence.

[[diagram:wake-turbulence-avoidance]]
Picture two side views. In the first, you are taking off behind a heavier aircraft that has just departed. Its vortices start at its rotation point, so you lift off before that point, and climb above its flight path. In the second, you are landing behind a heavier aircraft that has just landed. Its vortices stop at its touchdown point, so you stay at or above its approach path, and touch down beyond that point.

The fourth is the spiral dive, and the pull-out. An unrecognised spiral, after inadvertent entry into cloud or a distraction on a long leg, builds speed fast with the nose low and the bank steep. The danger is in the recovery. At high speed the wing can make far more lift than the structure can carry. So a hard pull with the wings still banked can reach the limit load before anything stalls.

[[diagram:spin-vs-spiral-dive]]
Picture a spin and a spiral dive side by side. In the spin, both wings are stalled, and the airspeed is low and roughly steady. In the spiral dive, the wings are not stalled, and the airspeed is high and increasing. The recovery from the spiral is to close the throttle, roll level with aileron and coordinated rudder, then ease out gently.

Let's work out how much g a spiral recovery can pull. The clean stall speed is 50 kt at today's weight. The spiral has reached 130 kt. The aeroplane is normal category, limited to plus 3.8 g. Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
First, the speed ratio to the stall. 130 divided by 50 is 2.6. Second, the most load factor the wing can make before it stalls. 2.6 squared is 6.8 g. Third, compare. 6.8 g is above the 3.8 g limit, and above the 5.7 g ultimate load.

So the answer is 6.8 g. At this speed the stall gives no protection at all. A hard pull could bend or break the wing before the wing stalls. Close the throttle, roll level first so the lift points up, then ease out with gentle back pressure.

And the fifth is steep turns at speed. A 60 degree turn is 2 g. A 75 degree turn is about 3.9 g, past the normal category limit. Tightening a steep turn at cruise speed, to avoid traffic or to keep a ground feature in sight, can get there faster than the g meter in your stomach suggests.

[[widget:bank-angle]]
Picture the load factor climbing with bank: creeping to 1.41 at 45 degrees, doubling by 60, and reaching the normal category limit near 75.

Question. Why does the spiral recovery start with closing the throttle and rolling level, not with pulling back?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because at high speed the wing can make more lift than the structure can carry. So a pull with the wings banked mainly tightens the turn, and adds load factor. Closing the throttle stops the speed building. And rolling level points the lift upward, so the pull-out needs the least g.

## Bringing it together

You are flying a four-seater back to Jandakot from Merredin, with three friends on board. So the aeroplane is above its utility weight, and the limit is the normal category's 3.8 g. The POH placard gives VA at maximum weight. At today's weight you are close to it, so the placard figure is about right.

The forecast warned of a moderate easterly, and turbulence below 3,000 ft west of the scarp. At the top of descent, you reduce power so the speed stays in the green. Approaching the scarp, you slow further, to the POH's turbulence penetration speed, before the first bump rather than after the third.

Approach asks you to keep the speed up on a long final. You do. But you wait for the needle to come below VFO before the first stage of flap goes out. And you check it again before the next stage, because full flap has a lower VFE. Every limit in the lesson was in play, and none of them was broken, because each was anticipated.

Question. On the same flight, solo with half fuel, would you fly the turbulence penetration speed from the placard, or a lower one? And why?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: a lower one, where the POH gives VA or the penetration speed by weight, because both fall with the square root of the weight. At a light weight, the wing reaches the limit load at a lower speed. So the maximum-weight figure leaves you above the protected speed.

## Key points

Let's recap.

VNE is the red line, never crossed. Beyond it come flutter, and structural and control failure. VNO is the top of the green arc, set by the design gust loads. Above it is the yellow arc, for smooth air only.

VA is not on the dial. Below it, a full, abrupt deflection stalls the wing before the limit load. VA equals the stall speed times the square root of the limit load factor. That is about 1.95 times the stall speed for 3.8 g. And it is lower at a lower weight. VB is the turbulence penetration speed, and where it is not published, you fly a speed near VA.

The limit loads, with no permanent deformation, are plus 3.8 and minus 1.52 g for the normal category, plus 4.4 and minus 1.76 for utility, and plus 6.0 and minus 3.0 for aerobatic. The design, or ultimate, load is 1.5 times the limit.

VFE is the most with flap out, the top of the white arc. VFO is the most while the flap moves, and it is lower where it differs.

And the limits get broken by power-on descents into rough air, by mountain waves and turbulence taken above VA, by wake encounters met with full multi-axis inputs, by spiral dives recovered with a hard banked pull, by steep turns tightened at speed, and by flap near the go-around above its limits. The defence is to anticipate. Slow down before the rough air. And in a spiral, close the throttle and roll level before easing out.

A final exam tip. Expect a VA calculation. That is the stall speed times the square root of 3.8, then times the square root of the weight ratio for a lighter weight. And expect a question on which way VA moves. It falls with weight. Have by heart the normal category plus 3.8 g and minus 1.52 g, the 1.5 safety factor, and 2 g at 60 degrees. Know which limits are on the dial, VNO, VNE and VFE, and which are not, VA, VB and VFO. The turbulence speed is the one to slow to before the rough air, not after. And your POH figures win over any in this lesson.

That's the end of POPC 2.2.
