This is POPC 2.7, Airworthiness and equipment, for the private licence. By the end you will know what the certificate of registration and the certificate of airworthiness are for, how to build the equipment list for any flight from its scenario, what maintenance you may do yourself, and how to read a maintenance release and give a verdict. There are worked examples and questions along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (POPC 2.7)

The exam wants three things from you. First, say what the certificate of registration and the certificate of airworthiness are for. Second, from a flight scenario, work out the communication, normal and emergency equipment the aircraft must carry. And third, your responsibilities as pilot in command, in four parts. The daily inspection. Recording and reporting defects. The maintenance a PPL or CPL holder may carry out. And reading a copy of a maintenance release, to decide whether it is valid, which classes of operation it allows, and which defects or endorsements are outstanding, and whether they affect airworthiness.

## Why this matters

As a student, you flew a club aeroplane that someone else had prepared, on a flight your instructor had approved, mostly within sight of Jandakot or Serpentine. As a private pilot, you take that same aeroplane, or one you own a share of, to Kalbarri, Esperance or Rottnest with friends on board. And nobody else checks it.

You decide whether the paperwork makes it legal. You decide whether the equipment on board matches the flight you have planned. And you decide what you may fix yourself when a bulb blows at a country strip.

The recreational lesson on airworthiness built the chain of documents and inspections. This lesson adds what the PPL exam asks on top. Why the two certificates exist. How to extract the equipment list for a scenario. What maintenance you may do. And how to read a maintenance release line by line, and give a verdict.

## What you already know from the RPL

The recreational lesson laid out the chain that tells you an aircraft is fit to fly today. A certificate of airworthiness that stays in force while the aircraft is maintained. A certificate of registration. The aircraft's own POH. And the maintenance release, the document you check before every flight.

You learned the four questions you ask of the maintenance release. Is it current in hours and date? Is the daily certified today? Is there no grounding defect? And is nothing due before you land? You learned the difference between the certified daily inspection and the uncertified pre-flight. And you learned how a defect is endorsed in Part 2, and locks the aircraft until an engineer deals with it.

Picture the documents as links in a chain: the certificate of registration, the certificate of airworthiness, the flight manual or POH, and the maintenance release. Every link points to the maintenance release, and to its four checks. Current in hours and date. Daily certified today. No grounding defect. And nothing due before the end of the flight.

None of that changes at PPL level. What changes is the depth. The exam gives you a maintenance release and asks for a verdict. It gives you a route and asks for the equipment. And it asks which jobs you may do yourself.

## Two certificates, two questions (2.7.1)

Think of a car. Its registration papers say which car it is, and who is responsible for it. Its roadworthy inspection says it was safe when checked. An aircraft has the same split.

The certificate of registration records that the aircraft is entered on the Australian civil aircraft register, with its VH marks. And it identifies the registered operator, the person legally responsible for it, under Part 47 of the regulations. It answers the question: which aircraft is this, and who answers for it? It is the aircraft's identity document. And it is a legal document, not an engineering one. It says nothing about whether the aircraft is in good condition. But an unregistered aircraft cannot be flown.

The certificate of airworthiness answers a different question. Was this aircraft built to an approved design, and is it kept to it? It certifies that the aircraft conforms to its type design, and was in a condition for safe operation when the certificate was issued, under Part 21. It has no expiry date. Instead, it remains in force as long as the aircraft is maintained in accordance with the regulations and its approved maintenance program, and is operated within its flight manual. Stop the maintenance, or operate outside the flight manual, and the certificate no longer means what it says.

The certificate of airworthiness is issued in a category that defines the operations permitted. Normal, utility or aerobatic for standard aircraft, or a special category for experimental and limited aircraft. That is where the limits on what you may do with the aircraft start. An aircraft certificated in the normal category is not one to loop, whatever its owner thinks of its handling.

Neither certificate tells you that the aircraft is fit to fly today. That is the job of the maintenance release, the day-to-day evidence of continuing airworthiness. So together, the three documents answer three questions. Registration: who the aircraft is. The certificate of airworthiness: that it was built, and is maintained, to standard. And the maintenance release: that it is fit to fly now.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Treating the certificate of airworthiness as proof that the aircraft is airworthy today. It certifies conformity to the type design, and it stays in force only while the maintenance and operating conditions are met. The document that shows current serviceability is the maintenance release.

Question. An aircraft's maintenance release expired last week. Is its certificate of airworthiness still meaningful, and may it fly?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the certificate is in force only while the aircraft is maintained as the regulations and its maintenance program require. With the maintenance release expired, that condition is not met. So the aircraft may not fly until it is maintained and released again.

## Working out the equipment for a flight (2.7.2)

The equipment an aircraft must carry is not one fixed list. The Part 91 Manual of Standards sets a base for the kind of operation: day VFR, night VFR, or IFR. Then it adds items according to where you fly. How high, over what, how far from the aerodrome, and in which airspace. So the exam gives you a scenario, and expects you to build the list. Work through it in four groups, in the same order every time.

The first group is the normal equipment: the flight and engine instruments. These are what you need to fly the aeroplane under day VFR at all. For a day VFR private flight in a light aeroplane, there are five. An airspeed indicator, to stay above the stall and below the structural limits. An altimeter, to fly the minimum heights, the cruising level and the circuit. A magnetic compass, the one direction reference that needs no power. A clock or watch showing hours, minutes and seconds, for navigation and timing. And engine instruments, with fuel quantity indication.

Night and IFR add lighting, attitude and heading instruments, a turn and slip indicator, and the navigation equipment for the route. None of these is required for day VFR.

The second group is communication equipment. Radio and transponder requirements follow from airspace and height, because they exist so that air traffic control and other aircraft can talk to you and see you.

A VHF radio is required in four cases. For flight in controlled airspace. At certified and registered aerodromes. At CTAF aerodromes where radio is required. And above 5,000 ft. A transponder, with altitude encoding, is required in Class A, C and E airspace, and above 10,000 ft. Verify the current list in the Part 91 Manual of Standards and the AIP. And an ADS-B transmitter is required where the rules require it.

The third group is navigation equipment. The charts and documents for the route, and navigation aids as needed for the airspace. For day VFR, the main item is current charts covering the whole route, and ERSA.

The fourth group is emergency equipment. This is where the scenario details matter most, because each item answers a particular way of coming to grief. There are seven items.

First, an ELT, an emergency locator transmitter. It is required for flights beyond the vicinity of the departure aerodrome, which means more than 50 nm, with exemptions for some local flights. Beyond about 50 nm, a search could not quickly cover the area you might be in. So the aircraft must be able to tell rescuers where it is.

Second, a first aid kit. Third, a fire extinguisher in the cabin, for aircraft above a certain seat count. Check the Manual of Standards for the figure.

Fourth, life jackets, for flight over water beyond gliding distance of land. If the engine stops there, the landing is a ditching. Fifth, life rafts and signalling equipment, for extended over-water flight.

Sixth, survival equipment, for flight over designated remote areas, where you might wait days for rescue. That means water, signalling equipment, and the items prescribed.

And seventh, oxygen. It is required for flight above 10,000 ft beyond the time limit, and above the altitude where it is always required.

[[diagram:survival-priorities]]
Picture the survival priorities after a forced landing in remote country, as a list in order. Protection comes first: first aid and shelter. Then location, helping rescuers find you. Then water. And food last. Stay with the aircraft. It is easier to see than a person, it gives shade, and the search follows your notified route. And activate the ELT, which is why remote and distant flights must carry one.

Each of the trigger words in a scenario points at an item. Height: a radio above 5,000 ft, and a transponder and oxygen above 10,000 ft. Airspace: the radio and transponder. Distance from the departure aerodrome: the ELT. Water: life jackets and rafts. And a remote area: survival equipment. Read the scenario for those words, then walk the four groups.

Let's work one: equipment for a remote inland flight. It is a private day VFR flight from Jandakot to Meekatharra, about 350 nm, with three people on board. You cruise at 9,500 ft in Class G airspace, once clear of the Perth control zone and steps. Suppose the route crosses a designated remote area on the AIP map, and there is no water crossing. Build the list. Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 10]]
Group one, normal equipment. The airspeed indicator, altimeter, magnetic compass, a clock or watch with seconds, engine instruments, and fuel quantity indication. Nothing extra, because it is day VFR.

Group two, communication. A VHF radio, because the cruise is above 5,000 ft, and the departure is from a controlled aerodrome. A transponder is not required by the list, for Class G below 10,000 ft. It would be, if the route entered Class A, C or E, or went above 10,000 ft.

Group three, navigation. Current charts for the whole route, and ERSA.

Group four, emergency. An ELT, because the flight goes more than 50 nm from Jandakot. Survival equipment, for the remote area. A first aid kit. A fire extinguisher, if the seat count requires it. No life jackets, because there is no water. And no oxygen, because you stay below 10,000 ft.

So the list followed the scenario word by word. The 9,500 ft brought the radio. The 350 nm brought the ELT. The remote area brought the survival kit. And what was absent, water and height above 10,000 ft, took items off.

Here is one for you to try, in the other direction. A private day VFR flight from Jandakot across to Rottnest Island and back, about 20 nm each way, at 1,500 ft, with two people on board. Part of it is over open water, out of gliding distance of land. Which emergency items does the scenario add, and which does it not?
[[pause 8]]
The answer: it adds life jackets, because part of the flight is over water beyond gliding distance of land. There is no ELT on the distance test, because the flight stays within 50 nm of Jandakot. There is no remote-area survival equipment, and no oxygen. The first aid kit, and a fire extinguisher if the seat count requires one, are carried as for any flight. And a VHF radio is needed anyway, because Jandakot is in controlled airspace. Whether this counts as extended over-water flight, for life rafts, depends on the distance set in the Manual of Standards, so check it.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Reading the ELT rule as over remote areas, or over water. The trigger in the rule as given is distance from the departure aerodrome: more than 50 nm. A short over-water hop can need life jackets and no ELT. And a long trip over farmland can need an ELT and no life jackets.

Question. You replan the Meekatharra flight at 11,500 ft, to get above a cloud layer. Which items does that add?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: a transponder with altitude encoding, because you are above 10,000 ft. And oxygen, once your time above 10,000 ft passes the limit. The higher level removes nothing already on the list.

## The pilot in command's responsibilities (2.7.3a and b)

The responsibilities are the same as in the recreational lesson. At PPL level, you meet more of them yourself. There are four.

The first is daily inspections. Before the first flight of the day, the pilot in command confirms that a daily inspection has been certified on the maintenance release. As a private pilot, you may perform and certify it yourself, when you are authorised to do so on the type. That authorisation is the difference between doing a careful walk-around, and signing a daily. The daily is done to the written schedule. And your signature on the maintenance release tells every later pilot that day that it was done. Your own pre-flight inspection is still required before every flight, and it is never certified.

[[diagram:daily-vs-preflight-inspection]]
Picture the two inspections side by side. On one side, the daily: once a day, before the first flight, to the written schedule, by an engineer or an authorised pilot, certified on the maintenance release, and valid for the rest of the day. On the other side, the pre-flight: before every flight, the POH walk-around, by the pilot in command, and not certified.

The second is recording. The pilot in command records the flight time on the maintenance release. That keeps the hours on it true, so the next pilot can tell how close the aircraft is to its next maintenance.

The third is defects. You endorse defects in Part 2 of the maintenance release. You write a clear description, the date and the time in service, and your signature and licence number. A defect found in flight is endorsed after landing. The aircraft then stays on the ground, until an engineer rectifies the defect, or endorses it as a permissible unserviceability, with any conditions.

And the fourth is reporting. Separately, you report defects affecting safety, under the safety reporting rules. The endorsement protects the next pilot of this aircraft. The report lets the system see the same fault across a fleet.

[[diagram:defect-endorsement-flow]]
Picture the path of a defect as a flow, left to right. A defect is found, or appears in flight and is dealt with after landing. It is endorsed in Part 2, with the description, the date and time in service, and the signature and licence number. And from there the aircraft cannot fly, until an engineer either rectifies it and signs it off, or endorses it as a permissible unserviceability with conditions.

Question. You are authorised to do daily inspections on the club aeroplane, and you certify one at {0800|oh eight hundred}. You fly again at {1400|fourteen hundred}. Which inspection do you do before the {1400|fourteen hundred} flight?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: only the pre-flight inspection. The daily you certified at {0800|oh eight hundred} is valid for the rest of the day. But the pre-flight is required before every flight, whoever did the daily.

## Maintenance you may do yourself (2.7.3c)

At a country strip on a Sunday, the navigation light bulb is dead, and the oil is a litre low. There is no engineer for 200 km. Can you fix it?

For some jobs, yes. Schedule 8 of the Civil Aviation Regulations lists the maintenance that the holder of a pilot licence may perform. It applies on a Class B aircraft, which means the private and aerial work categories, that they are entitled to fly. And it applies provided they are competent to do the job, and record it in the maintenance release or logbook.

The logic of the list is that every item is simple, needs no special tools and no engineering judgement, and can be checked by looking at it afterwards. Here is the list, in groups. Replenishing engine oil, hydraulic fluid and fuel. Replacing and servicing batteries. Replacing tyres and tubes, bulbs, fuses and spark plugs, the spark plugs with the correct type and gapping. Cleaning and lubricating. Replacing seats, seat belts and cabin furnishings. Removing and refitting cowlings, fairings and inspection panels. Replacing filters where no special tools are needed. And the daily inspection.

It excludes anything that needs a licensed engineer's judgement, special tools, or a test flight. A CPL holder has the same Schedule 8 privileges. And anything outside the list needs a maintenance authority, or a LAME, a licensed aircraft maintenance engineer.

So at the country strip, you may top up the oil and replace the bulb, and you must record both. Rigging a control cable, adjusting the carburettor, or changing a magneto is not on the list, whatever your mechanical skills.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Competent and recorded are part of the privilege, not decorations on it. A job on the list, done without the right parts, the right type of plug, or an entry in the maintenance release or logbook, is not authorised maintenance.

Question. Which of these may a PPL holder do on their own private aeroplane? Replace a blown landing light bulb. Replace a spark plug with the correct type and gap. Or adjust the idle mixture.
[[pause 5]]
The answer: the bulb and the spark plug are on the Schedule 8 list, provided you are competent and record the work. Adjusting the idle mixture needs an engineer's judgement, and possibly a ground run and test. So it is outside the list.

## Reading a maintenance release (2.7.3d)

The exam gives you a copy of a maintenance release, and three questions. Is it valid? What classes of operation does it allow? And what is outstanding, and does it affect airworthiness? Each question has a place on the document.

First, validity. Look at the issue date and the expiry, stated as a date or a time in service. For example, 12 months or 100 hours, whichever comes first. Compare the aircraft's current total time with the expiry time. Then look for maintenance due before expiry: an inspection due at a stated hour count, or an airworthiness directive, an AD, due by a date. Once that point is passed, the release is no longer valid, even though its own expiry is still ahead. Finally, check that the daily inspection has been certified for today.

Second, the classes of operation. The release states the categories of operation the aircraft is certified and maintained for: private, aerial work, charter, or air transport. Maintenance is more demanding for the commercial categories, so the release can only allow the operations the aircraft has been maintained for. An aircraft maintained to the private category cannot be used for charter.

Third, the defects and endorsements. Every entry in Part 2 has one of three statuses. The first is rectified, and signed off by an engineer. It is no longer a defect. The second is deferred as a permissible unserviceability, with conditions. For example: landing light unserviceable, day VFR only. The aircraft may fly, but only within those conditions. And the third is uncleared: still open.

An uncleared defect that affects airworthiness means the aircraft is not airworthy. So does a permissible unserviceability whose conditions you cannot meet on the flight you have planned. That second point is the one the exam likes. A permissible unserviceability is not a general permission. It is a permission for flights that keep to its conditions.

Let's work one: a maintenance release verdict. Today is 4 October. The release was issued on 18 May this year, at 1,876.3 hours, valid for 100 hours or 12 months. Its categories are private and aerial work. Maintenance due: an AD inspection at 1,950.0 hours. The aircraft now shows 1,942.6 hours. No daily is certified today, and you are authorised to do it. You plan a 1.6 hour private flight from Northam, in Class G airspace at 3,500 ft.

Part 2 shows three entries. The left fuel gauge reading low: rectified, and signed off by an engineer on 12 September. The transponder unserviceable: a permissible unserviceability, not to be flown in airspace where a transponder is required. And the right brake soft and pulling: endorsed by a pilot yesterday, and not cleared. Do you fly? This one takes a while, so pause as long as you like.
[[pause 15]]
Step one, the expiry. By hours, 1,876.3 plus 100 is 1,976.3 hours. By date, 18 May next year.

Step two, the maintenance due first. That is the AD at 1,950.0 hours. The hours at the end of your flight are 1,942.6 plus 1.6, which is 1,944.2. That is below it. So the release is valid for this flight.

Step three, the daily. It is not yet done, so you inspect to the schedule, and certify it before flight.

Step four, the classes. Private and aerial work, and your flight is private. Allowed.

Step five, the first two entries. The fuel gauge is rectified, with no effect. The transponder unserviceability needs no transponder airspace. Class G below 10,000 ft needs no transponder on the list, so the condition is met.

Step six, the brake. It is uncleared, and brakes affect safe ground handling. So the aircraft is not airworthy.

The verdict: you do not fly, until an engineer has dealt with the brake. Everything else on the release was in order. And that is exactly why it is worth reading to the end.

Here is one for you to try. A week later, the brake has been rectified and signed off, and the aircraft shows 1,944.2 hours. You plan a round trip from Northam to Geraldton and back, 6.5 hours in all, private, in Class G below 10,000 ft. Is the release valid for the whole trip? And does the transponder unserviceability stop you?
[[pause 8]]
The answer: the AD inspection is due at 1,950.0 hours. You would finish at 1,944.2 plus 6.5, which is 1,950.7 hours, 0.7 hours past it. So the release is not valid for the whole trip. You could fly at most 1,950.0 minus 1,944.2, which is 5.8 hours, before the AD inspection. The transponder unserviceability does not stop you, because Class G below 10,000 ft is not airspace where the list requires a transponder. But the AD does.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Stopping at the expiry line. The release's own expiry, 100 hours or 12 months, is rarely the first limit. An AD or inspection due at a lower hour count, or a date before expiry, comes first. And the question is always whether you will pass it before you land.

## Bringing it together

It is a Saturday trip. You and two friends are flying from Jandakot to Kalbarri, about 280 nm. The route runs along the coast, with a short stretch over the water at Jurien Bay. You cruise at 6,500 ft, on a westbound track, so even plus 500.

Before leaving home, you check the documents. The aircraft is registered. Its certificate of airworthiness is in the normal category. The maintenance release shows 31 hours to run, and no AD due inside them, against a 5.5 hour weekend. And the categories include private.

Then the equipment, group by group. The day VFR instruments are fitted. At 6,500 ft you need the VHF radio. The transponder is not required by the list in Class G below 10,000 ft. You take charts and ERSA for the whole coast. Kalbarri is more than 50 nm from Jandakot, so the ELT must be carried. The stretch over the water at Jurien Bay is within gliding distance of the shore at 6,500 ft. But you plan to drop lower along the coast on the way home. So you take life jackets for the three of you, rather than replan the descent around them.

At the aeroplane, you certify the daily, as you are authorised to, and do your pre-flight. At Kalbarri, the left navigation light bulb has blown. Replacing it is Schedule 8 maintenance. You have the bulb, and you are competent, so you fit it, and record it.

On the way home, the alternator warning light flickers for twenty minutes. After landing, you endorse it in Part 2, with the date, the time in service, your signature and licence number. You record the flight time. And the aeroplane waits for an engineer. Every responsibility in the subtopic appeared in one weekend.

Question. In that story, why could you fix the navigation light yourself, but not the alternator warning?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: replacing a bulb is on the Schedule 8 list, with no special tools or judgement needed. A flickering alternator warning needs diagnosis by an engineer. So you endorse it as a defect, and the aircraft is grounded until it is rectified, or accepted as a permissible unserviceability.

## Key points

Let's recap.

The certificate of registration says which aircraft it is, and who answers for it: its VH marks, and the registered operator. The certificate of airworthiness says it conforms to its type design. It stays in force while the aircraft is maintained and operated within the flight manual, and it is issued in a category. And the maintenance release says it is fit to fly today.

Build the equipment list from the scenario. Day VFR instruments. A radio by airspace, and above 5,000 ft. A transponder in Class A, C and E, and above 10,000 ft. Charts and documents. An ELT beyond 50 nm. Life jackets beyond gliding distance of land. Rafts for extended over-water flight. Survival equipment for remote areas. Oxygen above 10,000 ft. A first aid kit, and, by seat count, a fire extinguisher.

Confirm the daily is certified before the first flight of the day, or do and certify it yourself when authorised. The pre-flight is still yours, every flight.

Record the flight time. Endorse defects in Part 2, with the description, the date, the time in service, and your signature and licence number. And report defects that affect safety.

Schedule 8 maintenance covers oil, fuel and fluids, batteries, tyres, bulbs, fuses and plugs, cleaning, seats and belts, panels, simple filters, and the daily inspection. It applies on a Class B aircraft you may fly, if you are competent, and if it is recorded. Nothing needing an engineer's judgement, special tools or a test flight.

And for the maintenance release, check the validity: the date, the hours, any maintenance or AD due first, and today's daily. Check the classes: only those listed. And check the defects: rectified, deferred with conditions you must meet, or uncleared. An uncleared defect affecting airworthiness, or a permissible unserviceability whose conditions you cannot meet, grounds the aircraft for your flight.

A final exam tip. Registration says who the aircraft is. The certificate of airworthiness says it was built, and is maintained, to standard. The maintenance release says it is fit to fly today. Pilot maintenance is the Schedule 8 list: oil, fuel, tyres, bulbs, fuses, plugs, batteries, panels and cleaning, recorded in the release. For equipment questions, read the scenario for height, airspace, distance, water and remote area, then walk the groups. And for a maintenance release, check what falls due first, and whether it falls due before you land. Then test every permissible unserviceability against the flight you have actually planned.

That's the end of POPC 2.7.
