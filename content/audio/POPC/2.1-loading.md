This is POPC 2.1, Loading, for the private licence. By the end you will know what an index unit is and why loading systems use them, how the CG limits make an envelope, the ladder of weights from empty to ramp, how to check a floor loading limit, and a routine for each of the CASA workbook loading systems. There are worked examples along the way, with a pause to try each one before the answer.

## What the exam expects (POPC 2.1)

The exam wants five things from you. First, arm, moment, datum and station, and the index unit that loading systems use to scale moments. Second, the centre of gravity and its limits, and why they form an envelope. Third, empty weight, zero fuel weight and ramp weight. Fourth, the maximum take-off and maximum landing weights. And fifth, floor loading limits, and how to check them.

## Why this matters

As a private pilot you will fly heavier and further than you did as a student: three passengers, bags, and fuel for Jandakot to Kalbarri. Those loads sit near the limits, and the loading sheet in the POH, the pilot's operating handbook, is your only way of knowing before you start.

In the PPLA exam you load an aeroplane with one of the CASA workbook systems: ALPHA, BRAVO or CHARLIE. And CASA lists correct use of the various loading systems among the most common PPLA errors.

The physics is the lever from the lesson on weight and balance. Weight times arm, add, divide. This lesson adds index units, the weight ladder, floor loading, and a routine for each workbook system.

## From moments to index units (2.1.1a)

Start with what you know. The datum is the reference plane that all distances are measured from. The arm is the distance from the datum to an item's centre of gravity, positive aft. The moment is weight times arm, the item's turning effect about the datum. And a station is a place in the aeroplane identified by its arm, such as the front seats or the baggage compartment.

[[diagram:moment-arm-seesaw]]
Picture the weight and balance lesson's worked example as a balance beam, with the datum on the left and each load pushing down at its own arm. The total weight is 1,038 kg and the total moment is 2,235,400 kilogram millimetres. So the CG, the centre of gravity, is at 2,154 mm, just aft of the 2,120 mm limit.

Notice the size of those numbers. A 70 kg passenger sitting 3,300 mm behind the datum has a moment of 70 times 3,300. That is 231,000 kilogram millimetres. An empty aeroplane of nearly 700 kg has a moment approaching two million. Summing seven-digit numbers by hand in an exam room invites a dropped zero, and a CG a metre out.

So the loading systems divide every moment by a fixed number, and work with the result. That scaled moment is an index unit. Loading system CHARLIE defines 1 index unit as 100 kilogram millimetres. So the passenger's 231,000 kilogram millimetres becomes 2,310 index units. Every item is divided by the same 100, so the balance is unchanged.

Other systems choose other scalings. BRAVO uses the moment divided by 1,000, in inch-pounds. ALPHA and ECHO use index units read off graphs, with the scaling built into the scales. ALPHA also has an offset, which lets the empty aeroplane start at a negative number. The rule is simple. Always use the system's own definition, printed on its instructions page, and never carry one system's scaling into another.

Let's work one in CHARLIE. One index unit is 100 kilogram millimetres. The fuel arm is 2,950 mm, the baggage arm is 4,210 mm, and AVGAS has an SG, a specific gravity, of 0.72. Find the index units for 120 L of fuel and 40 kg of baggage. Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 8]]
First, the fuel mass. 120 times 0.72 is 86.4 kg. Second, the fuel moment. 86.4 times 2,950 is 254,880 kilogram millimetres. Divide by 100, and that is 2,548.8 index units. Third, the baggage. 40 times 4,210 is 168,400 kilogram millimetres, which is 1,684 index units.

So the fuel is 2,548.8 index units, and the baggage is 1,684: exactly the figures in CHARLIE's index unit table on page 14. The table is this arithmetic, done in advance.

The same arithmetic runs backwards. Divide an item's index units by its weight, and multiply by 100, and you recover its arm. CHARLIE's 7 kg of oil is 86.1 index units. 86.1 times 100, divided by 7, is 1,230 mm. That is well forward, near the engine. So a rear passenger with an arm forward of the pilot means a misread line.

[[diagram:units-conversion-ladder]]
Picture litres and kilograms as two scales side by side. At 0.72 kg per litre, 100 L is about 72 kg, and 120 L is 86.4 kg. Litres to kilograms, multiply. Kilograms to litres, divide.

Question. Why do loading systems use index units instead of raw moments?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: raw moments in kilogram millimetres run to six and seven digits, which invites slips. Dividing every moment by the same constant shrinks the numbers without changing the balance. In CHARLIE that constant is 100 kilogram millimetres. Multiply back at the end to get the CG.

## The centre of gravity and its envelope (2.1.1b)

The centre of gravity is the single point where the whole weight of the loaded aeroplane can be thought of as acting. It is found the same way in every system: the total moment divided by the total weight. With index units, the scaling has to come back out. So CHARLIE's instruction page gives this formula. The CG, in millimetres aft of the datum, equals the index units times 100, divided by the weight in kilograms.

The CG limits are the forward and aft positions between which the aeroplane has been shown to be stable and controllable. Forward of the forward limit, the tail cannot raise the nose for the flare. Aft of the aft limit, the aeroplane loses longitudinal stability, and may not recover from a stall or a spin.

[[diagram:cg-position-effects]]
Picture the two ways the CG can be wrong, side by side. Forward of the limit, the aeroplane is nose heavy and very stable, with a higher stall speed, and it may not have enough elevator to flare. Aft of the limit, it is tail heavy and over-sensitive in pitch, and stall and spin recovery may be impossible.

On most aeroplanes the limits are not two fixed numbers. At higher weights the wing flies at a higher angle of attack, and the tail has to work harder. So the forward limit moves aft as the weight rises. Drawn on a graph of weight against CG position, the limits make a shape: the CG envelope.

CHARLIE plots weight against CG in millimetres. BRAVO plots weight against moment. ECHO plots gross weight against total moment index. And ALPHA drops a line from its index scales onto the envelope. But the test is always the same. The point must lie inside the envelope, at every stage of the flight.

CHARLIE and BRAVO have two envelopes, one for each category: normal, and a smaller utility envelope. The utility category allows more manoeuvring, such as spins and steeper turns, for a lower maximum weight and a tighter CG range. A load can be legal in normal and not in utility, so the question tells you which applies.

[[workbook:fig9]]
Picture CHARLIE's envelope, with weight up the side and CG along the bottom. The forward limit is vertical up to about 820 kg, then slopes aft up to 1,115 kg. A smaller shaded utility envelope sits inside, stopping at 925 kg, with its aft limit at 2,896 mm. The normal envelope's aft limit is at 3,004 mm.

Question. Why does the forward CG limit often move aft at higher weights?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: at a higher weight, the wing must make more lift, and the tail must push down harder to balance a forward CG. Near the forward limit at a high weight, the elevator would run out of authority, especially in the flare. So the designers move the forward limit aft as the weight increases, and that makes the envelope slope.

## The weight ladder: empty, zero fuel, take-off, ramp and landing (2.1.1c and d)

A loading calculation builds the aeroplane up in layers, five rungs of a ladder, each with its own name and its own check.

The first is the empty weight, also called basic empty weight. It is the aeroplane as weighed, with its fixed equipment, its unusable fuel, and full or undrainable oil, as each workbook system specifies in its notes. It comes with its own moment or index units, and it is unique to that airframe. The exam gives it to you; in real life it comes from the weight and balance schedule.

The second is the zero fuel weight, ZFW. That is the empty weight plus everything you load except usable fuel: the oil if it is not already included, the occupants and the baggage. Many larger aeroplanes have a maximum zero fuel weight, the MZFW. ECHO's is 2,630 kg. It exists because people and bags in the fuselage bend the wing roots upward, while fuel in the wings does not.

The third is the take-off weight: the ZFW plus the fuel on board at the start of the take-off roll. It must not exceed the maximum take-off weight, the MTOW, the structural limit set by the strength of the airframe. That is 1,115 kg in CHARLIE's normal category. In BRAVO it is 1,000 kg, which is 2,200 lb. In ALPHA it is 1,633 kg, and in ECHO 2,950 kg.

The fourth is the ramp weight, the weight at engine start. That is the take-off weight plus the taxi and run-up allowance. Some aeroplanes have a maximum ramp weight slightly above the MTOW. That lets you load taxi fuel on top of a full take-off load, and burn down to the MTOW before lining up.

And the fifth is the landing weight: the take-off weight minus the fuel burned in flight. It must not exceed the maximum landing weight, the MLW, the structural limit for the touchdown loads on the undercarriage and its attachments. In light trainers the MLW often equals the MTOW. In ECHO it is lower: 2,725 kg, against an MTOW of 2,950 kg. So a full ECHO taking off at MTOW must burn 225 kg of fuel before it may land.

[[diagram:weight-ladder]]
Picture five columns standing side by side, growing as each rung adds its load. Each is checked against its own limit. The zero fuel weight against the MZFW, the take-off weight against the MTOW, any ramp weight against its maximum, and the landing weight against the MLW.

Fuel is the only load that changes in flight, and its tanks are rarely at the CG. As it burns, the loading point slides along a line, from the take-off point towards the zero fuel point. It slides forward if the tanks are aft of the CG, and aft if they are forward of it. So the workbook has you check the CG at both ends of that line, the ZFW and the take-off weight. If both are inside, the whole flight is. The landing point lies between them.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Checking only the take-off point. A load can be inside the envelope at take-off, and outside it at the ZFW. The typical case is a light pilot with heavy baggage in the rear compartment, and fuel tanks forward of the CG. As the fuel burns, the CG walks aft, out of the envelope. The exam builds questions on exactly this.

Question. Which is heavier, the ramp weight or the take-off weight, and by how much?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the ramp weight, by the taxi and run-up fuel. The ramp weight is the weight at engine start, and that fuel is burned before the take-off roll. Where an aeroplane has a maximum ramp weight above the MTOW, the difference is that allowance.

## Floor loading limits (2.1.1e)

A baggage compartment has two separate limits, and they protect different things. The first is the compartment's maximum weight. That is 122 kg in CHARLIE, 54 kg in BRAVO, and 55 kg and 155 kg for ECHO's compartments. It protects the floor beams, the tie-down points, and the compartment's contribution to the CG. The second is the floor loading limit. It protects the floor panel itself from being punched through or buckled by a concentrated load.

Think of a stiletto heel on a timber floor. In flat shoes, the same person does no harm. On a heel, the whole weight goes through a tiny area, and dents the boards. So a floor is rated for a maximum weight per unit area, in kilograms per square metre. ECHO's floor loading limit is 450 kilograms per square metre.

To check an item, the floor loading equals its weight in kilograms, divided by its contact area in square metres. A dense item, such as a battery or a case of tinned food, can be well inside the compartment's weight limit and still exceed the floor loading. The remedy is to stand it on a board, which increases the contact area. And the board's weight goes on the load sheet too.

Let's work one: a spare battery in the rear compartment. It weighs 30 kg, and its base is 0.2 m by 0.15 m. The limit is 450 kilograms per square metre. Is it legal as it stands, and if not, what contact area does it need? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 8]]
First, the contact area. 0.2 times 0.15 is 0.03 square metres. Second, the floor loading. 30 divided by 0.03 is 1,000 kilograms per square metre, more than twice the 450 limit. Third, the smallest area that keeps it legal. 30 divided by 450 is 0.067 square metres.

[[diagram:floor-loading-board]]
Now picture the same battery stood on a board of 0.4 m by 0.3 m. The same 30 kg is spread over 0.12 square metres. 30 divided by 0.12 is 250 kilograms per square metre, within the limit. So on its own base it is 1,000, and illegal. On the board it is 250, and legal. The battery was always within the compartment's weight limit. Only the floor loading made it illegal.

Here is one for you to try. A 40 kg box of survey equipment has a base of 0.25 m by 0.25 m. Does it meet a floor loading limit of 450 kilograms per square metre?
[[pause 8]]
The area is 0.25 times 0.25, which is 0.0625 square metres. The floor loading is 40 divided by 0.0625, which is 640 kilograms per square metre. That is above the 450 limit. It needs at least 40 divided by 450, which is 0.089 square metres of contact area. A board 0.3 m by 0.3 m would do: 0.09 square metres, for about 444. A larger board gives a sensible margin. So the answer: no, at 640 it is over the limit.

Question. A heavy item is within the compartment's maximum weight. Why might it still be illegal to carry it as it is?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because floor loading is weight per unit area, not total weight. A dense item with a small base can exceed the kilograms per square metre the floor is rated for. A board spreads it, and brings the loading down.

## Working the CASA loading systems

Every workbook system is the same calculation in a different costume, so use one order every time, in six steps.

First, read the system's instructions page: its units, its index unit definition, the SG, which is 0.72 in all of them, the MTOW for the category, and the compartment limits. Second, start with the empty aeroplane and its index units, as the question gives them. Third, add every load item except fuel, each from the system's table or graph, and total them. That is the ZFW line.

Fourth, convert the fuel from litres to kilograms by multiplying by 0.72, or to pounds for BRAVO. Find its moment or index units, and add it. That is the take-off weight line. Fifth, check the weights: take-off weight against the MTOW, each compartment against its own limit, and the ZFW against the MZFW where there is one. And sixth, find the CG, or the plotting point, at the ZFW and at the take-off weight, and check both against the envelope.

Do not round until the end. A take-off weight 0.2 kg under the MTOW is legal; rounded up early, it is not.

Now the systems one at a time. CHARLIE is a four-seater, worked entirely in arithmetic, in kilograms and millimetres. The instructions are on page 13. The index unit tables are on page 14: fuel at an arm of 2,950 mm, baggage at 4,210 mm, the two seat rows, and oil. The CG envelope is on page 15. The limits are an MTOW of 1,115 kg in the normal category and 925 kg in utility, and 122 kg in the baggage compartment.

[[workbook:charlie-index-units]]
Picture CHARLIE's index unit tables. Every row is the kilograms times the arm, divided by 100. The occupant table stops at 90 kg a person. So a heavier person, or two people in a row, means adding lines, or working kilograms times arm divided by 100 yourself.

Let's work a full load in CHARLIE: four people and bags. The empty aeroplane is 687 kg and 19,522 index units. The oil is 7 kg and 86.1 index units. Row 1 has two people of 80 kg. From the table, 80 kg in row 1 is 2,200 index units, so the pair is 160 kg and 4,400 index units. Row 2 has one person of 75 kg, which is 2,700 index units. The baggage is 40 kg, which is 1,684 index units. And the fuel is 120 L, which is 86.4 kg and 2,548.8 index units. Find the CG at the ZFW and at take-off, and check the weight. This one takes a while, so pause as long as you like.
[[pause 15]]
Step one, the zero fuel weight. 687, plus 7, plus 160, plus 75, plus 40, is 969 kg. The index units are 19,522, plus 86.1, plus 4,400, plus 2,700, plus 1,684. That is 28,392.1.

Step two, the CG at the ZFW. 28,392.1 times 100, divided by 969, is 2,930 mm.

Step three, the take-off weight. 969 plus 86.4 is 1,055.4 kg, under the 1,115 kg MTOW. The index units are 28,392.1 plus 2,548.8, which is 30,940.9.

Step four, the CG at take-off. 30,940.9 times 100, divided by 1,055.4, is 2,931.7. Call it 2,932 mm.

Step five, plot both points on the envelope. So the answer: 969 kg at 2,930 mm, and 1,055.4 kg at 2,932 mm, both inside. Both weights are legal, and the baggage is well inside its 122 kg limit.

The fuel sits at 2,950 mm, a little aft of the CG. So as it burns, the CG creeps forward, from 2,932 mm at take-off to 2,930 mm with dry tanks.

And the landing CG after burning 60 L? That is 43.2 kg, and 43.2 times 29.5 is 1,274.4 index units. The index units become 30,940.9 minus 1,274.4, which is 29,666.5. The weight becomes 1,055.4 minus 43.2, which is 1,012.2 kg. And 29,666.5 times 100, divided by 1,012.2, is 2,931 mm. That lies between the two ends, as it must.

Picture CHARLIE's envelope with a zero fuel point and a take-off point on it. Reset gives the workbook's own example. Change one load at a time, and watch the two points move.

The seat rows also hide their arms in the table. For row 1, 4,400 times 100, divided by 160, is 2,750 mm. For row 2, 2,700 times 100, divided by 75, is 3,600 mm. So the row 2 passenger is 850 mm further aft than the front seats. That is why a heavy back-seat passenger moves the CG aft far more than the same person in front.

Now try the workbook's own CHARLIE example. The ZFW is 1,014 kg, with 30,060.1 index units. With 140 L of fuel, the take-off weight is 1,114.8 kg, with 33,033.7 index units. Find both CGs and the fuel's index units, and comment on the take-off weight.
[[pause 15]]
The CG at the ZFW is 30,060.1 times 100, divided by 1,014. That is 2,964.5 mm. The CG at take-off is 33,033.7 times 100, divided by 1,114.8. That is 2,963.2 mm. Both are within the envelope in the workbook.

The fuel accounts for 33,033.7 minus 30,060.1, which is 2,973.6 index units. Its weight is 1,114.8 minus 1,014, which is {100.8|one hundred point eight} kg. That is 140 times 0.72. And 2,973.6 times 100, divided by {100.8|one hundred point eight}, is 2,950 mm, the fuel arm.

And the take-off weight is 0.2 kg under the 1,115 kg MTOW. Legal, with no margin at all. And illegal if you had rounded up during the sum.

Next, BRAVO, a four-seater from the American tradition. Weights are in pounds, arms are in inches, and moments are divided by 1,000, in inch-pounds per thousand, to keep them small. The instructions and the worked example are on page 11, and the loading graph and the CG envelope are on page 12.

[[workbook:fig8]]
Picture BRAVO's page as two graphs, one above the other. The top graph turns each item's weight in pounds into moment per thousand, along its own line: fuel, seats, cargo and baggage. The bottom graph is the envelope: loaded weight against moment per thousand, with the shaded utility area inside the normal one. You plot the totals at both the ZFW and the take-off weight.

Its limits come in both units. The MTOW is 1,000 kg, or 2,200 lb, in the normal category, and 841 kg, or 1,850 lb, in utility. The cargo compartment takes 154 kg, which is 339 lb. The baggage takes 54 kg, which is 120 lb. And the standard tanks hold 37 US gallons, at 6 lb per gallon.

So expect to convert. Kilograms to pounds is multiply by 2.205. Litres to pounds is two steps in one: times 0.72 for kilograms, then times 2.205 for pounds. Together, that is multiply by 1.587.

Let's work BRAVO's own figures, from the workbook's page 11 example. The empty weight is 1,260 lb at 80 inches. The ZFW is 1,950 lb, with a moment per thousand of {174.88|one hundred and seventy-four point eight eight}. The fuel is 140 L, entered as 221 lb at 91 inches. Find the take-off totals, the two CGs in inches, and the category. Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 10]]
First, the empty moment. 1,260 times 80 is 100,800 inch-pounds, so the moment per thousand is {100.80|one hundred point eight zero}.

Second, the fuel. 140 times 1.587 is 222 lb by the conversion, and the workbook's example rounds it to 221 lb. Its moment is 221 times 91, which is 20,111. So the moment per thousand is 20.11.

Third, the take-off totals. 1,950 plus 221 is 2,171 lb. And {174.88|one hundred and seventy-four point eight eight} plus 20.11 is {194.99|one hundred and ninety-four point nine nine}.

Fourth, the CGs, if you want them in inches. At the ZFW, 174,880 divided by 1,950 is 89.7 inches. At take-off, 194,990 divided by 2,171 is 89.8 inches.

Fifth, the weight check. 2,171 lb is under the 2,200 lb normal category MTOW, but over the 1,850 lb utility limit. So this load is normal category only. And the fuel at 91 inches is just aft of the CG, so burning it barely moves the point.

Now ALPHA, the graphical system. ALPHA is a six or seven-seat aeroplane, with a single chart on page 10 that does the moment arithmetic by geometry. The instructions on page 9 set the order, and following that order exactly is the whole skill.

[[workbook:fig7]]
Picture ALPHA's chart. Across the top is the index unit scale. Underneath is one horizontal scale for each station: three seat rows, nose baggage, rear baggage, and fuel. Each occupant row has two scales, at 50 or 77 kg per division, and baggage is 10 kg per division. Each scale carries an arrow showing which way that load moves the index, and the fuel has a nil scale. At the bottom is the CG envelope, with gross weight up the side and the 1,633 kg MTOW marked on it.

The method has six steps. First, mark the basic empty weight index units on the top scale, and the basic empty weight at the top of the weight column. Second, enter each load item's weight in the column, and total them: first to the ZFW, then add the fuel for the take-off weight. Third, on the envelope, draw horizontal lines at the ZFW and at the take-off weight.

Fourth, from the empty index point, drop vertically to the first load scale. Move in the direction of the arrow by the divisions for that item's weight. For example, 154 kg on a scale of 77 kg per division is 2 divisions. Fifth, repeat, scale by scale, down to the rear baggage.

And sixth, from the rear baggage point, drop a line straight down through the envelope. Where it crosses the ZFW line is the zero fuel point. Where it crosses the take-off line is the take-off point. Both must be inside the envelope, and the take-off weight must not exceed 1,633 kg. If either is outside, reorganise the load and start again.

Why is the fuel scale not used for the vertical line? Because ALPHA's fuel sits just about at the CG. So the take-off point lies on the same vertical as the zero fuel point, and only the weight line differs.

The common errors in ALPHA are four. Moving the wrong way along a scale, so follow the arrow. Miscounting divisions, because 77 kg per division is not 50. Using the forward-facing scale for an aft-facing seat. And reading against the wrong weight line. A ruler and a sharp pencil prevent most of them.

Question. In ALPHA, why are the ZFW and take-off points read on the same vertical line?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because the vertical line carries the index after all the non-fuel loads, and the fuel effectively changes the weight without moving that line. The two points differ only in which horizontal weight line you read them against. The ZFW before the fuel, and the take-off weight after it.

ECHO has the fullest set of limits in the workbook. Besides those weights, the compartments take 55 kg forward and in each wing locker, and 155 kg at the rear. Fuel goes in the main tanks at an arm of 1,780 mm, and the auxiliary tanks at 2,800 mm. And its seats weigh 5 kg each and may be removed. ## Bringing it together

You are planning Jandakot to Rottnest and on to Busselton in a CHARLIE-type four-seater, with the empty figures from its own weight and balance schedule. You write the table in the system's order: empty, oil, row 1, row 2, baggage. The baggage is a heavy esky with a small base. Weight divided by footprint says it needs a board, so the board goes on the sheet too. You total to the ZFW, and find the CG: index units times 100, divided by the weight.

Then the fuel. Litres times 0.72, its index units at the 2,950 mm arm, and the take-off totals, a few kilograms under the 1,115 kg MTOW. Both points go on the envelope. With the fuel aft of the CG, the point moves forward as it burns, so the landing CG lies between the two you checked.

A quarter of an hour on paper beats finding the aft limit in the flare at Busselton.

## Key points

Let's recap.

An index unit is a scaled moment. In CHARLIE, 1 index unit is 100 kilogram millimetres. Use each system's own definition, and multiply back to get the CG.

The CG is the total moment divided by the total weight: in CHARLIE, index units times 100, divided by the weight. The limits form an envelope, with the forward limit moving aft as the weight rises. Check the category, normal or utility.

The empty weight includes unusable fuel and oil. The ZFW is everything but usable fuel. The ramp weight adds the taxi fuel to the take-off weight. Check the CG at the ZFW and at the take-off weight, because the fuel burn slides the point between them.

The MTOW and MLW are structural limits. The MTOW is 1,115 kg for CHARLIE in the normal category, 1,000 kg for BRAVO, 1,633 kg for ALPHA, and 2,950 kg for ECHO, with an MLW of 2,725 kg and an MZFW of 2,630 kg.

And floor loading is the weight divided by the contact area, against a limit in kilograms per square metre. In ECHO that limit is 450. Spread dense items on a board, and count the board.

A final exam tip. CASA lists correct use of the loading systems among the most common PPLA errors. Read the system's instructions page every time. Use its SG of 0.72, its arms, and its index unit definition. Check the CG at the ZFW and at the take-off weight, and do not round until the end. In ALPHA, follow each scale's arrow and count the divisions carefully. In BRAVO, convert litres to pounds by multiplying by 1.587. And in CHARLIE, remember the times 100.

That's the end of POPC 2.1.
