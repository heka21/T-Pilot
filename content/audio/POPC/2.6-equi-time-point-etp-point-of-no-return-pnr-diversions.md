This is POPC 2.6, Equi-time point, point of no return, and diversions. By the end you will know when each point matters and how they differ, you will be able to work out both with wind, and you will be able to decide in a couple of minutes which diversion your fuel allows. There are worked examples along the way, so pause and try each one before the answer.

## What the exam expects (POPC 2.6)

The exam wants three things from you. First, describe the situations in which a pilot would want to know the equi-time point or the point of no return, and recognise one when the exam describes it. Second, with a constant cruise level and TAS, find where the equi-time point lies between two points, in still air and with wind. And third, given the fuel on board and the groundspeeds, work out which of three options needs the least fuel, reserves included, and which are available at all. The three options are continuing to the destination, returning to departure, and diverting to an alternate.

## Why this matters

Fly east from Jandakot towards Kalgoorlie, and after Merredin the Wheatbelt gives way to salt lakes and scrub, with a handful of strips 50 nm or more apart. Fly up the coast towards Geraldton, or out to the Abrolhos, and there are stretches where the only flat ground is the beach or the sea.

On legs like these, two questions sit in the back of a good pilot's mind the whole way. If something goes wrong now, is it quicker to go on, or to go back? And have I still got the fuel to go back at all? The first question is answered by the equi-time point. The second is answered by the point of no return. And both are worked out on the ground before you go, when the arithmetic is easy.

The third skill is the in-flight version. Something has changed, you have three choices, and you need to know in two minutes which ones your fuel allows. The exam asks for the ETP as a number, asks you to tell the ETP from the PNR, and gives you a fuel comparison to complete.

## Two questions, two points

Picture yourself an hour and a half out of Jandakot on the way to Kalgoorlie, with an easterly wind on the nose. Two different kinds of trouble could come up.

In the first, a passenger becomes ill, or the oil pressure starts to sag, or smoke drifts from behind the panel. Fuel is not the issue. Time is. You want to be on the ground as soon as possible, and the question is simply whether the destination ahead or the aerodrome behind is closer in minutes. The point on your track where the two are exactly equal is the equi-time point, the ETP. From there, it takes the same time to continue to the destination as to return to the departure point. Before the ETP, turning back is quicker. After it, continuing is quicker. The same idea works between any two aerodromes, such as two alternates either side of a long leg. But the exam's version is departure and destination.

In the second, the destination's weather has gone bad. A TAF amendment brings thunderstorms to Kalgoorlie, or the cloud on the horizon is lower than forecast, and you will not get through. Now the question is not which is quicker, but whether you can get back at all, with your fixed reserve still in the tanks. The furthest point along the track from which you can return to the departure aerodrome with the reserve intact is the point of no return, the PNR. Before it, returning is an option. After it, you are committed. Whatever happens ahead, you will be landing somewhere ahead, because you no longer have the fuel to come home.

That difference is the whole of the first element. The ETP is about time, and does not care how much fuel you have. The PNR is about fuel, and moves every time your fuel changes.

When do you need them? Whenever going on and going back are both real options, and neither is obviously right. There are five typical cases. A long leg over country with no usable aerodromes between departure and destination, which describes much of inland WA. A leg over water, or along a remote coast, where a forced landing would be a ditching. A destination with a marginal TAF, where the plan is to go and look, and turn back if it is not good enough. A flight planned with little fuel margin, where you want to know in advance where the last chance to turn back is. And any flight carrying a passenger whose health might turn, where the time to the nearest aerodrome matters.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Its favourite distractor swaps the definitions. The ETP is about equal time to two places, and is independent of fuel. The PNR is the furthest point from which you can return with the reserve intact, so it depends on fuel. If an option says the PNR is the point where the time to go on equals the time to go back, it is describing the ETP.

Question. You load an extra 30 L before departure. Does the ETP move? Does the PNR move?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the ETP does not move. It depends only on the distance and the two groundspeeds, and fuel is not part of it. The PNR moves further from departure, because more fuel means more endurance for the out-and-back trip.

## The equi-time point in still air

Start with the simplest case. No wind, a constant cruise level, and a constant TAS. Your groundspeed is then the same in both directions, because with no wind, groundspeed equals TAS whichever way you point.

Now think about what equal time means when the speed is the same both ways. Time is distance divided by speed. If the speed is the same, equal times need equal distances. And the point where the distance on equals the distance back is the midpoint of the track. So in still air, the ETP is halfway, whatever the distance and whatever the TAS.

That is the second element as the syllabus states it. It feels obvious, because in still air the aeroplane does not care which way it is going. The interesting part is what happens when it does.

Question. In still air, a 260 nm leg is flown at 120 kt TAS. Where is the ETP, and would it move if the TAS were 100 kt?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: at 130 nm, the midpoint. In still air the groundspeed is the same both ways, so equal times need equal distances. Changing the TAS changes both groundspeeds equally, and the ETP stays halfway.

## Why wind moves the ETP into wind

Add a headwind on the way out. Your groundspeed outbound, the groundspeed on, drops below the TAS. If you turned round, the same wind would become a tailwind, and your groundspeed home would rise above the TAS.

[[diagram:groundspeed-headwind-tailwind]]
Picture two bars drawn to scale, one above the other. The same TAS of 105 kt, into a 20 kt headwind, gives a groundspeed of 85 kt. With a 20 kt tailwind, it gives 125 kt. So the trip home, with the wind behind you, is faster than the trip on.

Now stand at the midpoint. The distances on and back are equal, but you would fly home faster than you would fly on. So at the midpoint, going back is quicker. To make the times equal, you must move further along the track. That adds distance to the fast trip home, and takes it off the slow trip on, until the extra distance home exactly balances its higher speed. So the ETP has moved towards the destination, which is the direction the wind is coming from. Reverse the wind, and the same reasoning moves the ETP back towards departure, again towards where the wind comes from. Hence the rule: the ETP moves into wind.

[[diagram:etp-pnr-geometry]]
Picture three copies of a 180 nm leg flown at 110 kt TAS, one above the other. In still air, the ETP sits at the midpoint, 90 nm. With a 20 kt headwind outbound, it slides 16 nm towards the destination, to 106 nm. With a 20 kt tailwind outbound, it slides 16 nm back towards departure, to 74 nm. Each time, it moves towards where the wind comes from.

Now the formula, built from that idea. Call the distance from departure to the ETP x. From the ETP, the distance home is x, and the distance on is the total distance minus x. At the ETP the two times are equal. So x divided by the groundspeed home equals the distance on, divided by the groundspeed on. Cross-multiply, and collect the x terms on one side. The result is the formula the exam uses.

The ETP distance from departure equals the total distance, times the groundspeed home, divided by the sum of the two groundspeeds.

Read the formula as a check on the reasoning. In still air, the two groundspeeds are equal, the fraction is a half, and the ETP is at the midpoint. With a headwind outbound, the groundspeed home is the larger, the fraction is more than a half, and the ETP is beyond the midpoint. The groundspeed home goes on top. That is the one detail to get right.

The formula also has a built-in check. At the ETP, the two times must be equal. So once you have the distance, divide it by the groundspeed home. Then divide the remaining distance by the groundspeed on. If the two times do not agree, you have put the wrong groundspeed on top.

Let's work one: the ETP with a 20 kt headwind outbound. The track is 180 nm, and the TAS is 110 kt. Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 8]]
Step one, the groundspeed on. 110 minus 20 is 90 kt.

Step two, the groundspeed home. 110 plus 20 is 130 kt.

Step three, the ETP. 180 times 130 is 23,400. 90 plus 130 is 220. And 23,400 divided by 220 is {106.4|one hundred and six point four}. Call it 106 nm from departure.

Step four, the check. Home from the ETP: {106.4|one hundred and six point four} divided by 130 is 0.82 hours, about 49 minutes. On from the ETP: 180 minus {106.4|one hundred and six point four} is 73.6, and 73.6 divided by 90 is also 0.82 hours, about 49 minutes. They agree.

So the ETP is 106 nm from departure, 16 nm beyond the 90 nm midpoint. If trouble comes at 100 nm, turning back is still the quicker way to the ground, even though the destination is only 80 nm away.

Here is a second one: a 200 nm leg, a TAS of 110 kt, and a 10 kt headwind outbound.
[[pause 8]]
The groundspeed on is 110 minus 10, which is 100 kt. The groundspeed home is 110 plus 10, which is 120 kt. The ETP is 200 times 120, which is 24,000, divided by 220. That is {109.1|one hundred and nine point one}, so 109 nm from departure.

The check. On: 200 minus {109.1|one hundred and nine point one} is 90.9, and divided by 100 that is 0.91 hours. Home: {109.1|one hundred and nine point one} divided by 120 is also 0.91 hours. Equal, so the right groundspeed is on top.

So the answer is 109 nm. A 10 kt wind moved the ETP 9 nm from the midpoint. The 20 kt wind in the first example moved it 16 nm, on a shorter leg. The stronger the wind relative to the TAS, the further the ETP moves.

Now try one in the other direction. A 240 nm leg, a TAS of 120 kt, with a 15 kt tailwind outbound. Where is the ETP, and on which side of the midpoint?
[[pause 8]]
The groundspeed on is 120 plus 15, which is 135 kt. The groundspeed home is 120 minus 15, which is 105 kt. The ETP is 240 times 105, which is 25,200, divided by 135 plus 105, which is 240. That gives 105 nm from departure. That is 15 nm short of the 120 nm midpoint.

The check. Home: 105 divided by 105 is 1.0 hour. On: 135 divided by 135 is also 1.0 hour. So the answer is 105 nm, short of halfway. The tailwind outbound is a headwind on the way home, so the ETP moves back towards departure, into wind.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Two errors account for most wrong ETP answers. Putting the groundspeed on at the top, instead of the groundspeed home, gives the mirror-image answer: the same distance on the wrong side of the midpoint. And forgetting that the ETP moves into wind. With a headwind outbound, it is beyond halfway, not short of it. The time check catches both.

Question. Why does the ETP move towards the destination when there is a headwind outbound?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because the trip home would be downwind, and therefore faster. At the midpoint, returning would take less time than continuing. So the point of equal time has to be further out, where the longer distance home balances its higher groundspeed.

## The point of no return depends on endurance

The ETP needed only distances and groundspeeds. The PNR needs one more thing: how long the aeroplane can stay in the air on the fuel it is allowed to use. That figure is the safe endurance for the out-and-back trip.

It is the fuel on board, less the taxi allowance, less the fixed reserve, and less any holding you insist on keeping for the return, all divided by the fuel flow. The reserve is taken out first, because it is not yours to spend. The PNR is defined as the point from which you return with the reserve intact.

Think about flying out to the PNR and straight back. The fuel you burn going out, plus the fuel you burn coming back, must exactly equal the fuel available. At a constant fuel flow, fuel is proportional to time. So in time, the same statement is: the time out plus the time back equals the endurance. And the distance out to the PNR is the same as the distance back from it. Those two facts fix the PNR.

Put them together, and the time out comes out as a formula. The time to the PNR equals the endurance, times the groundspeed home, divided by the sum of the two groundspeeds. Multiply that by the groundspeed out to get the distance. So the PNR distance from departure equals the endurance, times the groundspeed out, times the groundspeed home, all divided by the sum of the two groundspeeds.

The check this time is fuel, not time. The time out and the time back must add up to the endurance.

Let's work one: a PNR with 2.5 hours of fuel available. The TAS is 110 kt, with a 10 kt headwind outbound. So the groundspeed out is 100 kt, and the groundspeed home is 120 kt. The safe endurance, after the taxi allowance and the reserve, is 2.5 hours. Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 10]]
Step one, the PNR distance. 2.5 times 100 times 120 is 30,000. 100 plus 120 is 220. And 30,000 divided by 220 is {136.4|one hundred and thirty-six point four}. Call it 136 nm.

Step two, the time to the PNR. {136.4|One hundred and thirty-six point four} divided by 100 is 1.36 hours, about 82 minutes after setting course.

Step three, the time back from the PNR. {136.4|One hundred and thirty-six point four} divided by 120 is 1.14 hours, about 68 minutes.

Step four, the check. 1.36 plus 1.14 is 2.50 hours, the whole endurance.

So the PNR is 136 nm out, reached about 82 minutes after setting course. If you have not turned back by then, the return is no longer an option with the reserve intact. In still air, the same fuel would have taken you to 2.5 times 110, divided by 2, which is {137.5|one hundred and thirty-seven point five} nm. The headwind pulled the PNR about a mile closer to home.

[[diagram:pnr-fuel-out-and-back]]
Picture that trip as a line out and a line back. Going out, against the wind at 100 kt, takes 82 minutes. Coming back, with the wind at 120 kt, takes 68 minutes. Together they use the whole 150 minutes of safe endurance. And the fixed reserve is still in the tanks on landing.

That last observation is the counter-intuitive part. A tailwind outbound does not push the PNR further out. Swap the groundspeeds, to 120 kt out and 100 kt home, and the formula gives exactly the same 136 nm, because the two groundspeeds appear in it symmetrically. What changes is the time. At 120 kt out, you reach it after {136.4|one hundred and thirty-six point four} divided by 120, which is 1.14 hours, about 68 minutes. That is sooner than with the headwind. So any wind, from either direction, brings the PNR closer than in still air. You always spend longer flying into the wind on one leg than you save flying with it on the other.

Here is one for you to try. The usable fuel is 117 L after taxi. The fixed reserve is 18 L. The fuel flow is 36 L an hour. The TAS is 100 kt, with a 20 kt headwind outbound. How far out is the PNR, and how long after setting course do you reach it?
[[pause 10]]
The fuel available is 117 minus 18, which is 99 L. So the endurance is 99 divided by 36, which is 2.75 hours. The groundspeed out is 80 kt, and home is 120 kt. The PNR is 2.75 times 80 times 120, which is 26,400, divided by 200. That is 132 nm. The time to the PNR is 132 divided by 80, which is 1.65 hours, or 99 minutes.

The check. The time back is 132 divided by 120, which is 1.1 hours. And 1.65 plus 1.1 is 2.75 hours, the whole endurance. So the answer: 132 nm, reached 99 minutes after setting course.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. The endurance in the PNR formula is the fuel you may spend, not the fuel in the tanks. Take out the taxi fuel and the fixed reserve, and any holding you want to keep for the return, before dividing by the fuel flow. Using total fuel puts the PNR too far out. And that is the one direction in which the error can hurt you.

Question. With the same wind, why does the PNR move but the ETP stay put when you carry more fuel?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the PNR is set by fuel out plus fuel back equalling the fuel available. So more fuel lets you go further before turning. The ETP compares two times to two places from your position, and has no fuel term in it at all.

## Diversions: which option needs the least fuel?

The third element is the in-flight problem. You are somewhere along the track. Something has changed. You have three options, and a fixed amount of fuel. The exam wants you to find, for each option, how much fuel it needs, including the reserve. And so, which options are available, and which needs the least.

Before any arithmetic, the order of thinking matters, because the arithmetic only answers part of the question. There are seven steps.

First, fly the aeroplane. Hold height and heading while you think. A diversion begun in a hurry often ends up off track and lost. Second, decide what kind of problem it is. If it is time-critical, such as illness, a failing engine or smoke, the nearest aerodrome in time wins, and the ETP tells you which way that is. If it is weather or fuel, the fuel comparison decides. Third, list the options: continue to the destination, return to departure, or divert to a suitable alternate. Fourth, work the fuel for each, and strike out any that would eat into the reserve.

Fifth, check the survivors against the things fuel does not cover. The weather at the aerodrome. The daylight left. The runway, and its suitability for your aeroplane. And whether you can get fuel there. Sixth, commit, turn onto the new heading, and work out a new ETA. And seventh, tell someone. Make a radio call to Centre or the FIS, and amend your flight notification or SARTIME, so nobody starts a search for you at the original destination.

[[diagram:sartime-timeline]]
Picture a SARTIME as a deadline on a timeline. If it passes without being cancelled or amended, the search and rescue system starts alerting, whatever the reason you landed somewhere else.

Now the fuel comparison. For each option the method is the same three steps. First, the time: the distance divided by the groundspeed for that option, times 60 for minutes. Second, the fuel: the time multiplied by the fuel flow. And third, add the fixed reserve of 30 minutes, and any holding that aerodrome requires, and compare the total with the fuel on board.

Any option whose total exceeds the fuel on board is not available. Flying it would mean landing with less than the reserve. Among the options that remain, the one needing the least fuel is usually the best, subject to the fifth step of the sequence.

Let's work three options from a position 100 nm out. You are 100 nm from departure, with 80 nm to go to the destination. An alternate lies 45 nm away, off to the side. The groundspeeds are 85 kt on to the destination, into a headwind; 125 kt back to departure; and 100 kt to the alternate. The fuel flow is 36 L an hour, the fixed reserve is 18 L, which is 30 minutes, and there are 55 L on board. Work all three. This one takes a while, so pause as long as you like.
[[pause 15]]
Option one, proceed to the destination. 80 divided by 85, times 60, is 56 minutes. The fuel is 34 L. Add the reserve, and it needs 52 L. With 55 L on board, it is available, with 3 L spare.

Option two, return to departure. 100 divided by 125, times 60, is 48 minutes. The fuel is 29 L. Add the reserve, and it needs 47 L. Available, with 8 L spare.

Option three, divert to the alternate. 45 divided by 100, times 60, is 27 minutes. The fuel is 16 L. Add the reserve, and it needs 34 L. Available, with 21 L spare.

So all three are available, and the alternate needs the least fuel. Returning is cheaper than continuing despite the greater distance, because of the wind. And if the destination then required 30 minutes of holding, another 18 L, proceeding would need 70 L. That is impossible with the reserve intact, and the decision is made for you.

Notice what the wind did there. The destination was 20 nm closer than home. Yet going home cost 5 L less, because the groundspeed home was 40 kt higher. That is the same effect that moves the ETP into wind, showing up in fuel instead of time. And with a constant fuel flow, least fuel and least time are the same answer. They part company only when holding is added to one option.

[[diagram:diversion-fuel-ladder]]
Picture the three options as three stacked bars, standing against a line at the 55 L on board. Each bar is the trip fuel with the 18 L reserve on top: 52 L, 47 L and 34 L, all below the line. Now add 18 L of holding to the destination bar. It grows to 70 L, and crosses the line by 15 L. Any bar that crosses the line is not available.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. The fixed reserve is never used for holding, or for the diversion itself. Add holding on top of the reserve, for any aerodrome whose weather requires it, and compare the total with the fuel on board. An answer that uses the reserve to hold is the distractor.

Question. Why can returning to departure need less fuel than continuing to a destination that is closer?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: fuel depends on time, and time depends on groundspeed as well as distance. With a headwind outbound, the groundspeed home is much higher. So a longer distance home can take less time, and therefore less fuel, than a shorter distance into wind.

## Bringing it together

A summer morning. You are flying Jandakot to Kalgoorlie, about 295 nm, solo, with 150 L usable. The forecast gives a 25 kt easterly, a direct headwind for the trip out. Your TAS is 110 kt, so the groundspeed on is 85 kt, and home is 135 kt. The fuel flow is 32 L an hour, the taxi is 3 L, and the fixed reserve is 30 minutes at 32 L an hour, which is 16 L. These distances are illustrative, so measure your own off the chart.

On the ground, you work the ETP. 295 times 135 is 39,825, divided by 220. That is 181 nm from Jandakot. The check: home, 181 divided by 135 is 1.34 hours. On, 295 minus 181 is 114, divided by 85, is also 1.34 hours.

Then the PNR. The fuel available is 150 minus 3 minus 16, which is 131 L. So the endurance is 131 divided by 32, which is 4.09 hours. The PNR is 4.09 times 85 times 135, divided by 220. That is 213 nm, which you reach 2.5 hours after setting course. You write both on the flight log, with the times, beside the track.

In flight, at 210 nm, about 25 nm past Southern Cross, the fuel log shows 68 L. Kalgoorlie's TAF is amended, with TEMPO thunderstorms. So 60 minutes of holding is now required there. You are past the ETP. So for a time-critical problem, Kalgoorlie would be the place. But this one is a fuel problem, so out comes the table.

[[diagram:taf-change-groups]]
Picture a TAF as a timeline with its change groups drawn as bars. A TEMPO bar marks spells of 30 to 60 minutes, which is why it brings 60 minutes of holding. An INTER marks spells under 30 minutes, which bring 30.

So, the Kalgoorlie diversion. You are 210 nm from Jandakot, with 85 nm to Kalgoorlie, and Southern Cross is 25 nm behind you. The groundspeeds are 85 kt on and 135 kt home. The fuel flow is 32 L an hour, the reserve is 16 L, and you have 68 L on board. Work the three options. Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 15]]
Option one, continue. 85 nm at 85 kt is 60 minutes, which is 32 L. The holding is 60 minutes, another 32 L. Add the reserve: 32 plus 32 plus 16 is 80 L. That is more than the 68 L on board, so it is not available.

Option two, return to Jandakot. 210 divided by 135, times 60, is 93 minutes. The fuel is 50 L. Add the reserve, and it needs 66 L. Available, with 2 L spare.

Option three, divert to Southern Cross. 25 divided by 135, times 60, is 11 minutes. The fuel is 6 L. Add the reserve, and it needs 22 L. Available, with 46 L spare.

So Southern Cross needs the least fuel, by a wide margin. Returning is technically possible. But with 2 L spare, you are only 3 nm short of the PNR you calculated on the ground. That is exactly what the planning predicted.

Before you turn, the fifth step. Check ERSA for Southern Cross's runway and whether fuel is available there. Confirm the weather is not affected by the same storms. And note the daylight left. Then turn, call Melbourne Centre with the diversion, and amend the SARTIME.

The ETP told you where speed would have pointed you. The PNR told you, before you ever left the ground, that this was the region where going home would become marginal. And the table made the decision in a couple of minutes.

Question. In the Kalgoorlie story, you were past the ETP. Why did that not settle the decision?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because the ETP answers only the time question, which matters for a sick passenger or a failing engine. This was a fuel problem. The holding required at Kalgoorlie made continuing impossible with the reserve intact. So the fuel comparison decided, not the ETP.

## Key points

Let's recap.

The ETP is where the time to go on equals the time to go back. Use it for time-critical problems. The PNR is the furthest point from which you can return with the reserve intact. Use it when the destination may become unusable. Both matter on long legs over remote country or water, to doubtful destinations, and with thin fuel margins.

In still air, the ETP is at the midpoint. With wind, it moves into wind. The ETP distance equals the total distance, times the groundspeed home, divided by the sum of the groundspeeds. And the times on and back must come out equal. The PNR distance equals the endurance after reserves, times the groundspeed out, times the groundspeed home, divided by the sum of the groundspeeds. Any wind brings it closer than in still air.

For each diversion option, find the time from the distance and groundspeed, and the fuel from the time and flow. Then add the 30 minute reserve and any holding. Reject any option that exceeds the fuel on board. Choose the least-fuel survivor, after checking the weather, the daylight and the aerodrome. Then tell someone, and amend the SARTIME.

A final exam tip. Expect an ETP calculation with wind, a question that tests whether you know the ETP is about time and the PNR about fuel, and a three-option fuel table. For the ETP, put the groundspeed home on top, and check that the two times agree. For the fuel table, work the time, then the fuel, then add the reserve and any holding on top. And read the question for whether it wants the options that are available, or the one that needs the least fuel. Avoid rounding until the last step.

That's the end of POPC 2.6.
