This is POPC 2.5, Completing a VFR flight plan, the PPL completion standard. By the end you will have a sequence for turning a package of data into a complete plan: the route, the cruising levels, the times, the fuel ledger, the loading, and the latest time you can depart. There is one complete plan worked from start to finish, with a pause to try each step before the answer.

## What the exam expects (POPC 2.5)

The exam wants two things from you. First, work from a given package: a departure point and two landing points, a weather and operational briefing, the passengers and baggage, and the aeroplane's performance data. And second, from that package, complete a flight plan. It chooses a safe VFR route and cruising levels from the table of cruising levels. It works out the trip fuel, holding fuel, alternate fuel and reserves. It keeps the aeroplane within its weight and balance limits. And it gives the latest time you can depart.

## Why this matters

Everything you have learned for the PPL meets in one place. The night before a weekend trip, with a WAC on the table, the forecasts on a screen, and three people wanting to come to the Wheatbelt for lunch.

Navigation, weather, air law, loading and performance each feed a number into the next. And a mistake early in the chain spoils everything after it. The PPL exam tests it the same way: one scenario, a page of data, and a run of questions, from the cruising level for the first leg to the latest time you can start the engine. This lesson gives you the sequence, the reason for each step, and one complete plan worked from start to finish.

## What you are given, and what each piece is for

The first element is a list of inputs. Look at them as a planner does, by asking what question each one answers. The exam package is large, and only some of it matters for each question. There are four pieces.

First, the departure place and two landing points. They fix the geometry: the tracks, distances, terrain and airspace for each leg, and the aerodromes whose forecasts, runways and daylight you need.

Second, the weather and operational briefing. That is the forecast package, the NOTAMs and the ERSA entries. The forecasts are the GAF, the graphical area forecast; the GPWT, the grid point winds and temperatures; and the TAFs for each landing point. They give you the winds, the cloud that limits your level, and the TAF tests.

Third, the passengers and baggage. They are the payload, and they decide how much fuel you can carry.

And fourth, the performance data: the POH or the workbook charts. TAS, the true airspeed, and the fuel flow at a power setting and level. The climb and descent figures. And the take-off and landing charts.

So read the question first, then go to the piece of the package it needs. A cruising level needs the magnetic track and the cloud. Minimum fuel needs the times, the flows and the TAFs. The latest departure needs the times and the daylight table.

## The planning sequence

The order matters, because each step uses the result of the one before. Fuel needs the times. The times need the groundspeeds. And the groundspeeds need the level, because wind and TAS change with height. There are nine steps.

First, the route: the tracks, the distances, and the lowest safe altitude for each leg. Second, the weather: the wind and temperature at the candidate levels, the cloud and visibility along the route, and the TAF test at each landing point. Third, the cruising level for each leg. Fourth, the performance: TAS, heading, groundspeed and time for each leg. Fifth, the fuel: the minimum fuel required, then the fuel you will carry. Sixth, weight and balance with that fuel. Seventh, take-off and landing performance at each aerodrome, at those weights. Eighth, the latest departure time. And ninth, notification: lodge the flight plan or SARTIME, and brief the passengers.

Steps five and six are a loop. Fuel adds weight, and taking weight out can mean less fuel.

## Choosing a safe route (2.5.2a)

Lay the route out on the WAC, with the VNC or VTC wherever controlled or restricted airspace is near your tracks. You want a line that is legal, navigable and safe.

Legal first. The route must stay clear of controlled airspace you cannot enter, of active restricted areas, and of terrain you cannot clear at the minimum heights. Leaving Jandakot eastbound, for example, you are under the Perth Class C steps, whose lower limits rise with distance from Perth. Your level on the first leg is capped by the step above you, until you are clear of them.

[[diagram:airspace-cross-section]]
Picture a slice through the sky near a major airport. A Class C control zone rises from the surface at the airport. Around it, the Class C steps sit like an upside-down wedding cake, their lower limits rising the further out you go. Underneath them is Class G. Close in, the floor of controlled airspace above you is low, and a VFR flight without a clearance must stay below it.

Navigable next. Pick tracks over features you can map-read: a highway, a railway, a town, a lake. A straight line across featureless farmland is shorter, but harder to fly. Then measure each leg. The true track, converted to magnetic track with the variation from the chart. And the distance, in nautical miles.

Safe last, and this gives you a number. For each leg, find the lowest safe altitude, the LSALT. That is the highest terrain or obstacle within the track tolerance either side of the leg, plus 1,000 ft. And it is never less than the VFR minimum heights. It is the floor under every cruising level you consider. Below it, an error in navigation has no margin over the high ground.

[[diagram:minimum-heights]]
Picture the VFR minimum heights in two side views. Over a town or a gathering, 1,000 ft above the highest obstacle within 600 m. Anywhere else, 500 ft above the highest feature within 300 m. That is the absolute floor, and the LSALT sits well above it.

## Reading the weather into the plan

Take three things from the briefing for each leg.

First, the wind and temperature at your candidate levels, from the GPWT. The wind gives you the heading and groundspeed. The temperature gives you the TAS. And a headwind that strengthens with height can make a lower level faster.

Second, the cloud base and visibility along the route, from the GAF. VFR means staying in VMC. Above 3,000 ft above mean sea level, or 1,000 ft above the ground, whichever is higher, that means 1,000 ft vertically clear of cloud, as well as 5,000 m visibility. So a forecast base of 5,000 ft puts a lid at about 4,000 ft on any level you choose. Note any forecast turbulence or thunderstorms that would change the route altogether.

And third, the TAF at each landing point, tested against the alternate minima for the arrival window, as in the lesson on flight plan preparation. A TEMPO deterioration below the minima means 60 minutes of holding fuel. An INTER means 30 minutes. And a permanent deterioration means an alternate that itself passes the test. You carry those answers straight into the fuel step.

[[diagram:taf-change-groups]]
Picture a TAF as a timeline, with its change groups drawn as bars across it. An FM group cuts in and replaces everything for good. A TEMPO bar holds spells under an hour, so you carry 60 minutes of holding. INTER spells last under 30 minutes, so you carry 30.

## Cruising levels (2.5.2a and b)

A cruising level has to satisfy several conditions at once. Think of a window that closes from above and from below.

From below, the level must be at or above the LSALT for the leg.

From above, three things close it. It must keep you in VMC, which means 1,000 ft below the cloud base in most of the airspace you will use. It must be below any CTA step you have no clearance to enter. And the oxygen rule above 10,000 ft, and the time you would spend up there, make the high levels unattractive in an unpressurised trainer.

Inside that window, the table of cruising levels in the AIP decides which levels you may use. Above 5,000 ft, a VFR flight on a magnetic track of {000|zero zero zero} to {179|one seven nine} degrees flies odd thousands plus 500 ft. That is 5,500, 7,500 or 9,500. On {180|one eight zero} to {359|three five nine} degrees, it flies even thousands plus 500 ft. That is 6,500 or 8,500.

The reason is separation. IFR traffic uses the whole thousands, and VFR sits 500 ft between them. And the east-west split puts opposite-direction VFR aircraft 1,000 ft apart. Below 5,000 ft the table does not bind. But using the same pattern above 3,000 ft is good practice, for the same reason.

[[diagram:vfr-cruising-levels]]
Picture a compass rose split down the middle by magnetic track. The eastern half, {000|zero zero zero} to {179|one seven nine}, takes odd thousands plus 500. The western half, {180|one eight zero} to {359|three five nine}, takes even thousands plus 500. A memory hook: odd and east go together.

The table works on magnetic track. Not heading, which includes drift. And not true track. The boundary is exact: {179|one seven nine} degrees is eastbound, and {180|one eight zero} is westbound.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Using the true track, or the heading, to choose a level. On a track near north or south, the difference can move you across the {000|zero zero zero} or {180|one eight zero} boundary. Convert the true track to magnetic with the chart variation, and then look at which half of the compass it falls in.

Question. A leg has a magnetic track of {192|one nine two} degrees. The LSALT is 3,400 ft, and the cloud base is forecast at 9,000 ft. Is 7,500 ft a correct cruising level?
[[pause 5]]
The answer: no. A track of {192|one nine two} degrees magnetic is in the westbound half. So above 5,000 ft, the level must be an even thousand plus 500: 6,500 or 8,500 ft. Of those, 8,500 ft is only 500 ft below the cloud base. So 6,500 ft is the one that also keeps 1,000 ft clear of cloud.

## Performance for each leg

With the level chosen, the POH gives you the TAS for your power setting, at that pressure height and temperature. With the wind from the GPWT, the navigation computer gives you the heading, the drift and the groundspeed. The time for each leg is the distance divided by the groundspeed.

[[diagram:heading-track-drift]]
Picture the aeroplane from above, with a wind blowing from its left. The nose points along the heading. But the wind carries the aeroplane sideways, so it moves over the ground along the track. The angle between the heading and the track is the drift. So you point the nose upwind of the track by the drift angle, and the groundspeed comes out of the same triangle.

A full plan splits each leg into climb, cruise and descent. The POH climb table gives the time, fuel and distance to the top of climb. And you plan the top of descent so that you arrive at circuit height without a dive. Exam questions often simplify this, with one groundspeed or one fuel flow per leg. Then add the legs for the total flight time.

## Fuel: building the minimum fuel figure (2.5.2c)

Fuel planning is a ledger. Each line answers a different what-if, always in the same order, so nothing is counted twice or left out. For a day VFR private flight in a piston aeroplane, there are six lines.

The first line is taxi and run-up fuel. That is the POH allowance, typically 2 to 4 L. It is burned before take-off, but it must be in the tanks at engine start. On a trip with stops, allow it for every start.

The second is trip fuel. That is the fuel to fly the climb, cruise and descent to the destination, at the POH fuel flows. Climb fuel is higher than cruise, which is why the POH tables it separately.

The third is holding fuel, where a TAF requires it. 60 minutes for a TEMPO, and 30 minutes for an INTER. It covers waiting overhead for a forecast spell of bad weather to pass.

The fourth is alternate fuel, where an alternate is required. That is the fuel from the destination to the alternate, at cruise consumption. It covers the case where the weather does not improve at all.

The fifth is the fixed reserve. That is 30 minutes at the holding fuel flow, from the Part 91 Manual of Standards. Verify it against the current version. It is the fuel you plan to land with, and never plan to use. It is not available for holding or for a diversion. Those have their own lines above it.

And the sixth is contingency, or discretionary, fuel. It is a margin for a wrong forecast, or a diversion round a shower. It is good practice, though not required for private VFR. Many pilots carry 10 percent of the trip fuel.

Add lines one to five, and six if you carry it, to get the minimum fuel required. Compare it with the fuel you plan to carry. The difference is your margin. Then convert to mass for the loading sheet. AVGAS is about 0.72 kg per litre, so multiply litres by 0.72. Keep the whole ledger in one unit, record it on the flight plan form, and do not round the intermediate steps.

[[diagram:units-conversion-ladder]]
Picture litres and kilograms as two scales side by side. At about 0.72 kg per litre, 100 L of AVGAS is about 72 kg.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Using the fixed reserve as holding fuel. The reserve is what you land with after everything else has gone wrong, including the hold. If the TAF calls for 30 minutes of holding, that is a separate 30 minutes, on top of the 30 minute reserve. And the exam's distractor is the answer that counts only one of them.

Question. Why is the fixed reserve worked at the holding fuel flow, rather than the cruise flow?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because the reserve is time in the air with nowhere better to go, flown at the economical holding power setting, rather than at cruise. The ledger states it as 30 minutes at the holding flow, so the litres depend on that lower flow.

## Weight and balance with the fuel on board (2.5.2d)

Load the aeroplane in the loading system for its type, as in the PPL lesson on loading. The empty weight, the pilot and passengers seat by seat, the baggage, and the fuel at 0.72 kg per litre. Then check four things, in order.

First, the take-off weight against MTOW. Second, the take-off weight against the climb weight limit, from the take-off chart at the departure aerodrome's pressure height and temperature. On a hot day at an inland strip, that can be lower than MTOW. Third, the CG at zero fuel weight and at take-off weight, both inside the envelope. And fourth, the landing weight at each landing point, which falls as fuel burns.

[[widget:weight-and-balance]]
Picture the CG envelope, with weight up the side and CG along the bottom, and a point for the loaded aeroplane. Add the fuel last, and watch the point move. It must stay inside the envelope, both full and empty, and below the weight limit at the top.

If the aeroplane is over a limit, something comes off. Baggage. Fuel, but never below the minimum fuel required. Or a passenger. And every change sends you back round the loop.

Then check the take-off and landing performance with the workbook charts. Is each runway long enough for the take-off weight there? And is the landing distance available enough for the landing weight?

Question. Full tanks put the aeroplane 9 kg over MTOW, with three people and their bags. What can you trade, and what limits the trade?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: you can take out fuel, about 13 L for 9 kg, or 9 kg of baggage. The fuel can come out only as long as what is left still meets the minimum fuel required for the whole plan, reserves included. If it would not, the baggage or a passenger has to come out instead.

## The latest departure time (2.5.2e)

A day VFR flight must be on the ground at least 10 minutes before last light at the final landing point, with any holding allowed for. Last light comes from the daylight tables or graphs, for the date and the place. So you work backwards from that deadline, in five steps.

First, find last light at the final landing point, from the table. Second, subtract 10 minutes. That is the latest arrival time. Third, subtract the total flight time of all the legs. Fourth, subtract any holding you may have to do along the way. And fifth, subtract the planned ground time at each intermediate stop.

The result is the latest time of departure. Check it against the aerodrome and restricted area hours. Then convert it to UTC for the flight plan form. In Western Australia, WST is UTC plus 8 hours.

[[diagram:utc-and-wst-strip]]
Picture two time strips, UTC above and WST below, lined up so the same instant sits in the same column. WST is UTC plus 8 hours, so you subtract 8 hours from a WST time to get UTC. For example, {1530|fifteen thirty} WST is {0730|oh seven thirty} UTC on the same day.

Use the last light at the place where you will finish, not where you start. In Western Australia, last light inland is earlier than at the coast on the same evening.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Leaving the holding, or the ground time at the stops, out of the backwards count. If the TAF makes you carry 30 minutes of holding fuel, it can also make you spend 30 minutes holding. And that half hour comes out of your daylight, just as surely as it comes out of your tanks.

## A complete plan, worked through

Here is one plan, from start to finish. The aeroplane and its figures are a fictional trainer, and the distances and terrain are illustrative. For a real flight, measure your own off the chart.

The task. Depart Jandakot, land at Cunderdin, then Northam, then return to Jandakot, all on one afternoon. Three people: the pilot at 80 kg, a front passenger at 75 kg, and a rear passenger at 70 kg. And 20 kg of baggage. No fuel is available at the two stops.

The aeroplane. The TAS is 108 kt at the planned levels. The cruise fuel flow is 34 L an hour, and the holding fuel flow is 27 L an hour. The taxi allowance is 2 L per start. The usable capacity is 160 L. The basic empty weight is 760 kg, and the MTOW is 1,111 kg.

The briefing. The wind is {270|two seven zero} degrees true at 20 kt at all the levels you are considering. The cloud base is 7,000 ft. The Cunderdin and Jandakot TAFs are above the minima, with no change groups. But the Northam TAF has an INTER group, with showers below the alternate minima over your arrival window. Last light at Jandakot is {1857|eighteen fifty-seven} WST. And you plan 30 minutes on the ground at Cunderdin, and 40 minutes at Northam.

Start with the route, the levels and the times. The legs are measured off the WAC. Jandakot to Cunderdin is 74 nm, on a magnetic track of about {068|zero six eight} degrees, with an LSALT of 2,500 ft. Cunderdin to Northam is 29 nm, on about {267|two six seven} degrees. And Northam to Jandakot is 48 nm, on about {237|two three seven} degrees. Choose a level for each leg, and work out the times. Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 10]]
Leg one. Track {068|zero six eight} is eastbound, so odd thousands plus 500. 5,500 ft is above the LSALT, 1,500 ft below the cloud, and a level you climb to once the Perth steps are behind you. The wind is almost a tailwind, for a groundspeed of 126 kt. 74 divided by 126, times 60, is 35.2 minutes.

Leg two. It is a short westbound leg, below 5,000 ft, so 3,500 ft. It is into a direct headwind, at 88 kt. 29 divided by 88, times 60, is 19.8 minutes.

Leg three. Track {237|two three seven} is westbound. Below 5,000 ft the table does not bind. So 4,500 ft keeps you below the steps on the way back into Jandakot, and follows the even plus 500 pattern. The groundspeed is 91 kt. 48 divided by 91, times 60, is 31.6 minutes.

The total flight time is 35.2, plus 19.8, plus 31.6. That is 86.6 minutes. The tailwind gives you the first leg cheaply, and charges for it on the way home: 77 nm in 51 minutes, against 74 nm in 35.

Next, the fuel ledger. The total flight time is 86.6 minutes at 34 L an hour. The holding flow is 27 L an hour. The Northam INTER requires 30 minutes of holding. No alternate is required. And there are three starts, at 2 L each. Find the minimum fuel required.
[[pause 10]]
Line one, taxi. 3 times 2 is 6 L.

Line two, trip fuel. 86.6 minutes at 34 L an hour is 49.1 L.

Line three, holding for Northam. 30 minutes at 27 L an hour is 13.5 L.

Line four, alternate. 0 L.

Line five, the fixed reserve. 30 minutes at 27 L an hour, another 13.5 L.

So the minimum fuel required is 6, plus 49.1, plus 13.5, plus 0, plus 13.5. That is 82.1 L. With a 10 percent contingency of 4.9 L, it is 87.0 L. Notice that holding and reserve are the same size here. But they are two separate lines, and both are in the total.

Now the loading, and the fuel you can carry. The basic empty weight is 760 kg. The occupants are 80, 75 and 70 kg. The baggage is 20 kg, and the MTOW is 1,111 kg. How much fuel can you carry?
[[pause 8]]
Step one, the zero fuel weight. 760, plus 80, plus 75, plus 70, plus 20, is 1,005 kg.

Step two, the room for fuel under MTOW. 1,111 minus 1,005 is 106 kg. Divided by 0.72, that is 147 L. Full tanks, 160 L, would be {115.2|one hundred and fifteen point two} kg, and put you 9.2 kg over.

Step three, choose 130 L. 130 times 0.72 is 93.6 kg. The ramp weight is 1,005 plus 93.6, which is 1,098.6 kg. After the first 2 L of taxi fuel, which is 1.4 kg, the take-off weight is 1,097.2 kg, under MTOW.

Step four, the margin over the minimum. 130 minus 82.1 is 47.9 L. That is about 43 L more than the minimum with contingency.

So the answer: up to 147 L, and 130 L chosen, for a take-off weight of 1,097.2 kg. Then plot the CG at zero fuel and take-off weight, and check the climb weight limit and the runway lengths.

Last, the latest departure time. Last light at Jandakot is {1857|eighteen fifty-seven} WST. The total flight time is 86.6 minutes, taken as 87. The holding is 30 minutes. And the ground time is 30 minutes at Cunderdin and 40 at Northam. When must you depart, in WST and in UTC?
[[pause 10]]
Step one, the latest arrival. {1857|Eighteen fifty-seven} minus 10 minutes is {1847|eighteen forty-seven} WST.

Step two, the time to take off the clock. 87, plus 30, plus 30, plus 40, is 187 minutes. That is 3 hours and 7 minutes.

Step three, the latest departure. {1847|Eighteen forty-seven} minus 3 hours and 7 minutes is {1540|fifteen forty} WST.

Step four, for the flight plan form, subtract 8 hours. That is {0740|oh seven forty} UTC.

So the latest departure is {1540|fifteen forty} WST, which is {0740|oh seven forty} UTC. And round the flight time up, not down. For a deadline, a fraction of a minute counts as a whole one.

Here is one to try yourself. A different day, a single destination, the same aeroplane. The trip fuel is for 1 hour 50 minutes at 34 L an hour. There is one start at 2 L, and no holding. The destination TAF has an FM group that brings cloud below the alternate minima for the whole arrival window. So you nominate an alternate 40 nm beyond it, flown at a groundspeed of 100 kt. What is the minimum fuel required, in litres and in kilograms?
[[pause 10]]
The trip fuel is 110 minutes at 34 L an hour, which is 62.3 L. The alternate is 40 nm at 100 kt, which is 24 minutes. At the cruise flow, that is 13.6 L. The reserve is 30 minutes at 27 L an hour, which is 13.5 L. And the taxi is 2 L.

So the minimum fuel required is 2, plus 62.3, plus 0, plus 13.6, plus 13.5. That is 91.4 L. And 91.4 times 0.72 is 65.8 kg. A permanent deterioration needs an alternate, not holding. And the alternate fuel is flown at cruise consumption, not the holding flow. The answer again: 91.4 L, or 65.8 kg.

Question. In the worked plan, why did the INTER at Northam change the latest departure time, as well as the fuel?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because the holding fuel is there so that you can actually hold, for up to 30 minutes. And if you do, you arrive at Jandakot 30 minutes later. The backwards count from last light has to include that possible delay, so the departure moves 30 minutes earlier.

## Bringing it together

At {1300|thirteen hundred} on the day, a new TAF comes out for Jandakot, with a TEMPO group of thunderstorms over your arrival time. The TAF test now requires 60 minutes of holding at Jandakot.

That is a new line in the ledger: 60 minutes at 27 L an hour, which is 27 L. It moves the minimum fuel from 82.1 L to {109.1|one hundred and nine point one} L. That is still inside the 130 L you planned to carry, and the weight is unchanged.

But the latest departure time changes. Sixty more minutes of possible holding moves it from {1540|fifteen forty} to {1440|fourteen forty} WST. And if it is already {1500|fifteen hundred} when you have briefed, the plan as drawn is dead.

So you leave out Northam, with its INTER holding and its ground time, to recover about 70 minutes. Or you go another day. Nothing in that decision needed a new skill. Only the same ledger and the same backwards count, rerun with one new input. And that is exactly what the exam tests.

Question. The passengers ask to bring an extra 15 kg of bags after the plan is complete. Which steps of the plan do you have to redo?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the weight and balance, with the new zero fuel weight and CG, and the take-off and landing performance at the new weights. If the extra weight pushes you over MTOW, you must take out fuel, and then recheck that what remains still meets the minimum fuel required. The route, the levels and the times stay as they were.

## Key points

Let's recap.

The route, the briefing, the payload and the performance data each answer a different question. Go to the piece each question needs.

Plan a route clear of airspace you cannot enter, active restricted areas and high terrain, over features you can map-read. The LSALT is the highest terrain or obstacle in the tolerance, plus 1,000 ft.

Each level must be above the LSALT, in VMC, below any CTA step, and below the oxygen levels. Above 5,000 ft, fly odd plus 500 on magnetic tracks {000|zero zero zero} to {179|one seven nine}, and even plus 500 on {180|one eight zero} to {359|three five nine}.

The fuel ledger, in order. Taxi. Trip. Holding: 60 minutes for a TEMPO, 30 minutes for an INTER. Alternate, at the cruise flow. The fixed reserve, 30 minutes at the holding flow. And optional contingency. Keep one unit, no rounding until the end, and litres to kilograms at 0.72.

Check MTOW, the climb weight limit, the CG at zero fuel and take-off weight, and the landing weights. Take out baggage or fuel, never below the minimum, and repeat.

And the latest departure is last light at the final landing point, minus 10 minutes, minus the total flight time, minus any holding, minus the ground time at the stops. Then convert to UTC.

A final exam tip. Expect one scenario, and a chain of questions on it. Keep the fuel ledger in the order taxi, trip, holding, alternate, reserve, and in one unit. Holding and the 30 minute reserve are separate lines. Choose cruising levels on magnetic track: odd plus 500 eastbound, from {000|zero zero zero} to {179|one seven nine}, and even plus 500 westbound. Work the latest departure time backwards from last light minus 10 minutes, through the total flight time, including holding and stops. And have the conversion figures to hand: 0.72 kg per litre, and WST is UTC plus 8.

That's the end of POPC 2.5.
