This is POPC 2.4, Flight plan preparation. By the end you will know what briefing you must get and when, where the weather comes from, which charts to take, how to make sure someone will look for you, and how to decide from a forecast whether you need holding fuel or an alternate, and how much fuel that adds. There are worked examples and questions along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (POPC 2.4)

The exam wants three things from you. First, apply the pilot in command's duty to get a weather and operational briefing before planning a VFR flight. Second, for a given route, choose the visual charts, list the operations for which a briefing is mandatory, list the weather services and how to get them, and apply the flight notification requirements and the preferred ways to submit one. And third, from an aerodrome forecast, decide whether holding or an alternate is required. If so, nominate a suitable alternate, and work out the extra fuel.

## Why this matters

On a weekday evening, you sit down to plan Saturday's flight from Jandakot to Geraldton. Two hours up the coast, past the restricted areas around Pearce and Lancelin, to an aerodrome whose weather you cannot see from Perth.

Everything that keeps that flight safe is decided at the kitchen table, before the engine starts. You need the forecasts and the NOTAMs. You need the right charts for each part of the route. You need somebody who will start a search if you do not turn up. And you need enough fuel, not just to get there, but to cope if the forecast says Geraldton might be under low cloud when you arrive.

The exam turns each of those into a question, and the last one into a calculation. Given a TAF and an ETA, is holding or an alternate required, and how many litres does it add? This lesson works through the planning in the order you do it, and ends with a complete fuel plan.

## The briefing: what you must get, and when

The rule starts from a simple idea. The pilot in command is the one person who will be in the aeroplane when the weather and the airspace do whatever they do. So the pilot in command must find out about both beforehand. Before any flight, you must obtain the weather and operational information reasonably necessary for the safe conduct of the flight.

The Part 91 rules make a meteorological briefing mandatory in two cases. That briefing means the forecasts for the route and the destination. The first case is flights beyond the vicinity of the departure aerodrome, which means more than 50 nm, or any flight to another aerodrome. The second is any flight where the weather could affect it. And good practice is a briefing for every flight. Verify the current wording in chapter 7 of the Part 91 Manual of Standards. In practice, that means every cross-country you will fly as a private pilot.

The forecasts must be current when you study them. And if you studied them more than an hour before departure, you refresh them before you go. The reason is that forecasts are amended. The TAF you read at {0700|oh seven hundred} can have been replaced by {0830|oh eight thirty}, with a new TEMPO group for the very period you will arrive in.

The operational briefing is the other half. The NOTAMs for the departure aerodrome, the destination, the alternates and the route. The AIP Supplements. And any head-office NOTAMs. These carry the temporary changes that override ERSA and the charts: a runway closed for works, a restricted area activated for an exercise, an AWIS out of service.

Question. You collected the forecasts at {0715|oh seven fifteen}, and you are about to depart at {0840|oh eight forty}. What must you do, and why?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: obtain the forecasts again and review them, because more than an hour has passed. A TAF can be amended at any time. And an amendment could add a deterioration in your arrival period that the earlier copy did not show.

## Where the weather comes from

The Bureau of Meteorology produces the products, and Airservices and the Bureau deliver them. Knowing both halves answers the exam's where-would-you-get-it questions.

First, the products. For the route, the Bureau issues the GAF, the graphical area forecast, and the GPWT, the grid point wind and temperature forecast. For each aerodrome, it issues the TAF, the aerodrome forecast, and the METAR and SPECI observations. And it issues the SIGMET and AIRMET warnings of hazardous weather.

Second, how you get them. Before flight, from the NAIPS briefing system, on the web or the app, as a location or route briefing. Or from the Bureau's aviation weather site, or by phone from the Airservices briefing office. In flight, from the flight information service, FIS, on the area frequency. FIS can give you the latest TAF, METAR and SIGMET on request. And from the ATIS and AWIS broadcasts at aerodromes along the way.

[[diagram:taf-anatomy]]
Picture a TAF laid out group by group, left to right, each with its label. First, the aerodrome, the issue time and the validity period. Then the wind, the visibility and the cloud. Then the change groups, such as FM and TEMPO. And at the end, the temperatures and QNH. The change groups are the ones that decide your fuel, as you will hear shortly.

Question. Halfway to Geraldton, you want the latest Geraldton TAF. Who do you ask, and how?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: flight information service, on the area frequency for your position, which you find in the NAV COMM section of ERSA or on the chart. FIS can read you the latest TAF, METAR and any SIGMET on request.

## Charts for the route

Each visual chart is drawn for a job. And the job is set by how much detail you need at that stage of the flight. There are three charts, plus ERSA.

The WAC, the world aeronautical chart, is at a scale of {1:1,000,000|one to one million}. It covers the whole country in sheets, and it is the chart for navigating across open country by its terrain, towns and roads. Select the WAC, or WACs, covering the whole route, using the WAC index on the PCA, the planning chart.

The VNC, the visual navigation chart, adds the airspace detail that matters near controlled airspace: the steps of Class C, the restricted areas, and the reporting points. You need it for any part of the route near controlled airspace.

The VTC, the visual terminal chart, is the large-scale chart for departure and arrival in a terminal area. It shows the lanes of entry, the VFR routes and the approach points. And you carry ERSA for the aerodromes.

[[diagram:chart-features-panel]]
Picture four chart panels side by side. The WAC shows terrain, towns, roads, railways, rivers and aerodromes, but no airspace. The VNC adds controlled airspace, restricted areas, frequencies and reporting points. The VTC shows the terminal area in detail, with control zones, steps, VFR routes and frequencies. And the en route chart for low level shows airspace, routes and aids, but no terrain at all. So: the WAC for terrain across open country, the VNC for airspace near controlled areas, and the VTC for the terminal area.

For a flight from Jandakot to Kalgoorlie, that is the Perth VTC for the departure, the Perth VNC through the Perth and Pearce airspace, and the Perth and Kalgoorlie WACs for the rest.

Question. For Jandakot to Geraldton, which chart would you use to plan the departure from the Perth terminal area, and which for the open country north of Lancelin?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the Perth VTC for the departure, because it shows the terminal area lanes and approach points at the scale you need. Then the Perth VNC for the airspace beyond. North of the controlled airspace, the WAC covering the route takes over, with the VNC kept handy where restricted areas lie near the track.

## Flight notification: who knows you are out there

Every safe outcome after a forced landing starts with someone noticing you are missing. The flight notification rules exist so that someone always will.

A VFR flight is not required to submit a flight plan, unless it will operate in controlled airspace or wants SAR cover. But the pilot in command must leave some form of search and rescue watch. There are three kinds. First, a flight notification, which is a flight plan lodged with Airservices. Second, a SARTIME lodged with Airservices. That is the time by which you will report your arrival, after which the search and rescue system acts. And third, a flight note left with a responsible person, who will notify SAR if the aircraft is overdue.

The preferred methods of submission are electronic. NAIPS, on the web or the app, comes first, for flight plans and SARTIMEs. Then by phone to the Airservices briefing office. And in flight, by radio to FIS. A flight into or through controlled airspace needs a flight plan, either full details or an abbreviated plan by radio, so that air traffic control has the details. And a flight plan is also the easiest way to get a SARTIME.

[[diagram:sartime-timeline]]
Picture a timeline running left to right. Before the flight, you nominate a SARTIME in UTC, with a sensible margin after your planned arrival. If you are delayed, you extend it by radio or phone before it expires. On landing, you cancel it at once. But if it is neither cancelled nor amended by that time, the search and rescue system begins its alerting phases. First, attempts to contact you. Then a communications search. And then a physical search, by the Joint Rescue Coordination Centre.

The notification is a promise, so it has to stay true. Amend it if the flight changes: the ETA, the route or the aircraft. And cancel the SARTIME on arrival. A SARTIME you forget to cancel starts a search for an aeroplane that is safely in its hangar.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. A VFR flight does not need a flight plan. That is true, but incomplete. It still needs a SAR watch of some kind: a flight notification, a SARTIME, or a flight note with a responsible person. And entering controlled airspace does need a flight plan.

## The arrival test: is holding or an alternate needed?

Now the calculation the exam builds on. You are planning to arrive at an aerodrome you cannot see, and the only evidence about its weather is a forecast. The rules ask one question. Does that forecast give you enough confidence of a visual arrival? And if not, what is your way out?

Start with the window. The forecast is tested over a window, not at the ETA alone. It runs from 30 minutes before the ETA to 60 minutes after it. The window allows for arriving early, for arriving late, and for the forecast's timing being a little out.

Within that window, the decision uses the alternate minima for VFR. There are four tests. The destination TAF must show cloud not more than scattered below 1,500 ft above the aerodrome. Visibility of at least 8 km. No probability of fog, dust, thunderstorms or other phenomena below the minima. And the crosswind within limits. Verify the current figures in chapter 7 of the Part 91 Manual of Standards and in the AIP. The PPL lesson on air service operations explains each one.

[[diagram:taf-arrival-window]]
Picture the Geraldton example on a timeline from {0230|oh two thirty} to {0630|oh six thirty} UTC. The ETA is {0400|oh four hundred}, so the window runs from {0330|oh three thirty} to {0500|oh five hundred}. The base conditions pass the minima. But a TEMPO bar, from {0300|oh three hundred} to {0600|oh six hundred}, covers the whole window. It brings broken cloud at 1,200 ft and 4,000 m visibility, and both fail the alternate minima.

Next, what a failure costs. If a forecast condition in the window fails that test, what you must carry depends on how long the forecast says the bad weather will last. Short-lived bad weather can be waited out. Permanent bad weather cannot. There are five cases.

First, a TEMPO deterioration, with fluctuations under 60 minutes, requires 60 minutes of holding fuel. Second, an INTER deterioration, under 30 minutes, requires 30 minutes of holding fuel. Third, a PROB condition is treated according to the group it qualifies. A {PROB30|prob thirty} TEMPO of fog requires the TEMPO holding, or an alternate. Fourth, a permanent condition below the minima requires an alternate aerodrome. That means an FM or BECMG change to below the minima, or base conditions below them. The alternate must itself meet the minima for the arrival period, and you carry fuel to fly from the destination to it. And fifth, if no forecast is available, you need an alternate, or the special provisions for departing without one.

[[diagram:taf-change-groups]]
Picture an example TAF as a timeline from {0600|oh six hundred} to {1800|eighteen hundred} UTC. At {1000|ten hundred}, an FM group cuts in and replaces the main conditions for good. Between {0600|oh six hundred} and {0900|oh nine hundred}, a TEMPO bar adds spells of 4,000 m in showers, with broken cloud at 2,000 ft, each spell lasting 30 to 60 minutes. Underneath, the other groups. BECMG is a gradual change. INTER means fluctuations of less than 30 minutes. And PROB gives a probability. Test every group that touches your window.

The logic of the holding figures is this. You carry enough fuel to outlast the worst single spell the forecast describes. A TEMPO spell can last up to an hour. An INTER fluctuation lasts less than half an hour. For a TEMPO or an INTER, you may instead nominate an alternate. But a permanent deterioration leaves you no holding option, because waiting does not help.

[[diagram:alternate-decision-flow]]
Picture that logic as a decision flow, top to bottom. Lay every TAF group against the window. If nothing in the window is below the minima, carry the trip fuel and the fixed reserve. If something is, ask how long it lasts. An INTER: 30 minutes of holding, or an alternate. A TEMPO: 60 minutes of holding, or an alternate. A permanent change: an alternate. And the fixed reserve sits on top of all of them.

Now the fuel figures. Holding fuel is calculated at the holding fuel flow from the POH, or the cruise flow if none is given. Alternate fuel is the fuel for the diversion distance at cruise consumption. The fixed reserve is on top of all of it. For day VFR in an aeroplane below 5,700 kg, that is 30 minutes, from chapter 19 of the Part 91 Manual of Standards. Verify it. The fixed reserve is the fuel you plan never to use. Holding and alternate fuel are fuel you have planned that you might use.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Testing the ETA instead of the window. An ETA of {0400|oh four hundred} with a TEMPO starting at {0430|oh four thirty} looks clear. But the window runs to {0500|oh five hundred}, so the TEMPO is in it, and holding or an alternate is required. Work out the window first, then lay every change group against it.

Let's work the Geraldton example. The ETA at Geraldton is {0400|oh four hundred} UTC. The TAF gives {15012KT|one five zero degrees at twelve knots}, visibility 10 km or more, and {SCT030|scattered cloud at three thousand feet}. Then a TEMPO from {0300|oh three hundred} to {0600|oh six hundred}: 4,000 m in showers of rain, with broken cloud at 1,200 ft. The holding flow is 28 L an hour, the cruise flow is 36 L an hour, and AVGAS is 0.72 kg per litre. What must you carry? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 10]]
Step one, the window. From 30 minutes before to 60 minutes after the ETA, that is {0330|oh three thirty} to {0500|oh five hundred} UTC.

Step two, the base conditions. {SCT030|Scattered cloud at three thousand feet} and 10 km or more both pass the minima.

Step three, the TEMPO. Between {0300|oh three hundred} and {0600|oh six hundred}, which covers the whole window, broken cloud at 1,200 ft and 4 km visibility. Both are below the minima.

Step four, the requirement. For a TEMPO, that is 60 minutes of holding fuel.

Step five, the holding fuel. 1 hour at 28 L an hour is 28 L. That is 28 times 0.72, which is 20 kg.

Step six, the fixed reserve, 30 minutes at the same flow. Half of 28 is 14 L, carried as well.

Or, instead of holding, you nominate an alternate. Dongara is 25 minutes away, and qualifies if its TAF meets the minima for your arrival there. The alternate fuel is 25 sixtieths of an hour at 36 L an hour, which is 15 L.

So the answer: either 28 L of holding fuel, or 15 L to Dongara. Carry whichever you choose, plus the 14 L reserve, and record the decision on the plan.

One note on format. Those TAF times are hours UTC, as the lesson gives them. A current TAF writes a change period with the day first. So a TEMPO from {0300|oh three hundred} to {0600|oh six hundred} on the 3rd would read {0303/0306|zero three zero three, stroke, zero three zero six}.

[[diagram:units-conversion-ladder]]
Picture litres and kilograms as two scales side by side. AVGAS weighs 0.72 kg per litre, so multiply litres by 0.72 for kilograms. 100 L is about 72 kg.

Now, choosing an alternate. An alternate is not any aerodrome near the destination. It has to give you a better chance of a visual arrival than the destination does, and it has to be usable. So you use the ERSA checks from the lesson on ERSA. There are four.

First, its own forecast must meet the alternate minima for your arrival period there. That is later than your destination ETA, by the diversion time. Second, it should not be under the same weather. An aerodrome 15 nm down the coast, inside the same band of low cloud, is no way out. Third, it must be usable. No PPR you cannot get on the day, a runway long enough on the chart, and daylight at the time you would arrive. And fourth, it must be close enough that the alternate fuel is reasonable.

Dongara works in the Geraldton example, if its TAF is clear for its own window. It is close, it has a usable runway, and a shower passing Geraldton may well miss it. But if the deterioration were a wide band of coastal fog, an inland aerodrome would be the better choice.

Here is one for you to try. Your ETA at a coastal destination is {0230|oh two thirty} UTC. Its TAF has an FM group: from {0300|oh three hundred} UTC, 3,000 m in drizzle, with broken cloud at 800 ft. That is a permanent change. Your chosen alternate is 40 minutes away, and its TAF is clear. The cruise flow is 36 L an hour. Is holding allowed, and how much extra fuel do you carry for the deterioration?
[[pause 10]]
The window is {0200|oh two hundred} to {0330|oh three thirty} UTC. The FM change at {0300|oh three hundred} falls inside it, and brings 3,000 m and broken cloud at 800 ft, below the minima. It is permanent, so holding is not an option. An alternate is required.

The alternate fuel is 40 sixtieths of an hour at 36 L an hour, which is 24 L. That goes on top of the trip fuel and the fixed reserve. So the answer: no holding, an alternate, and 24 L. And check that the alternate's TAF meets the minima for your arrival there, at about {0310|oh three ten} UTC, with the window around it.

Question. Why does a TEMPO allow holding fuel, but an FM change to below the minima does not?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: holding works only if the bad weather will end while you wait. A TEMPO spell lasts up to an hour, so 60 minutes of fuel outlasts it. An FM change is permanent for the rest of the forecast period. Waiting does not bring the weather back, so you need somewhere else to land.

## A complete fuel plan

The holding and alternate figures are only part of a fuel plan. Put the whole thing together in the order you would write it on the plan, and compare the total with what the tanks hold.

Let's work one, with an INTER at the destination. The trip time is 1 hour 45 minutes. The cruise flow is 36 L an hour, and it is also used for holding, because the POH gives no holding flow. The destination TAF has an INTER below the minima across your window. The fixed reserve is 30 minutes. And the usable fuel is 110 L. What fuel is required, and what is the margin? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 10]]
Step one, the trip fuel. 1.75 hours at 36 L an hour is 63 L.

Step two, the holding for an INTER, 30 minutes. Half of 36 is 18 L.

Step three, the fixed reserve, 30 minutes. Another 18 L.

Step four, the fuel required. 63 plus 18 plus 18 is 99 L, plus the taxi allowance from your POH or operator, which is not included here.

Step five, the margin. 110 minus 99 is 11 L. At 36 L an hour, that is 11 thirty-sixths of an hour, which is 18 minutes of flight.

So the answer: 99 L required, leaving 11 L, about 18 minutes. The flight is legal with full usable fuel. But the margin is only 18 minutes before the taxi allowance, and a headwind stronger than forecast eats it quickly.

If the forecast had been a TEMPO instead, holding would be 36 L, and the total 117 L. That is more than the tanks hold. So you would plan an alternate within range, or a fuel stop, or a later arrival outside the TEMPO.

[[diagram:fuel-plan-ladder]]
Picture that fuel plan as a stack, standing beside a column for the 110 L of usable fuel. At the bottom, the trip, 63 L. On top of it, 18 L of holding, then the 18 L reserve. The stack reaches 99 L, leaving 11 L of the column above it. Now swap in a TEMPO's 36 L of holding, and the stack grows to 117 L, 7 L taller than the tanks.

Keep the figures unrounded until the end. The exam's fuel policy says so. A total rounded down at an intermediate step can turn a flight that does not fit into one that appears to.

Question. In that fuel plan, could you use the 18 L fixed reserve to cover a longer hold if the INTER showers lingered?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: no. The fixed reserve is the fuel you plan to land with, and never use. Holding and alternate fuel are separate, and on top of it. If the hold goes on until only the reserve is left, you divert, or land without delay.

## Bringing it together

It is Friday evening, and you are flying Jandakot to Geraldton tomorrow, with an ETA of {0400|oh four hundred} UTC. That is {1200|twelve hundred} WST.

You open NAIPS. The GAF shows showers along the coast. The Geraldton TAF has the TEMPO you have just worked. And the NOTAMs show an exercise activating a restricted area near Lancelin.

You get out the Perth VTC for the departure, the Perth VNC for the Pearce and Lancelin airspace, and the WAC for the coast to Geraldton.

The arrival window, {0330|oh three thirty} to {0500|oh five hundred}, sits inside the TEMPO. So you choose: 28 L of holding, or 15 L to Dongara. Dongara's TAF is clear, so you nominate it. You add the 15 L and the 14 L reserve to the trip fuel, and check the total against the tanks.

You lodge a flight plan on NAIPS with a SARTIME, because you will cross controlled airspace on the way out. On Saturday morning at {0700|oh seven hundred} WST, you check the TAF again, since your first look was the evening before. It has not changed. And on arrival, you cancel the SARTIME from the apron. Every element of the subtopic was a step in one plan.

## Key points

Let's recap.

The pilot in command must obtain the weather and operational information for the flight. That is the forecasts for the route and destination, the NOTAMs, the AIP Supplements and the head-office NOTAMs. Refresh forecasts studied more than an hour before departure.

The charts: WACs for the route, from the WAC index on the PCA. The VNC near controlled airspace. The VTC for terminal areas. And ERSA.

A meteorological briefing is mandatory beyond the vicinity of the departure aerodrome, and whenever the weather could affect the flight.

The weather comes from the Bureau of Meteorology: the GAF, the GPWT, the TAF, the METAR and SPECI, and the SIGMET and AIRMET. Before flight you get them from NAIPS, the Bureau's aviation site, or the briefing office. In flight, from FIS, the ATIS and the AWIS.

Every VFR flight needs a SAR watch: a flight notification, a SARTIME, or a flight note with a responsible person. And a flight plan for controlled airspace. Submit electronically, through NAIPS, first. Amend it if things change, and cancel the SARTIME on arrival.

Test the destination TAF over the window from 30 minutes before to 60 minutes after the ETA, against the VFR alternate minima. An alternate must meet the minima for its own arrival period, must not share the destination's weather, and must be usable.

And the fuel. A TEMPO below the minima needs 60 minutes of holding. An INTER needs 30 minutes. A permanent deterioration needs an alternate, with the fuel to reach it. Holding is at the POH holding flow, or the cruise flow. Alternate fuel is at the cruise flow. And the fixed reserve, 30 minutes for day VFR, goes on top of all of it.

A final exam tip. The arrival window is 30 minutes before to 60 minutes after the ETA. A TEMPO needs 60 minutes of holding, and an INTER 30. A permanent deterioration needs an alternate that itself passes the test. The 30 minute fixed reserve is never used for holding or diversion. And in a fuel calculation, write each line with its unit: trip, holding or alternate, reserve, and taxi. Round only at the end, and compare the total with the usable fuel.

That's the end of POPC 2.4.
