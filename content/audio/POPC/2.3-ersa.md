This is POPC 2.3, Using ERSA for VFR operations. By the end you will know what each part of ERSA is for, and you will have a fixed order for working any aerodrome entry, from whether you may land at all to which runway the wind favours, whether it is long enough, how you join, and what time things happen. There are worked examples and questions along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (POPC 2.3)

The exam wants one thing from you, and it is a big one. Use every item of ERSA information that bears on a VFR flight by day. Find it, decode it, and turn it into a decision about the flight.

## Why this matters

In the recreational lesson on documentation, you learned to read an ERSA entry: the runway line, the restricted area listing, the UTC hours. That was reading. The PPL asks for something harder, which is using it.

Plan Jandakot to an unfamiliar strip in the Wheatbelt, or up the coast towards Kalbarri. ERSA is the only briefing you will get on the place before you are overhead it. Whether you are allowed to land. Which runway the afternoon wind will favour. Whether that runway is long enough on a hot day. Which way the circuit goes. What frequency everyone is on. And whether the restricted area beside your track will be active when you pass.

The exam hands you an extract for an aerodrome you have never heard of, and asks what you must do before arriving, which runway to use, or what a remark means for you. This lesson gives you a fixed order for working any entry, and shows how each item feeds a decision you already know how to make.

## The book and what each part is for

ERSA, the En Route Supplement Australia, is the permanent picture of places and facilities. Its sections are worth knowing by what you use them for, because a question will often name the information, and expect you to know where it lives. There are seven parts.

First, the introduction and legend. It holds the abbreviations, the layout of an aerodrome entry, and the symbols on the aerodrome diagrams. You use it for decoding entries.

Second, GEN, the general section, which holds your planning tools in the exam. Conversion tables, the wind component table, and sunrise and sunset tables for the major aerodromes. Flight planning codes and forms, and the SARTIME and flight notification procedures. Fuel availability codes. The remote areas and their survival equipment. The ATIS and AWIS systems, and phone numbers.

Third, FAC, the facilities section, which holds the aerodrome entries. Each gives the location, elevation, variation, operator and PPR, the runway table and diagram, lighting, obstacles, communications, navigation aids and fuel, the local traffic regulations, and the remarks. You use it for every destination and alternate. The runway length and surface go into the performance charts, and the elevation gives you pressure height.

Fourth, PRD: the prohibited, restricted and danger areas, with their limits, hours, authority and status. You use it for route planning and clearances.

Fifth, NAV COMM: the navigation aids and communications facilities. That is where the frequencies for the flight information service areas live, and the aids on your route.

Sixth, EMERG, the emergency procedures, which you use in flight. The lessons on emergencies and SAR cover them.

And seventh, the runway distances supplement. It gives TORA, TODA, ASDA and LDA for aerodromes with declared distances. You use the TODA and LDA with the performance charts.

Two features of the whole book shape how you read it. Times are UTC, so that one book serves every time zone. In Western Australia you add 8 hours. And runway lengths and widths are in metres, while elevations and heights are in feet. Most careless ERSA errors are one of those two.

The other thing to hold on to is what ERSA is not. It is amended on a cycle. Anything that has changed since, or that is temporary, comes by NOTAM or AIP Supplement, and overrides it. So every ERSA check ends with the NOTAMs for the aerodrome and the route.

Question. You want to know whether AVGAS is available at your destination, and how to pay for it. Where do you look, and what do you check afterwards?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the aerodrome's FAC entry in ERSA, which gives the fuel types and the payment arrangements. The fuel codes are explained in GEN. Then the NOTAMs, because a fuel outage is exactly the kind of temporary change that does not appear in ERSA.

## A fixed order for any aerodrome

An aerodrome entry has a dozen blocks. A pilot who reads them in whatever order the eye falls on them misses one. A pilot who works the same seven questions every time does not. The order follows the flight. Can I go there at all? Where will I land? How do I join? What is there? What is around it? When? And what if it goes wrong?

[[diagram:ersa-entry-anatomy]]
Picture an ERSA entry for an invented aerodrome, with a label on each block. At the top, the header, with the elevation, variation and time zone. Below it, the runway table, with dimensions in metres, surface, slope and lighting. Then the communications and aids. And at the bottom, the remarks and local traffic regulations.

Step one: can I use it? Look for PPR, the operating hours, the aerodrome category, any restriction on aircraft types, and fuel and the hours it is available.

Step two: the runways. Which runway suits the forecast wind, using the wind component table in GEN. Its length and width in metres, and the TODA and LDA if they are declared. Its surface and slope, for the take-off and landing charts. And its lighting, if your arrival is near last light.

Step three: the procedures. The circuit height and direction for that runway, because right-hand circuits are stated. Noise abatement. Any non-standard joining. Parachuting, gliding or helicopter activity. The CTAF, the common traffic advisory frequency. Whether radio is mandatory, at a certified or registered aerodrome. And reporting points.

Step four: the services. The AWIS or ATIS frequency and phone number, the navigation aids, and the fuel type and payment method.

Step five: the airspace. Any restricted areas or control zones around the aerodrome, their hours and the controlling authority, from the PRD section and the chart.

Step six: the time. Convert the ERSA hours and the restricted area hours from UTC to WST, Western Standard Time, and compare them with your ETA. And check sunrise and sunset for the last light rule.

And step seven: the emergency. The nearest alternate from FAC, and the EMERG section for the search and rescue arrangements.

The rest of this lesson works through the steps that need more than a look-up. It uses the invented aerodrome in that picture, Example Creek. Here are its details. The elevation is 1,050 ft, and the variation is 1 degree west. Runway {06|zero six} and {24|two four} is 1,200 m long, sealed and lit, with a slope of 0.5 percent up to the east. Runway {12|one two} and {30|three zero} is 800 m of gravel, unlit. It has a CTAF, an AWIS and PPR, and right-hand circuits on runway {24|two four}.

## Can I go there at all?

Before you spend time on runways, find out whether you may land. PPR, prior permission required, means the operator wants a phone call first, and the number is in the header. Landing without it is a breach of the operator's conditions. And the call often tells you what ERSA cannot. The strip was graded on Tuesday. The sheep are in the paddock beside the threshold. The fuel bowser is out.

Look also for the operating hours, and any restrictions on aircraft type or weight. And look at the fuel block: the type, whether it is available at all hours or only when the operator is there, and how you pay. A destination with no fuel changes your fuel plan for the leg home. So it feeds straight into the lesson on flight plan preparation.

## Choosing the runway: wind, length and surface

This is where ERSA meets the performance work from the lesson on take-off and landing performance. And it is where the exam can join two subtopics in one question. There are two questions to answer: which runway suits the wind, and is it long enough?

Start with the wind. A runway's designator is its magnetic direction to the nearest 10 degrees, with the last zero dropped. But a forecast wind in a TAF or GAF is given in degrees true. To compare them, convert the wind to magnetic with the variation in the ERSA header. Variation west, magnetic best: add it. Variation east, magnetic least: subtract it. At Example Creek the variation is only 1 degree, but the habit is what matters, because elsewhere it is larger.

Then find the angle between the wind and each runway, and split the wind into a headwind component and a crosswind component. ERSA GEN carries a wind component table for this. The arithmetic it tabulates is simple. The crosswind is the wind speed times the sine of the angle. The headwind is the wind speed times the cosine of the angle.

[[widget:crosswind-component]]
Picture a wind arrow meeting the runway at an angle, split into a part along the runway and a part across it. The crosswind part grows fast. At 30 degrees off the runway, half the wind is already across it. At 60 degrees, nine-tenths of it is.

Let's work one: choosing between the runways at Example Creek. The forecast wind is {280|two eight zero} degrees true at 15 kt. The variation is 1 degree west. Treat each runway as pointing at its designator times ten. Find the components on runway {24|two four} and runway {30|three zero}. Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 10]]
Step one, the wind in magnetic. The variation is west, so add. {280|two eight zero} plus 1 is {281|two eight one} degrees magnetic.

Step two, runway {24|two four}, which points {240|two four zero} degrees magnetic. The angle is {281|two eight one} minus {240|two four zero}, which is 41 degrees. The sine of 41 degrees is 0.656, so the crosswind is 15 times 0.656, which is 9.8 kt. The cosine is 0.755, so the headwind is 15 times 0.755, which is 11.3 kt.

Step three, runway {30|three zero}, which points {300|three zero zero} degrees magnetic. The angle is {300|three zero zero} minus {281|two eight one}, which is 19 degrees. The sine is 0.326, so the crosswind is 4.9 kt. The cosine is 0.946, so the headwind is 14.2 kt.

So runway {24|two four} gives 9.8 kt across and 11.3 kt down the runway. Runway {30|three zero} gives 4.9 kt across and 14.2 kt down it. Runway {30|three zero} is better aligned. But it is 400 m shorter, and gravel. Runway {24|two four} gives a 10 kt crosswind on a long sealed surface.

Which you choose depends on two numbers that are not in ERSA. Your aeroplane's demonstrated crosswind, from the POH. And the landing distance from the chart, for an 800 m gravel strip with a 14 kt headwind. ERSA gave you the inputs. The POH decides.

Here is one for you to try. The forecast changes to {200|two zero zero} degrees true at 20 kt. Which runway end is into wind, and what are its components?
[[pause 10]]
The wind in magnetic is {200|two zero zero} plus 1, which is {201|two zero one}. Runway {24|two four}, at {240|two four zero}, is 39 degrees off. The crosswind is 20 times the sine of 39 degrees, 0.629, which is 12.6 kt. The headwind is 20 times the cosine, 0.777, which is 15.5 kt.

Runway {12|one two}, at {120|one two zero}, is 81 degrees off. That is almost a direct crosswind: 20 times the sine of 81 degrees is 19.8 kt, with only 3.1 kt of headwind.

So the answer is runway {24|two four}, with about 13 kt across it. Check that against the POH demonstrated crosswind. And remember that runway {24|two four} has right-hand circuits.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. The wind in a forecast is true. The wind from a tower or an ATIS is magnetic, and runway designators are magnetic. Converting an ATIS wind a second time, or forgetting to convert a forecast wind, moves the angle by the variation. Where the variation is small, the error hides. In the exam, it may not be small.

Now the second question: is it long enough? The runway table gives the physical runway: its length and width in metres, its surface and its slope. But where the aerodrome has declared distances, the runway distances supplement gives four figures for each direction. These are the ones you compare with the performance charts. Picture the runway from the start of the take-off roll.

The first is TORA, the take-off run available. That is the length you can roll on.

The second is TODA, the take-off distance available. That is the TORA plus any clearway beyond the end, over which you may climb to the screen height. This is what you compare with the chart's take-off distance to 50 ft.

The third is ASDA, the accelerate-stop distance available. That is the TORA plus any stopway, where you could stop after an abandoned take-off.

And the fourth is LDA, the landing distance available, measured from the landing threshold. A displaced threshold is one moved along the runway to clear an obstacle on the approach. It makes the LDA shorter than the runway. That is why you compare the chart's landing distance with the LDA, and not with the length in the runway table.

Let's work one: declared distances against the charts. A hypothetical runway has a TORA of 1,100 m, a TODA of 1,250 m, and an LDA of 950 m, because its threshold is displaced. Your workbook chart gives a take-off distance to 50 ft of 1,020 m, and a landing distance from 50 ft of 980 m, for today's weight, pressure height and temperature. Does each one fit? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 8]]
First, the take-off. Compare the chart's take-off distance with the TODA. 1,250 minus 1,020 is 230 m to spare.

Second, the landing. Compare the chart's landing distance with the LDA. 950 minus 980 is minus 30 m. The landing does not fit.

So the answer: 230 m to spare leaving, and 30 m short arriving. The runway is long enough to leave, but not to return to at this weight. The physical length would have hidden that, because the displaced threshold took the margin away. Lighten the aeroplane, wait for cooler air, or choose another runway or aerodrome. And apply any factor your POH, or the rules for your operation, require on top of the chart figures.

[[diagram:declared-distances]]
Picture that runway from above, with take-off and landing towards the right. The pavement, 1,100 m long, is the TORA. Beyond its far end, a clearway extends it to the TODA of 1,250 m, and a stopway gives the ASDA. At the near end, the threshold is displaced 150 m along the runway, so the LDA starts there, and is only 950 m. Take-off distance goes against the TODA, which includes the clearway. Landing distance goes against the LDA, which starts at the displaced threshold.

The surface and slope go into the same charts. Gravel and grass lengthen both distances. And a slope helps one way, and hurts the other. At Example Creek, runway {24|two four} runs downhill. It rises to the east, and you take off towards the west. So a take-off on {24|two four} is helped by the slope, and so is a landing on {06|zero six}.

[[diagram:runway-slope-take-off-and-landing]]
Picture two side views of a sloping runway. Taking off uphill, part of the thrust climbs the slope, so the run is longer. A 2 percent upslope can add around 10 percent to the take-off distance. Landing uphill, the slope helps you slow down, so the roll is shorter. Landing downhill, gravity fights the brakes. So with a light wind, land uphill and take off downhill.

Question. A runway has a displaced threshold at one end. Which declared distance does it shorten, and which chart figure do you compare it with?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: it shortens the LDA for landings in that direction, because the landing distance is measured from the threshold. Compare it with the landing distance from the chart. The take-off distances for that direction are normally not reduced, because you may usually still roll from the start of the pavement. The declared distances tell you for certain.

## Joining and operating: procedures and frequencies

Once the runway is chosen, ERSA tells you how the local traffic flows. The local traffic regulations and the remarks are where an aerodrome's departures from the standard live. And they are where the exam finds its questions.

The circuit is left-hand unless the entry says otherwise. So right-hand circuits are stated, for the runways that have them. At Example Creek, that is runway {24|two four}. Look also for a circuit height other than the standard. Noise abatement areas to avoid, such as a town, a homestead or a stud farm. A non-standard join. And gliding, parachuting or helicopter activity that will share the circuit with you.

The communications block gives the CTAF at a non-controlled aerodrome. At a controlled one, it gives the tower and ground frequencies, and the hours of the tower. Outside tower hours, a controlled aerodrome becomes a CTAF, and the entry says so. Note whether radio is mandatory, at a certified or registered aerodrome. The same block gives the AWIS or ATIS frequency and phone number. That is how you get the actual wind, QNH and temperature before you commit to a runway. And NAV COMM gives the area frequencies for the flight information service along the route.

Lighting is the last runway item, and by day it matters only at the edge of the day. If your arrival could slip towards last light, the lighting line tells you whether the runway is lit at all. Where it has pilot activated lighting, PAL, the line gives the frequency, and ERSA sets out the method of activation. An unlit runway gives you no margin at all.

Question. An entry lists right-hand circuits for one runway only. You are landing on the reciprocal end. Which way is the circuit?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: left-hand, the standard. A circuit direction remark applies to the runway it names. The other end of the same strip keeps the standard left-hand circuit, unless the entry says otherwise.

## Around and above it: airspace and time

The aerodrome sits in airspace, and ERSA, with the chart, tells you what that airspace is doing. A control zone or a restricted area near the aerodrome shows on the chart. The PRD section gives a restricted area's hours, its controlling authority, and its conditional status. {RA1|R A one} means plan through. {RA2|R A two} means plan to avoid. And {RA3|R A three} means no clearance.

[[diagram:airspace-active-check]]
Picture the pre-flight check as four steps, top to bottom. First, the chart, for the boundaries and limits. Second, the PRD section of ERSA, for the hours of activity and the controlling authority. Third, the NOTAMs, for activations, changes and temporary restricted areas. And fourth, if in doubt, call the controlling authority or ATC. An active restricted area needs a clearance. Without one, stay out.

Every time in that check is UTC. So is every time in the FAC entry: tower hours, fuel hours, and AWIS hours where they are limited. The PPL step is to put them all on one timeline with your own ETA, in WST. Add 8 hours, and watch for the day change when a UTC period crosses midnight.

[[diagram:utc-to-wst-activity]]
Picture two clock scales, one above the other, UTC on top and Western Standard Time below, lined up so that each moment sits directly above itself. An area listed active from {2200|twenty-two hundred} to {0600|oh six hundred} UTC shows as a bar on the top scale. Directly below, the same bar runs from {0600|oh six hundred} to {1400|fourteen hundred} WST. Add 8 hours.

Then the daylight. As a day VFR pilot, you must land before the end of daylight. So the last thing on the timeline is last light at the destination, not at your departure point. The PPL lesson on time works through the daylight graphs and their conversion. ERSA GEN carries sunrise and sunset tables for the major aerodromes, and the AIP daylight graphs cover everywhere else. Verify the current layout of both. Whatever source you use, convert it to WST, and compare it with your latest arrival, including the time you might spend on a diversion.

Question. The FAC entry for a controlled aerodrome gives tower hours that end at {1000|ten hundred} UTC. You plan to arrive at {1730|seventeen thirty} WST. What do you expect to find?
[[pause 5]]
The answer: {1000|ten hundred} UTC is {1800|eighteen hundred} WST. So the tower will still be open at {1730|seventeen thirty}, and you need a clearance to enter the control zone. Arrive after {1800|eighteen hundred} WST, and the entry's after-hours procedure applies, which is normally a CTAF.

## When it goes wrong: alternates and EMERG

The last step is the one you hope not to use. Before you leave, pick the nearest suitable alternate from FAC, and run the same checks on it in brief. Can you land there? Is the runway long enough? Is there fuel? If the forecast requires an alternate, this is the one you will nominate and fuel for, as the lesson on flight plan preparation explains.

The EMERG section, the pink pages, holds the emergency procedures you use in flight. Radio failure. The emergency transponder codes. Interception. And the search and rescue arrangements. GEN holds the SARTIME and flight notification procedures. And for flights into remote areas, it holds the survival equipment requirements.

## Bringing it together

It is a Saturday in autumn: Jandakot to Example Creek, with an ETA of {0300|oh three hundred} UTC. You work the seven steps.

Step one. PPR, so you phone the operator on Friday. AVGAS is available, so you can plan to refuel there.

Step two. The TAF wind is {280|two eight zero} degrees true at 15 kt. With the 1 degree west variation, that is {281|two eight one} magnetic. That gives 10 kt across runway {24|two four}, and 5 kt across runway {30|three zero}. Your POH's demonstrated crosswind is comfortably above 10 kt. And the landing chart says 800 m of gravel is tight at your weight. So you plan runway {24|two four}: 1,200 m, sealed, downhill to the west.

Step three. The remarks say right-hand circuits on {24|two four}, so you plan a right-hand downwind join, and note the CTAF. Step four. You will call the AWIS on the way for the actual wind and the QNH.

Step five. A restricted area beside your track is listed active from {0000|zero zero zero zero} to {0800|oh eight hundred} UTC, Monday to Friday. It is Saturday, so it is not active, unless a NOTAM says otherwise.

Step six. An ETA of {0300|oh three hundred} UTC is {1100|eleven hundred} WST, many hours before last light.

Step seven. You pick an alternate within 30 minutes, with a sealed runway and fuel.

Then the NOTAMs. They tell you the AWIS is unserviceable. So you will set QNH from the area forecast, and read the wind off the windsock. Seven steps, one book, and nothing about the arrival is a surprise.

Question. In that story, why did the NOTAM check come last, and what would you have missed without it?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because NOTAMs carry what changed after ERSA was published, so they override it. Without them, you would have planned on an AWIS that was not working. The same check catches a closed runway, an activated restricted area, or a fuel outage.

## Key points

Let's recap.

Where things are. FAC holds each aerodrome: the header, the runway table, communications, lighting, fuel, the local traffic regulations and the remarks. PRD holds the restricted areas. The runway distances supplement holds TORA, TODA, ASDA and LDA. GEN holds the tables, the codes and the SARTIME procedures. NAV COMM holds the frequencies. And EMERG holds the in-flight emergencies. Times are UTC, and WST is UTC plus 8. Lengths are in metres, and heights are in feet.

How to use it. Work every entry in the same order. Can I use it? Runways. Procedures. Services. Airspace. Time. Emergency. Then the NOTAMs. Convert a forecast wind from true to magnetic before comparing it with the runway designators. Compare the chart's take-off distance with the TODA, and its landing distance with the LDA. Note the circuit direction runway by runway. And put every UTC time, and last light, on one WST timeline with your ETA.

A final exam tip. ERSA times are UTC, runway dimensions are metres, and elevations are feet. The runway table and the local traffic regulations are where most ERSA questions come from. Read the whole runway line, including the slope direction. Then read the remarks for anything that applies to the runway in the question. A circuit direction remark applies only to the runway it names. And when a question gives declared distances, take-off goes against the TODA, and landing against the LDA.

That's the end of POPC 2.3.
