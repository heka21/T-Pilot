This is PHFC 2.7, Vision, spatial disorientation, illusions. By the end you will know how the eye works by day and by night, what it cannot do in flight, how to scan for traffic, and why your balance senses mislead you without a horizon. You will be able to work out the direction of any illusion from its mechanism. There are a few questions along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (PHFC 2.7)

The exam wants a long list from you, in two halves. The first half is vision. How the eye works by day and by night, and how to dark adapt. Its limits at night and in flight. Prescriptions and sunglasses. And what sets up a mid-air collision, and how to scan.

The second half is disorientation. What it is. The three balance systems, and why they are not reliable in flight. The illusions they produce, and how to overcome them. And the visual illusions, including those on the landing approach.

## Why this matters

You will look for a white Jabiru against a white cumulus over the Swan Valley. You will judge your height over the glare of a salt lake near Kalgoorlie. And you will come back into Jandakot at last light, with the ocean black behind you. In each case your senses give a confident answer that can be wrong.

Mid-air collisions, and loss of control after disorientation, are two classic ways competent pilots die. Both start with trusting a sense where it cannot work. This lesson gives every fact its mechanism, so you can work out the direction of any illusion for yourself.

## How the eye works by day and by night

The eye works like a camera. Light enters through the cornea, the clear curved window at the front, which does most of the focusing. It passes through the pupil, whose size the coloured iris sets: wide in the dark, small in bright light. The lens fine-tunes the focus by changing shape. And the image lands on the retina, the light-sensitive lining at the back, which sends it to the brain along the optic nerve.

The retina has two kinds of receptor, and much of this lesson follows from how differently they behave.

Cones need plenty of light. They see colour and fine detail. And they are packed into a small pit at the centre of the retina, called the fovea. Whatever you look straight at lands on the fovea. That is why only a small patch of your view is ever truly sharp.

Rods work in low light. They are far more sensitive than cones. But they see only shades of grey, they give poor detail, and they are very good at detecting movement. They lie around the periphery, and there are none in the fovea.

[[diagram:eye-anatomy-rods-and-cones]]
Picture a cross-section of the eye, with light coming in from the left, through the cornea, the pupil and the lens, onto the retina at the back. Right in the middle of the back is the fovea, all cones. The rods spread around it, out to the edges, and there are none in the fovea. So at night, an object you stare at disappears. Look 10 to 15 degrees to one side.

By day the cones do the work. At night there is too little light for them, so the rods take over. Vision becomes grey, blurred and peripheral.

Where the optic nerve leaves the eye, there are no receptors. That is the blind spot. With both eyes open, each eye covers the other's blind spot, and you never notice. But if a window post blocks one eye's view, an aircraft can sit unseen in the other eye's blind spot.

## Night vision and dark adaptation

Walk from a lit hangar onto a dark apron, and at first you see almost nothing. Half an hour later, you see the taxiway edges. That is dark adaptation: the receptors rebuilding the light-sensitive pigment that bright light bleaches. The cones adapt in about 7 to 10 minutes, and then stop improving. The rods take about 30 minutes to reach full sensitivity. And one glance at a bright light bleaches the pigment again.

So the three methods of dark adapting all keep bright light off the rods. First, avoid bright light for about 30 minutes before a night flight, and wear sunglasses in strong sun earlier that day. Second, keep the cockpit lighting dim. Red light preserves adaptation, because rods barely respond to it. But red markings on charts vanish under it, so dim white is the common compromise. And third, close one eye when a bright light is unavoidable, to keep that eye adapted.

Several things degrade night vision, mostly by starving the oxygen-hungry retina. Hypoxia affects night vision first, and from low altitude: noticeably from about 5,000 ft. Carbon monoxide from smoking does the same at sea level. Alcohol, fatigue, a shortage of vitamin A, from which the rod pigment is built, age, and glare all reduce it too.

Now the limitation the exam tests most. Because the fovea has no rods, there is a night blind spot in the centre of your vision. Stare at a faint light at night, and it fades, because its image sits on the one patch of retina that cannot see in the dark. The fix is off-centre viewing. Look about 10 to 15 degrees to one side, so the image falls on the rods. And keep the eyes moving too, because an image held still on the rods also fades within seconds.

Question. Why does a faint light disappear when you look straight at it at night?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: looking straight at it puts its image on the fovea, which has only cones and no rods. In the dark the cones do not work, so the centre of your vision is blind. Look 10 to 15 degrees to one side, so the image falls on the rods.

## What the eye cannot do in flight

There are six limits here. The first is spotting aircraft and wires. Only the fovea sees detail, and it covers a tiny part of the sky. Peripheral vision notices movement. But a small aircraft that is not moving across your view goes undetected. Each fixation needs about 1 to 2 seconds to focus and register. So a sweep of the eyes that never stops sees almost nothing. A light aeroplane head-on is a thin wing edge and a propeller disc. Powerlines and wires are thinner still, and usually seen only by their towers. That is why low flying along a wheatbelt valley is so dangerous.

The second is empty-field myopia. With nothing to focus on, the lens relaxes to a resting focus about 1 to 2 m ahead. Over a featureless blue sky, above a hazy cloud layer, or in haze that hides the horizon, you believe you are looking into the distance. But your eyes are focused just beyond the windscreen. The cure is to focus on something distant every few seconds: a wingtip, a cloud edge, or a ground feature. Then search the sky at that focus.

The third is glare: light much brighter than its surroundings. A low sun, sunlight off cloud or a salt lake, or light scattered by a dirty windscreen. It dazzles you and cuts contrast, so aircraft and terrain blend into the background. The classic case is flying west into Jandakot late on a summer afternoon, into the sun and the sea glare.

The fourth is colour vision. Aviation uses colour to carry meaning: navigation lights, tower light signals, PAPI lights, runway lighting and chart symbols. A colour defect may make you misread any of them, so colour vision is assessed at the medical.

The fifth is the common defects, mismatches between the eye's focusing power and its length. Myopia, or short sight: the image of a distant object forms in front of the retina, so distant objects blur, which is bad for spotting traffic. Hyperopia, or long sight: the image of a near object would form behind the retina, so near objects blur. Astigmatism: the cornea is curved more in one direction than the other, so the image is distorted. And presbyopia: the lens stiffens with age, and cannot round up for near focus. From about 40, charts and labels get harder to read, while distance vision may be fine.

And the sixth is flicker vertigo. Light flickering at about 4 to 20 times a second can cause dizziness, nausea and disorientation, and in rare cases seizures. It comes from the sun through a turning propeller, at low RPM, taxiing, or descending towards a low sun, and from a strobe reflecting off cloud. Break the flicker. Change RPM, look away or shade your eyes, and turn off the strobes in cloud.

## Glasses and sunglasses

Vision changes so slowly that you do not notice, and an out-of-date prescription means you see traffic later than you think. If the medical requires correction, keep the prescription current, and carry any spare pair it requires.

Sunglasses are flight equipment, and there are five things to look for. First, neutral grey, so that all colours darken evenly, and light signals and PAPIs keep their meaning. Second, good optical quality, free of distortion. Third, roughly 10 to 15 percent light transmission. That is enough to cut glare without hiding things in shadow. Fourth, not polarised. Polarising lenses can black out LCD displays. And they can remove the sun glint off another aircraft, which is often the first thing you see of it. And fifth, not photochromic, because the windscreen blocks the ultraviolet that darkens them.

Question. Why are polarised sunglasses a poor choice for flying, when they cut road glare so well?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: they cut glare by blocking light polarised in one plane. That also removes the glint of sun off another aircraft, and can black out LCD screens. And the glint is often your first sight of traffic.

## See-and-avoid and scanning

Mid-air collisions mostly happen in good weather, by day, near aerodromes. In the circuit, on joining, and on converging tracks near training areas. That is where traffic concentrates, and where pilots are busiest with other things.

The geometry is the key. Two aircraft on converging tracks that will collide keep a constant relative bearing. From your seat, the other aircraft stays fixed on the windscreen. It does not drift sideways, and it does not rise or fall. It does not move, so your movement-hunting peripheral vision ignores it. It grows slowly while it is distant, and then very fast in the last few seconds. Pilots describe it blossoming out of nowhere.

Small targets, windscreen posts and wings blocking the view, head-down workload, empty-field myopia and glare complete the trap. A study by the ATSB, the Australian Transport Safety Bureau, shows how unreliable unaided looking is.

[[diagram:constant-bearing-collision]]
Picture the two aircraft from above, on converging tracks, with their positions marked at equal moments in time. Join each pair with a line of sight, and every line is parallel to the one before. That is a constant relative bearing. From the cockpit, the other aircraft sits still on the windscreen and only grows, so peripheral vision never notices it.

Scanning technique works with the eye's limits. Divide the sky into sectors of about 10 to 15 degrees, and fix on each for about 1 second, systematically across the windscreen. Focus on something distant first. Scan most where traffic is likely. Clear blind spots by lifting a wing before turning, or by weaving gently in the climb. And use the radio and the clock code to build a picture of who is where.

[[diagram:clock-code-traffic]]
Picture an aeroplane heading {090|zero nine zero}, with a clock face laid flat around it, and twelve o'clock on the nose. Traffic at two o'clock is 60 degrees right of the nose, so it bears {150|one five zero}. Traffic at ten o'clock is 60 degrees left, and bears {030|zero three zero}. Twelve o'clock is always the nose, whatever the heading.

Question. An aeroplane sits motionless at your ten o'clock, slowly growing. Why is that more dangerous than one sliding across the windscreen?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: no movement against the windscreen means a constant relative bearing, the geometry of a collision course. And it gives your peripheral vision no movement to detect. One moving across the windscreen will pass ahead or behind.

## Disorientation and your three balance senses

Spatial disorientation is an incorrect perception of your attitude, motion or position relative to the earth: believing you are level when banked, or climbing when level.

Your sense of balance blends three systems. First, the eyes, by far the strongest. Give them a horizon, and you know your attitude instantly. Second, the vestibular apparatus of the inner ear, beside the cochlea from the lesson on the anatomy of the ear. And third, the proprioceptive senses. These are pressure and stretch receptors in the skin, muscles and joints, which feel how you are pressed into the seat. They are the seat of the pants.

[[diagram:ear-anatomy]]
Picture a cross-section of the ear. In the fluid-filled inner ear, beside the coiled cochlea, sit the semicircular canals and the otolith organs. Those two parts make up the vestibular apparatus, and their design explains every illusion that follows.

The semicircular canals are three fluid-filled loops at right angles, one per axis. In each is a small flap of hair cells, the cupula. When your head starts to rotate, the canal turns with it, but the fluid lags, by inertia, and bends the cupula. You feel rotation.

Two flaws follow. A rotation that starts very gently bends the cupula too little to register. So a slow roll goes unnoticed. And in a steady rotation, friction soon brings the fluid up to speed. The cupula straightens, and the sensation dies away in about 10 to 20 seconds. So the canals sense the start and stop of rotation, not a steady turn.

[[diagram:semicircular-canal-fluid-lag]]
Picture the horizontal canal seen from above, in three moments of a turn to the right. First, the rotation starts: the fluid lags and bends the cupula, and you feel the turn. Second, the turn is steady: after about 10 to 20 seconds the fluid has caught up, the cupula stands upright, and the sensation has gone. Third, the rotation stops: the fluid runs on and bends the cupula the other way, and you feel a turn in the opposite direction.

The otolith organs are pads of hair cells topped with tiny crystals. Gravity pulls the crystals and bends the hairs, and that tells you which way is down. A linear acceleration pulls them in exactly the same way. So the otoliths cannot tell gravity from acceleration. They report only the direction of the total force. The seat of the pants shares the flaw. In a balanced turn, the total force presses you straight down into the seat, at any bank angle.

So the inner ear and the seat of the pants are not reliable in flight. They miss slow changes. They stop sensing steady turns. And they confuse acceleration with tilt. With a horizon, the eyes overrule them. Without one, in cloud, on a dark night, in haze, or over featureless ground, they are all you have. And they mislead you with total conviction.

Question. In a steady, balanced turn in cloud, why do neither your inner ear nor your seat of the pants tell you that you are turning?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the canals sense only changes in rotation. In a steady turn the fluid catches up, and the signal fades within 10 to 20 seconds. And the total force in a balanced turn presses you straight into the seat. So the otoliths and the seat of the pants report level.

## The vestibular illusions

[[diagram:vestibular-illusions]]
Picture four small scenes: the leans, a dark take-off that feels like a pitch nose-up, the graveyard spiral, and a single light in the dark that seems to wander. All four feel completely real, and the message in each is the same: believe the attitude indicator.

The first is the leans. Without a horizon, the aeroplane rolls slowly into a bank, too gently for your canals, so you still feel level. You notice the attitude indicator, and roll briskly back to level. That roll does register, as a roll from level into a bank the other way. So now you feel banked the opposite way, although you are level. The urge is to lean, or roll, back towards the original bank. Hold the wings level on the attitude indicator, and let the sensation fade.

The second is linear acceleration, the somatogravic illusion. Accelerate forward, and the otolith crystals lag backwards, exactly as they would if your head tilted back. The otoliths combine gravity and the acceleration into one force, pointing down and back. And without a horizon, you perceive that as a nose-up pitch.

The perceived tilt follows that combined force. The tangent of the tilt equals the acceleration divided by g. So a forward acceleration of a quarter of g feels like about 14 degrees nose-up.

The danger is any strong acceleration near the ground without a horizon: a take-off over unlit country, or a go-around at night, with full power and flap coming up. The pilot feels a steep climb, pushes forward, and flies a serviceable aeroplane into the ground. Deceleration does the reverse. It feels like nose-down, and the pilot pulls up towards the stall.

[[diagram:somatogravic-resultant-vector]]
Picture two arrows at the pilot's head on a dark take-off. One points straight down: gravity. The other points backwards: the lag of the crystals. The otoliths feel only their sum, an arrow tilted backwards, and the brain takes that as down.

The third is unperceived pitch, roll and yaw. Any change below the canals' threshold goes unnoticed. A gentle wing drop while you refold a chart, a slow nose-down drift while you retune the radio, and the aeroplane eases into a descending turn you do not feel. Without a horizon, this is how a spiral dive begins. The lesson on stalling, spinning and spiral dives picks that up.

The fourth is angular acceleration, the Coriolis illusion. In a prolonged turn, the yaw canal has adapted. Now move your head sharply, down to the fuel selector, or round to the back seat. That swings canals into and out of the plane of the turn, so fluid starts and stops in several at once. The result is a violent sensation of tumbling, about an axis you are not rotating on. Avoid sudden head movements in turns, and in cloud.

The fifth is the graveyard spin. In a spin of several turns, the canals adapt, and the rotation seems to slow. On recovery, the rotation stops. The fluid runs on, and bends the cupula the other way. So you feel a spin in the opposite direction. Without visual references, a pilot who applies rudder against this false spin re-enters the original one.

The sixth is the graveyard spiral. The same adaptation in a prolonged turn makes the wings feel level. The aeroplane, still banked, loses height. The pilot sees the altimeter unwinding, and pulls back. But with the wings banked, back pressure tightens the turn. The descent steepens, the speed builds, and the spiral winds tighter. And if the pilot does roll level, the canals report a turn the other way, and the pilot rolls back in.

And the seventh is autokinesis. Stare at a single light against darkness, a star, or a lone farmhouse light in the wheatbelt, and after several seconds it seems to move. The eye's small involuntary movements are normally judged against the surrounding scene. With nothing else in view, the brain reads them as movement of the light. Pilots have chased stars, thinking they were aircraft. Do not fixate. Keep the eyes moving, and check the light against a fixed reference, such as the window frame.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set: the direction. In the leans, you feel banked opposite to the bank you just corrected. In the somatogravic illusion, forward acceleration feels nose-up, and the fatal response is pushing forward. Work each one out from the mechanism, and the direction is never in doubt.

Question. Why is a go-around at night more dangerous for the somatogravic illusion than the approach was?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: on the go-around, the aeroplane accelerates strongly, close to the ground. The otoliths cannot separate that acceleration from gravity, so it feels like a steep nose-up pitch. And pushing forward to lower it flies you towards the ground.

## When illusions strike and how to beat them

Illusions occur in three situations. First, when outside visual cues are poor or absent: cloud, a dark night, haze, smoke, dust, water, or featureless terrain. Second, when the cues are misleading, such as a sloping cloud bank. And third, during accelerations, prolonged turns and head movements, the moments when the inner ear is at its worst.

The defining experience is a conflict between the instruments and your senses. The attitude indicator says wings level, and your whole body says banked left. The instruments rest on gyroscopes that do not adapt, do not lag, and do not confuse acceleration with tilt. Your senses are doing what they were built for, in an environment they were not built for. Disorientation does not feel like confusion. It feels like certainty, and that is what makes it dangerous.

You are more susceptible when fatigued, after alcohol or with a hangover, when hypoxic, on medication, stressed or overloaded, unwell, or out of instrument practice. Each reduces your ability to keep cross-checking against an insistent feeling.

To overcome illusions, do four things. First, believe the instruments. Fly the attitude indicator, cross-check the others, and let the feeling fade. Second, keep your instrument skills current, so the scan is your default under stress. Third, avoid the situations. No cloud or dark nights without the training and the rating, and turn back early from deteriorating visibility. And fourth, minimise head movements, and do not stare at single lights.

Question. In thickening bushfire haze, the attitude indicator shows 20 degrees of right bank, while you feel level. What do you trust, and why?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the attitude indicator. Your canals do not register a slow roll, and the seat of the pants reads a balanced bank as level. So feeling level proves nothing. Roll level on the instrument, accept the false sensation that follows, and turn towards clearer conditions.

## Visual illusions

The eyes can be fooled too. Not by failing, but by being shown a scene that looks like something it is not.

Start with false horizons. The brain accepts the first line that looks like a horizon. A sloping cloud bank, a sloping ridge, or at night a line of coastal or road lights can be taken as level, and you bank or pitch to match it. Offshore from Perth at night, the dark sea and sky can merge, so that boat lights look like stars. Cross-check the attitude indicator whenever the horizon is uncertain.

Next, depth perception. You judge height and distance from texture, and from familiar objects: fences, trees, paddocks. Over water, snow, desert and salt lakes there is no texture. And you usually believe you are higher than you are. Lake Lefroy near Kambalda, or a glassy sea off Rottnest, gives no cue at all.

Haze, smoke and dust cut contrast, so objects look further away. You tend to fly lower, to reach a runway that seems distant. And entering fog on an approach can feel like the nose pitching up, so you steepen the approach. Use the altimeter and a planned descent. Over featureless surfaces, use a shoreline or a road as a reference.

Then windscreens. Rain on the windscreen bends the light, and can make you feel higher than you are, so you fly a lower approach. A scratched, dirty or crazed screen scatters light into glare, and hides small targets. And a curved screen distorts at its edges.

Now landing illusions. On approach, you judge your angle by the shape the runway makes in the windscreen. Your brain has learned the picture of a correct approach to your usual runway. Change the runway, and it misreads the new picture as a change of angle.

The pattern is simple. Whatever makes you feel high makes you fly too low. Whatever makes you feel low makes you fly too high.

[[diagram:visual-illusions-panels]]
Picture six small panels in two rows. Top left, the black hole: you feel high, fly too low, and land short. Next, a narrow or long runway looks like a normal one seen from higher up: you feel high, and fly too low. A wide runway: you feel low, and fly too high. Bottom row, an upsloping runway, its far end higher than the near end, looks steeper: you feel high, and fly too low. A downsloping runway looks flattened: you feel low, and fly too high. And a sloping cloud top, a false horizon you bank to match. Sloping approach terrain works the same way: ground rising towards the threshold makes you feel high, and fly low. The fix in every case is the PAPI, or a planned descent profile.

The deadliest is the black hole approach. At night, over dark terrain or water, with nothing lit between you and the runway, the runway lights are all you can see. With no ground texture to give height, your brain judges the approach by the runway's shape, and tends to fly a path that keeps that shape constant. That path is not a straight slope. It is a curve that sags below the glidepath, low and shallow, and meets the ground short of the threshold. And you feel high all the way down.

In WA, it is a night approach to a lit country strip surrounded by dark bush or wheatbelt, or to a coastal runway from over the sea. Approaches over water by day give a milder version.

[[diagram:black-hole-approach-profile]]
Picture a side view, with the lit runway on the right. The normal glidepath is a straight line down to the threshold. The black hole path sags below it, and meets the dark scrub short of the runway.

The countermeasure for all of these is to fly the numbers, not the picture. Use the PAPI, where there is one. Plan a descent profile of height against distance, and check it on the altimeter. And look up the runway's width and slope in ERSA, the En Route Supplement, beforehand.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Every illusion that makes you feel high leads to an approach that is too low, and lands short. That means the black hole, the narrow or long runway, and the upslope. The distractors swap the direction. So ask yourself: do I feel high, or low? The error is the opposite.

Last, relative motion. Sit in a stationary aeroplane while the one beside you rolls forward, and you feel yourself rolling back. The brain takes movement of a large part of the scene as your own. Check a fixed reference before you act.

## Bringing it together

A winter return to Jandakot from Northam, running late. Westbound over the scarp, into a low sun and coastal haze, you fight glare and empty-field myopia. A Warrior on a converging track sits fixed at your one o'clock. You find it only because you scan in 10 degree steps, through a clean screen, in grey, non-polarised glasses.

By the circuit the sun is down, the sea is black, and the city lights make a tilted false horizon. On final, the dark ground short of the runway is a small black hole. So you fly the PAPI and the planned heights, not the picture.

Then a kangaroo forces a go-around. Full power pushes you into the seat, and it feels like a steep climb. You hold the climb attitude on the instrument, because you know why.

## Key points

Let's recap. Cones, in the fovea, give colour and detail by day. Rods, in the periphery and none in the fovea, see in dim light and detect movement. Cones adapt in 7 to 10 minutes, rods in about 30, and one bright light undoes it. Hypoxia from about 5,000 ft, carbon monoxide, alcohol, fatigue, age and glare degrade night vision. At night, look 10 to 15 degrees off-centre, and keep the eyes moving.

In flight, fixations take 1 to 2 seconds, and wires and head-on aircraft are hard to see. Empty fields relax your focus to 1 to 2 m. Myopia blurs distance, hyperopia blurs near, astigmatism distorts, and presbyopia comes from about 40. Flicker at 4 to 20 per second causes flicker vertigo. Keep prescriptions current. Sunglasses: neutral grey, 10 to 15 percent transmission, not polarised.

Mid-airs happen in good weather, by day, near aerodromes, and a constant bearing means collision. Scan in 10 to 15 degree sectors, about 1 second each.

Disorientation is an incorrect perception of attitude, motion or position relative to the earth. The eyes dominate the inner ear and the seat of the pants. The canals miss slow changes, and adapt in 10 to 20 seconds. The otoliths and the seat of the pants cannot tell acceleration from gravity.

The leans feel banked the opposite way. Acceleration feels nose-up. Unperceived changes lead to a spiral. The graveyard spin and spiral come from adaptation, then a false opposite sensation. Coriolis comes from a head movement in a turn. And a light you stare at seems to move. Fatigue, alcohol, hypoxia, drugs and stress make all of it worse. Believe the instruments.

Sloping cloud makes false horizons. Water, salt lakes and haze defeat depth judgement. Rain on the screen makes you feel high. Narrow, long or upsloping runways, and the black hole, make you fly low. Wide or downsloping runways make you fly high.

A final exam tip. Have the figures by heart: rods 30 minutes, cones 7 to 10, off-centre 10 to 15 degrees, scan sectors 10 to 15 degrees for about 1 second, canals 10 to 20 seconds, flicker 4 to 20 per second. For an illusion, reason from the mechanism. Feeling high makes you fly low, and the black hole lands you short. When your senses disagree with the attitude indicator, believe the instruments.
