This is PHFC 2.5, Basic knowledge of the anatomy of the ear. By the end you will know which part of the ear hears and which part balances, why ears block on the descent and not on the climb, and what noise does to your hearing, your understanding of radio calls, and your fatigue. There is a calculation to try in your head and a few questions, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (PHFC 2.5)

The exam wants three things from you. First, outline how the ear works, for hearing and for balance. Second, explain what the eustachian tube is for, and what changes of atmospheric or cabin pressure do to the ear. And third, state the effects of noise: on your hearing, in the short term and the long term, on how well you understand speech, and on fatigue.

## Why this matters

Your ears do two jobs in the cockpit, and you rely on both on every flight. The first job is hearing. The tower at Perth giving you a clearance through the Class C step. Another aircraft calling turning base at Serpentine. A change in the engine note that tells you something is wrong.

The second job is balance. The organs that tell you which way is up, and whether you are turning, sit in the same small space as the hearing organ. Their limitations are the source of the illusions in the lesson on vision, spatial disorientation and illusions.

The ear is also the part of your body most affected by flying itself. It feels every change of pressure on the climb and the descent. And it takes hours of engine noise, at a level that slowly destroys hearing. The exam asks which part does what, why ears block on descent, and what noise does in the short and the long term.

## How the ear hears

Sound is a pressure wave in the air. The air is squeezed and stretched in rapid alternation, hundreds or thousands of times a second. The number of squeezes per second is the frequency, measured in hertz, and it is what you hear as pitch. The size of the pressure change is the intensity, which you hear as loudness.

The ear's job is to turn those tiny pressure waves in the air into nerve signals. It does it in three stages, in three parts: the outer ear, the middle ear and the inner ear.

[[diagram:ear-anatomy]]
Picture a cross-section of the ear, with the outside world on the left and the skull on the right. On the left is the outer ear: the ear flap, and the canal running inwards to the eardrum. In the middle is the middle ear, a small air-filled space holding three tiny bones, with a tube running down from it to the throat. That is the eustachian tube. On the right, buried in bone, is the fluid-filled inner ear. It holds a coiled snail shell, the cochlea, for hearing. Above the cochlea sit three loops at right angles, the semicircular canals, and next to them the otolith organs, for balance.

Now the three parts in turn. The outer ear is the part you can see, the ear flap, and the ear canal that runs inwards to the eardrum. The eardrum is a thin, tight membrane across the end of the canal. The flap and the canal collect sound, and channel it to the eardrum, which vibrates in step with the pressure waves.

The middle ear is a small air-filled cavity behind the eardrum. Across it runs a chain of three tiny bones, the ossicles. First the hammer, attached to the eardrum. Then the anvil. Then the stirrup, whose footplate sits in a small window into the inner ear. The chain turns the eardrum's vibration into mechanical movement, and passes it on.

The chain also solves a problem. The inner ear is filled with fluid. And sound travelling from air into fluid is mostly reflected. It is the same reason you hear very little of a conversation on the shore when your head is under water. The ossicles collect the force from the large eardrum, and deliver it onto the much smaller window of the inner ear. That concentrates it, so the vibration gets into the fluid.

For that chain to work, the eardrum must be free to vibrate. And that needs the same air pressure on both sides of it. Hold that thought. It is the whole reason for the eustachian tube.

The inner ear is a set of fluid-filled passages in the bone of the skull. The hearing part is the cochlea, a coiled tube shaped like a snail shell. When the stirrup pushes on the fluid, a wave runs along the cochlea. Lining it are rows of fine hair cells. Each one is tuned, by its position along the coil, to a particular range of frequencies. High frequencies are near the entrance. Low frequencies are further in. When the wave bends a hair cell, it fires. The signals travel along the auditory nerve to the brain, which hears them as sound.

Two facts about the hair cells matter for the rest of this lesson. They are delicate, and loud sound damages them. And they do not grow back. A hair cell that dies is gone for good, along with your ability to hear the frequencies it handled.

Question. Why does the middle ear need the same air pressure on both sides of the eardrum?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the eardrum has to vibrate freely, to pass sound to the ossicles. If the pressure is higher on one side, the drum is pushed in or out, and stretched tight. So it vibrates poorly. Hearing is muffled, and if the difference is large, it hurts.

## How the ear balances

The inner ear holds a second set of organs, the vestibular apparatus, which senses movement of the head. It shares the fluid, the bone and the nerve supply with the cochlea. That is why some ear problems bring vertigo as well as deafness. The vestibular apparatus has two parts.

The first part is the semicircular canals. They are three fluid-filled loops, set at right angles to each other, like the three axes of an aeroplane. Between them, they sense angular acceleration, in roll, pitch and yaw.

When your head starts to rotate, the bony loop turns with it. But the fluid inside lags behind, because of its inertia. It is like the water in a glass, which stays still when you start to turn the glass. The lagging fluid bends a small gelatinous flap with hair cells in it. That tells the brain you are rotating. If the rotation carries on steadily, friction brings the fluid up to speed, the flap straightens, and the signal stops. So the canals sense only changes in rotation. They do not sense steady turning.

The second part is the otolith organs. They are two small patches of hair cells, covered by a jelly layer with tiny crystals in it. The crystals make the layer heavier than the fluid around it. When you tilt your head, gravity pulls the weighted layer sideways and bends the hairs. When you accelerate forwards, the layer lags behind and bends them the same way. So the otoliths sense linear acceleration and gravity, the direction of down. And they cannot tell the difference between a tilt and an acceleration.

Those are the two limitations. Canals that stop signalling in a steady turn. And otoliths that confuse acceleration with tilt. They are the source of the balance illusions in the lesson on vision, spatial disorientation and illusions. The leans, the graveyard spiral, and the false climb on a dark take-off.

[[diagram:vestibular-illusions]]
Picture four small scenes. In the first, the leans. After a slow roll you did not notice, and a quick correction, you feel banked the opposite way while you are level. In the second, a dark take-off. The forward acceleration feels like a nose-up pitch, and pushing forward flies you into the ground. In the third, the graveyard spiral. The canals have adapted to a long turn, the wings feel level, and pulling back only tightens the spiral. And in the fourth, a single light in the darkness that seems to wander. The message across all four: they feel completely real, so believe the attitude indicator.

So, to sum up the three parts. The outer ear, the ear flap and canal ending at the eardrum, collects sound and channels it to the eardrum. The middle ear, an air-filled cavity with the hammer, anvil and stirrup, turns the eardrum's vibration into mechanical movement, and passes it to the inner ear. And the inner ear, filled with fluid, holds the cochlea for hearing and the vestibular apparatus for balance. It converts vibration into nerve signals, and senses head movement.

Question. Which part of the ear senses a roll into a turn, and why does the sensation fade if the turn continues?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the semicircular canals, which sense angular acceleration. In a steady turn, the fluid catches up with the canal walls, the sensing flap straightens, and the signal stops. So you feel level, although you are still turning.

## The eustachian tube and pressure changes

The middle ear is an air-filled box, sealed on the outside by the eardrum. If it were sealed completely, every change of outside pressure would push the eardrum in or out. Hearing would suffer with every change of height or weather.

The eustachian tube is the vent. It runs from the middle ear down to the back of the throat. Its purpose is to equalise the pressure in the middle ear with the outside air, so the eardrum can vibrate freely. It also lets the middle ear drain into the throat.

The tube does not behave the same way in both directions. And that one fact explains everything the exam asks about it. Its upper part, near the ear, is held open by bone. Its lower end, in the throat, is soft tissue that lies closed, like a slit. It opens only when muscles pull it apart, as you swallow or yawn. So it works like a one-way valve. It is easy to push air out through. It is hard to draw air in through.

On the climb, the outside pressure falls. The air in the middle ear is now at a higher pressure than outside, and it pushes outwards, on the eardrum and down the tube. Air at higher pressure, pushing on a slit-like valve from the inside, forces it open. A small puff escapes, the pressure equalises, and you feel the familiar pop. That happens every so often, all the way up, without you doing anything. It is rarely a problem.

On the descent, the outside pressure rises. The air in the middle ear is now at a lower pressure than outside. The eardrum is pushed inwards. And the higher pressure in the throat squeezes the soft end of the tube shut. Air will only go up the tube into the middle ear if you open it actively, by swallowing, yawning or moving the jaw. Do that often, a little at a time, and the ear equalises comfortably. Leave it too long, and the pressure difference holds the tube closed so firmly that swallowing no longer opens it.

[[diagram:ear-pressure-equalisation]]
Picture two cross-sections of the ear, side by side. On the left, the climb: the air in the middle ear expands, and vents easily down the tube to the throat. On the right, the descent: the outside pressure pushes the eardrum in, and the air has to get back up the tube. The soft end of the tube stays closed. Swollen by a cold, it may not open at all. The fix is written underneath: climb back, equalise, and descend more slowly.

If the lining of the tube is swollen from a cold or hay fever, it may not open at all. That is an ear block. The injury it causes is called otic barotrauma. It brings pain that can be severe, muffled hearing, sometimes vertigo, and in severe cases a ruptured eardrum. The physics behind this, Boyle's law and the shrinking of the middle-ear air on descent, is in the lesson on atmospheric pressure changes.

Now how to equalise on the descent. There are four things to do.

First, swallow, yawn, or move the jaw from side to side. Each one opens the soft end of the tube for a moment.

Second, the Valsalva manoeuvre. Pinch the nose, close the mouth, and gently blow, to push air up the tubes into the middle ears. Gently, because a hard blow against a blocked tube can damage the ear.

Third, descend at a moderate rate, and equalise often, before discomfort builds, not after.

And fourth, wake sleeping passengers before the descent. People swallow far less when they are asleep. So a sleeping passenger's ears do not equalise, and they wake up in pain.

If a block does occur, climb back to the level where it was comfortable. That lowers the outside pressure, reduces the difference across the eardrum, and lets the tube open. Equalise, then descend again, more slowly. A decongestant may help in an emergency. But it is not a reason to fly with a cold. It can wear off during the flight, and leave you blocked on the way down.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. The option says: ear trouble happens on the climb, when the air expands. But the climb is the easy direction. Air pushes out through the tube by itself. Ear block happens on the descent, because air must be drawn back in through a valve that tends to stay shut. The fix is to climb again, and then descend slowly while equalising. Not to keep descending through the pain.

Question. Why does the eustachian tube let air out easily, but not back in?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: its lower end, in the throat, is soft tissue that lies closed. Higher pressure in the middle ear, on the climb, pushes it open from the inside. Higher pressure in the throat, on the descent, squeezes it shut. So it opens only when you swallow, yawn, or use the Valsalva manoeuvre.

## Noise and what it does

Start with how noise is measured. The ear handles an enormous range of sound intensity, from a whisper to a jet engine. To make that range manageable, sound level is measured in decibels, on a logarithmic scale. That means each step on the scale multiplies the energy, rather than adding to it.

The figure to know is that every increase of 3 decibels doubles the sound energy. So 88 decibels carries twice the energy of 85. And 94 decibels carries eight times as much. You will often see the letter A after a decibel figure. It means the measurement is weighted to match the ear's sensitivity, which is greatest at the middle frequencies of speech.

The cabin of a typical piston single is roughly 85 to 100 decibels, and more near the engine. Most of that comes from the propeller, the engine exhaust and the airflow. And it is there for the whole flight.

Now hearing loss, in the short term and the long term. Short-term loss is a temporary threshold shift. Your threshold is the quietest sound you can hear, and after noise exposure it rises for a while. The hair cells have been overworked, and they need time to recover. After a noisy flight, sounds seem muffled, and you may have ringing in the ears, called tinnitus. Hearing usually recovers within hours to a day. A temporary shift is a warning. The exposure was enough to damage the ear if it is repeated.

Long-term loss is noise-induced hearing loss, and it is permanent. Repeated exposure kills hair cells in the cochlea, and they do not grow back. It usually appears first at the high frequencies, around 4,000 hertz. That is partly because the hair cells for those frequencies sit near the entrance of the cochlea, where the energy of every sound arrives first. It is painless and gradual, so you often do not notice it until it is advanced. Ageing adds its own high-frequency loss, called presbycusis. The two together can leave an older pilot with a serious handicap on the radio.

A commonly used guide is that exposure above about 85 decibels, averaged over 8 hours, risks permanent damage. And each 3 decibel increase halves the safe exposure time. That halving follows directly from the decibel scale. 3 decibels more is twice the energy, so you reach the same total dose in half the time.

[[diagram:noise-levels-and-hearing-damage]]
Picture a line falling from left to right, with sound level along the bottom and safe time going up the page. At 85 decibels on the left, the line is at 8 hours. Each 3 decibels to the right halves it. About 4 hours at 88. 2 at 91. 1 at 94. 30 minutes at 97. And only 15 minutes at 100 decibels, the top of a typical piston cabin. A headset that lowers the level at your ear moves you back to the left, and buys you time.

Let's work one. Use the guide: 8 hours at 85 decibels, halving for every 3 decibels more. On a cross-country to Geraldton, the level at your ear is 94 decibels. What is your safe exposure time? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
Step one, the excess over 85. 94 minus 85 is 9 decibels. Step two, the number of halvings. 9 divided by 3 is 3. Step three, the safe time. 8 hours, halved 3 times, which is 8 divided by 8. That is 1 hour.

So a two-hour leg at that level is twice the daily dose, by this guide. A headset that brings the level at your ear down to 85 decibels restores the full 8 hours. That is why hearing protection is part of the aeroplane, not an optional extra.

Here is one for you to try. A pilot flies with an old headset whose ear seals have hardened, and the level at his ear is 97 decibels. By the guide, what is his safe exposure time?
[[pause 6]]
The answer: the excess is 97 minus 85, which is 12 decibels. 12 divided by 3 is 4 halvings. 8 hours halved 4 times is 8 divided by 16, which is 0.5 hours. So about 30 minutes. Twelve decibels sounds like a small change. But it is four doublings of the energy, so sixteen times the dose rate. How headsets cut the noise, and how to choose and look after hearing protection, is the subject of the lesson on hearing protection.

Next, speech intelligibility. Understanding speech depends heavily on the consonants, sounds like {s, t, f, th and k|ess, tee, ef, tee aitch and kay}. They are what distinguish one word from another, and they are carried by the higher frequencies. Vowels are louder and lower.

Background noise masks speech. The steady engine and airflow noise drowns the quiet, high-pitched consonants first. So you hear that someone spoke, but not exactly what they said. Five and nine start to blur. So do climb and descend to. So do two and to.

[[diagram:speech-masking-by-cabin-noise]]
Picture a sketch of sound level going up the page, against pitch running from low on the left to high on the right. A broad band of engine and airflow noise covers the whole width. On the left, the vowels stick up above the noise, loud and low, so you hear that someone spoke. On the right, the consonants sit low and quiet, buried inside the noise band, so similar words blur. And right in that same high band, around 4,000 hertz, is where noise-induced hearing loss begins.

In the cockpit, that means radio calls are misheard, read-backs are wrong, and passenger briefings are missed. The brain fills the gaps with what it expects to hear. That is a well-known route to taking a clearance meant for someone else. High-frequency hearing loss makes it much worse, because it removes exactly the frequencies the consonants live in.

Turning the volume up helps less than you would expect, because it amplifies the noise as well. Reducing the noise at the ear with a good headset improves understanding directly.

And last, fatigue. Continuous noise, and the vibration that comes with it, is an environmental stressor. The brain keeps working to filter it out, and to pick speech out of it. That effort is not free. Over a long flight, noise increases fatigue, irritability and workload. And it reduces concentration, and the ability to monitor. You arrive at the end of a three-hour cross-country more tired than the flying alone explains. And that is the moment you most need to be sharp: joining the circuit at an unfamiliar aerodrome.

Question. Why does high-frequency hearing loss make radio calls harder to understand, even when they are loud enough to hear?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the consonants that tell words apart are carried by the higher frequencies. Lose those, and you hear that someone is speaking, but you cannot tell similar words apart. And cockpit noise masks the same frequencies further.

## Bringing it together

You are flying a PPL cross-country from Jandakot to Albany, about two and a half hours, in a training aeroplane. The passenger headset has a cracked ear seal. Your passenger has a mild cold, and falls asleep over the wheatbelt. The flight touches all three things the exam wants.

The cracked seal lets cabin noise in at near full level. So your passenger is getting far more than a safe daily dose. They will step out with muffled hearing and ringing ears: a temporary threshold shift, and a warning. And on your own side, hours of noise will leave you more tired than you expect on arrival.

Before the descent into Albany, you wake the passenger. A sleeping passenger with a cold will not swallow, and the descent is when the eustachian tubes must let air back in. You descend at a moderate rate. You ask them to swallow and yawn often. And you are ready to level off, or climb back, if they report ear pain.

And when the Albany CTAF, the common traffic advisory frequency, gets busy, you will understand the calls better if the noise at your own ear is low. Because it is the consonants that the noise takes first.

## Key points

Let's recap.

How the ear hears. The outer ear collects sound onto the eardrum. The three ossicles of the air-filled middle ear carry the vibration into the fluid-filled inner ear. There the hair cells of the cochlea turn it into nerve signals, and those hair cells do not grow back.

How the ear balances. The vestibular apparatus does it. The semicircular canals sense angular acceleration. The otoliths sense linear acceleration and gravity.

The eustachian tube connects the middle ear to the throat. It equalises the pressure across the eardrum, and it drains the middle ear. It vents easily on the climb, but it must be opened actively on the descent. A cold-swollen tube causes ear block and barotrauma on descent. Swallow, yawn or Valsalva often. If it blocks, climb back, equalise, and descend more slowly.

Noise. In the short term, it causes a temporary threshold shift, and tinnitus. In the long term, it causes noise-induced hearing loss: permanent, painless, and first around 4,000 hertz. The guide is 85 decibels over 8 hours, halving the time for every 3 decibels. Noise masks the high-frequency consonants of speech, so calls are misheard. And it adds to fatigue on long flights.

A final exam tip. Know which part does what. The cochlea hears. The semicircular canals sense angular acceleration, in roll, pitch and yaw. The otoliths sense linear acceleration and gravity. The eustachian tube equalises middle-ear pressure. And ear block happens on descent, because the tube lets air out easily but not back in. For noise, long-term exposure gives permanent high-frequency loss, gradual and painless. The figures to have are 85 decibels over 8 hours, and 3 decibels halving the safe time.

That's the end of PHFC 2.5.
