This is PHFC 2.2, Health and fitness. By the end you will know why and how pilots are medically examined, how the body absorbs and clears alcohol, what a hangover really is, what drugs and common medicines do to a pilot, and how long to wait after giving blood. There are calculations and questions along the way, with a pause to try each one before the answer.

## What the exam expects (PHFC 2.2)

The exam wants four things from you. First, medical standards: why you are examined and how often, how to find a DAME, what the examination involves, and CASA's role, including the principle that only conditions that are a hazard to flight safety disqualify you. Second, alcohol: how the body absorbs it and gets rid of it, what a hangover is, how long recovery takes, what alcohol and drugs do to your judgement and senses, and how to recognise and correct dehydration. Third, drugs: why drug abuse is a behavioural problem, what a psychoactive substance is, the effects of cannabis, amphetamines and opiates, and the side effects of common medicines. And fourth, blood donation: its effect on flying, and the recommended wait before your next flight.

## Why this matters

Most student pilots in Perth are of an age when Friday nights are social. And most flying schools run lessons early on Saturday mornings. That collision is where this lesson lives.

How long does a night out actually stay in your body? Why is the legal 8 hours only a floor? And why is the morning-after headache a reason not to fly, even when a breath test would read zero? The same physiology explains why the hay fever tablet from the chemist at Jandakot's shopping centre can be a bigger hazard than the hay fever.

The exam tests this subtopic in three ways. With a short calculation: when is the alcohol gone? With facts about elimination and hangovers that have a tempting wrong answer. And with recall of drug side effects, and of the blood donation wait.

## Medical standards: why, how often and who

An aeroplane is unforgiving of a pilot who suddenly stops functioning. A medical condition can do that with no warning: a heart rhythm disturbance, an epileptic seizure, a diabetic low, a stroke. Other conditions do their damage quietly: failing eyesight, hearing loss, a mental illness that distorts judgement.

The aviation medical examination exists to find both kinds before they show up in the air. And to manage them, so that a pilot who has one can keep flying safely where possible.

Part 67 of the regulations sets the classes of medical certificate. There are three to know. Class 1 is for commercial and air transport pilots. Class 2 is for private pilots. And a Basic Class 2, and other recreational-level standards, may be usable for some recreational licence operations, with restrictions.

Picture three cards side by side. On the left, the Class 2. It is issued by a DAME, with CASA, and it is the standard private pilot medical, with no extra operational limits. In the middle, the Basic Class 2. Any general practitioner, a GP, can issue it, to the commercial driver standard. It limits you to private, day VFR flights below 10,000 ft, in piston aircraft up to 8,618 kg, with no more than 5 passengers. On the right, the RAMPC, the recreational aviation medical practitioner's certificate. Any GP can issue it, to the private driver standard. It limits you to single-engine piston aircraft up to 1,500 kg, day VFR, below 10,000 ft, private operations, no aerobatics, and no more than 1 passenger. The pattern: the easier the medical is to get, the tighter the limits.

Next, how often. A certificate is valid for a set period. That period depends on the class, and on your age. And it gets shorter as you get older, because the risk of the sudden incapacitation conditions rises with each decade, above all heart disease. Check Part 67 for the current periods, rather than relying on memory. The certificates a recreational licence holder can use are set out in the lesson on licence privileges and limitations.

Then, finding a DAME. A designated aviation medical examiner is a doctor CASA has approved to conduct aviation medicals. You find one with the medical examiner search on the CASA website. Perth has several, and some regional centres have one.

The process has four steps. First, apply through CASA's online medical records system, and fill in your medical history honestly. Second, book with a DAME, and bring your history and any specialist reports for conditions you have declared. Third, attend the examination. It covers eyesight, for distance, near and colour. Hearing. Blood pressure. Heart and lungs. A urine test. An ECG, a heart rhythm recording, at set ages. And a general check. Fourth, the DAME either issues the certificate, or, for anything outside straightforward limits, refers your case to CASA.

Finally, CASA's role. It sets the standards, and it decides the difficult cases. Its guiding principle, and the one the exam asks about, is that only conditions that present a hazard to flight safety are disqualifying. Having a condition is not the question. The question is whether it could make you incapacitated or impaired in flight.

Many conditions are acceptable with treatment, with monitoring, or with a condition on the certificate. That might be "must wear distance correction", or a requirement for regular specialist reports. Being honest on the form is how you get to that outcome. Hiding a condition is how you lose the certificate altogether.

Question. A pilot has well-controlled high blood pressure. Does that automatically disqualify her from holding a medical?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: no. CASA disqualifies only conditions that present a hazard to flight safety. A controlled condition is often acceptable, perhaps with monitoring, or a condition on the certificate. The DAME and CASA assess it case by case.

## How the body handles alcohol

Start with absorption. When you drink, alcohol passes from the stomach into the small intestine. The small intestine has a huge surface area, and absorbs it rapidly into the bloodstream. A little is absorbed through the stomach wall. Most of a drink reaches the blood within about 30 to 90 minutes.

Food in the stomach holds the drink there longer. So absorption is slower, and the peak is lower and later. But food does not reduce the total. All of it gets into the blood eventually. Once in the blood, alcohol spreads into all the body's water. That includes the brain, and, importantly for a pilot, the fluid of the inner ear.

Now elimination. About 90 percent or more of the alcohol is broken down by the liver. The rest leaves in breath, urine and sweat, which is why a breath test works.

The liver's enzymes work at a fixed maximum pace. So the liver removes a fixed amount per hour, however much is waiting. That is roughly one standard drink, 10 grams of alcohol, per hour.

Think of a single checkout operator in a supermarket. A long queue does not make her scan faster. And nothing you do to the queue changes her pace.

That is why nothing speeds it up. Coffee makes you more awake, but it does not remove alcohol. A cold shower, exercise, fresh air and sleep do not touch the liver's rate. Only time works.

Several factors affect elimination, and the blood alcohol you reach. Body size and composition: a smaller person, or one with more body fat and less body water, reaches a higher concentration from the same drink. Sex: on average, women reach a higher level from the same amount. Liver health. Regular drinking habits. Food. And some medications. These change the starting blood level more than the hourly rate. So two pilots who drink the same thing do not have the same morning. The smaller one starts higher, and takes longer to reach zero.

Now the rules. Do not consume alcohol within 8 hours before acting as a crew member. Do not fly while affected by it. And the prescribed blood alcohol limit for safety-sensitive aviation activities is {0.02|zero point zero two} grams per 100 millilitres of blood. Verify the current text of the regulations. The rules themselves are covered in the lesson on conditions of flight.

Put the rule beside the one-drink-an-hour rate, and you can see at once that 8 hours is a minimum, not a guarantee. Anyone who drank more than about eight drinks is still carrying alcohol when the 8 hours expire.

Now try one yourself. At a wedding at a Swan Valley winery, you have ten standard drinks, the last at midnight. Your lesson is booked for {0900|nine a m}. Which limit decides when you can fly, and can you make the lesson?
[[pause 6]]
The answer. Liver clearance is 10 drinks times 1 hour, which is 10 hours. So blood alcohol is near zero at about {1000|ten a m}. The 8-hour rule gives {0800|eight a m}. The later time governs. So you can fly at {1000|ten a m} at the very earliest.

You cannot make a {0900|nine a m} lesson, legally or safely, even though the 8 hours have passed. And after that many drinks, a hangover will follow. So rebook for the next day.

Question. A friend says a strong coffee and a run will get him clear for his morning flight. Why is he wrong?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the liver removes alcohol at a fixed rate, about one standard drink an hour. Neither caffeine nor exercise changes that rate. Coffee only makes him feel more alert, while the alcohol is still there.

## What alcohol and drugs do to a pilot

Alcohol is a depressant. It slows the brain's processing. Many illicit drugs act on the same faculties, through different chemistry. The syllabus names six things they degrade, and each matters in a particular part of a flight.

First, judgement and risk perception. You accept weather, fuel margins or a tight gap you would refuse sober. And the first faculty to fail is the one that would warn you.

Second, comprehension. Radio calls, clearances and instructions take longer to understand, and are misunderstood more often.

Third, attention to detail. Checklist items are skipped, a fuel cap is left off, a wrong frequency is dialled.

Fourth, the senses. Vision suffers, especially night vision, and the eyes' ability to track. And the balance system in the inner ear is disturbed.

Fifth, coordination. Smooth, accurate control inputs, such as a flare or a crosswind correction, become coarse.

And sixth, reaction time. It lengthens, so the response to an engine failure, or to a gust in the flare, comes late.

Alcohol also makes the body's cells less able to use oxygen. That is histotoxic hypoxia. So a pilot with alcohol in the blood suffers the effects of altitude sooner. The types of hypoxia are in the lesson on hypoxia.

## The hangover

A hangover is the collection of after-effects of heavy drinking. They appear as blood alcohol falls, and they persist after it has reached zero. They include headache, nausea, fatigue, thirst, poor concentration, sensitivity to light and noise, and disturbed balance. A hangover is not simply "still being drunk". Four things cause it at once.

The first is dehydration. Alcohol makes the kidneys pass more water than you drink. So you wake dried out, with the headache and poor concentration that dehydration brings on its own.

The second is toxic breakdown products. As the liver processes alcohol, it makes intermediate chemicals. They cause the nausea, and the general poisoned feeling.

The third is low blood sugar and poor sleep. Alcohol disturbs the body's control of blood sugar, and destroys the quality of sleep. So you are fatigued, even after a full night in bed.

And the fourth is the inner ear. Alcohol diffuses into the fluid and structures of the balance organs, and changes how they respond to gravity. It goes in slowly, and comes out slowly. So it can leave positional vertigo, a spinning sensation when you tilt your head, for many hours after blood alcohol has returned to zero.

The effect on flying follows from those causes. Slower and poorer thinking. Reduced attention. Fatigue. And a balance system giving false signals. In an aeroplane, that means a much higher risk of disorientation in turbulence or poor visibility. You'll find more in the lesson on vision, spatial disorientation and illusions.

Now, the hangover and blood alcohol. The two are related, but separate. Blood alcohol measures what is still in your blood. The hangover measures the damage the alcohol has done. A pilot with a hangover can have zero blood alcohol, and still be seriously impaired. A breath test that reads zero proves only that you are legal, not that you are fit.

[[diagram:blood-alcohol-curve-and-hangover]]
Picture a graph of the wedding example: ten standard drinks, the last at midnight. Time runs left to right. The blood alcohol line rises while you drink, and keeps rising for 30 to 90 minutes after the last drink, as the drinks are absorbed. Then it falls in a straight line, at about one standard drink an hour, reaching about zero around {1000|ten a m}. A marker at {0800|eight a m} shows the 8-hour rule expiring, with alcohol still in the blood. And a second band, the hangover, starts as the blood alcohol falls, and carries on to the right long after the line reaches zero, for 24 hours or more after heavy drinking.

Then the recovery period. The more you drank, the higher your peak blood alcohol, and the longer both the elimination and the hangover last. The recovery is not finished when the alcohol is gone. After heavy drinking, allow 24 hours or more before flying.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. "The morning after, her blood alcohol is zero, so she is fit to fly." Zero blood alcohol is not fitness. The hangover, the dehydration and the inner ear effects continue after the alcohol has gone, and they impair her just as surely. The exam also likes "coffee speeds elimination". It does not.

Question. Why can a pilot feel the room spin when he turns his head the morning after a heavy night, even with no alcohol left in his blood?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: alcohol soaked slowly into the inner ear's balance organs, and it leaves them slowly too. So they still respond wrongly to gravity after the blood is clear. The result is positional vertigo, which in flight is a disorientation risk.

## Dehydration and rehydration

Dehydration matters here for two reasons. Both a hangover and a hot day cause it. And its symptoms look like other problems.

The symptoms of dehydration are thirst, headache, a dry mouth, dark urine, fatigue, dizziness, especially on standing, and poor concentration. Thirst is a late signal. By the time you are thirsty, you are already short of water. Dark urine is the most reliable early sign you can check yourself.

The best fluids for rehydration are the ones the body absorbs fastest, and keeps. There are two.

The first is water. The gut absorbs it quickly, and it has nothing in it that makes the kidneys pass more.

The second is diluted electrolyte drinks, also called oral rehydration solutions. They also replace the salts lost in sweat. A little salt and sugar, in the right proportion, actually helps the gut take up water. That is why they are used to treat gastro and heat exhaustion.

There are also things to avoid. Alcohol, and large amounts of caffeine, make the kidneys pass more water, so you lose more than you drink. And very sugary drinks, such as undiluted soft drink and cordial, rehydrate slowly. Concentrated sugar slows the emptying of the stomach, and draws water into the gut rather than out of it.

On a February day at Northam, a litre of water before you walk out to the aeroplane does more for your landing than any amount of cola.

## Drugs

First, why drug abuse is a behavioural problem. Drug abuse is using a substance in a way that harms safety or health. The key word for the exam is behavioural. It describes what you do, not what has happened to your body. So it is independent of two things.

It is independent of dependence, or addiction, which is a state in which the body or mind needs the drug. A pilot who has never been addicted to anything abuses a drug the first time he smokes cannabis the night before a lesson.

And it is independent of frequent use. One use before a flight is abuse. The frequency does not matter.

The point is that you cannot excuse a dangerous choice by saying "I'm not an addict", or "I hardly ever do it". The hazard is the drug in your system when you fly.

Next, psychoactive substances. A psychoactive substance is any substance that changes how the brain works: mood, perception, thinking or behaviour. They come in two groups. Illicit substances are illegal: cannabis, ecstasy, heroin. Non-illicit substances are legal, and many are in every home: alcohol, caffeine, nicotine, codeine, sedatives, and some cold and allergy medicines. Legal does not mean harmless in a cockpit.

Their adverse effects on a pilot fall into a short list. Impaired perception. Slower information processing. Longer reaction time. Poor judgement. And after-effects, such as a come-down, drowsiness, or a hangover-like state, that outlast the obvious effects.

Now three drug groups the exam names. The pattern is that each one impairs for longer than the user feels it.

Cannabis, also called marijuana or ganja, distorts the perception of time and distance. It causes poor short-term memory. And the user cannot divide attention between tasks. It acts on the parts of the brain that handle memory and the sense of time. And it is stored in body fat, and released slowly. That is why its effects on complex tasks such as flying last a day or more after the user feels normal. Impairment lasts 24 hours or more, and residues persist for weeks.

Amphetamine-type drugs, including ecstasy, are stimulants. They make the user feel alert and capable, with false confidence and risk-taking, while degrading judgement. Then, when they wear off, the body pays back the borrowed energy, with exhaustion and depression on the come-down. The effects last hours, followed by a day or more of fatigue.

Opiates, such as codeine and heroin, are depressants of the brain and of breathing. They cause drowsiness, slowed reactions and poor concentration, for many hours. Codeine is an opiate. That is why the over-the-counter and prescription painkillers that contain it are on the do-not-fly list.

Question. A student says he smoked cannabis on Friday night, but feels fine for his Saturday lunchtime lesson. Why is that not good enough?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: cannabis impairs the perception of time and distance, short-term memory and divided attention for 24 hours or more, well after the user feels normal. Feeling fine is not evidence of being unimpaired. And using it before flying is drug abuse, whatever the frequency.

Finally, over-the-counter and prescription medicines. The medicines that cause most trouble for pilots are not illicit at all. The syllabus names six, with their side effects.

First, aspirin. It causes stomach irritation, and a tendency to bleed more easily. Also, the pain or fever it masks may be a reason not to fly.

Second, antihistamines, for hay fever and allergies. They cause drowsiness and slowed reactions. Many older types are strongly sedating, and the effect can last into the next day.

Third, nasal decongestants. They cause a rapid heart rate and anxiety. And they can wear off in flight, with rebound congestion leaving the ears or sinuses blocked just as you descend.

Fourth, amphetamines, prescribed for some conditions. They bring overconfidence, then fatigue.

Fifth, tranquillisers and sedatives, which cause drowsiness lasting into the next day.

And sixth, antibiotics. Here the infection being treated matters most. Some antibiotics also cause nausea, or sensitivity to light.

The rule from the lesson on basic health applies to all of them. Check with a DAME before flying on any medication, and try it on the ground first.

## Blood donation

Giving a unit of whole blood removes about 470 mL. That has two effects on a pilot.

First, there is less blood volume to keep the brain supplied. So standing up quickly, pulling g, or a hot cockpit can make you faint.

Second, you have fewer red cells, and so less oxygen-carrying capacity. That is a mild anaemic hypoxia, which lowers your tolerance to altitude. The body replaces the fluid quickly, but the red cells much more slowly.

[[diagram:hypoxia-types-and-causes]]
Picture the chain that carries oxygen from the air to the body's cells, running left to right, with four links. The first link is the lungs, where altitude can starve you: hypoxic hypoxia. The second is the blood's carrying capacity, broken by carbon monoxide, smoking, blood donation or anaemia: anaemic hypoxia. The third is circulation, broken by positive g, cold or shock: stagnant hypoxia. And the last is the cells' use of oxygen, broken by alcohol, some drugs or cyanide: histotoxic hypoxia. Blood donation breaks the second link. The air and the lungs are fine, but the blood cannot carry enough oxygen.

The commonly recommended wait is at least 24 hours before flying. Recovery varies between individuals, with fitness, body size and hydration. A small person who gives the same 470 mL as a large one has lost a bigger share of their blood. Plasma or platelet donation differs from whole blood, because most of the red cells are returned to you. Ask your DAME about your own case. And tell the donor centre that you are a pilot.

## Bringing it together

It is Friday evening. You have a dual navigation lesson from Jandakot at {1000|ten a m} tomorrow, and a friend's birthday dinner tonight. You strained your back moving furniture on Thursday. So before dinner, you take two of the codeine-containing painkillers the pharmacist recommended. At dinner you have four standard drinks, the last at {2230|ten thirty p m}.

Work it through. Four drinks clear in about four hours. So the alcohol is near zero by about {0230|two thirty a m}. The 8-hour rule gives {0630|six thirty a m}. On alcohol alone, you are legal for a {1000|ten a m} departure. Four drinks over an evening, with food, should not leave a serious hangover. But you will be a little dry, so drink water at breakfast.

The painkiller is the bigger question. Codeine is an opiate. It causes drowsiness, and slows reactions, for many hours. Alcohol adds to that sedation. And you have not cleared it with a DAME.

Then there is the back strain itself. It raises the question from the lesson on basic health: could you apply full rudder and brake in an emergency?

The honest answer is to call your instructor in the morning. The alcohol is not what stops this lesson. The medicine and the injury might.

## Key points

Let's recap, in four parts.

Medicals catch conditions that could incapacitate or impair you. Their validity shortens with age. You find a DAME through the CASA website. The DAME examines you, and either issues the certificate or refers your case to CASA. And CASA disqualifies only conditions that are a hazard to flight safety.

Alcohol is absorbed in 30 to 90 minutes. The liver removes it at a fixed rate, about one standard drink an hour, and nothing speeds it up. The 8-hour rule and the {0.02|zero point zero two} limit are minimums. A hangover is impairment that outlasts the alcohol. It comes from dehydration, toxic by-products, poor sleep, and alcohol in the inner ear. The heavier the drinking, the longer the recovery: 24 hours or more. Rehydrate with water, or diluted electrolyte drinks. Not alcohol, caffeine or sugary drinks.

Drug abuse is a behaviour, independent of addiction or frequency. Psychoactive substances, legal or not, slow perception, processing and reaction. Cannabis impairs for 24 hours or more. Amphetamines bring a come-down. And opiates sedate. Watch the side effects of antihistamines, decongestants, aspirin, sedatives and antibiotics.

And a blood donation reduces blood volume and oxygen-carrying capacity. Wait at least 24 hours, longer for some people, and ask your DAME.

A final exam tip. Have these by heart. About one standard drink per hour. 8 hours bottle to throttle, as a minimum. {0.02|zero point zero two} grams per 100 millilitres. 24 hours or more after heavy drinking. And 24 hours after giving blood. In an alcohol calculation, work out both the liver time and the 8-hour time, and take the later. The distractors are predictable. Coffee or exercise speeds elimination: false. Zero blood alcohol means fit: false. Drug abuse requires addiction or frequent use: false.

That's the end of PHFC 2.2.
