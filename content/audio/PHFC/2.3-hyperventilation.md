This is PHFC 2.3, Hyperventilation. By the end you will know what over-breathing does to the body, how to recognise it, how to tell it from hypoxia, and the simple things that stop it within a few minutes. There are a few questions along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (PHFC 2.3)

The exam wants two things from you. First, recognise hyperventilation from its symptoms, and say what to do to stop it. And second, define hyperventilation, and recall what causes it.

## Why this matters

Hyperventilation is the one physiological problem in this unit that you can give yourself on the ground. Or in the circuit at Jandakot at 1,000 ft, with nothing wrong with the aeroplane or the air. All it needs is anxiety. A first solo. A check flight. A moment of being unsure of your position over the wheatbelt. Or a passenger who is frightened of the bumps on a hot afternoon.

Its symptoms are frightening, and they make the anxiety worse. They also overlap with the symptoms of hypoxia, which is the far more dangerous condition at altitude. This lesson explains what over-breathing actually does to the body, how to recognise it, how to tell it from hypoxia, and the simple things that stop it within a few minutes.

The exam asks for four things. The definition. The causes. The symptoms that set it apart. And the correct action, including the case where you cannot tell which of the two conditions you have.

## What breathing is for

Every cell in your body burns fuel with oxygen, and produces carbon dioxide as waste. It is the same way the engine burns fuel with the oxygen in the air and produces exhaust. The blood carries oxygen from the lungs to the cells, and carries carbon dioxide back to the lungs. So each breath does two jobs at once. It brings in fresh oxygen, and it blows off carbon dioxide.

Here is the part most people do not expect. Your brain does not set your breathing rate mainly by measuring oxygen. It sets it mainly by measuring carbon dioxide, written CO2. Sensors in the brainstem watch the level of CO2 in the blood. More precisely, they watch the acidity it creates, because carbon dioxide dissolved in the blood forms a weak acid.

When CO2 rises, as it does when you exercise, the blood becomes slightly more acidic. The sensors detect it, and you breathe faster and deeper. When CO2 falls, the urge to breathe falls with it. Think of it as a thermostat. The setting it holds is a steady CO2 level.

At rest that thermostat gives you about 12 to 16 breaths a minute. That is exactly enough to remove the carbon dioxide your body is producing, and to keep the blood's acidity in its narrow normal range. So a modest level of carbon dioxide in the blood is not a waste product to be got rid of at all costs. It is one of the body's control signals, and several systems depend on it being where it should be.

Question. What does the brain mainly measure to decide how fast you breathe?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the level of carbon dioxide in the blood, through the acidity it creates. More CO2 makes you breathe faster and deeper. Less CO2 reduces the drive to breathe. Oxygen plays a much smaller part at normal altitudes.

## What hyperventilation is

Now breathe faster or deeper than your body needs, for a minute. You are still producing carbon dioxide at the resting rate. But you are blowing it off much faster. The CO2 level in the blood falls below normal, a state called hypocapnia. And with less carbonic acid, the blood becomes more alkaline.

That gives you the definition the exam wants. Hyperventilation is breathing at a rate or depth greater than the body needs, so that too much carbon dioxide is blown off, and the level of carbon dioxide in the blood falls. Hold on to that. The problem is not too much oxygen. The problem is too little carbon dioxide.

Three things follow from the low CO2, and between them they explain every symptom.

The first: the blood vessels of the brain constrict. Carbon dioxide is the main signal that keeps the brain's arteries open. When it falls, they narrow, and blood flow to the brain drops. Less blood means less oxygen delivered, however rich that blood is.

The second: haemoglobin holds on to its oxygen. In the lungs, the haemoglobin in the red cells picks oxygen up. In the tissues, it is supposed to let it go. Carbon dioxide and acidity are what tell haemoglobin to release it. They are highest in hard-working tissue, which is exactly where oxygen is needed. With low CO2 and alkaline blood, haemoglobin grips its oxygen more tightly, and releases less of it to the cells.

The third: nerves and muscles become irritable. Alkaline blood reduces the amount of free calcium available to the nerves. And nerves short of calcium fire on their own. That produces the tingling and the pins and needles. In more severe cases, it produces muscle spasms that cramp the hands.

Put the first two together, and you have the central paradox of hyperventilation. The brain is short of oxygen, even though you are breathing in more oxygen than ever. The blood leaving the lungs is fully loaded. It is just not getting to the brain in enough quantity. And when it gets there, it will not let go.

Question. A hyperventilating pilot is breathing in more air than normal. Why is the brain short of oxygen?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: over-breathing lowers the blood's carbon dioxide. Low CO2 narrows the brain's blood vessels, so less blood reaches it. And it makes haemoglobin hold on to its oxygen, so less is released to the brain cells.

## What causes it

The causes have one thing in common. Something makes you breathe more than your CO2 thermostat would ask for. There are six of them.

The first is anxiety, fear, stress or excitement. In light aircraft this is the most common cause, by a long way. The body's alarm response prepares you for physical action, and one of the things it does is speed up your breathing. In a cockpit there is no physical action, so the extra breathing is not needed, and the CO2 falls. Typical triggers are a first solo, a check flight or flight test, getting lost, deteriorating weather, an unusual noise from the engine, or a passenger's fear of turbulence.

The second is pain or shock, which drive the breathing rate up in the same way. The third is motion sickness, which often comes with rapid, shallow breathing. The fourth is heat and vibration, which act as background stressors and raise the breathing rate.

The fifth is hypoxia. At altitude the body detects a shortage of oxygen, and it responds by breathing harder. That extra breathing blows off CO2. So a hypoxic pilot can also be hyperventilating. This is the link that makes the two conditions so easy to confuse.

And the sixth is deliberate deep breathing. For example, taking a few deep breaths to calm down. Or over-breathing under heavy workload without realising it.

[[diagram:stress-performance-curve]]
The anxiety cause explains why hyperventilation clusters around particular moments in training. Picture a curve shaped like an upside-down U, with arousal running from left to right and performance going up the page. On the far left, too little arousal gives boredom and inattention. At the peak in the middle, a moderate level of arousal sharpens you, and performance is at its best. Past the peak, on the right, the curve falls away into anxiety, narrowed attention, poor memory and panic. The same alarm response that narrows your attention also speeds your breathing. So hyperventilation is one of the physical signs that you have gone over the top of the curve.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. The option says: hyperventilation is caused by altitude. Altitude can contribute, through hypoxia. But the commonest cause is anxiety. And hyperventilation happens at any altitude, including on the ground. If an exam option says it occurs only above 10,000 ft, it is wrong.

## Recognising it

Hyperventilation usually develops over a minute or so, and it feeds on itself. The symptoms are alarming. The alarm makes you more anxious. The anxiety makes you breathe harder. And the harder breathing deepens the symptoms. Knowing the symptoms in advance is what breaks that circle, because a symptom you recognise is far less frightening than one you do not.

[[diagram:hyperventilation-vicious-circle]]
Picture that as a loop, running clockwise. At the top, anxiety: stress, fear, a first solo. On the right, over-breathing, faster or deeper than you need. At the bottom, the CO2 falls: the brain's vessels narrow, and haemoglobin grips its oxygen. On the left, the symptoms: dizziness, tingling and spasm, which are alarming, and lead back up to more anxiety at the top. Off to the right is a panel headed: break the circle. Recognise it. Slow down, to one breath every 4 to 5 seconds. Talk out loud. And at altitude, use oxygen or descend. At the bottom of that panel is the one thing not to do: take deep breaths.

Each symptom traces back to those three effects of low CO2. Let's go through them.

Dizziness and light-headedness come from the reduced blood flow to the brain. Tingling, or pins and needles, in the fingers, the toes, and around the lips, comes from irritable nerves. You may feel hot or cold, and you may sweat.

Vision can be disturbed: blurred vision, or tunnel vision. That is because the eye and the visual parts of the brain are very sensitive to their oxygen supply. Then there is a rapid heart rate, and a sense of anxiety or panic, which is part cause and part effect.

Next, muscle spasms or stiffness, especially in the hands, which may cramp into a claw. This is the most distinctive sign, and the one that hypoxia does not usually produce.

And eventually, unconsciousness. Once you are unconscious, breathing returns to normal under the brainstem's control. The CO2 rises, and you recover. On the ground, that is a harmless faint. In an aeroplane, the unconsciousness may already have been fatal.

So the classic picture is tingling plus dizziness, in someone who is anxious and breathing quickly. But notice that rapid breathing is not always obvious from the inside. Over-breathing can be deep rather than fast. And many people do not realise they are doing it until someone points it out.

Question. Which symptom points most strongly to hyperventilation rather than hypoxia?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: muscle spasm or stiffness, especially in the hands, together with tingling around the lips and fingers, in an anxious, fast-breathing person. The low CO2 and alkaline blood make the nerves irritable. Hypoxia does not usually do that.

## Hyperventilation or hypoxia?

Hypoxia is a shortage of oxygen reaching the body's tissues. At altitude, it is caused by the falling pressure of the air. The air is still about 21 percent oxygen. But there is less of everything in each breath, so less oxygen is pushed into the blood. Hypoxia has a lesson of its own. It matters here because its symptoms overlap with those of hyperventilation, and because one can cause the other.

[[diagram:atmosphere-pressure-oxygen-vs-altitude]]
Picture a graph with altitude running along the bottom and pressure going up the side. The upper curve is the total pressure of the air. It starts high on the left, at {1013 hPa|ten thirteen hectopascals} at sea level, and falls to the right: about 700 at 10,000 feet, and about 500, half, at 18,000 feet. The lower curve is the oxygen. Oxygen stays about 21 percent of the air, so its share of the pressure falls in step with the total: about 213 hectopascals at sea level, but only about 147 at 10,000 feet. The percentage does not change. The amount of oxygen in each breath does.

Now set the two conditions side by side. Start with the cause. Hyperventilation is too little CO2, from over-breathing. Hypoxia is too little oxygen reaching the tissues.

Next, altitude. Hyperventilation can happen at any altitude, including on the ground. Hypoxia happens mainly at altitude, although carbon monoxide, alcohol or blood loss can also cause it.

Then onset. Hyperventilation comes on fairly rapidly, often with anxiety. Hypoxia is gradual and insidious, and often goes unnoticed.

Then the typical signs. Hyperventilation brings tingling, muscle spasm, dizziness and rapid breathing. Hypoxia brings euphoria, poor judgement, and cyanosis, which is blue lips and nail beds.

And breathing. In hyperventilation it is fast or deep. In hypoxia it may be normal until late.

[[diagram:hyperventilation-vs-hypoxia]]
Picture that as two columns, with hyperventilation on the left and hypoxia on the right, and the comparison in rows: what it is, where, onset, and the tell-tale signs. Below those rows, a band across both columns holds the symptoms they share: dizziness, tingling and visual disturbance. Under that, the fix for each: slow the breathing on the left, oxygen or descent on the right. And across the bottom, the rule for when you are unsure up high: oxygen first.

Put the comparison together, and the difference in character stands out. Hyperventilation feels bad. You know something is wrong, and you are frightened by it. Hypoxia often feels good. It brings a false sense of well-being, called euphoria, and it erodes the judgement you would need to notice it. That is why hypoxia is the more dangerous of the two. And it is why the rule for the uncertain case is built the way it is.

So here is the rule when you cannot tell. If you are above about 10,000 ft, with oxygen available, treat it as hypoxia first. Go on to oxygen, or descend, and then slow your breathing.

The reasoning is an asymmetry of consequences. Suppose it is hypoxia, and you treat it as hyperventilation. You spend your few minutes of useful consciousness breathing slowly in thin air, and you may not get another chance. Now suppose it is hyperventilation, and you give oxygen. You have done no harm. And the act of controlling your breathing on the mask treats the hyperventilation too. Without oxygen on board, descent is the treatment for hypoxia, and it does no harm to a hyperventilating pilot either.

[[diagram:hypoxia-time-of-useful-consciousness]]
Picture a set of bars, one for each altitude, with the altitudes down the left side and the time of useful consciousness running along the bottom. That is how long a seated, resting person can still act sensibly once the oxygen supply fails. At 18,000 feet, at the top, the bar is long: 20 to 30 minutes. At 25,000 it has shrunk to 3 to 5 minutes. At 35,000 it is 30 to 60 seconds. Exercise, smoking, fatigue and a rapid decompression all shorten it. The point to take away: the higher you are, the more a wrong guess costs.

At the levels where most RPL and PPL flying happens, well below 10,000 ft, hypoxia in a healthy pilot is unlikely. Anxiety is the likely cause of tingling and dizziness. But keep the other causes of hypoxia in mind at any height. Carbon monoxide from a leaking cabin heater produces dizziness and headache at 2,000 ft as readily as at 9,000. You'll find that in the lesson on toxic hazards.

Question. Why is giving oxygen the right first action when you cannot tell hypoxia from hyperventilation at altitude?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: oxygen cures hypoxia, and does no harm in hyperventilation. Treating real hypoxia as hyperventilation wastes the short time of useful consciousness. So the asymmetry favours oxygen, or descent, first. Then slow your breathing.

## Stopping it

The cause is breathing too much, so the cure is to breathe less, until the carbon dioxide builds back up. There are five things to do, and each does that in a different way.

First, recognise it. Tingling plus dizziness while anxious is the classic picture. Name it to yourself. This is over-breathing, and it is not dangerous if I control it. Naming it breaks the panic that drives the cycle.

Second, deliberately slow your breathing rate and depth back to normal. That is about one breath every 4 to 5 seconds, or 12 to 15 breaths a minute, back in the normal resting range. Breathe in gently through the nose, pause, and breathe out slowly. Your body keeps producing CO2 at the normal rate, and now less is being blown off. So the level recovers within a few minutes, and the symptoms fade.

Third, talk out loud. Call out your checks, make a radio call, talk to your passenger. You cannot over-breathe while speaking, because speech forces slow, controlled breathing out. This is the most practical method in a cockpit. And it also gives your attention something useful to do.

Fourth, at altitude, use oxygen or descend, in case the cause is hypoxia.

And fifth, breathing into a paper bag, so that you re-breathe your own carbon dioxide. That works on the ground, but it is rarely practical in a cockpit. And it must never be used when hypoxia is possible, because it lowers the oxygen in each breath too.

Now two things not to do. Do not take deep breaths to calm down. It is the instinct, and it is exactly wrong. Deep breaths blow off more CO2, and make the symptoms worse. And do not ignore it. It can progress to unconsciousness, and a pilot who faints in the circuit does not wake up in time.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. One of the options says: take several deep breaths. It is there because it is what people say to someone who is anxious. For hyperventilation it is the wrong action. The correct answer slows the rate and depth of breathing.

Once you are breathing normally, deal with whatever caused the anxiety, so that it does not start again. If you are lost, run the lost procedure step by step, aloud. If it is a passenger, give them something to do. Holding the map, watching for traffic, or describing the view all keep them talking.

## Bringing it together

You are flying a PPL cross-country from Jandakot to Kalgoorlie at 9,500 ft, on a hot afternoon. In the right seat is a friend who has never been in a light aeroplane. Over Southern Cross, the thermals make it bumpy. Your friend goes quiet and grips the seat. Then she says her fingers are tingling, and she feels light-headed. You notice she is breathing fast.

You run the lesson over it. The most likely cause is hyperventilation, driven by anxiety. A nervous first-time passenger, bumpy air, rapid breathing, tingling and dizziness. All of it is on the hyperventilation side. But you are at 9,500 ft without oxygen. So mild hypoxia is possible, and it could be adding to the picture.

So you take the actions that treat both. You descend to a lower level, where the air is smoother and denser. You talk to her calmly, and you get her talking back: you ask her to read out the next town on the map. You coach her breathing, slow and gentle, about one breath every 4 or 5 seconds. Within a few minutes, the tingling should fade.

And if it does not, or if you start to feel the same symptoms yourself, you consider whether something else is at work. Carbon monoxide from the cabin heater, for example.

## Key points

Let's recap.

Recognise hyperventilation by dizziness, tingling in the fingers and around the lips, visual disturbance, a racing heart, and anxiety. The distinctive sign is muscle spasm in the hands. Untreated, it ends in unconsciousness.

Combat it by slowing the rate and depth of breathing, to one breath every 4 to 5 seconds, and by talking aloud. Never take deep breaths. If you are in doubt above about 10,000 ft, with oxygen available, treat it as hypoxia first. Oxygen or descent, then slow breathing.

Hyperventilation is breathing more than the body needs, so too much CO2 is blown off. Low CO2 narrows the brain's blood vessels, and makes haemoglobin hold on to its oxygen. So the brain goes short of oxygen, despite the extra breathing.

The causes: anxiety and stress above all. Then pain, motion sickness, heat and vibration, hypoxia, and deliberate deep breathing. And it can happen at any altitude.

A final exam tip. The definition is about carbon dioxide, not oxygen: too much CO2 blown off. The commonest cause is anxiety, and it can happen at any height, including on the ground. The correct action is to slow the breathing and talk aloud. And if the question puts you above about 10,000 ft with oxygen, unsure whether it is hypoxia or hyperventilation, the answer is oxygen first.
