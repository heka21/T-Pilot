This is PHFC 2.1, Basic health. By the end you will be able to say how everyday health, from a skipped breakfast to a head cold to a worry from home, affects the way you fly, what you must do before flying on any medicine, and what you are responsible for in keeping yourself fit. There are a few questions along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (PHFC 2.1)

The exam wants three things from you. First, how everyday health affects the way you fly. That covers diet and exercise, the risk factors for heart disease, colds and blocked sinuses, stomach upsets, headache and migraine, pregnancy, injuries, ageing, alcohol and smoking, giving blood, dehydration, and your emotional state. Second, that you must get clearance from a DAME, a designated aviation medical examiner, before flying on any medication. And third, what you, the pilot, are responsible for in keeping yourself medically fit to fly.

## Why this matters

A training aeroplane has one pilot, and nobody to take over. Picture a solo navigation exercise from Jandakot to Northam and back. Everything that happens depends on one brain, two eyes, two ears and four limbs, all working properly for two hours.

A head cold can take away a slice of that. So can a skipped breakfast on a 38 degree February morning, a night out, or a worry you brought from home. And the slices add up.

Your medical certificate says you met the standard on the day of the examination. It says nothing about how you are this morning. The exam tests this subtopic as judgement. Which factor causes which problem? When does ear pain strike? Who decides you are fit? And what must you do before taking a new medicine?

## The pilot as a system

Think of your body the way you think of the aeroplane. The aeroplane needs fuel, oil, a sound structure and working instruments. You need five things too. Fuel, which is food and water. Oxygen delivered to the brain. A sound structure, meaning no injury that stops you reaching the controls. Working sensors: your eyes, ears and balance. And a clear processor, a brain free of drugs, fatigue and distraction.

Every factor in this lesson attacks one of those. When you meet a new one, ask which part of the system it degrades, and whether it could fail suddenly. That turns a long list into a few ideas.

Two kinds of failure matter. The first is subtle impairment. It slowly degrades your performance without you noticing. Think of dehydration, a hangover, low blood sugar, or anxiety.

The second is sudden incapacitation. It takes you out of the loop in seconds. Think of a heart attack, a faint, a violent attack of gastro, or a migraine that blanks part of your vision.

A solo pilot can sometimes fly through subtle impairment, though worse than usual. But sudden incapacitation in a single-pilot aeroplane is usually fatal. Most of the medical standard exists to screen out that second kind.

Picture the pilot drawn as five systems, like the systems of an aeroplane, each with the health factors that attack it. Fuel is attacked by skipped meals, heavy meals, dehydration and food poisoning. Oxygen delivery is attacked by the heart risk factors, smoking and blood donation. Structure is attacked by injuries, pregnancy and ageing. The sensors are attacked by colds and hay fever blocking the ears and sinuses, migraine aura, and ageing eyes and ears. And the processor, the brain, is attacked by alcohol, medication, anxiety, depression, fears and headache. A few factors are marked as able to incapacitate you in seconds: a heart attack, food poisoning, a migraine, or a faint. Those are what the medical screens for.

## Fuel, fitness and water

Start with diet. Your brain runs almost entirely on glucose, delivered by the blood. And it has very little store of its own. Skip breakfast before an early lesson, and by mid-morning your blood sugar is falling. Concentration slips. You become irritable and shaky. And your judgement degrades in exactly the way that makes you least likely to notice.

A large, heavy meal has the opposite problem. Blood is diverted to digestion, and you feel drowsy. The fix is a balanced meal before you fly, and a snack and water in your flight bag. Then the fuel supply is steady through the flight.

Next, exercise. Regular, moderate exercise makes the heart and lungs more efficient. So a fit pilot copes better with fatigue, stress, heat, g, and the mild oxygen shortage of higher altitudes. Exercise also improves sleep. The one caution is timing. An exhausting session immediately before flying leaves you tired and dehydrated, which defeats the purpose.

Then dehydration. You lose water through sweat and breathing all the time. And you lose it much faster in a cockpit sitting on a hot apron, with the sun through the canopy. Even modest dehydration brings headache, fatigue, poor concentration and slower thinking. It also lowers your tolerance to g, because there is less blood volume to keep the brain supplied.

Thirst arrives late. By the time you feel thirsty, you are already short. So in a Western Australian summer, drink water before you go to the aeroplane, and carry it with you. The detail of symptoms, and the best fluids for rehydration, are in the lesson on health and fitness.

Question. Why does skipping breakfast before an early lesson degrade your flying, even though you feel only mildly hungry?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the brain runs on glucose from the blood, and stores very little. As blood sugar falls, concentration, mood and judgement decline. And the impairment includes your ability to notice that you are impaired.

## The heart: coronary risk factors

The arteries that feed the heart muscle itself are the coronary arteries. Over years, fatty deposits can narrow them. When one narrows or blocks suddenly, part of the heart muscle is starved of oxygen. The result can be chest pain, a heart attack, or a disturbed heart rhythm that can cause collapse within seconds.

For a pilot, this is the textbook sudden incapacitation. It is why the medical examination checks your blood pressure, and at set ages, an ECG, a recording of the heart's rhythm.

The coronary risk factors are the things that make that narrowing more likely. There are four.

First, smoking. It damages the lining of the arteries, and makes the blood more likely to clot.

Second, high cholesterol, which supplies the fatty deposits.

Third, obesity. It raises blood pressure and cholesterol, and makes diabetes more likely.

And fourth, hereditary factors: a family history of heart disease at a young age.

You cannot change your parents. But the other three are under your control, and they add together. A pilot who smokes, carries extra weight and has high cholesterol has a much higher risk than one with any single factor.

The exam asks you to recognise these as risk factors for incapacitation. Not as causes of immediate impairment. So the four again: smoking, cholesterol, obesity, and family history.

## Colds, hay fever and blocked sinuses

Your head contains air spaces. There is the middle ear, behind each eardrum. And there are the sinuses, in the bones of the face and forehead. Each one connects to the throat or nose through a narrow passage. That passage is lined with the same soft tissue as the inside of your nose. The middle ear vents through the eustachian tube. In flight, these passages let the air pressure inside equalise with the changing cabin pressure. The lessons on atmospheric pressure changes, and on the anatomy of the ear, explain that in full.

A cold, hay fever or a sinus infection makes that lining swell and fill with mucus. Now follow a flight.

On the climb, the air inside expands. It usually forces its way out, even through a narrowed passage.

On the descent, the outside pressure rises. Air has to get back in, through a passage that is now swollen shut. It cannot. So the higher outside pressure pushes on the eardrum, or on the sinus lining. The result is severe pain, muffled hearing, sometimes vertigo, and in the worst case a ruptured eardrum. That is an ear block, or a sinus block. And it happens at the end of the flight, when you are busiest.

[[diagram:ear-pressure-equalisation]]
Picture two cross-sections of the ear, side by side. On the left is the climb. The air in the middle ear expands, and vents easily down the eustachian tube to the throat. On the right is the descent. Outside pressure pushes the eardrum inwards, and air must get back up the tube. But the tube's soft lower end stays closed, and when a cold has swollen it, it may not open at all. That is the ear block. The fix shown with it: climb back up, equalise, and descend more slowly.

A cold brings more than blocked passages. A fever, and the general feeling of being unwell, slow your thinking. And the medicines people take for colds bring their own problems. A decongestant may shrink the lining enough to get you airborne, then wear off during the flight. That leaves you with a block on descent, and no remedy. Many cold and hay fever tablets also contain antihistamines, which cause drowsiness.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. It says the climb is when trapped air causes trouble, so a head cold hurts on the way up. Not for the ears and sinuses. Expanding air vents out easily. The trouble comes on descent, when air must get back in through a swollen passage. Gut and teeth problems are the ones that come on the climb.

## Gut, head and injuries

Three more factors, starting with the gut. Food poisoning and gastroenteritis cause cramps, vomiting and diarrhoea, with very little warning. And they dehydrate you quickly. A sudden attack of severe cramps in the circuit is a real incapacitation. You cannot fly an approach well while doubled over. Even after the worst is over, you remain weak and dehydrated. So do not fly until you have fully recovered, and are eating and drinking normally.

Next, headaches and migraines. An ordinary headache is a distraction that competes for your attention. And it is often a symptom of something else worth knowing about: dehydration, hunger, fatigue, a hangover, or the early stage of an illness.

A migraine is different in kind. Many migraine sufferers get an aura. That is a disturbance of vision, such as flashing zigzag lines, or a blank patch. It is followed by a severe headache, nausea, and sensitivity to light and noise. Flying through that is not possible safely. Do not fly with a migraine. And check any migraine medication with a DAME, because several cause drowsiness.

Then injuries. The question for any injury is whether you can still do everything the aeroplane might demand, including in an emergency. A sprained ankle may let you walk. But it may not let you apply full rudder in a crosswind, or stand on the brakes after an engine failure on take-off. A strapped wrist may not manage a firm pull on a go-around. Pain itself distracts. And painkillers strong enough to help may sedate you. If you could not handle an emergency with the injury you have today, you are not fit to fly today.

Question. A fellow student with a sprained right ankle says he can drive, so he can fly. What question should he ask himself instead?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: whether he could apply full rudder and maximum braking in an emergency, such as a crosswind landing, or an engine failure on the runway. Driving needs far less leg force than those. And any strong painkiller he is taking adds its own impairment.

## Pregnancy

Pregnancy changes the body steadily over nine months. The syllabus asks about two things: when to stop flying, and how it changes your fit in the cockpit.

Early in pregnancy, three things can affect a flight: nausea, fatigue, and a tendency to faint, because blood pressure is lower than usual.

Later, the growing abdomen changes the cockpit ergonomics. That means how far you can reach the controls. Whether the control column has full travel without striking you. And how the lap belt and harness sit. A lap belt must lie low across the hips, never across the abdomen. In a small cockpit, there may come a point where the belt cannot be worn properly, and full control movement is no longer possible.

Now the guidance, which you must confirm for yourself. Tell your DAME as soon as you know you are pregnant. Flying during an uncomplicated pregnancy may continue, with the DAME's advice. And the DAME will tell you when to stop. The exact point is set case by case, and the rules change, so check Part 67 of the regulations and current CASA guidance. Any complication is a reason to stop, and to seek advice at once.

## Ageing

Ageing brings slow, predictable changes. Reaction times lengthen a little. The lens of the eye stiffens, so close focus goes, usually in your forties. That is called presbyopia, and it makes charts and instruments blurry without reading glasses. Night vision deteriorates, because less light reaches the retina. Hearing loses its higher frequencies. And the risk of heart disease rises with each decade.

On the other side of the ledger, experience compensates for much of this. An older pilot often reads a situation earlier, and makes fewer of the decisions that need fast reactions to rescue.

The medical system deals with ageing in two ways. It examines older pilots more often. And it attaches conditions, such as "must wear corrective lenses". Your part is to keep those glasses with you, and to be honest about the changes you notice.

## Alcohol and smoking

Alcohol is a depressant of the brain. Even small amounts degrade judgement, coordination and reaction time. And the first faculty to go is the one that would tell you that you are impaired.

Alcohol also makes the body's cells less able to use the oxygen they receive. That is a form of oxygen starvation called histotoxic hypoxia. So a drinker reaches the effects of altitude sooner.

The rule is at least 8 hours, "bottle to throttle", and a blood alcohol limit of {0.02|zero point zero two} grams per 100 millilitres. Verify the current text of the regulations. And 8 hours is a minimum, not a guarantee. How the body absorbs and clears alcohol, and why a hangover is impairment in its own right, are taught in the lesson on health and fitness.

Now smoking. Smoking fills the blood with carbon monoxide from the burning tobacco. Carbon monoxide binds to haemoglobin, the oxygen-carrying protein in the red blood cells. It binds roughly two hundred times more strongly than oxygen does, and it does not let go quickly. The haemoglobin it occupies cannot carry oxygen.

A heavy smoker can have several percent of their haemoglobin blocked this way before the engine even starts. So their brain is as short of oxygen as a non-smoker's would be some thousands of feet higher. This is anaemic hypoxia. The air is fine. The blood cannot carry it. Night vision, which is very sensitive to oxygen, suffers first. And in the long term, smoking is also the leading coronary risk factor.

[[diagram:hypoxia-types-and-causes]]
Picture a chain of links, running from the air on the left to the body's cells on the right. There are four places it can break, and each break is a type of hypoxia. At the first link, the lungs, low oxygen pressure at altitude gives hypoxic hypoxia. At the second, the blood cannot carry enough oxygen, from carbon monoxide, smoking, blood donation or anaemia. That is anaemic hypoxia. At the third, the blood is not circulating well, from positive g, cold or shock. That is stagnant hypoxia. And at the last link, the cells cannot use the oxygen, from alcohol, some drugs or cyanide. That is histotoxic hypoxia. So smoking and blood donation break the carrying link. And alcohol breaks the last link.

Question. A smoker and a non-smoker fly together at 8,000 ft. Why is the smoker more affected by the altitude?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: carbon monoxide from smoking occupies part of the smoker's haemoglobin, so less is free to carry oxygen. Add the lower oxygen pressure at altitude, and the smoker's brain receives less oxygen than the non-smoker's. It is as if they were flying higher.

## Blood donation

Giving a unit of whole blood removes a sizeable share of your circulating blood. The body restores the fluid volume within a day or so. But the red cells take weeks to replace.

In the meantime, two things are reduced. The first is the volume of blood available to keep the brain supplied when you pull g, or stand up quickly. The second is its oxygen-carrying capacity, another form of anaemic hypoxia. The risk is fainting.

The commonly recommended wait is at least 24 hours before flying. How that varies between individuals, and between types of donation, is in the lesson on health and fitness. So, at least 24 hours on the ground after giving blood.

## Your emotional state

Your attention is a limited resource. Flying an aeroplane uses a large part of it. And anything else that demands attention takes it away from the flight. Three emotional states matter: anxiety, depression and fears.

Start with anxiety. Anxiety is a state of heightened alertness to a threat. A little arousal improves performance. A lot narrows your attention to one thing, crowds out working memory, and leads to fixation and poor decisions. The source might be the flight itself, such as a check flight or a first solo navigation exercise. Or it might be something outside it: an exam tomorrow, a sick relative, money worries. Anxiety is also the most common cause of hyperventilation, which has its own lesson.

[[diagram:stress-performance-curve]]
Picture a graph shaped like an upside-down U. Arousal runs along the bottom, and performance runs up the side. On the left, with too little arousal, performance is low: boredom and inattention. In the middle, at a moderate level, performance peaks. On the right, with too much arousal, it falls away again: anxiety, narrowed attention, poor memory, and panic. A little stress helps. A lot hurts.

Next, depression. Depression slows thinking and saps motivation. That includes the motivation to check things carefully, and to make the conservative decision. It also affects sleep, so fatigue comes with it.

Depression is a medical condition, not a weakness. Many pilots fly while it is treated, including on some antidepressants. But only with CASA approval, through a DAME. Never self-manage it. And never stop or start such medication without the DAME knowing.

Then fears. A fear might be of some aspect of flying, such as stalls, turbulence or a particular manoeuvre. Or it might be a fear of failure. Either way, fears distort judgement in a particular way. You may avoid the action you fear, even when it is the correct one, such as a go-around after a bad approach. Recognising a fear, and talking it through with your instructor, usually removes much of its power.

The practical test for all three is this. If your mind is somewhere else at the holding point, you are not fully flying the aeroplane. Talk to someone, and do not fly while preoccupied.

## Medication and the DAME

Every medicine has an intended effect, and side effects. A pilot cares about both. And about a third thing: the condition the medicine is treating may be a reason not to fly in itself. A headache tablet hides the headache, but not the dehydration or illness behind it. A decongestant opens the nose, but the infection is still there.

The designated aviation medical examiner, the DAME, is a doctor approved by CASA to conduct aviation medicals, and to advise on aviation fitness. There are four rules, for the exam and for real life.

First, check with a DAME, or with CASA, before flying on any medication. That means prescription or over-the-counter, including herbal products, unless you already have clearance for that medication.

Second, try any new medication on the ground first, to see how it affects you. Commonly that means at least 24 to 48 hours.

Third, do not fly on drowsy antihistamines, sleeping tablets, or strong painkillers such as codeine. Nor on anything labelled "may cause drowsiness" or "do not operate machinery".

And fourth, a "that's fine" from your doctor or pharmacist is not an aviation clearance. They know medicine. The DAME knows medicine and the cockpit.

Why the ground trial? People respond differently. And the first dose is when an unexpected reaction is most likely, such as drowsiness, dizziness, or an allergic response. You want to discover it on the couch, not at 3,500 ft.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. "It's available without a prescription, so it must be safe to fly on." In fact, over-the-counter cold and allergy tablets are among the commonest causes of drug impairment in pilots. Being sold off the shelf says nothing about how a medicine affects someone flying an aeroplane.

## Your responsibility to be fit

The medical certificate is a minimum, not a guarantee. CASA and the DAME set the standard, and check you against it every few years. Between examinations, the person who applies it is you.

Under Part 67 of the regulations, you must not exercise the privileges of your licence while you have a medically significant condition that could impair safe flight. And you must tell CASA, or a DAME, about medically significant conditions and changes. Verify the current text of those obligations.

In practice, your responsibilities come down to four.

First, assess yourself before every flight. A common checklist is IMSAFE. That stands for Illness, Medication, Stress, Alcohol, Fatigue, and Eating, which includes hydration.

Second, do not fly if you are unwell, injured, on unapproved medication, affected by alcohol or drugs, or excessively tired.

Third, report illnesses, injuries, hospital admissions, new medications and pregnancy, as Part 67 requires. And get clearance before you resume flying.

And fourth, keep your medical certificate current, and carry any spectacles it requires.

[[diagram:im-safe-checklist]]
Picture six tiles in a row, one for each letter of IMSAFE: Illness, Medication, Stress, Alcohol, Fatigue, and Eating and hydration. Each tile carries the question to ask yourself. A no to any one of them means do not fly. And the decision is yours, as pilot in command.

IMSAFE works because it forces a pause, and a separate answer to each question. The factors in this lesson do not usually arrive alone. A late night is fatigue. A couple of drinks is alcohol. No breakfast is eating. And a worry about the flight is stress. Each can be "not too bad", and together they can be enough to make you unsafe. One honest "no" is enough to cancel.

Let's work one: applying IMSAFE to a blocked nose. You wake with a blocked nose and a mild sore throat, on the morning of a solo navigation exercise. What do you do? Pause if you'd like to think it through.
[[pause 4]]
Start with illness. You have a cold, with a blocked nose. The ear and sinus risk on descent alone makes this a no.

Then medication. A decongestant to "get through it" adds a medication you have not cleared with a DAME. And it may wear off in flight.

So the decision: cancel and rebook. The flight will still be there next week. An ear block on descent into Jandakot, with a ruptured eardrum, would keep you on the ground far longer.

Question. You hold a current Class 2 medical. A friend says that means you are fit to fly any day it is valid. What is wrong with that?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the certificate says you met the standard on the day you were examined. Fitness for a particular flight is assessed by you, every day, against how you are now: illness, medication, stress, alcohol, fatigue and food.

## Bringing it together

It is a Saturday in January. You have a solo navigation exercise booked from Jandakot to Northam and back, departing at {1100|eleven hundred}. You were at a barbecue last night, and had two beers, finished by {2100|nine p m}. You slept badly because of a work problem. You skipped breakfast. And you have a mild headache. The forecast for Northam is 39 degrees.

Run the lesson's ideas over it. The alcohol is legal. Well over 8 hours have passed, and two beers are long gone. But the headache is information. Two beers, a hot night, poor sleep and no breakfast point to dehydration and low blood sugar. And the work problem is an emotional load that will compete for your attention.

None of these alone would cancel the flight. Together, on a hot day with a long solo leg, they make a poorer pilot than usual. The repairs are simple, and they take an hour. Water. A proper meal. And honest thought about whether the work worry will stay on the ground.

If the headache is gone and you are clear-headed by the briefing, fly. If not, rebook. That is a sound pilot-in-command decision, not a failure.

## Key points

Let's recap.

Every health factor either degrades part of the pilot system, or threatens sudden incapacitation. Diet and water keep the brain fuelled. The coronary risk factors, which are smoking, cholesterol, obesity and family history, raise the risk of a heart attack. Colds block the ears and sinuses on descent, not on the climb. Gastro and migraine can incapacitate you suddenly. An injury must not stop you handling an emergency.

Pregnancy changes your reach and how the harness fits, and the DAME advises when to stop. Ageing slows reactions and near vision. Alcohol, and the carbon monoxide from smoking, starve the brain of oxygen. Giving blood means at least 24 hours on the ground. Dehydration brings headache and poor concentration. And anxiety, depression and fears steal attention.

Any medication, prescription or over-the-counter, needs DAME clearance before you fly on it, and a ground trial first.

And you are responsible for your fitness on the day. Assess yourself with IMSAFE. Do not fly when unfit. Report significant conditions under Part 67. And keep your medical current.

A final exam tip. Expect a question on when a cold causes ear or sinus pain. The answer is on descent. Expect one asking which item is a coronary risk factor. One on what to do before flying on a new medication: see a DAME. And one asking who decides whether you are fit for today's flight. That last answer is always you, the pilot in command. The DAME and CASA set the standard and issue the certificate. You apply it every day.

That's the end of PHFC 2.1.
