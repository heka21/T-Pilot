This is PHFC 2.13, Human factors considerations. By the end you will be able to explain how your brain turns what you sense into a decision, what bends that decision, how to make better ones, and how stress and fatigue wear all of it down. There are several questions and a worked example along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (PHFC 2.13)

The exam wants four things from you. First, how the brain processes information and makes decisions: how what you sense becomes a mental picture, and how personality, pride, peer pressure, the urge to complete the flight, anxiety, over-confidence, boredom, complacency, memory and its limits, memory aids, workload, skill, experience and currency each shape a decision. Second, how good decisions are made, and how a pilot can make better ones. Third, stress: how it relates to arousal, its short and long-term effects, the environmental stressors of heat, cold, vibration and noise, and how to manage it. And fourth, fatigue: its causes and effects, the coping strategies, and how acute fatigue differs from chronic.

## Why this matters

Most accidents in light aeroplanes are not caused by an engine or a wing. They are caused by a decision.

Think of the pilot who pressed on into lowering cloud over the Darling Scarp. The one who launched from Jandakot after a broken night's sleep. The one who missed a radio call while juggling a clearance, a frequency and a QNH. Each had the skill to fly the aeroplane. And each made a choice, or missed a cue, that set up the accident.

This lesson looks inside the pilot. It explains how your brain builds its picture of the world, what bends your decisions, how to make better ones, and how stress and fatigue degrade all of it.

The exam tests it in two ways. With recall: the capacity of short-term memory, the shape of the arousal curve, and acute versus chronic fatigue. And with short scenarios, that ask you to name the trap a pilot fell into.

## From senses to a mental picture (2.13.1 a)

You are joining the circuit at Murrayfield. Your eyes take in the runway, the windsock, and a speck that might be traffic. Your ears take in a radio call. Your inner ear, and the seat of your pants, report a gentle bank. None of that is yet understanding. Turning it into a picture you can act on takes several stages, and each one has limits. There are five.

The first stage is sensing. The eyes, ears, balance organs and touch gather raw information. Each sense has a short-lived sensory store, that holds the raw impression for a fraction of a second. That is long enough for the brain to decide whether to pay attention to it. Most of it is never noticed, and simply fades.

The second is perception. The brain interprets what it has noticed. And it does so by comparing it with past experience, and with what it expects. That is why perception is fast. You recognise a runway without analysing every edge.

It is also why perception can be wrong. If you expect "cleared to land", you may hear it in a call that said "continue approach". If you expect the airfield to be ahead, a similar-looking strip will do. The brain fills gaps with what it thinks should be there.

The third is the mental model. From these perceptions, you build, and keep updating, a mental model of where you are, what the aeroplane is doing, and what is about to happen. Keeping that model accurate is situational awareness.

When the model and reality drift apart, every decision you make on top of it is wrong, however good your reasoning. For example, you believe you are downwind of the field, when you are actually abeam a different one.

The fourth is attention. Your capacity to process information consciously is limited. It is essentially a single channel. You can switch quickly between tasks. And well-practised skills, such as holding straight and level, can run in the background. But you cannot truly attend to two demanding tasks at once. Reading a clearance back while searching for traffic means one of them gets less than it needs.

The fifth is decision and action. With the picture built, you choose what to do, and act. Then you monitor the result through your senses again. That feedback loop is what keeps the model current.

So the basic model is senses, perception, decision, action, with feedback. Errors can enter at every stage. A cue not sensed. A cue misperceived. Attention elsewhere. A poor decision on a good picture. Or a good decision badly executed.

[[diagram:information-processing-model]]
Picture a row of five boxes, left to right. Senses, then attention, then perception, then decision, then action. Under the senses sits the sensory store, held for a fraction of a second, where most of what comes in fades. Under attention, many cues come in, but few get through, one demanding task at a time. Above the row, in the middle, sits the mental model, your situational awareness, with a feedback loop running from action back to the senses. What to notice: everything passes through a single-channel attention and a small working memory, and an error can enter at any stage.

Question. Why can expectation make you hear a clearance that was never given?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: perception interprets incoming information against experience and expectation, and fills gaps with what it predicts should be there. If you are expecting a particular clearance, an ambiguous or partly heard call is readily perceived as that clearance. Reading back, and listening for the acknowledgement of your readback, is the defence.

## Memory, and the tools that support it (2.13.1 v to vii)

Your mental model is built and stored in memory. And memory comes in two quite different kinds.

The first is short-term memory, also called working memory. It is the scratch pad you hold a clearance, a frequency or a QNH on while you use it. It is small and volatile. It holds about 7 items, plus or minus 2, for roughly 15 to 30 seconds, unless you rehearse them. And it is easily wiped by an interruption.

Try holding this while a passenger asks you a question. Climb to 2,500, squawk {3412|three four one two}, QNH {1018|ten eighteen}, contact {118.1|one one eight decimal one}. You will see the limit for yourself.

Grouping items into meaningful chunks stretches it a little. For example, you can hold three-four-one-two as thirty-four, twelve. Writing them down removes the problem.

The second kind is long-term memory. It is large and durable. It holds your knowledge of the rules, the procedures you have learned, and the motor skills of flying.

It is not perfectly reliable, though. Recall depends on cues. Memories can be reconstructed, rather than replayed. And recall suffers under stress. The emergency procedure you knew perfectly in the briefing room can be hard to retrieve with the engine coughing.

Because both kinds of memory are fallible, pilots use aides-memoire. These are written checklists. Mnemonics, such as BUMFH for the pre-landing checks, and HASELL before stalling. The habit of writing clearances on a kneeboard. And rules of thumb, such as 1 hPa per 30 ft.

They take load off working memory. And they give long-term memory a cue to retrieve the right procedure.

They have a weakness of their own. A rule of thumb applied outside the conditions it was made for, or a mnemonic recited without actually looking at the switches, gives false reassurance. The tool supports thinking. It does not replace it.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Short-term memory is not unlimited for a few seconds. Nor does it keep things for hours. It holds about seven items for well under a minute without rehearsal, and an interruption can wipe it. That is the reason to write down every clearance.

## What bends a decision (2.13.1 b)

Two pilots with the same training can look at the same weather, and make opposite decisions. The difference is usually not knowledge, but the human factors acting on the decision. The syllabus names them, and each one has a mechanism. There are six groups.

First, personality traits. An extrovert tends to act quickly, enjoy a challenge, and on average, accept more risk. An introvert tends to be more careful, but may be reluctant to ask for help, to query ATC, or to admit uncertainty on the radio. Neither is wrong. Each needs to know its own bias.

Second, pride and peer pressure. Nobody wants to look incompetent in front of passengers, other club members, or the instructor who sent them solo. Turning back, declaring that you are unsure of your position, or cancelling while others are flying, all feel like failures. That feeling pushes the decision away from the safe option.

Third, the desire to get the flight flown. It is often called get-there-itis, or plan continuation bias. Once a plan is under way, people tend to continue it, even as the evidence against it builds. The aeroplane has to be back at the club for the next booking. The hire is paid for. The destination is only twenty minutes away. It is the most common trap behind VFR-into-cloud accidents, because each small step of continuing seems reasonable on its own.

Fourth, anxiety, over-confidence, boredom and complacency. Anxiety narrows attention onto the thing you are worried about, so other cues are missed. Over-confidence reduces checking. You know the fuel is fine. Boredom, on a long quiet leg to Kalgoorlie, reduces vigilance. And complacency, the comfortable sense that nothing will go wrong because nothing has before, lets small errors through unchecked.

Fifth, workload. Both ends of the scale hurt. Too little gives boredom and complacency. Too much gives overload, where the brain starts shedding tasks, often the wrong ones, such as the lookout or the fuel check. Workload peaks are predictable: departure, arrival, and the circuit. So you prepare for them by doing what you can in the quiet phases.

And sixth, skill, experience and currency. Skill is what you can do. Experience is what you have done. Currency is how recently you did it. Skills fade without practice, especially fine ones like crosswind landings.

Experience helps only if it is relevant and recent. A thousand hours over the Wheatbelt says little about your ability to handle a gusty coastal crosswind. And a pilot who has not flown for three months is less sharp than their logbook suggests.

Question. Why is plan continuation bias so hard to resist in the air?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because the decision to continue is made in small steps, each of which seems reasonable on its own. Meanwhile the commitment already made, the time, the money, the people waiting, weighs on the decision. By the time the evidence is overwhelming, the safe options may already be gone.

## Making better decisions (2.13.2)

Good aeronautical decision-making is a process, not a flash of inspiration. The point of a process is to slow you down enough to see the options. That matters when stress, workload and the biases you just heard are all pushing you to act on the first idea.

A widely taught model is DECIDE. It has six steps, one for each letter.

First, detect that something has changed. A rough engine, a lowering cloud base, a radio call.

Second, estimate its significance. Does it matter, and how much time do I have?

Third, choose a safe outcome. What end state do I want? On the ground at an alternate, or back at the departure field?

Fourth, identify the actions that will get me there.

Fifth, do them.

And sixth, evaluate the result. Did it work, and what has changed now?

[[diagram:decision-making-decide]]
Picture the six steps arranged in a circle, like a clock face. Detect at the top. Then, going clockwise, estimate, choose, identify at the bottom, do, and evaluate, which leads back up to detect. In the centre are the words, keep going round. The last step is the one that makes it a loop. The result of your action is itself a change to detect.

Let's work one. You are at 4,500 ft between York and Northam, when the engine develops a slight roughness, and the RPM drops by about 100. Run it through DECIDE. Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
Detect. Roughness, and a small RPM drop. The oil pressure and temperature are normal.

Estimate. The engine is still producing power, so you have time. But it may get worse. And carburettor ice is likely in today's humid air.

Choose. The outcome you want is a safe landing at the nearest suitable aerodrome, Northam, with the engine problem diagnosed or fixed.

Identify. Carburettor heat on. A mixture check. A turn towards Northam. And a radio call on the area frequency.

Do. Apply the carburettor heat, turn, and make the call.

Evaluate. After the initial worsening, the roughness clears and the RPM recovers. That is consistent with carburettor ice. So you re-enter the loop. Either continue to Northam and land, or resume the flight with regular carb heat checks.

The model did not tell you anything you did not know. What it did was stop you skipping from rough engine straight to an action, without choosing where you wanted to end up.

There are other tools too. PAVE is a pre-flight risk check, with four letters. P is the pilot: am I fit? A is the aircraft: is it serviceable, and right for this trip? V is the environment: the weather, terrain, airspace and daylight. And E is external pressures: passengers, schedules, and the desire to get there. The E is where most get-there-itis lives. Naming it on the ground makes it easier to resist in the air. IMSAFE, the personal fitness check, comes up with fatigue, later in the lesson.

Now the methods of improving decision-making. There are five.

First, set personal minimums for weather, wind and fuel on the ground, and stick to them. A decision made calmly at home is better than one made under pressure in the air.

Second, plan alternates and a decision point before departure. For example: if the cloud base at Pinjarra is below 2,000 ft, I turn back.

Third, brief yourself, and your passengers, before each phase of flight, so the threats and the plan are fresh.

Fourth, recognise the five hazardous attitudes, and use the antidote thought for each. Anti-authority: the rules are usually right. Impulsivity: not so fast, think first. Invulnerability: it could happen to me. Macho: taking chances is foolish. And resignation: I'm not helpless, I can make a difference.

And fifth, train scenarios, debrief every flight honestly, and learn from others' incidents, in ATSB reports and in CASA's Flight Safety Australia.

Question. Why does setting personal minimums before the flight improve decisions in the air?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because the decision is made in advance, calmly, and without external pressures. In flight, you only have to compare the conditions with the limit. That is far harder for stress, pride or get-there-itis to bend than a fresh judgement made on the spot.

## Stress and arousal (2.13.3)

Think about the last time you did a practice forced landing with the examiner watching. A little nervousness sharpened you. You scanned faster, and your flying was crisp. Had you been terrified, you would have fumbled the checks. Had you been half asleep, you would have missed the best paddock. That is the relationship this section explains.

Stress is the body's response to a demand placed on it. The demand is a stressor. An engine failure, a difficult crosswind, a mortgage, a hot cockpit. Arousal is your level of alertness and activation, from drowsy at one end to panic at the other. Stressors raise arousal.

[[diagram:stress-performance-curve]]
Picture a graph with arousal along the bottom and performance up the side. The curve is an upside-down U. On the left, too little arousal: boredom, inattention and complacency, like a long cruise leg. At the top of the hump, in the middle, the best performance, at moderate arousal. On the right, too much: anxiety, tunnelling, poor memory and panic, like being lost in bad weather with a rough engine. Some arousal sharpens you. Too little breeds complacency, and too much narrows attention towards panic.

That upside-down U is often called the Yerkes-Dodson curve. A moderate level of arousal gives the best performance. The peak is lower for complex tasks than for simple ones. So a demanding situation in the air needs you calmer, not more wound up.

Stress comes in two time scales. Short-term stress, also called acute stress, is the rapid response to a demanding situation. The engine runs rough. You realise you are lost. A gust drops a wing in the flare. Adrenaline raises your heart rate and sharpens focus, which helps at first. If it is excessive or prolonged, performance degrades down the right-hand side of the curve.

Long-term stress, also called chronic stress, comes from ongoing problems in life. Work, money, relationships, illness in the family. It does not switch off when you strap in. Over time it causes fatigue, sleep problems, illness and depression. And in the cockpit it shows as preoccupation. Part of your limited attention is still on the problem at home.

Then there is environmental stress. The cockpit itself can be a stressor, and the syllabus names four.

First, heat. A light aeroplane on the apron at Kalgoorlie in January becomes an oven. Heat causes dehydration, fatigue and poor concentration. And you lose water through sweat faster than you notice.

Second, cold. Shivering, numb fingers, and poor dexterity. Cold also narrows the blood vessels, a cause of stagnant hypoxia, as the lesson on hypoxia explains.

Third, vibration. Continuous low-frequency vibration from the engine and propeller causes fatigue, and at some frequencies, blurred vision.

And fourth, noise. It is fatiguing in itself. It makes radio calls harder to hear, and it damages hearing over time. That is covered in the lesson on hearing protection.

They add up. A long summer flight in a hot, noisy, vibrating cabin leaves you more tired, and less sharp on arrival, than the same flight in a quiet, cool one.

So what does stress do to your performance? An overload of stress shows in predictable ways. Narrowed attention, or tunnelling: you fixate on one problem and stop scanning. Missed information: radio calls, warning lights, the fuel gauge. Poor memory: a checklist step forgotten, a clearance lost. Rushed or poor decisions, or no decision at all. Reverting to old habits: under pressure you do what you learned first, even on a type where it is wrong. And irritability, with an increase in errors of all kinds.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. "More stress makes you perform better." That is only true on the left of the curve. High stress narrows attention and degrades memory. It never widens your monitoring. The answer the exam wants for best performance is a moderate level of arousal.

Finally, managing stress. It follows the same logic as any threat. Recognise it, remove it if you can, and prepare for what remains. In practice, that means six things.

First, identify your stressors honestly, including the ones at home. Second, remove or reduce them where you can, and do not fly when you are preoccupied. Third, plan and prepare thoroughly, so the flight itself is less demanding. A good plan lowers in-flight stress more than anything else. Fourth, use relaxation, and slow, controlled breathing, when you notice your arousal climbing. Fifth, keep fit, sleep well and eat properly, so you have reserves. And sixth, talk problems through with an instructor, a friend or a professional.

Question. Why does a long-term problem at home affect your flying, even though you feel calm in the cockpit?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: chronic stress uses up part of your limited attention through preoccupation. It disturbs sleep, and wears down your health. Your arousal may look normal, but you have less capacity in reserve. So you miss cues and make errors more readily when the workload rises.

## Fatigue (2.13.4)

Fatigue is the state of reduced mental and physical capability that comes from too little sleep, too long awake, or too much effort. Its danger is the same as hypoxia's. A fatigued pilot is a poor judge of their own fatigue.

First, the causes. The main ones are lack of sleep and long duty days. Close behind is a disturbed circadian rhythm. That is the body clock that runs your alertness on a roughly daily cycle. Its low point is at about {0200|oh two hundred} to {0600|oh six hundred}. An early-morning departure after a short night puts you in that low, on a sleep deficit. Other causes add to these: high workload, stress, illness, heat, noise, vibration, dehydration, hypoxia, and poor diet.

Next, the effects. Fatigue slows reactions, impairs judgement, reduces attention, and produces lapses of memory. It leads you to accept lower standards: a sloppy approach will do. It makes you irritable. And in its extreme, it brings micro-sleeps, brief lapses of a few seconds that you may not notice.

Fatigue comes in two kinds, acute and chronic, and the difference is in the cause, the onset and the recovery.

Acute fatigue is short-term. It is the result of a single long day, heavy workload, or one poor night. It comes on over hours. And it is relieved by a good night's sleep, or rest.

Chronic fatigue accumulates over weeks, from ongoing sleep loss, stress or illness. One night's sleep does not fix it. Recovery needs extended rest, and often dealing with the underlying cause, sometimes with medical help.

So, acute: hours to build, and a night's sleep to fix. Chronic: weeks to build, and extended rest to fix.

There are three coping strategies.

First, sleep management. Regular sleep of 7 to 9 hours, in a dark, quiet room. No caffeine or alcohol near bedtime. And avoid flying in the circadian low after little sleep.

Second, relaxation. Breaks during the day, relaxation techniques, and time away from flying.

And third, fitness and diet. Regular exercise, regular balanced meals, and staying hydrated, especially in a WA summer.

Before every flight, the IMSAFE check asks the fatigue question alongside the others.

[[diagram:im-safe-checklist]]
Picture six tiles, in two rows of three, one for each letter. Across the top row: I for illness, M for medication, and S for stress. Across the bottom row: A for alcohol, F for fatigue, and E for eating and hydration. Each tile carries the question to ask yourself. A no to any of them means do not fly, and you, the pilot in command, are the one who decides.

Question. A pilot who has slept badly for three weeks sleeps ten hours before a flight. Is the fatigue gone?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: probably not. Three weeks of poor sleep is chronic fatigue, which accumulates, and is not cured by one good night. It needs extended rest, and attention to whatever has been disrupting the sleep.

## Bringing it together

You have booked the club aeroplane for an {0630|oh six thirty} departure from Jandakot to Busselton. It is a birthday breakfast for your partner, who is coming with you. Work was stressful all week, and you slept five hours. At the hold-short, the ATIS cloud base is lower than forecast, and close to your personal minimum.

Every element of this lesson is present. You are near the circadian low, on a sleep deficit. That is fatigue. You are carrying a week of chronic stress. You have a passenger and a booking pushing you to go. That is external pressure, and the desire to get the flight flown. And your perception of the cloud is coloured by wanting it to be fine.

The tools are also present. The personal minimum you set at home. The E in PAVE, for external pressures. And the S and the F in IMSAFE.

The good decision is to apply the minimum you set when you were calm. And if the base is below it, you drive to breakfast instead.

## Key points

Let's recap.

How the brain decides. Information flows from the senses, to perception, to decision, to action, with feedback. Perception uses expectation, and can mislead. Attention is a single channel. Short-term memory holds about 7 items, plus or minus 2, for 15 to 30 seconds. Long-term memory is large, but fallible under stress. Aides-memoire help, but do not replace thinking. And personality, pride and peer pressure, get-there-itis, anxiety, over-confidence, boredom, complacency, workload, and skill, experience and currency all bend decisions.

Better decisions. Decide by a process: DECIDE as a loop, and PAVE before flight. Improve decisions with personal minimums, pre-planned alternates and decision points, briefings, the antidotes to the hazardous attitudes, and honest debriefs.

Stress. Performance follows an upside-down U against arousal, best at a moderate level. Acute stress sharpens, then degrades. Chronic stress erodes health and attention. Heat, cold, vibration and noise are the environmental stressors. High stress narrows attention, loses information and memory, and brings back old habits. Manage it by identifying, removing, preparing and relaxing.

Fatigue. It comes from sleep loss, long days, the circadian low at about {0200|oh two hundred} to {0600|oh six hundred}, and environmental stressors. It slows reactions and judgement. Acute fatigue is relieved by sleep. Chronic fatigue needs extended rest. Cope with sleep management, relaxation, fitness and diet.

A final exam tip. Chronic fatigue is not cured by one good night's sleep. And a fatigued pilot is the worst judge of their own fatigue, just like a hypoxic one. Have these by heart. 7, plus or minus 2 items, for 15 to 30 seconds. Best performance at moderate arousal. The circadian low at about {0200|oh two hundred} to {0600|oh six hundred}. And the six steps of DECIDE. Scenario questions describe a pilot's behaviour, and ask you to name the factor. Pressing on to complete the trip is the desire to get the flight flown. Tunnel vision under pressure is high stress.

That's the end of PHFC 2.13.
