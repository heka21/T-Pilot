This is PHFC 2.8, Motion sickness. By the end you will know what really causes motion sickness, the things that make it worse, and the ways to combat it in flight, for yourself and for your passengers. There are a few questions along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (PHFC 2.8)

The exam wants three things from you. First, the underlying cause of motion sickness. Second, the factors that make it worse. And third, the ways of combating it in flight.

## Why this matters

Most student pilots feel queasy at some point early in training. Maybe on the third lesson of steep turns, on a bumpy February afternoon over the Jandakot training area. Or on a first flight in the back seat, while a friend does circuits. Almost everyone adapts. And knowing why it happens is a large part of adapting.

It matters beyond comfort. Motion sickness eats away at concentration and motivation long before anyone reaches for a sick bag. And a pilot who is vomiting is incapacitated, in a single-pilot aeroplane.

As a pilot in command, you will also carry passengers who have never flown in a light aeroplane. How you plan and fly the trip decides whether they enjoy it.

The exam asks for the cause, which is a sensory conflict, not a weak stomach. It asks for the things that make it worse. And it asks for the remedies, including why the pilot does not take the tablets the passengers might.

## Your brain's model of motion

Before you can see why motion sickness happens, you need to see how your brain knows how you are moving. It does not get the answer from one sense. It builds a picture from three, all at once.

The first is the eyes. They report movement of the scene around you: the horizon tilting, the ground sliding past, the cabin staying still.

The second is the vestibular apparatus of the inner ear. It reports accelerations of your head. The semicircular canals sense rotation, in roll, pitch and yaw. The otolith organs sense linear acceleration, and the pull of gravity. You met both in the lesson on the anatomy of the ear, and saw their limits in the lesson on vision, spatial disorientation and illusions.

[[diagram:ear-anatomy]]
Picture a cross-section of the ear, with the outside world on the left. The ear canal leads in to the eardrum and the small air-filled middle ear. On the right, buried in bone, is the fluid-filled inner ear. Beside its coiled cochlea sit three loops at right angles, the semicircular canals, which sense rotation. Next to them are the otolith organs, which sense linear acceleration and gravity. That is the inner ear's half of every picture of motion.

And the third is the proprioceptive senses, in the muscles, joints and skin. They report how you are pressed into the seat, and how your body is positioned. They are the seat of the pants.

On the ground, walking or driving, the three agree. Turn your head, and your eyes see the room swing, your canals feel the rotation, and your neck muscles feel the turn. The brain gets one consistent story.

It also learns, over a lifetime, what each of your own movements will feel like. So it predicts the signals before they arrive. When the prediction and the signals all match, you are not even aware of the process.

## The cause: a sensory conflict

Motion sickness is what happens when the senses tell the brain different stories about motion. Or when the story they tell does not match what the brain expected. The core conflict is between the eyes and the vestibular apparatus, with the seat of the pants joining in.

Picture the classic case. You are in the right seat over the Darling Range, in afternoon thermals. Your head is down, reading the frequency off a chart on your knee. The aeroplane is bouncing and rolling. Your inner ear reports every bump: up, down, roll left, roll right. But your eyes are fixed on a chart that is moving with you. So they report that you are perfectly still. Two senses, two pictures that cannot both be true. The brain cannot reconcile them, and it responds with sickness.

[[diagram:motion-sickness-sensory-conflict]]
Picture a pilot reading a chart in turbulence, with two labels on the left, one above the other. The eyes, head down on the chart, say: still. The inner ear, feeling the turbulence and the turns, says: moving. The two arrows meet in the middle at the conflict, two pictures of motion, and on the right is the sickness that follows. Along the bottom are four ways to make the senses agree: fresh air, eyes outside on the horizon, head still, and take the controls. And a last line: pilots, no anti-motion-sickness medication.

The conflict can run the other way too. In the back seat of a four-seater, with no view of the horizon, the cabin walls fill your view and look stationary. Meanwhile the vestibular system feels every turn and gust.

Or take a passenger watching the wingtip trace the horizon in a steep turn. Their eyes and inner ear disagree about which way is down. The seat of the pants and the otoliths say: straight down into the seat. But the eyes show the earth tilted at 45 degrees.

The second half of the cause is expectation. The brain predicts the motion that is coming. When you fly the aeroplane yourself, you know a turn is coming, because you started it. So the brain's prediction matches what the senses then report.

A passenger gets the motion with no warning. So does a student, while the instructor demonstrates. Their senses report movement the brain did not predict. That mismatch, between expected and actual motion, adds to the conflict between the senses. It is why the person at the controls is usually the last to feel sick.

Why the brain responds with nausea, of all things, is not fully settled. One widely quoted explanation is that the brain treats a sensory conflict as a sign of poisoning. Many toxins disturb the senses in just this way, and the response to suspected poison is to empty the stomach.

Whatever the reason, the important point for you is the cause: conflicting information from the balance senses. It is a normal response of a normal nervous system. It is not a weakness, and it is not a sign that you are unsuited to flying.

Now how it progresses. Motion sickness builds through a recognisable sequence, and the early stages are easy to miss. There are six stages.

[[diagram:motion-sickness-progression]]
Picture six steps, going down a staircase from left to right. First, loss of interest and a vague unease: you go quiet. Second, yawning and increased saliva. Third, pallor: the face goes pale. Fourth, cold sweating, often with a feeling of warmth. Fifth, nausea. And sixth, at the bottom, vomiting. Under the first steps is the label: act here. And across the top, your flying is already poor long before the last step.

Performance falls well before the sixth stage. Concentration and motivation drop in the first stages. The pilot stops flying accurately, stops looking out, and stops caring about the radio. An instructor watching a student go pale and silent knows what is happening before the student admits it. That is why the early signs matter. They are the time to act.

Question. Why is the pilot at the controls usually less likely to be sick than the passenger beside them, in the same turbulence?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the pilot makes the control inputs, and is looking outside. So the brain knows what motion is coming, and the eyes see the horizon move as the inner ear feels it. The passenger gets the motion unannounced, often while looking at the cabin. So their senses conflict with each other, and with what the brain expected.

## What makes it worse

Every aggravating factor does one of three things. It increases the conflict. It increases the amount of unfamiliar motion. Or it lowers the threshold at which the conflict makes you sick. Sort them that way, and the list becomes easy to remember.

Start with the factors that increase the conflict. There are four.

The first is looking inside the cockpit for long periods. Reading charts, programming a GPS, looking at a tablet or the instruments. The eyes see a still scene while the inner ear feels motion. That is the classic conflict.

The second is having no view of the horizon. A rear seat, a small side window, or a high instrument panel for a short passenger. Nothing outside gives the eyes the motion the inner ear is feeling.

The third is head movements, especially during turns. Moving the head while the aeroplane rotates sends confusing signals from several canals at once. It is the same mechanism as the Coriolis illusion, from the lesson on vision, spatial disorientation and illusions. Turning to talk to a back-seat passenger in a turn is a good way to make both of you queasy.

And the fourth is being a passenger rather than the pilot. You have no control over the motion, and no way to anticipate it.

Next, the factors that increase the motion. There are three.

The first is turbulence. Particularly the low-level thermal turbulence of a hot WA summer afternoon, when the air below the inversion is a mass of bumps from the sun-baked paddocks and suburbs. Mechanical turbulence in the lee of the Darling Scarp, in a strong easterly, has the same effect.

The second is manoeuvres. Steep turns, stalls, spins and aerobatics, and repeated accelerations up and down. Slow, repeated vertical motion is especially provocative. That is why a long ride through thermals is often worse than a single sharp bump.

And the third is unfamiliar motion. The inexperienced pilot's vestibular system has no stored pattern for the motions of flight. So every one of them is a surprise.

Last, the factors that lower your threshold. There are seven.

First, anxiety, fear and stress. A nervous student, a passenger on a first flight, or anyone worried about the next exercise feels sick sooner. Anxiety and nausea feed each other.

Second, heat and stuffy air. A cabin that has been sitting on the Jandakot apron in summer is hot before you start. And warmth and stale air are among the strongest aggravators.

Third, smells. Fuel, exhaust, hot oil, and the smell of someone else being sick.

Fourth, an empty stomach, or a heavy or greasy meal, before flight.

Fifth, alcohol and hangover, fatigue and illness. All of them lower your tolerance to many kinds of stress at once.

Sixth, hypoxia at altitude, and carbon monoxide from a leaking heater. Both can bring on nausea themselves. You'll find that in the lesson on toxic hazards.

And seventh, inexperience: a lack of adaptation. That is the single biggest factor in early training.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. The options sometimes offer a weak stomach, low blood sugar, or a disorder of the digestive system, as the cause of motion sickness. The cause is a sensory conflict between the eyes and the inner ear. Hunger and a heavy meal are aggravating factors, not the cause.

Question. A passenger in the back seat is reading a book during a bumpy flight over the hills. Which aggravating factors are at work, and what do they do?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: looking inside, at the book, makes the eyes report still, while the inner ear feels the bumps. That is the core sensory conflict. The turbulence supplies more of the motion. And being a passenger, with no control and no anticipation, adds the mismatch between expected and actual motion.

## Combating it in flight

Every remedy follows from the cause. Make the senses agree. Reduce the motion. Raise the threshold. And give the brain a way to anticipate what is coming.

First, make the senses agree. There are three ways.

Look outside, at the horizon. Now the eyes see the horizon tilt and bob in step with what the inner ear feels, and the conflict shrinks. Minimise your time head-down. When you have to read a chart, bring it up near the windscreen, so the horizon stays in your peripheral vision. And look up often.

Keep your head still. Move your eyes rather than your head. And avoid turning to look into the back of the cabin while the aeroplane is turning.

And take the controls. Flying the aeroplane lets the brain predict each motion before it happens. That removes the mismatch between expected and actual motion. Instructors use this routinely. A queasy student who is given straight and level to fly often recovers within minutes. For a nervous passenger, a simple task has some of the same effect, where it is safe and appropriate. Holding a heading, for example, or looking for a landmark.

Second, reduce the motion. Fly smoothly, with gentle turns and gradual changes of attitude. And stop the manoeuvring exercise for a while. Straight and level is the gentlest thing an aeroplane does.

Avoid turbulence where you can. Fly early in the morning, before the thermals build. Or climb above the thermal layer, into the smoother air on top. And plan routes around known rough areas, such as the lee of the scarp in a strong easterly.

Third, raise the threshold. Open the fresh-air vents, and keep the cabin cool. Cool air on the face is one of the most effective remedies there is. Loosen tight clothing, and sit upright. And reduce anxiety: a good briefing for passengers, a clear plan for the lesson, and an instructor who knows you are uncomfortable.

Fourth, speak up, and know when to stop. Tell your instructor early. The first signs, going quiet, yawning, feeling warm, are the time to say something, while the exercise can still be changed. Pushing on in silence makes it worse. And it wastes the lesson anyway, because you are not learning while you feel sick.

If the symptoms progress, return and land. Do not push on through severe nausea. Vomiting at the controls of a single-pilot aeroplane is an incapacitation. And carry sick bags for passengers, where they can reach them, and brief them on their use.

And last, medication: passengers yes, pilots no. Anti-motion-sickness tablets work on the brain's response to the sensory conflict. Most of them also cause drowsiness, blurred vision and slowed reactions. Those side effects are exactly what a pilot cannot afford. And many such drugs are unacceptable for flying without advice from a DAME, a designated aviation medical examiner. So the rule is this. A pilot does not take anti-motion-sickness medication, before or during flight, unless a DAME has approved it. Passengers may take it.

Question. Why does handing the controls to a queasy student often settle their stomach within minutes?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: flying the aeroplane makes the student look outside, at the horizon. So the eyes see the motion the inner ear feels. And the brain now predicts each motion, because the student commands it. Both kinds of mismatch shrink, so the trigger for the sickness weakens.

## Adaptation: why it gets better

The brain's model of motion is learned, and it keeps learning. Each flight teaches the vestibular system what the motions of a light aeroplane feel like, and how they relate to what the eyes see. So the conflict gradually stops being a surprise. This is adaptation. It is why most students who feel sick in the first few lessons are fine by first solo.

You can help it along with repeated, gradually increasing exposure. Short lessons at first. Smooth air, early in the day. Then progressively more manoeuvring, as tolerance grows.

A sensible plan for the next lesson after a queasy one is an earlier start, a light meal beforehand, and a shorter manoeuvring session. If susceptibility persists, talk to your instructor and to a DAME, rather than medicating yourself. And adaptation fades with time away from flying. So a long break can bring the queasiness back, for a lesson or two.

## Bringing it together

You take two friends for a Saturday flight from Jandakot up the coast to Lancelin and back. It is January, and the forecast is 38 degrees. Using this lesson, you plan the flight before you leave home.

You book an early slot, airborne by {0700|seven a m}, before the thermals build. You brief your friends: a light breakfast, not a big greasy one, and no drinks the night before. On the day, you park in the shade if you can, and air the cabin before start.

You put the more nervous friend in the front seat, with a view of the horizon, and give them a job. Spot the Lancelin sand dunes, and call out the beach towns on the way. You show the back-seat passenger the fresh-air vent and the sick bag. And you ask them to say early if they feel unwell, rather than reading on their phone.

You fly gentle turns, with no steep turns for fun. And you climb to the smoother air above the morning haze layer.

On the way home, the bumps arrive, and the back-seat passenger goes quiet and pale. Vents fully open. Eyes on the horizon. Head still. Smooth flying. And you head directly for home, rather than adding a scenic loop.

Question. Of all the remedies, why is eyes outside on the horizon the one that works on the cause itself?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the cause is the eyes and the inner ear disagreeing about motion. Looking at the horizon lets the eyes see the same tilting and bobbing that the inner ear feels. So the two senses tell one story. Fresh air and smooth flying help. But they lower the threshold, or the motion, rather than removing the conflict.

## Key points

Let's recap.

Motion sickness is caused by conflicting information from the balance senses. Mainly the eyes against the inner ear's vestibular apparatus, with the seat of the pants joining in. It is made worse by motion the brain did not expect. It is a normal response, not a weakness. And it erodes performance well before vomiting.

The aggravating factors do one of three things. Some increase the conflict: head-down work, no view of the horizon, head movements in turns, and being a passenger. Some increase the motion: turbulence, manoeuvres, and unfamiliar motion. And some lower the threshold: anxiety, heat, smells, hunger or a heavy meal, alcohol, fatigue, illness, hypoxia, carbon monoxide, and inexperience.

To combat it: fresh cool air, eyes outside on the horizon, a still head, taking the controls, smooth flying, and avoiding turbulence. Tell the instructor early, land if it progresses, and carry sick bags. Passengers may take medication. Pilots do not, without advice from a DAME. And gradual exposure builds adaptation.

A final exam tip. The cause is a sensory conflict between the eyes and the inner ear, never a weak stomach. The best remedies in flight are fresh air, eyes outside on the horizon, head still, and having the person fly. Aggravating factors include turbulence, heat, anxiety, head-down work and head movements. And medication is for passengers, not pilots, because of drowsiness and slowed reactions.
