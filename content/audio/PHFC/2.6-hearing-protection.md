This is PHFC 2.6, Hearing protection. By the end you will know what to wear to protect your hearing in and around aircraft, how to wear it, how to manage radio volume and the length of your exposure, and how to keep track of your hearing over a flying career. There are calculations to try in your head and a few questions, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (PHFC 2.6)

The exam wants one thing from you, with several parts. State the recommended ways of protecting your hearing in and around aircraft. That means what to wear, how to wear it, how to manage radio volume and exposure, and how to keep track of your hearing over a flying career. This subtopic builds directly on the effects of noise from the lesson on the anatomy of the ear.

## Why this matters

Climb out of Jandakot in a Cessna 172 or a Piper Warrior, and the noise in the cabin sits somewhere around 85 to 100 decibels for the whole flight. That is loud enough to damage hearing. And it does so silently. There is no pain, and nothing bleeds. The loss builds up one flight at a time, over hundreds of hours. Then high-pitched sounds, and the consonants in radio calls, start to vanish.

The same noise also tires you, masks readbacks, and hides the stall warning. So good protection is a safety measure on today's flight, as well as a health measure for your career.

The exam asks about this subtopic by recall. Which method works best. Which kind of headset suits which noise. What breaks a headset's protection. And what to do on the ground. This lesson gives each recommendation its reason, so the right answer is obvious when you see it.

## How noise damages hearing: a quick recap

You met the mechanism in the lesson on the anatomy of the ear. Here is the part that protection depends on. Sound is a pressure wave in the air. Your eardrum vibrates with it. The three small bones of the middle ear pass the vibration to the fluid-filled cochlea. And inside the cochlea, rows of fine hair cells bend with the fluid and fire the auditory nerve. Each hair cell is tuned to a band of frequencies.

Loud sound bends the hair cells harder than they are built for. After a noisy flight, some of them are fatigued. Sounds seem muffled, and your ears may ring. That is a temporary threshold shift, and it usually recovers within hours to a day.

Repeat the insult often enough, and hair cells die. They do not grow back. That is noise-induced hearing loss. It is permanent, painless and cumulative. It usually starts around 4,000 hertz, which is exactly where the consonants live that tell five from nine.

The damage depends on the dose: how loud, multiplied by how long. A commonly used guide is that about 85 decibels, averaged over 8 hours, risks permanent damage. And each 3 decibel increase halves the safe exposure time. That is because the decibel scale is logarithmic, and 3 decibels more is twice the sound energy.

[[diagram:noise-levels-and-hearing-damage]]
Picture a line falling from left to right, with sound level along the bottom and safe time up the page. At 85 decibels, on the left, the line is at 8 hours. Each 3 decibels to the right halves it. About 4 hours at 88 decibels. 2 hours at 91. 1 hour at 94. 30 minutes at 97. And only 15 minutes at 100 decibels, the top of a typical piston cabin.

That one rule tells you how every method of protection works. There are only two levers. Lower the level at your eardrum. Or shorten the time you spend at that level. Every recommendation in this lesson pulls one of the two. And the level lever is by far the stronger, because each 3 decibels you take off doubles the time you can safely spend.

Question. Why does taking 6 decibels off the level at your ear make such a large difference to your safe exposure time?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: each 3 decibels halves, or doubles, the sound energy, and the guide halves the safe time for every 3 decibels. Taking off 6 decibels doubles the time twice. So you can spend four times as long at the new level, for the same dose.

## Headsets: the first line of protection

The single most effective protection in a light aeroplane is a good aviation headset, worn properly, on every flight. It does two jobs at once. It blocks the cabin noise from your ears. And it delivers the radio and intercom directly into them, so you can hear speech without having to shout over the engine.

Start with how a passive headset blocks noise. A passive headset has no electronics for noise. It works by putting a barrier between the cabin and your ear. That barrier is a hard, heavy shell, the ear cup, lined with absorbent foam. The headband presses it against your head through a soft ear seal, or cushion. Sound reaching your ear has two ways in. Through the shell, which reflects and absorbs most of it. Or around the edge of the seal.

Two consequences follow from that design, and both matter in the exam.

The first: a passive barrier works much better on high-frequency sound than on low-frequency sound. Low-frequency sound has long wavelengths. It carries a lot of energy in slow, heavy pressure swings, which make the whole shell vibrate, and pass straight through. The deep drone of a piston engine, and the beat of the propeller, are low-frequency. So a passive headset takes the hiss and whine out of the cabin noise much more effectively than it takes out the drone.

The second: the protection is only as good as the seal. Sound behaves like water finding a gap. A small leak around the cushion lets a large share of the noise straight in, and the heavy shell is then doing nothing. Anything that holds the cushion off the skin opens a leak. The common ones are the arms of glasses, hair or a cap under the cushion, and cushions that have cracked or hardened with age and sun. A headset left on the coaming of an aeroplane, parked on the apron at Jandakot through a Perth summer, has cushions that go hard and crack far sooner than you would expect.

The practical recommendations follow directly, and there are four. First, check the attenuation rating when you choose a headset. Manufacturers publish how much the headset reduces noise, usually as a single number, and by frequency band. Second, replace the ear seals when they crack, harden or flatten. They are cheap. The hearing they protect is not. Third, keep the seal clear. Hair back, no cap under the cushion, and thin-armed glasses that let the cushion close around them. And fourth, adjust the headband so the cups press firmly and evenly. A loose headset is a leaking headset.

Now active noise reduction, ANR. An ANR headset is a passive headset with electronics added. A small microphone inside each ear cup listens to the noise that has got through. A circuit inverts it. It produces a sound wave that is the mirror image of the noise: a pressure peak wherever the noise has a trough, and a trough wherever it has a peak. It plays that through the speaker. Where the two waves meet, they cancel. That is called destructive interference. What reaches the eardrum is much quieter.

[[diagram:anr-noise-cancelling]]
Picture three waves stacked one above the other. At the top, the engine and propeller drone: a slow, low-frequency wave. In the middle, the opposite wave from the headset electronics, with a peak wherever the drone has a trough. Add the two together, and at the bottom is an almost flat line: much less noise at the eardrum. Underneath is the reminder that every headset also relies on passive protection, and that only works while the seal is intact.

The trick works best on exactly the noise that a passive headset handles worst. A low-frequency wave changes slowly. So the electronics have time to measure it, and produce the opposite wave in step with it. A high-frequency wave changes so fast that any small delay in the circuit puts the cancelling wave out of step. Then it adds to the noise instead of cancelling it. So ANR is designed to work on the low-frequency band, and the passive shell and seal look after the high frequencies. Together they cover the whole range. And ANR is especially effective against the low drone of a piston engine and propeller.

[[diagram:passive-vs-anr-attenuation-by-frequency]]
Picture a sketch of noise reduction going up the page, against pitch running from low on the left to high on the right. The curve for the passive shell and seal alone starts low on the left, letting much of the engine drone through, and rises to the right, where it blocks the hiss and whine well. A second curve, with ANR added, lifts the left-hand end, cancelling the drone, and then joins the passive curve on the right, where ANR adds little. Together they cover the whole range. And underneath both is the same condition: an unbroken ear seal.

There are two cautions. The first: the ANR electronics sit inside a passive headset, and they still depend on the passive seal. A broken seal lets in more noise than the circuit can cancel. The second: ANR needs power, from batteries or the aircraft. If it fails, a good ANR headset should still give you its passive protection. So you are no worse off than with a passive headset, but you will notice the drone come back. Carry spare batteries on a long flight.

The other benefit of ANR is fatigue. The low-frequency drone is the part of cabin noise you feel in your chest, and it is what wears you down over a long leg. Taking it out leaves you fresher at the far end, which is when you need to be sharp for the arrival.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. ANR does not make a headset better at everything. It is effective against low-frequency noise, the engine and propeller drone. High-frequency noise is handled by the passive shell and the seal. That is why the seal still matters on an ANR headset. And it is why a broken seal ruins either kind.

Question. A passive headset and an ANR headset are both worn with thick-framed glasses that lift the cushions. Which one loses protection?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: both. An ANR headset is a passive headset with electronics added, and both depend on the cushion sealing against the head. A gap lets noise in around the cushion. And the ANR circuit cannot cancel more noise than it was designed for.

## Earplugs: protection that goes inside the ear

An earplug blocks the ear canal itself, so noise has to pass through the plug to reach the eardrum. There are three common kinds. Soft foam plugs that you roll and insert. Pre-moulded flanged plugs. And plugs custom-moulded to your own ear canal. Good plugs, properly fitted, give good attenuation.

Like a headset, a plug works only if it seals. Foam plugs must be inserted properly, and there are three steps.

[[diagram:earplug-insertion]]
Picture three steps from left to right, and then the result. In the first, you roll the foam plug into a thin cylinder. In the second, you pull the top of the ear up and back, to straighten the canal. In the third, you push the plug well in, and hold it there while it expands to fill the canal. Then the result, two ears side by side. On one, the plug is well in, sealed and protecting. On the other, the plug rests in the outer part of the canal, half sticking out, and it protects very little.

A plug that keeps working its way out needs refitting.

Earplugs have two uses in flying. The first is in a very noisy aircraft: an open-cockpit or high-powered type, or an old aeroplane with a poor cabin. There, plugs can be worn under a headset for extra protection. Combining two barriers gives more protection than either alone. But it gives less than the two figures added together, because some sound reaches the inner ear through the bones of the skull, and no plug or cup stops that. The second use is on the ground. A pair of foam plugs in your pocket is protection you can use anywhere.

With plugs under a headset, check two things before take-off. Check you can still hear the radio clearly at a moderate volume. And check you can hear the aural warnings: the stall warning horn, and the gear warning in a retractable. Protection that hides the stall warning has traded one hazard for a worse one.

Cotton wool and tissue are not hearing protection. They do not seal the canal, and the fibres pass sound easily. And fingers in the ears on the apron are not a plan either.

Question. You wear foam plugs under your headset for the first time. What do you check before you taxi, and why?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: that you can still hear the radio at a reasonable volume, and that the stall warning and any other aural warnings are audible. Plugs lower everything you hear, wanted sounds included. And a missed warning is a safety risk on its own.

## Radio volume: the protection you can undo yourself

Here is the trap that catches pilots with good headsets. The engine is loud, and the radio is hard to understand, so you turn the volume up. But every bit of extra volume is extra sound energy, delivered straight to your eardrum. It comes on top of the cabin noise that leaks in. Turning the radio up to override the engine adds to the dose. It does not protect anything.

The better answer is to lower the noise the radio has to compete with. With a well-sealed headset, and especially with ANR, the background level at your ear falls. Then the radio becomes intelligible at a lower volume.

So the recommendation is to keep the radio and intercom as low as gives clear speech. Fix poor intelligibility with a better seal, or with ANR, not with more volume. If you find yourself turning the volume up through a flight, ask why. Often it is a seal that has slipped. Or it is ears fatigued by the noise dose so far.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. The option says: turn the volume up so you can hear the radio over the engine. That increases the sound at the eardrum, and it is the opposite of protection. The right answer always reduces the noise reaching the ear.

## On the ground: run-ups, refuelling and passengers

Hearing damage does not care whether you are airborne. You might be standing beside an aeroplane during an engine run. Walking past a run-up bay. Refuelling while another aircraft starts up on the next bay. Or helping passengers in and out near a running engine. All of these expose you to noise at least as loud as the cabin, and often louder. And there is no cabin shell around you.

The recommendation is simple. Wear protection whenever you are near running engines: on the apron, at the run-up bay, and around the refuelling point. Earmuffs or plugs for anyone working outside the aircraft. And the headset goes on before start, not after the engine is running.

Your passengers need the same. Give each passenger a headset, or at least plugs, for the flight. And show them how to fit it properly in the passenger briefing. A passenger in the back seat without protection on a two-hour flight is taking the full cabin dose.

## Managing exposure: the time lever

The dose is level multiplied by time. So the second lever is time. You cannot shorten a cross-country, but you can avoid adding to the day's total. There are three ways.

First, short breaks in quiet between flights. They let fatigued hair cells recover. Picture two training flights back to back, with the headset off in the run-up area between them. That is a bigger dose than the same two flights with a quiet break.

Second, avoid other loud noise the same day. A concert, power tools, a shooting range, a motorbike ride home. Your ears add up the whole day's noise, not just the aviation part.

And third, notice the warning signs. Ringing in the ears, called tinnitus, or muffled hearing after a flight, is a temporary threshold shift. Your ears are telling you the dose was too high. Treat it as a signal to improve your protection, not as normal.

Let's work one, a flight to Kalbarri. It is a 3-hour flight in a piston single, with a cabin level of about 94 decibels. Use the guide: 85 decibels for 8 hours, with the safe time halving for each 3 decibels more. First, what is the dose unprotected? Then, what is it if a well-sealed headset brings the level at your ear down to 82 decibels? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 8]]
Step one, the unprotected safe time at 94 decibels. 94 minus 85 is 9 decibels, which is three halvings. 8 hours divided by 8 is 1 hour.

Step two, the dose for a 3-hour flight unprotected. 3 hours divided by 1 hour is 3. That is three times the daily limit.

Step three, the protected safe time at 82 decibels. 85 minus 82 is 3 decibels below the guide, which is one doubling. 8 times 2 is 16 hours.

Step four, the dose for the same flight protected. 3 hours divided by 16 hours is 0.19, about a fifth of the daily limit.

So the same flight goes from three times the safe dose to a fifth of it, purely by lowering the level at the ear. One caution: the 82 decibels here is an illustration, not a headset rating. Check the rating of your own headset.

Here is one for you to try. Your headset keeps the level at your ear to 88 decibels on a 2-hour flight. Then you spend another hour in the run-up area, without protection, at about 91 decibels. What fraction of the daily guide have you used?
[[pause 8]]
The answer: at 88 decibels the guide gives 4 hours, so 2 hours is 0.5 of the limit. At 91 decibels it gives 2 hours, so 1 hour is another 0.5. The total is 0.5 plus 0.5, which is 1.0. The whole daily guide. The unprotected hour on the ground cost as much as the two hours in the air.

## Hearing checks: knowing where you stand

Noise-induced loss creeps up so gradually that you rarely notice it yourself, until it is well advanced. The only way to see it coming is to measure it.

Your hearing is assessed at your aviation medical examinations. That is done with audiometry, a hearing test across a range of frequencies, at intervals set under Part 67. Check the current requirements with your DAME, your designated aviation medical examiner, because they change.

Beyond the minimum, get an audiogram early in your flying career, as a baseline. Later tests can then be compared with it. A dip appearing around 4,000 hertz is the early signature of noise damage. It shows up long before you would hear the difference.

See a doctor if you notice ringing in the ears, or muffled hearing, that does not clear within a day. Or if you find yourself regularly asking ATC to say again. Persistent tinnitus or muffled hearing is not something to fly on and hope it goes away.

Question. Why get a baseline hearing test early in your training, rather than waiting for the medical to find a problem?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: noise damage is gradual and painless, so you will not notice it yourself until it is advanced. A baseline lets later tests show the first change, usually a dip around 4,000 hertz. And it shows it while there is still time to improve your protection.

## Bringing it together

You are planning a Saturday cross-country from Jandakot to Kalbarri. It is about three hours each way, in a 172 with a cabin level in the 90s. Your old passive headset has hard, cracked cushions, and you wear glasses with chunky frames. On the last long flight you landed tired, with ringing ears, after asking Perth Centre for two repeats.

Every part of that has a fix from this lesson. New ear seals, and thin-armed glasses, close the leak. An ANR headset takes out the low-frequency drone that was wearing you down. With the background lower, the radio goes down to a comfortable volume, and the repeats stop.

Foam plugs in your flight bag cover the run-up, and the refuel at Geraldton. The passenger gets a headset, and a thirty-second briefing on fitting it. Sunday is quiet: no concert, no power tools. And the ringing ears are now on your list for the next medical.

## Key points

Let's recap. The best protection is reducing the noise that reaches the ear.

Wear a well-sealed aviation headset on every flight. Add ANR for the low-frequency engine and propeller drone.

Keep the ear seals in good condition, and clear of glasses arms, hair and caps.

Use properly inserted earplugs where they are needed: under a headset in very noisy aircraft, and on the ground. But make sure you can still hear the radio and the warnings.

Keep the radio volume as low as gives clear speech.

Protect yourself and your passengers around running engines.

Limit your total daily exposure, and take quiet breaks.

And have regular hearing tests, from a baseline early in your career.

A final exam tip. Expect questions on four things. The most effective method: reduce the noise at the ear, with a well-sealed headset or plugs. What ANR is good at: low-frequency noise. What spoils a headset: a broken seal, from glasses arms, hair or worn cushions. And ground protection: wear it near running engines, and give passengers headsets. The wrong answers include turning the volume up, cotton wool, and the idea that protection is only needed on long flights. And have the figures from the anatomy of the ear to hand: about 85 decibels over 8 hours, with the safe time halving for each 3 decibels.

That's the end of PHFC 2.6.
