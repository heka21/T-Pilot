This is PHFC 2.12, Hypoxia. By the end you will be able to sort any cause of hypoxia into one of four types, say why night vision goes first and why a hypoxic pilot feels fine, describe the stages as you climb, quote a time of useful consciousness, and match the right remedy to each type. There are a couple of short calculations and several questions along the way, so pause whenever you want a moment to think.

## What the exam expects (PHFC 2.12)

The exam wants the causes of hypoxia, and then five things about it. First, what it does to night vision. Second, why its behavioural effects are so dangerous: losing self-criticism, over-confidence, and a false sense of security. Third, its symptoms, and how they progress as you climb. Fourth, what makes a person more susceptible. And fifth, how each form of hypoxia is prevented or treated.

## Why this matters

Hypoxia is the one physiological hazard that hides itself from the person who has it.

In WA you meet it in quieter ways than a decompression at FL250. A long eastbound leg over the Darling Range at 11,500 ft, to stay above the cloud. A night flight home from Geraldton. A cabin heater with a cracked exhaust muff, on a cold July morning out of Northam. Or a pilot who flies the day after giving blood.

In each case, the brain gets less oxygen than it needs. And the first thing to go is the judgement you would use to notice.

The exam asks you to sort causes into the four types, and to know that night vision suffers from about 5,000 ft. It asks you to recognise the behavioural danger, to quote an approximate time of useful consciousness, and to choose the right remedy for each type.

## What hypoxia is, and the four ways to get it (2.12.1)

In the lesson on the atmosphere, you followed oxygen from the air to the cells. Into the lungs, across the alveolar wall, onto haemoglobin, round the body with the heart, and into the cells that burn it.

Hypoxia is what happens when that chain fails to deliver enough. It is a lack of sufficient oxygen in the body's tissues to keep them working normally. The brain, and the retina of the eye, use oxygen fastest and have no reserve. So thinking and vision suffer first.

The chain has four links, and each one can break. That gives the four types of hypoxia. The exam expects you to place any cause in the right one.

[[diagram:hypoxia-types-and-causes]]
Picture the chain drawn left to right. Air, then lungs, then blood, then circulation, then cells. Under the links hang four columns, one for each type of hypoxia. Hypoxic hypoxia breaks it at the lungs, from altitude. Anaemic hypoxia breaks it in the blood, from carbon monoxide. Stagnant hypoxia breaks it in the circulation, from g or cold. And histotoxic hypoxia breaks it in the cells, from alcohol. All four are insidious.

Now each type in turn.

First, hypoxic hypoxia, also called altitude hypoxia. This is the air link. The blood and the heart are fine. But the air arriving in the lungs has too low a partial pressure of oxygen to load the haemoglobin fully. This is the hypoxia of altitude. Flying high without supplemental oxygen, or a pressurised cabin losing its pressure. For a private pilot, it is the one most often meant by hypoxia.

Second, anaemic hypoxia, also called hypaemic hypoxia. This is the carrier link. The air is fine, but the blood cannot carry enough oxygen. Either there is too little haemoglobin, from anaemia, blood loss, or a recent blood donation. Or the haemoglobin is occupied by something else. That something is usually carbon monoxide, from a leaking exhaust in the cabin heater, or from smoking. Carbon monoxide binds to haemoglobin far more tightly than oxygen. So even a small amount takes many carriers out of service.

Third, stagnant hypoxia. This is the delivery link. The blood is loaded, but it is not getting where it is needed fast enough. Positive g in a steep turn pools blood in the legs, away from the head and eyes. That is in the lesson on acceleration g effects. Cold narrows the blood vessels in the limbs. Shock, heart problems, and sitting cramped for hours do the same.

And fourth, histotoxic hypoxia. This is the user link. Oxygen reaches the cells, but they cannot use it, because they have been poisoned. Alcohol is the common cause for pilots. Some drugs, and at the extreme, cyanide, also do it.

So, to put names to links once more. Hypoxic is low oxygen pressure in the lungs. Anaemic is blood that cannot carry it. Stagnant is blood not circulating well. And histotoxic is cells that cannot use the oxygen delivered.

The types add together. A smoker with a mild hangover, flying at 10,000 ft on a cold morning, has a little of three of them at once. And the effects stack.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Carbon monoxide poisoning happens at any altitude, and its symptoms look like altitude hypoxia, so it is tempting to file them together. But it is anaemic hypoxia. The problem is the blood's carrying capacity, not the air pressure. And alcohol is histotoxic, not anaemic. The blood carries oxygen normally. The cells cannot use it.

Question. A pilot gave blood the day before flying. Which type of hypoxia are they at greater risk of, and why?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: anaemic hypoxia. Losing blood reduces the amount of haemoglobin available to carry oxygen. So even at a normal partial pressure, the blood delivers less oxygen to the tissues, until the body has replaced it.

## The real danger: you feel fine (2.12.1 b)

Picture a pilot an hour into a cruise, at a height where they are slowly becoming hypoxic. They feel good. Better than good. Relaxed, a little pleased with themselves, sure that the flight is going well.

They have not updated the fuel log for a while. They let a radio call slide. Their heading has wandered ten degrees. But none of that bothers them. If you asked them, they would tell you they are fine, and they would believe it.

This is what makes hypoxia so dangerous. It is insidious. It comes on gradually and without pain. And the part of the brain that suffers first is the part that does the checking.

The typical early behavioural changes are four. First, euphoria, a sense of well-being, or sometimes the opposite, irritability or belligerence. Second, over-confidence, a belief that you are performing well when you are not. Third, lack of self-criticism, so errors go unnoticed, or seem not to matter. And fourth, a false sense of security, so a deteriorating situation does not alarm you.

Meanwhile judgement, memory, arithmetic and coordination are all getting worse. Remember from the lesson on the atmosphere that the urge to breathe comes from carbon dioxide, not oxygen. So there is no breathlessness either. The person who has hypoxia is very often the last to recognise it.

The conclusion for your flying is blunt. You cannot rely on how you feel to tell you whether you are hypoxic. You rely on the altitude rules. On supplemental oxygen when you are above them. On knowing your own early symptoms from training. And on objective checks: the fuel log that does not add up, the radio call you missed, and a pulse oximeter if you carry one.

Question. Why can't a pilot rely on feeling well as evidence that they are not hypoxic?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because hypoxia's early effects include euphoria, over-confidence and loss of self-criticism, and it produces no breathlessness. The faculties you would use to judge your own condition are the first to be impaired. So feeling fine is exactly what a hypoxic pilot reports.

## Night vision goes first (2.12.1 a)

Think about the back of your eye. The retina has two kinds of light sensors. Cones, packed into the centre, give colour and fine detail in daylight. Rods, spread around the edge, are far more sensitive. They let you see in dim light, in black and white. You covered the anatomy in the lesson on vision, spatial disorientation and illusions.

[[diagram:eye-anatomy-rods-and-cones]]
Picture a cross-section of the eye, with light coming in from the left, through the cornea, the pupil and the lens, and focused on the retina at the back, on the right. Right in the centre of the retina is the fovea, packed with cones and no rods. The rods lie around the edge. Where the optic nerve leaves, there are no receptors at all, and that is the blind spot. What to notice: the rods round the edge do your night seeing, and they are among the most oxygen-hungry cells in the body. So they suffer before you notice anything else.

The retina is part of the brain in origin, and it uses oxygen at a very high rate. The rods are especially sensitive to the oxygen supply. That makes night vision the earliest measurable casualty of altitude.

It begins to deteriorate from about 5,000 ft cabin altitude. That is well below the height at which you would notice any other effect. And by 10,000 ft it may be reduced substantially. Dim lights look dimmer. The horizon on a dark night is harder to find. And you need more light to read the chart.

For night flying, the practical rule is to consider supplemental oxygen at lower altitudes than you would by day. Carbon monoxide and smoking make it worse at any height, because they reduce oxygen delivery before you have even left the ground. A smoker's night vision at sea level can be like a non-smoker's at several thousand feet.

Question. From about what cabin altitude does hypoxia start to degrade night vision, and why so low?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: from about 5,000 ft. The rods of the retina are among the most oxygen-sensitive cells in the body. So they are affected by the small fall in oxygen partial pressure, long before thinking or coordination are noticeably impaired.

## Symptoms and how they develop with altitude (2.12.1 c)

Hypoxia does not switch on at a particular height. It develops by degrees, as the oxygen partial pressure falls. Textbooks divide it into four stages by cabin altitude. The heights are approximate, and vary with the person and the day.

The pattern is a band every 5,000 ft or so above 10,000 ft. The indifferent stage runs from sea level to 10,000 ft. The compensatory stage runs from 10,000 to 15,000 ft. The disturbance stage runs from 15,000 to 20,000 ft. And above 20,000 ft is the critical stage.

Hear the stage names as a story.

In the indifferent stage, the body is unbothered. Haemoglobin stays nearly fully loaded, and only night vision shows a change, from about 5,000 ft. Little else is noticeable.

In the compensatory stage, the body notices and fights back. Breathing and heart rate increase, to keep the oxygen flowing. And for a while it succeeds. That is exactly why the effects of a long cruise there, drowsiness, poor judgement and reduced coordination, build after some time.

In the disturbance stage, the compensation is no longer enough. The impairment is clear: poor judgement, memory and coordination, cyanosis, tunnel vision and euphoria. The symptoms are obvious to an observer, if not to the pilot.

In the critical stage, the brain is failing. There is rapid mental and physical incapacity, then unconsciousness.

[[diagram:hypoxia-stages-by-altitude]]
Picture a column of cabin altitude rising from sea level at the bottom to 25,000 ft at the top, split into four bands. At the bottom, indifferent, up to 10,000 ft, with a mark at about 5,000 ft where night vision starts to fade. Above it, compensatory, up to 15,000 ft. Then disturbance, up to 20,000 ft. And at the top, critical. What to notice: each stage creeps in while you feel fine, and above 20,000 ft incapacity comes fast.

The symptoms themselves vary between individuals. That is why you are told to learn your own. The common ones fall into five groups.

First, mental. Euphoria or belligerence, impaired judgement, slow thinking, poor memory, and drowsiness.

Second, physical. Headache, dizziness, tingling, hot and cold flushes, an increased breathing rate, and poor coordination.

Third, visual. Blurred vision, tunnel vision, and the earlier loss of night vision.

Fourth, what others see. Cyanosis, a blue tinge to the lips and fingernails, as the blood carries less oxygen.

And finally, unconsciousness.

Several of these, dizziness, tingling and visual disturbance, are also the symptoms of hyperventilation. And the two often go together. An anxious pilot who realises something is wrong at altitude can start over-breathing.

[[diagram:hyperventilation-vs-hypoxia]]
Picture two columns side by side. On the left, hyperventilation. It is too little carbon dioxide, from over-breathing. It can happen at any altitude, even on the ground, it comes on fairly rapidly, often with anxiety, and its tell-tale signs are tingling and muscle spasm. On the right, hypoxia. It is too little oxygen reaching the tissues, mainly at altitude. It is gradual and insidious, and its tell-tale signs are euphoria, poor judgement and cyanosis. Along the bottom, shared by both: dizziness, tingling and visual disturbance. If you are unsure at altitude, treat for hypoxia first.

Now, time of useful consciousness. At high altitude, the question is not whether you will be affected, but how long you have to do something about it.

Time of useful consciousness, or TUC, is the time from losing your oxygen supply to losing the ability to take effective action. That means the ability to put on a mask, or to start a descent. It ends well before you become unconscious.

The standard textbook values are for a seated, resting person. The pattern is that the time shrinks from minutes to seconds as you climb. At 18,000 ft, it is 20 to 30 minutes. At 22,000 ft, 5 to 10 minutes. At 25,000 ft, 3 to 5 minutes. At 30,000 ft, 1 to 2 minutes. At 35,000 ft, 30 to 60 seconds. And at 40,000 ft, only 15 to 20 seconds.

[[diagram:hypoxia-time-of-useful-consciousness]]
Picture six bars, one under the other, with the altitude on the left, from 18,000 ft at the top to 40,000 ft at the bottom. Each bar runs out to the right along a time scale, from seconds to half an hour. The top bar is long, 20 to 30 minutes. Each bar below is shorter, down to 15 to 20 seconds at the bottom. A note across the middle marks the value after a rapid decompression at 25,000 ft: 1.5 to 2.5 minutes. What to notice: time of useful consciousness falls from tens of minutes to seconds, and a rapid decompression roughly halves it.

TUC is shortened by exercise, smoking and fatigue. And it is shortened by a rapid decompression, which roughly halves it. The sudden drop in pressure pulls oxygen back out of the blood into the lungs, so you start from a lower reserve.

Let's work one. A pressurised aircraft cruising at FL250 suffers a rapid decompression. TUC at 25,000 ft, for a resting person, is about 3 to 5 minutes. And a rapid decompression roughly halves it. How long does the pilot have? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
Start with the value at 25,000 ft: 3 to 5 minutes. Now halve it for the rapid decompression. 3 divided by 2 is 1.5. And 5 divided by 2 is 2.5. So the pilot has 1.5 to 2.5 minutes.

Oxygen masks on immediately, then start the emergency descent. Every second spent diagnosing the problem before the mask is on comes out of a budget measured in tens of seconds. The answer again: 1.5 to 2.5 minutes.

Now try one yourself. A pressurised piston twin, cruising at 30,000 ft, loses its cabin pressure suddenly. Roughly how long does the pilot have to get the mask on?
[[pause 6]]
The value at 30,000 ft for a resting person is 1 to 2 minutes. A rapid decompression roughly halves it. 1 divided by 2 is 0.5, and 2 divided by 2 is 1. So the pilot has about 30 seconds to 1 minute. Mask first, then descend.

Question. What is the difference between time of useful consciousness and time to unconsciousness?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: TUC ends when you can no longer take effective action to save yourself, such as putting on a mask or starting a descent. You may remain conscious for some time after that, but uselessly. So TUC is always the shorter, and it is the one that matters.

## Who gets it sooner (2.12.1 d)

Two pilots fly side by side at the same altitude. One is fine. The other is drowsy, and making mistakes.

The difference is susceptibility. That is anything that lowers the oxygen supply, raises the oxygen demand, or adds a second type of hypoxia on top of the first. Keep those three ideas in mind as we go through the list.

First, higher altitude, and longer time there. The lower the partial pressure, the faster the effects. And the compensatory stage only holds for so long.

Second, a rapid rate of ascent. The body has no time to adjust.

Third, physical activity. Moving around, or even working hard at the controls in turbulence, raises the oxygen demand of the muscles.

Fourth, cold. Shivering burns oxygen. And cold narrows the blood vessels, adding stagnant hypoxia.

Fifth, smoking and carbon monoxide. Carbon monoxide already occupies some of the haemoglobin before you take off, adding anaemic hypoxia.

Sixth, alcohol and some medications. These impair the cells' use of oxygen, adding histotoxic hypoxia. And alcohol's effect is stronger at altitude.

Seventh, fatigue, illness, anaemia, and recent blood donation. Those mean less reserve, and less carrying capacity.

And eighth, poor fitness, and individual differences. Some people simply tolerate altitude less well. And the same person varies from day to day.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Fitness helps, but it does not make you immune. A fit athlete at 15,000 ft without oxygen is hypoxic. They may just compensate a little longer. No amount of fitness or experience changes the partial pressure of oxygen in the air.

Question. Why does smoking before flight increase your susceptibility to altitude hypoxia?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: smoke contains carbon monoxide, which occupies some of the haemoglobin before you leave the ground. You start the flight with anaemic hypoxia already. So the fall in oxygen partial pressure at altitude affects you sooner and harder.

## Combating hypoxia (2.12.1 e)

Each type breaks a different link in the chain, so each needs a different fix. Match the remedy to the link.

Start with altitude hypoxia, the hypoxic kind. It is a pressure problem. So the fixes are the two from the lesson on the atmosphere: more total pressure, or a higher oxygen fraction.

In practice, that means four things. First, descend below 10,000 ft. Second, use supplemental oxygen. The requirements for unpressurised aircraft above 10,000 ft are in the Part 91 MOS. There are limited allowances, so check the current Part 91 MOS for the exact conditions, and for the passenger requirements. Third, in a pressurised aircraft after a decompression, oxygen mask first, then descend. And fourth, learn your own symptoms, in an altitude chamber or in hypoxia awareness training, so that you recognise your own early signs.

Next, anaemic hypoxia. It is a carrier problem. Remove the carbon monoxide source, and give the haemoglobin back to oxygen. Cabin heat off. Fresh-air vents open. Use 100 percent oxygen if it is carried. And land as soon as possible. Prevent it by not smoking before flight, not flying after blood donation, and having the exhaust and heater muff inspected.

[[diagram:carbon-monoxide-cabin-heater]]
Picture a cutaway of the aeroplane's nose, with the engine on the right and the cabin on the left. Cabin heat is fresh air, warmed in a muff wrapped around the exhaust. A crack in the exhaust inside the muff puts colourless, odourless carbon monoxide straight into the heating air, and out at the pilot's feet. The response: heat off, vents open, and land.

Then stagnant hypoxia. It is a delivery problem. Limit the g, keep warm, and move your limbs on long flights.

And finally histotoxic hypoxia. It cannot be treated in the air. Oxygen does little to help when the cells cannot use it. The only remedy is prevention. No alcohol before flight, observing at least the legal minimum time between drinking and flying. And no unapproved drugs. The rules are in the lessons on toxic hazards and on conditions of flight.

Let's work one. You are crossing the Darling Range eastbound in a non-turbocharged single. You plan 11,500 ft for an hour, to get above turbulence and cloud. Pause if you'd like to think it through.
[[pause 6]]
First, is the plan above 10,000 ft? Yes, by 1,500 ft. Second, what applies? Check the Part 91 MOS oxygen requirement for the planned time at that level. Third, what else? Remember that your judgement may be impaired even if you feel well. And night, smoking or fatigue would make it worse. So 9,500 ft may be the better plan.

Here's another trap the exam likes to set. Hypoxia and hyperventilation share symptoms: dizziness, tingling and visual disturbance. And the exam offers "breathe slowly into a bag" for a pilot at altitude. But hypoxia brings euphoria and cyanosis, and happens at altitude. Hyperventilation brings muscle spasm, and can happen anywhere. At altitude, with oxygen available, treat for hypoxia first.

Question. Why does supplemental oxygen fix altitude hypoxia, but do little for histotoxic hypoxia?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: altitude hypoxia is a shortage of oxygen pressure in the lungs, which a higher oxygen fraction restores. In histotoxic hypoxia, the oxygen is delivered, but the cells are poisoned and cannot use it. So more oxygen does not help. The cure is not drinking, or not taking the drug, in the first place.

## Bringing it together

It is a winter evening at Albany. You are planning to fly home to Jandakot after dinner, in an aeroplane whose heater you need on a cold night. The forecast has cloud tops near 9,000 ft along the coast. And you had a cigarette after the meal.

Run it through the lesson.

It will be night. So your night vision starts to suffer from about 5,000 ft. And the smoke has already put carbon monoxide on your haemoglobin, so it will suffer more.

A cruise above the cloud at 10,500 ft would put you above 10,000 ft, where the Part 91 MOS oxygen requirement needs checking. And it would put you into the compensatory stage, where you would feel fine.

The cold and the heater add their own risks. Shivering raises your oxygen demand. And a cracked heater muff would add more carbon monoxide.

Each factor is small. Together, they stack across three of the four types. A lower route under the cloud, or a night's sleep and a morning departure, removes most of them.

## Key points

Let's recap.

Hypoxia is too little oxygen reaching the tissues. There are four types. Hypoxic, from altitude and low partial pressure. Anaemic, where the blood cannot carry it: carbon monoxide, smoking, blood loss or donation. Stagnant, from poor circulation: g, cold and shock. And histotoxic, where the cells cannot use it: alcohol and drugs.

Night vision is the first casualty. It begins to deteriorate from about 5,000 ft cabin altitude, and may be substantially reduced by 10,000 ft.

Hypoxia is insidious. Euphoria, over-confidence, loss of self-criticism and a false sense of security mean the pilot feels fine while judgement fails. Rely on rules and oxygen, not on how you feel.

The stages. Indifferent, to 10,000 ft. Compensatory, 10,000 to 15,000 ft. Disturbance, 15,000 to 20,000 ft. Critical, above 20,000 ft. Time of useful consciousness is about 20 to 30 minutes at 18,000 ft, 3 to 5 minutes at 25,000 ft, and seconds above 35,000 ft. A rapid decompression roughly halves it.

Susceptibility rises with altitude and time, a fast ascent, activity, cold, smoking, alcohol and drugs, fatigue, illness, anaemia, blood donation, and poor fitness.

And the remedies. Altitude hypoxia: descend, or use oxygen, with the Part 91 MOS requirement above 10,000 ft. Anaemic: remove the carbon monoxide source, and use oxygen. Stagnant: limit g, keep warm, and move. Histotoxic: prevention only.

A final exam tip. Expect one question on which type of hypoxia a cause produces. Carbon monoxide is anaemic, alcohol is histotoxic, and g and cold are stagnant. Expect one on why hypoxia is dangerous: the pilot does not recognise it. Expect the 5,000 ft night vision figure. Expect a time of useful consciousness value, and 3 to 5 minutes at 25,000 ft is the favourite. And expect the response to suspected hypoxia: oxygen if available, and descend below 10,000 ft.
