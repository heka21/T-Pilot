This is PHFC 2.9, Acceleration g effects. By the end you will be able to say what positive and negative g do to your heart and circulation, to your vision and to your consciousness, in the order they happen, and what raises or lowers your tolerance. There are a couple of calculations and a few questions along the way, so pause whenever you want a moment to think.

## What the exam expects (PHFC 2.9)

The exam wants one thing from you, in three parts. Describe what positive and negative accelerations do, first to the heart and circulation, second to vision, and third to consciousness.

## Why this matters

You will feel g on your first lesson of steep turns. In a 60 degree turn over the Jandakot training area, you are pressed into the seat at twice your weight. Your arms feel heavy, and your head wants to sag. Pull out of a spiral dive too hard, and you can go well beyond that in a second. Hit a sharp down-gust over the scarp, or push the nose over abruptly, and you float up against the harness in negative g.

None of this is exotic. What makes it worth understanding is the order in which things fail. Under positive g, your vision goes before your consciousness. That gives you a warning, provided you know what it means and ease off.

The exam asks for that order. It asks what g does to the blood, what the difference is between positive and negative g, and what raises or lowers your tolerance.

## What g is

When an aeroplane accelerates, everything in it, you included, has to be pushed along with it. In straight and level flight, the seat pushes up on you with a force equal to your weight. That is 1 g.

In a turn or a pull-up, the wing makes more lift than the weight. The aeroplane accelerates towards the centre of the turn, and the seat has to push on you harder to take you with it. You feel heavier.

So g is that force, expressed as a multiple of normal gravity. It is the same number as the aeroplane's load factor, the ratio of lift to weight, which you met in the lessons on turning and on stalling, spinning and spiral dives. At 2 g, everything weighs twice as much. Your head, your arms, and the blood in your body.

For the body, direction matters. The convention uses the long axis of the body, from head to feet.

The first is positive g. It pushes you down into the seat, from head towards feet. It comes from pull-ups, from steep turns, and from pulling out of a dive or a spiral.

The second is negative g. It lifts you out of the seat, from feet towards head. It comes from pushing the nose over sharply, from outside manoeuvres, and from severe turbulence.

In a level, balanced turn, the load factor depends only on the bank angle. The load factor equals one over the cosine of the bank angle.

Picture the aeroplane from behind, rolling into steeper and steeper bank, with the load factor counting up beside it. At first it creeps up slowly. Then, as the bank passes 60 degrees, it climbs steeply.

Let's work one. A level, balanced turn at 45 degrees of bank. The pilot's normal weight is the equivalent of 80 kg. What load factor, and what felt weight? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
The cosine of 45 degrees is 0.707. One over 0.707 is 1.41, so the load factor is 1.41 g. The felt weight is 80 times 1.41, which is 113 kg equivalent.

So at 45 degrees you feel about 40 percent heavier. That is comfortable. But every part of you is heavier by the same proportion, including the blood the heart has to lift to your brain. The answer again: 1.41 g, and a felt weight of 113 kg.

Now try one yourself. What g do you pull in a level turn at 70 degrees of bank?
[[pause 6]]
The cosine of 70 degrees is 0.342. One over 0.342 is 2.92, so call it 2.9 g. The step from 45 degrees, at 1.41 g, to 70 degrees doubles the g. That is because one over the cosine climbs steeply as the bank passes 60 degrees. At 2.9 g, a relaxed pilot is in the range where heaviness and the first greying of vision can begin. So a level turn at 70 degrees is 2.9 g.

## Why g is a problem of blood

The body copes well with being heavy for a short time. Muscles and bones can take a lot. The real problem is blood, because blood is a fluid, and fluids flow downhill under load.

Your brain sits roughly 30 cm above your heart. At 1 g, the heart pumps blood up that column to the head all day without effort. The pressure needed to lift a column of fluid depends on three things: its density, its height, and the acceleration acting on it. Put in words, the pressure equals the density of the blood, times the load factor times gravity, times the height of the column.

The height and the density of blood do not change. But at a higher load factor, the effective gravity is that many times larger. So the pressure the heart must find, just to get blood up to head level, rises in direct proportion to the g. At 3 g, the column weighs three times as much. Above a certain g, the heart cannot supply that extra pressure, and the blood reaching the head dwindles.

There is a second, slower effect lower down. Below the heart, the same extra weight of blood stretches the veins of the legs and abdomen, and blood pools there. Less blood returns to the heart. So the heart has less to pump out, and the pressure at head level falls further.

[[diagram:g-blood-column]]
Picture two seated pilots, side by side. On the left, one at 1 g in straight and level flight. On the right, one at 3 g in a steep turn or pull-up. In each, a column of blood rises about 30 cm from the heart up to the eyes. In the pilot on the right, that column weighs three times as much, and below the heart, blood is pooling in the legs and lower body. The point to notice is that the pressure needed grows in step with the g.

The body fights back. Pressure sensors in the arteries of the neck detect the fall. Reflexes then speed up the heart and tighten the blood vessels, to raise the pressure. But those reflexes take several seconds to act. A slow build-up of g gives them time. A sudden one hits before they can respond. That is why the same g is far worse when it arrives quickly.

[[diagram:g-effects-on-vision]]
Now picture a strip of small windows, each showing the pilot's view at a different g. On the far left is negative g, about minus 2 to minus 3 g, where blood rushes to the head and the view turns red. Next is 1 g, a normal view. Then, moving right, the view goes grey and dim at 2 to 3 g, narrows to a tunnel at 3 to 4 g, goes black at 4 to 5 g, and at 5 to 6 g and above, the pilot is unconscious. What to notice: grey-out, tunnel vision and black-out all come before loss of consciousness. Vision goes first, so ease off at the first greying.

Question. Why is a sudden pull to 3 g more likely to affect your vision than the same 3 g built up over several seconds?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the reflexes that raise the heart rate and tighten the blood vessels take several seconds to act. With a slow onset, they keep up, and the pressure at head level holds. With a sudden one, the pressure falls before they respond.

## Positive g: circulation, vision, consciousness

Now let's take positive g through the three parts the exam names. First the circulation, then vision, then consciousness.

First, the circulation. Under positive g, blood is driven towards the feet. It pools in the legs and lower body. Less returns to the heart, the heart pumps out less, and the blood pressure at head level falls. Blood flow to the eyes and the brain drops. The heart rate climbs as the body tries to compensate.

Starving the tissues of oxygen because the blood is not circulating where it is needed is a form of stagnant hypoxia. You'll find more on that in the lesson on hypoxia.

[[diagram:hypoxia-types-and-causes]]
Picture the oxygen chain as a line running from the air, into the lungs, into the blood, around the body, and into the cells. Each of the four types of hypoxia breaks that chain at a different link. Stagnant hypoxia breaks it at the circulation. Positive g causes stagnant hypoxia: the blood is carrying its oxygen, but it is not circulating to the brain and eyes.

Second, vision. The eyes are affected before the brain, for a mechanical reason. The eyeball is a pressurised ball. The fluid inside it pushes back on the small blood vessels feeding the retina, so blood must arrive at more than that internal pressure to get in. As g rises, and the pressure at head level falls, the retina's supply fails, while the brain, which has no such back-pressure, is still getting enough.

The effects arrive in a fixed order. The figures are approximate, for a relaxed, unprotected person, and individuals vary widely. The pattern is that vision dims, then narrows, then goes, and only after that do you lose consciousness.

Here are the bands. From 2 to 3 g, you feel heavy, and it is hard to raise your arms and head. Grey-out may begin. From 3 to 4 g comes grey-out, the loss of colour and dimming, and tunnel vision, the loss of peripheral vision. From 4 to 5 g comes black-out, a total loss of vision while you are still conscious. And from 5 to 6 g and above comes G-LOC, g-induced loss of consciousness.

Take each stage as a picture. Grey-out is the world losing colour and brightness, as if someone turned down the contrast. Tunnel vision is the edges closing in, until you see the world through a narrowing tube. Black-out is total loss of sight. Throughout all three you are conscious. You can hear and think. And if you relax the back pressure, your vision recovers within seconds as the blood returns.

Third, consciousness. Beyond black-out, the brain's own supply becomes too small, and the pilot loses consciousness. That is G-LOC, short for g-induced loss of consciousness. Recovery is not instant. There is a period of confusion and disorientation afterwards, often with no memory of the event. The total incapacitation can last 15 to 30 seconds or more.

Near the ground, that is fatal. In a spiral dive that is already losing height fast, it is fatal at any height a light aeroplane normally flies.

The order is the useful part. Vision goes before consciousness. Grey-out and tunnel vision are a warning that the blood supply to the head is failing. The only correct response is to ease the back pressure at once. With a very rapid onset of g, the visual stages can pass too quickly to notice. That is one more reason to apply g smoothly.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Grey-out is not a limit marker, to fly up to and hold. It is the first sign that the blood supply to your head is failing, and the next stages can follow in seconds. Ease off at the first greying. And the order is grey-out, tunnel vision, black-out, then unconsciousness. An exam option that puts unconsciousness before black-out is wrong.

Question. Why does the retina lose its blood supply before the brain does?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the fluid pressure inside the eyeball pushes back against the vessels feeding the retina, so blood must arrive at more than that pressure to get in. As g lowers the pressure at head level, the retina's supply fails first, while the brain, with no such back-pressure, still gets enough.

Now, raising your tolerance. Everything that raises your tolerance does one of two things. It gives the reflexes time, or it helps hold blood above the heart. There are three ways.

First, apply g gradually, so the reflexes can keep up.

Second, tense the leg and abdominal muscles. That squeezes blood out of the lower body and stops it pooling. And use a straining manoeuvre. You strain against a closed throat while breathing in short bursts. That raises the pressure in the chest, and the blood pressure with it.

And third, be fit, rested, well hydrated and fed. Low blood sugar and dehydration both lower tolerance.

Turn that around, and everything that weakens the body's response lowers your tolerance. Alcohol and hangover. Hypoxia. Heat. Fatigue. And illness. A pilot who is dehydrated after a hot morning on the apron at Jandakot greys out sooner in the afternoon's steep turns.

The aeroplane can usually take more than you can. A normal category aeroplane is built for a limit load factor of plus 3.8 g. That sits right in the band where an unprotected pilot greys out or blacks out.

[[diagram:load-factor-limits]]
Picture a scale of load factor running left to right, negative on the left and positive on the right, with three bars stacked one above the other, one for each category of aeroplane. Each bar reaches right to its positive limit and left to its negative one. The normal category reaches plus 3.8 g and minus 1.52 g. The utility category reaches plus 4.4 and minus 1.76 g. The aerobatic category reaches plus 6.0 and minus 3.0 g. For a normal category aeroplane the ultimate load is 1.5 times the limit, 5.7 g. What to notice: the normal category's plus 3.8 g sits in the grey-out to black-out band for a relaxed pilot. In a light aeroplane, your eyes are an early warning of the airframe's limit too.

## Negative g: circulation, vision, consciousness

Push the nose over sharply, and the g reverses. Now the force acts from feet to head, and the blood goes the other way.

In a training aeroplane, you meet negative g less often than positive, and usually briefly. It might be a sharp down-gust in rotor below the scarp on an easterly day. An abrupt push to avoid traffic. Or a careless pushover at the top of a zoom climb. Loose items float up from the floor, and your head heads for the roof. A firmly tightened harness is what keeps you in the seat, and in reach of the controls.

First, the circulation. Blood is forced towards the head. The pressure in the vessels of the head and neck rises. You feel fullness and pressure in the head, and a headache. With enough negative g, small blood vessels in the eyes and face can burst.

The same pressure sensors in the neck now read a large rise, and the reflex is the reverse of before. They slow the heart. That causes a second problem. When the negative g suddenly stops, the heart is beating slowly, the blood drains back down, and the flow to the brain can drop sharply.

Second, vision. The eyes become congested with blood. Vision may be reddened or blurred, with watering and pain. That is red-out. The red colour is often put down to the lower eyelid being pushed up across the eye, rather than to blood in the eye itself.

Third, consciousness. Tolerance to negative g is much lower than to positive g. Around minus 2 to minus 3 g is uncomfortable to painful. Prolonged negative g can lead to confusion and unconsciousness. Light aeroplanes are also built for less of it. The normal category negative limit is minus 1.52 g.

The most dangerous combination is the push-pull effect. That is going from negative g straight into positive g. During the negative g, the heart slows and the blood vessels relax, in response to the high pressure in the head. When positive g follows immediately, those defences are pointing the wrong way. The heart is slow, the vessels are open, and the blood drains from the head faster than usual.

So positive g tolerance is sharply reduced, and G-LOC can come suddenly, at a g you would normally handle. A pushover followed by a hard pull sets it up. So does a recovery from an inverted attitude.

Question. Why can a pull-up straight after a sharp pushover cause G-LOC at a g you would normally tolerate?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: during the negative g, the high pressure in the head slows the heart and relaxes the blood vessels. When positive g follows at once, those reflexes are working against you, so blood leaves the head faster than they can respond. This is the push-pull effect.

## Bringing it together

You are practising unusual attitude recoveries with your instructor, in the training area south of Jandakot, on a bumpy summer afternoon. The instructor hands you the aeroplane nose-low, banked 60 degrees, with the speed building. It is a spiral dive.

Your instinct is to haul back. Trace what that does. With the wings banked, pulling only tightens the spiral. And pulling hard and fast at high speed sends the g past plus 4 in a second, too quickly for your reflexes. Your vision greys and narrows as the ground rushes up.

The correct recovery, from the lesson on stalling, spinning and spiral dives, is also the g-friendly one. Throttle closed. Roll the wings level. Then ease out of the dive, keeping the pull smooth, so the g stays moderate and builds slowly. You tense your legs and abdomen as you pull. If the edges of your view start to grey, you relax the back pressure a little.

Moments later, a sharp down-gust lifts you against the harness. You note the negative g. And you do not follow it with a hard pull, because you know the push-pull effect.

[[diagram:spin-vs-spiral-dive]]
Picture a spin and a spiral dive side by side. On the left, the spin. Both wings are stalled, the airspeed is low and roughly steady, and the rate of descent is high. On the right, the spiral dive. The wings are not stalled, the airspeed is high and increasing, and the rate of descent is very high. Rolling level first, then easing out gently, keeps the g within what you and the airframe can take.

## Key points

Let's recap.

The heart and circulation. Positive g pools blood in the legs and lowers the pressure at head level. The heart has to lift the blood as many times harder as the g. The reflexes take several seconds, so a rapid onset is worst. Negative g drives blood to the head, raises the head pressure, and slows the heart.

Vision. Under positive g, vision fails first, because the eye's internal pressure resists its blood supply. Grey-out, then tunnel vision, then black-out while still conscious. That is roughly plus 3 to plus 5 g for a relaxed person. Negative g brings red-out, with congested and painful eyes.

Consciousness. Beyond black-out comes G-LOC, at roughly plus 5 to 6 g and above, with 15 to 30 seconds or more of incapacitation and confusion. Negative g tolerance is far lower. About minus 2 to minus 3 g is painful. And the push-pull effect cuts positive g tolerance sharply.

To raise tolerance, use gradual g, muscle tensing and straining, fitness, hydration and food. Alcohol, hypoxia, heat, fatigue and illness all lower it.

A final exam tip. Know the order under rising positive g: grey-out, tunnel vision, black-out, then unconsciousness. Vision goes before consciousness, so grey-out is the signal to ease off. Positive g pools blood in the lower body and starves the head. Negative g sends blood to the head, with red-out, headache and a slowing heart. Expect a load factor question, where the load factor in a level turn is one over the cosine of the bank angle. And expect a question on which things increase tolerance. The answers are gradual onset, tensing and straining, fitness and hydration. Never alcohol or hypoxia.
