This is PHFC 2.11, The atmosphere and associated problems. By the end you will be able to say what the air is made of, how its temperature and pressure change as you climb, how your lungs and blood move oxygen around, and why the partial pressure of oxygen, not its percentage, is the number that counts. There are a couple of calculations and several questions along the way, so pause whenever you want a moment to think.

## What the exam expects (PHFC 2.11)

The exam wants three things from you. First, the make-up of the air by its main gases, and how temperature and pressure change as you climb. Second, how the lungs and the circulation deliver oxygen to the body, and carry carbon dioxide away. And third, what the partial pressure of oxygen is, and why it, not the oxygen percentage, is what matters.

## Why this matters

Every physiological problem in the rest of this unit, from hypoxia, to blocked ears, to the bends after diving, starts with one fact. The air thins as you climb.

On a trip from Jandakot to Kalgoorlie at 7,500 ft, you are breathing air whose pressure is about three-quarters of what it was on the apron. Your body has quietly adjusted. Climb another few thousand feet over the Darling Range to get above the bumps, and the adjustment starts to run out.

This lesson builds the foundation. What the air is made of. How its temperature and pressure change with height. How your lungs and blood move oxygen around. And why the partial pressure of oxygen is the number that counts.

The exam asks for the composition of air, an ISA temperature or two, and the mechanism of oxygen transport. And it almost always has a question designed to catch anyone who thinks the air at altitude contains less oxygen.

## What the air is made of (2.11.1)

Take a lungful of air on the beach at Cottesloe. Most of it does nothing for you.

About four-fifths is nitrogen, an inert gas your body breathes in and out unchanged. About a fifth is oxygen, the gas every cell in your body needs to release energy from food. The last one percent is mostly argon, with a trace of carbon dioxide, about 0.04 percent, and other gases.

So the round figures for dry air are these. Nitrogen, about 78 percent. Oxygen, about 21 percent. And argon, carbon dioxide and other gases, about 1 percent.

On top of these, the air holds a variable amount of water vapour. That ranges from almost none on a dry easterly day in the Wheatbelt, to several percent in a humid tropical air mass over the Kimberley. That is why the figures are quoted for dry air.

Now the key point of the whole lesson. The atmosphere is constantly stirred by weather, so the gases stay thoroughly mixed. The proportions stay the same, from sea level to well above any height a light aeroplane can reach. Air at 18,000 ft is still 21 percent oxygen.

What changes as you climb is not the recipe. It is the amount of air in each lungful. And the measure of that amount is the pressure.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. "At altitude the air contains less oxygen." It sounds right, and it is the exam's favourite distractor. The percentage of oxygen is unchanged at every height you will fly. What falls is the total pressure, and with it the pressure of the oxygen. An answer that says the oxygen percentage has fallen is wrong.

## Why pressure falls as you climb (2.11.1)

Picture the atmosphere as a tall stack of blankets. The blanket at the bottom carries the weight of all the others, so it is squashed flat. A blanket near the top carries almost nothing, and stays fluffy.

Air behaves the same way. Atmospheric pressure at any height is simply the weight of the column of air above you, pressing on each unit of area. At sea level, in the ISA, the International Standard Atmosphere, that column weighs enough to give {1013 hPa|ten thirteen hectopascals}.

Climb, and there is less air above you, so the pressure falls. Air is compressible, so the lower layers are squeezed denser by the weight above them.

That has a consequence the exam likes. Pressure falls fastest near the ground, where the air is densest. It falls more slowly higher up, where each thousand feet contains less air. Near sea level you lose roughly 1 hPa for every 30 ft. That is the same rule of thumb you use when setting the altimeter subscale. Altimetry is covered in the lesson on conditions of flight.

Because each layer is thinner than the one below, the pressure does not fall in a straight line. It halves over a fixed-ish height interval.

Here are the ISA figures to know, in pairs of height and pressure. At sea level, {1013 hPa|ten thirteen hectopascals}. At 10,000 ft, about 700 hPa, roughly 0.7 of sea level. At 18,000 ft, about 500 hPa, half of sea level. At 25,000 ft, about 375 hPa, roughly 0.37. And at 34,000 ft, about 250 hPa, a quarter.

The temperatures that go with them run from plus 15 degrees at sea level, to minus 5 at 10,000 ft, minus 21 at 18,000 ft, minus 35 at 25,000 ft, and minus 53 at 34,000 ft.

Now look at the fractions. By 18,000 ft, you have climbed above half of all the air in the atmosphere. The pressure is about half the sea level value. It halves again, to a quarter, by about 34,000 ft. It took 18,000 ft to lose the first half, but 16,000 ft to lose half of what was left, because the air you climb through gets thinner all the way up.

The upper one is the total pressure, {1013 hPa|ten thirteen hectopascals} at sea level, about 700 at 10,000 ft, and about 500 at 18,000 ft. The heights a VFR pilot in a non-turbocharged single flies, up to about 10,000 ft, sit in the steep lower part of the curve. Between the ground and 10,000 ft, you have already lost about 30 percent of the pressure. That is why the body starts to notice above that height, which is the subject of the lesson on hypoxia. And it is why trapped gas in your ears, sinuses and gut expands most on the first part of the climb, which is covered in the lesson on atmospheric pressure changes.

## Why temperature falls as you climb (2.11.1)

You know from driving up into the hills that it gets cooler with height. The reason is that the atmosphere is heated mainly from below.

Sunlight passes through the air largely without warming it, and heats the ground. The ground then warms the air in contact with it. The further you are from that heated surface, the cooler the air. Air that rises also expands as the pressure on it falls, and expanding air cools.

The real atmosphere varies from day to day. So aviation uses an agreed average, the International Standard Atmosphere, or ISA. There are four facts to know about it.

First, the temperature at sea level is plus 15 degrees. Second, it falls at about 2 degrees per 1,000 ft. That is called the lapse rate. Third, it keeps falling until the tropopause, at about 36,000 ft, where it reaches about minus 56.5 degrees. And fourth, above the tropopause, it stays constant.

The 2 degrees per 1,000 ft figure is a rounded rule. The exact ISA value is 1.98. The rounded rule gives the formula you need. The ISA temperature equals 15, minus 2 times the altitude in thousands of feet.

The tropopause matters to airline pilots, and to weather, because it caps thunderstorm tops. But it does not matter to your cruising level. What matters to you is that the ISA gives a reference against which the real day is compared. A hot afternoon at Northam is ISA plus 20. That difference drives density height, which you meet in the lesson on take-off and landing performance.

At the tropopause, near 36,000 ft, it reaches about minus 56.5 degrees. Then it turns straight up, constant. Let's work one. What is the ISA temperature at 8,000 ft? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
First, thousands of feet. 8,000 divided by 1,000 is 8. Next, the temperature drop. 2 times 8 is 16 degrees. Then the ISA temperature. 15 minus 16 is minus 1 degree.

So on a standard day, you would expect the air at 8,000 ft to be just below freezing. Any visible moisture there is a potential icing hazard, and the cabin will need heat. The answer again: minus 1 degree at 8,000 ft.

Now try one yourself. What is the ISA temperature at 6,500 ft, a common westbound VFR cruising level on the way home to Perth?
[[pause 6]]
Thousands of feet: 6.5. The drop: 2 times 6.5 is 13 degrees. The ISA temperature: 15 minus 13 is 2 degrees. Half thousands work the same way. Multiply by 6.5, not by 6 or 7. So the ISA temperature at 6,500 ft is 2 degrees.

Question. Why does the pressure fall faster in the first 5,000 ft of a climb than between 15,000 and 20,000 ft?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because air is compressible. The lower layers carry the weight of everything above them, and are squeezed denser. So each thousand feet near the ground contains more air, and climbing through it removes more pressure. Higher up the air is thinner, so each thousand feet removes less.

## How you get oxygen from the air to your cells (2.11.2)

Oxygen is only useful once it reaches the cells that burn it. Above all, that means the cells of your brain and the retina of your eye, which use oxygen fastest and have no store of it.

Getting it there takes two systems working together. The respiratory system brings air into contact with blood. The circulatory system carries the blood around the body. Let's follow one breath on its way.

Start with breathing in. You do not suck air in. Your diaphragm, the dome of muscle under the lungs, contracts and flattens. The muscles between your ribs lift the ribcage. The chest gets bigger, the pressure inside the lungs drops slightly below the outside air pressure, and air flows in to fill the space. Breathing out is mostly passive. The muscles relax, and the chest springs back and pushes air out.

The air travels through the nose or mouth, and down the windpipe, the trachea. It splits into two bronchi, one to each lung. Then it runs through smaller and smaller branches, like the twigs of a tree, until it reaches millions of tiny air sacs called alveoli.

The alveoli are where the work is done. Each one is wrapped in a net of the finest blood vessels, the capillaries. The wall between the air and the blood is only a cell or two thick.

Next, gas exchange. Oxygen does not get pumped across the alveolar wall. It diffuses. Gas molecules move from where their pressure is higher to where it is lower, the way a drop of ink spreads through a glass of water.

Air in the alveoli has a higher oxygen pressure than the blood arriving from the body, which has given up much of its oxygen. So oxygen flows into the blood. The blood arriving from the body carries a higher carbon dioxide pressure than the alveolar air. So carbon dioxide flows the other way, into the lungs, to be breathed out.

Hold on to that idea. The driving force is a difference in pressure. Everything in the partial pressure section later depends on it.

Then, carrying the oxygen. Very little oxygen dissolves in blood plasma. Almost all of it is carried by haemoglobin, an iron-containing protein packed inside the red blood cells. Haemoglobin picks oxygen up where the oxygen pressure is high, in the lungs. It releases it where the pressure is low, in the working tissues.

That is what makes it such a good carrier. It is also its weak point. Carbon monoxide binds to haemoglobin far more strongly than oxygen does, and takes its place. That is why a leaking cabin heater is so dangerous, as the lesson on toxic hazards explains.

Now the two loops of the circulation. The heart is two pumps side by side, driving blood round two loops.

First, the right side pumps oxygen-poor blood, returning from the body, through the lungs. There it picks up oxygen and gives up carbon dioxide.

Second, the left side pumps the now oxygen-rich blood out through the arteries to the body's tissues. In the capillaries of the tissues, oxygen leaves the blood. The cells use it to release energy from food, and they produce carbon dioxide as waste. The blood returns to the heart through the veins.

Carbon dioxide makes the return journey mostly dissolved in the plasma, in the form of bicarbonate, and is released again in the lungs.

Picture the body in outline. At the top, the lungs, full of tiny air sacs, with the diaphragm curved beneath them. In the middle, the heart, drawn as two pumps side by side. At the bottom, the body's tissues: brain, eyes and muscles. The right side of the heart sends oxygen-poor blood up through the lungs. The left side sends oxygen-rich blood out through the arteries to the tissues, and the blood comes back through the veins. What to notice: diffusion moves the gases at each end, oxygen into the blood at the lungs, and out of the blood at the tissues.

So the whole chain looks like this. Air into the lungs. Oxygen across the alveolar wall. Onto haemoglobin. Round the body, driven by the heart. Off the haemoglobin, into the cells. Used by the cells. Every link can fail, and each failure has a name.

[[diagram:hypoxia-types-and-causes]]
Picture that chain as a line, from the air on the left to the cells on the right, with four breaks marked along it. Hypoxic hypoxia is a failure at the lungs. Anaemic hypoxia is a failure in the blood. Stagnant hypoxia is a failure in the circulation. And histotoxic hypoxia is a failure in the cells.

That picture is the bridge to the next lesson. Low oxygen pressure in the lungs, from altitude. Less usable haemoglobin, from carbon monoxide, blood donation or anaemia. Poor circulation, from g, cold or shock. Or cells unable to use the oxygen they are given, from alcohol or some drugs. Those are the four types of hypoxia in the lesson on hypoxia.

Question. What carries almost all of the oxygen in your blood, and where?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: haemoglobin, inside the red blood cells. It loads oxygen in the lungs, where the oxygen pressure is high, and unloads it in the tissues, where the pressure is low. Only a small amount travels dissolved in the plasma.

Finally in this section, what makes you breathe. You might expect your body to breathe harder when oxygen runs low. Mostly, it does not. The brain's breathing centre controls the rate and depth of breathing mainly by sensing the level of carbon dioxide in the blood, and the acidity that carbon dioxide causes.

When you exercise, you produce more carbon dioxide, the level rises, and you breathe harder. When you sit still in the cockpit, carbon dioxide production is steady, and so is your breathing.

That design has two consequences you will meet again.

The first: hypoxia creeps up unfelt. At altitude your oxygen supply falls, but your carbon dioxide does not rise. So nothing triggers the feeling of breathlessness. You can be short of oxygen with no sensation of being short of air.

The second: over-breathing upsets the balance. If anxiety makes you breathe faster and deeper than you need, you blow off too much carbon dioxide. The level falls below normal, and you get dizziness, tingling, and eventually muscle spasm. That is hyperventilation, which has its own lesson.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. It offers "the level of oxygen in the blood" as the main control of breathing. It is the level of carbon dioxide. That is precisely why hypoxia gives no warning of breathlessness.

Question. Why does a pilot becoming hypoxic at altitude not feel short of breath?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because the urge to breathe comes mainly from rising carbon dioxide, not falling oxygen. At altitude, carbon dioxide production is unchanged. So the breathing centre sees nothing wrong, while the oxygen supply to the brain falls.

## Partial pressure of oxygen (2.11.3)

Go back to the stack of blankets. But now imagine each blanket woven from three colours of thread. 78 percent blue, for nitrogen. 21 percent red, for oxygen. And one percent green, for the rest.

The total pressure at the bottom of the stack is shared between the colours in exactly those proportions. The share of the pressure due to one gas in a mixture is that gas's partial pressure. And the rule that the partial pressures add up to the total is Dalton's law.

So for oxygen, the partial pressure of oxygen equals 0.21 times the total pressure.

Why does this matter more than the percentage? Go back to diffusion. Oxygen crosses the alveolar wall because its pressure in the alveoli is higher than in the blood. The rate at which it crosses, and how fully the haemoglobin loads up, depends on that pressure. It does not depend on what fraction of the air oxygen happens to be.

A gas at 21 percent of {1013 hPa|ten thirteen hectopascals} pushes far harder than the same gas at 21 percent of 500 hPa. When the oxygen partial pressure falls, less oxygen diffuses into the blood, and the haemoglobin leaves the lungs less than fully loaded.

Here are the partial pressures at three levels. At sea level, 0.21 times {1013|ten thirteen} is about 213 hPa. That is about {160 mmHg|one hundred and sixty millimetres of mercury}. At 10,000 ft, 0.21 times 700 is about 147 hPa, roughly 70 percent of sea level. And at 18,000 ft, 0.21 times 500 is about 105 hPa, half the sea level value.

The pattern is simple, because the 21 percent never changes. The oxygen partial pressure is always the same fraction of sea level as the total pressure is. Total pressure halves by about 18,000 ft, so the partial pressure of oxygen halves too, with the oxygen fraction still 21 percent.

Let's work one. At 5,000 ft the total pressure is about 843 hPa. Oxygen is 21 percent of the air, and the sea level oxygen partial pressure is about 213 hPa. What is the oxygen partial pressure at 5,000 ft, and what fraction is that of sea level? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
The partial pressure is 0.21 times 843, which is 177 hPa. Compare that with sea level. 177 divided by 213 is 0.83.

So at 5,000 ft, each breath pushes oxygen into your blood with about 83 percent of the sea level pressure. A healthy pilot sitting still notices nothing. But this is already the height at which night vision begins to suffer, as the lesson on hypoxia explains. The answer again: 177 hPa, about 83 percent of sea level.

Now, what the lungs actually see. The air deep in your lungs is not quite outside air. By the time it reaches the alveoli, it has been warmed and saturated with water vapour. And it is mixed with the carbon dioxide coming out of the blood. Both take up part of the total pressure. So the oxygen partial pressure in the alveoli is noticeably lower than the figures you just heard. The water vapour and carbon dioxide shares do not shrink as you climb. So they take a bigger bite out of a smaller total, the higher you go.

Haemoglobin is forgiving at first. Over a range of oxygen pressures near sea level, it stays almost fully loaded. So the drop to 5,000 or 8,000 ft makes little difference to how much oxygen the blood carries.

Below a certain alveolar pressure, its grip weakens, and saturation falls away steeply. In practice that point is reached somewhere above about 10,000 ft. That is why blood oxygen saturation drops noticeably there. It is why the symptoms of hypoxia begin to appear. And it is why the rules on supplemental oxygen start at about that height. The lesson on hypoxia gives the requirement and its source.

[[diagram:oxygen-saturation-curve]]
Picture a sketch with cabin altitude along the bottom, and how fully the haemoglobin is loaded up the side. From sea level, the line runs almost flat along the top, nearly full, past 5,000 and 8,000 ft. Then, somewhere above about 10,000 ft, it bends and falls away steeply. What to notice: on the flat part, height barely changes what your blood carries. Past the bend, it does, and that is where hypoxia symptoms begin.

So how do you put the pressure back? If the problem is a low oxygen partial pressure, there are only two ways to fix it, and both follow from the formula.

The first is to raise the total pressure. Descend, or pressurise the cabin, so the air you breathe is at a lower cabin altitude.

The second is to raise the oxygen fraction. Breathe supplemental oxygen. That lifts the fraction above 21 percent, so the same low total pressure gives a higher oxygen partial pressure.

To see the second one in round numbers, ignore water vapour. Say you want back the sea level 213 hPa of oxygen at 18,000 ft, where the total is about 500 hPa. Then the gas you breathe would need to be 213 divided by 500, which is about 43 percent oxygen. That is the principle behind every oxygen system, from a cannula in a {Cessna 210|Cessna two ten} to an airliner's drop-down mask.

Question. At 18,000 ft, which has halved compared with sea level: the oxygen percentage, the total pressure, or the oxygen partial pressure?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the total pressure and the oxygen partial pressure have both halved. The oxygen percentage is still 21 percent, because the gases stay mixed at all the heights you fly.

## Bringing it together

You plan a summer trip from Jandakot to Esperance, and the forecast has the smoothest air at 9,500 ft. You work through what the atmosphere is doing to you there.

The ISA temperature is 15, minus 2 times 9.5, which is minus 4 degrees. Though on a hot summer day, the real air will be much warmer than ISA.

The pressure is a little above 700 hPa. So every breath holds roughly 70 percent of the oxygen molecules it held on the ground, and it is still 21 percent of the air. The partial pressure of oxygen is about 150 hPa, well down from 213. In the alveoli it is lower still, once water vapour and carbon dioxide take their share. And you are close to the point where haemoglobin's saturation starts to fall away.

Your breathing centre is watching carbon dioxide, which has not changed. So it will not warn you. If you are tired, a smoker, or flying at night, you will be affected sooner. Those factors are in the lesson on hypoxia.

None of this means 9,500 ft is unsafe. It means you understand why the next few thousand feet are a different matter.

Question. Name the two ways to raise the oxygen partial pressure you breathe, and how each works.
[[pause 4]]
The answer: increase the total pressure, by descending or by pressurising the cabin. Or increase the oxygen fraction, by breathing supplemental oxygen. Either one raises the oxygen partial pressure, so more oxygen diffuses into the blood.

## Key points

Let's recap.

The air and how it changes. Dry air is about 78 percent nitrogen, 21 percent oxygen, and 1 percent other gases. The proportions do not change with height. Pressure falls with height, fastest near the ground, at about 1 hPa per 30 ft, to about half at 18,000 ft. The ISA temperature is plus 15 degrees at sea level. It falls about 2 degrees per 1,000 ft, to about minus 56.5 degrees at the tropopause, at about 36,000 ft.

Getting oxygen to the cells. The diaphragm draws air into the alveoli. Oxygen diffuses into the blood, and carbon dioxide diffuses out. Haemoglobin in the red cells carries the oxygen. The right side of the heart pumps blood through the lungs, and the left side pumps it to the body. Breathing is driven mainly by the carbon dioxide level. That is why hypoxia gives no breathlessness.

Partial pressure. The partial pressure of oxygen is its share of the total pressure, 0.21 times the total. It is the pressure, not the percentage, that drives oxygen into the blood. And it falls in step with the total pressure: about 213 hPa at sea level, 147 at 10,000 ft, and 105 at 18,000 ft.

A final exam tip. The percentage of oxygen at 18,000 ft is still about 21 percent. What has halved is the total pressure, and so the oxygen partial pressure. Have these by heart. 78, 21 and 1 for the air. Plus 15 degrees, and 2 degrees per 1,000 ft, for the ISA. {1013 hPa|ten thirteen hectopascals} at sea level, and about half at 18,000 ft. Haemoglobin as the oxygen carrier. Carbon dioxide as the breathing trigger. And for any partial pressure question, the oxygen partial pressure equals 0.21 times the total pressure.

That's the end of PHFC 2.11.
