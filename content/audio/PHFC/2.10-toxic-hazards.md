This is PHFC 2.10, Toxic hazards. By the end you will know where carbon monoxide comes from in a light aeroplane, what it does to your blood, how to recognise it and what to do about it, and what fuel vapour, smoke and other aviation products do to you. There are a few questions along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (PHFC 2.10)

The exam wants two things from you. First, carbon monoxide: where it comes from in an aeroplane, its symptoms, what it does to the body, and how to treat it. And second, what breathing air contaminated by fuel, and by other noxious or toxic aviation products, does to you.

## Why this matters

On a July morning at Northam, the paddocks are white with frost, and the cabin is 3 degrees. The first thing you do after start is pull the cabin heat knob. In most light singles, that heat comes from a metal jacket wrapped around the exhaust. And if the exhaust inside it has cracked, you are now breathing engine exhaust.

The poison in it, carbon monoxide, cannot be seen, smelt or tasted. And its first effect is to dull the judgement you would need to notice it.

Fuel vapour, smoke from hot wiring, and the chemicals that ag aircraft carry across the wheatbelt are the other toxic hazards of light aviation. Each has a simple first response that you need to know before you meet it.

The exam asks for the sources, the symptoms, the treatment, and the classic trap. You will not smell carbon monoxide.

## Carbon monoxide: what it is and where it comes from

Burning fuel combines carbon from the fuel with oxygen from the air. With plenty of oxygen, each carbon atom takes two oxygen atoms, and becomes carbon dioxide. When the oxygen supply is short, some carbon atoms get only one, and become carbon monoxide, CO for short. It is a product of incomplete combustion.

A piston engine never burns its fuel completely. And for much of the time it runs rich of the chemically perfect mixture, to keep the cylinder temperatures down. So its exhaust always carries carbon monoxide.

CO is colourless, odourless and tasteless. None of your senses can detect it. Exhaust gas does have a smell, from the other things in it. So an exhaust smell in the cabin is a clue that exhaust, and therefore CO, is getting in. But you can be poisoned by CO with no smell at all. A small leak, or a leak whose smelly parts have been absorbed or diluted, can still deliver enough CO.

So how does it get into the cabin? The classic source is the cabin heater. Most piston singles heat the cabin by passing fresh air through a metal shroud wrapped around part of the exhaust system. That shroud is called the heater muff. The air picks up heat from the hot exhaust pipe, and flows into the cabin at your feet.

It is cheap, simple and effective. And it puts the cabin's heating air millimetres away from the exhaust gas, separated only by the wall of the pipe. Exhaust pipes live a hard life of heating and cooling, vibration and corrosion. A crack in the section inside the muff puts exhaust gas straight into the heating air, and straight onto the floor of the cabin.

[[diagram:carbon-monoxide-cabin-heater]]
Picture a cutaway of the aeroplane's nose, with the engine on the right and the cabin on the left. The heater muff wraps around the exhaust pipe. Fresh air flows into the muff, picks up heat, and comes out of the heater outlet at the pilot's feet. Now put a crack in the exhaust inside the muff. Exhaust gas, with its colourless, odourless carbon monoxide, rides the heating air straight to your feet. The lesson to take from it: heater off and fresh air on, at the first headache.

That is why winter and heater use are the danger times. In a WA summer, the heater stays off and the vents are open. On a frosty wheatbelt morning, or a long cold climb, the heater is on and the vents are shut.

There are three other sources.

First, exhaust leaks forward of the firewall. Cracked exhaust stacks, failed gaskets, and gaps in the firewall, the cowling and the floor seals can all let exhaust from the engine bay into the cabin.

Second, exhaust re-entering the cabin on the ground. That happens when you run the engine with the doors or windows open with a tailwind. Or when you taxi and hold behind another aircraft whose exhaust blows into your vents.

And third, smoking. Tobacco smoke is loaded with CO. A heavy smoker carries several percent of their haemoglobin already bound to CO before they even get into the aeroplane. And so does everyone breathing their smoke in a closed cabin.

Question. Why is the risk of CO poisoning in a light single much higher in winter than in summer?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because the usual source is the cabin heater, which warms air in a muff around the exhaust. In winter, the heater is on and the fresh-air vents are closed. So any exhaust leaking into the muff goes straight into a sealed cabin. In summer, the heater is off, and fresh air dilutes any leak.

## What carbon monoxide does to the body

To see why such an invisible gas is so dangerous, follow the oxygen. In the lungs, oxygen passes into the blood and attaches to haemoglobin. Haemoglobin is the protein in red blood cells that carries oxygen around the body to the tissues. Each haemoglobin molecule has a limited number of sites for oxygen.

Carbon monoxide attaches to exactly the same sites. And it binds about 200 to 250 times more readily than oxygen. Haemoglobin with CO attached is called carboxyhaemoglobin. Every site taken by CO is a site that cannot carry oxygen.

So even a small concentration of CO in the air steadily takes over a large share of your haemoglobin. You are breathing normally. The air in your lungs has its full share of oxygen. But your blood can no longer carry it.

[[diagram:carboxyhaemoglobin-binding]]
Picture two panels side by side. On the left, clean air, and each haemoglobin molecule has all its seats filled with oxygen. On the right, there is only a little carbon monoxide in the cabin air, yet CO has taken over most of the seats. What to notice: because CO binds about 200 to 250 times more readily than oxygen, a trace of it takes over most of the seats on haemoglobin. And it lets go only slowly, over many hours.

That is anaemic hypoxia, also called hypaemic hypoxia. It is oxygen starvation caused by blood that cannot carry enough oxygen, rather than by a lack of oxygen in the air. You meet the other kinds of hypoxia in the lesson on hypoxia.

[[diagram:hypoxia-types-and-causes]]
Picture the oxygen chain as a line, running from the air, into the lungs, into the blood, and on to the cells. Each type of hypoxia breaks the chain at a different link. Carbon monoxide breaks it at the blood. The air and the lungs are fine, but the blood cannot carry the oxygen to the cells. That is anaemic hypoxia.

Three features make it worse than it sounds.

First, it is insidious. Your urge to breathe is driven mainly by the carbon dioxide level in your blood, not by its oxygen content. With CO poisoning, your carbon dioxide is normal. So nothing makes you feel short of breath in the early stages. Meanwhile the brain, the organ most sensitive to oxygen shortage, is the first to suffer. And the first thing to go is judgement, the very faculty you would need to recognise the problem.

Second, it clears slowly. CO lets go of haemoglobin only slowly. Breathing ordinary air, it can take many hours to clear. Walking away from the source does not make you well. A pilot poisoned on the morning leg is still affected on the afternoon one.

And third, it adds to altitude. At altitude there is less oxygen pressure in the lungs to begin with. You'll find that in the lesson on the atmosphere and associated problems. CO takes away part of the blood's carrying capacity on top of that, so the two effects stack. A smoker, whose blood already carries some CO, is effectively at a higher physiological altitude than the altimeter shows, and feels the effects of altitude sooner. The same goes for night vision, which is the first casualty of oxygen shortage. That is covered in the lesson on vision, spatial disorientation and illusions.

[[diagram:atmosphere-pressure-oxygen-vs-altitude]]
Picture a graph with altitude running along the bottom, and pressure up the side. Two lines fall away from left to right as you climb. The upper one is the total air pressure, {1013 hPa|ten thirteen hectopascals} at sea level, and about half that at 18,000 ft. The lower one is the oxygen's share of it, always about 21 percent of the air, so it falls in step. That is the oxygen pressure you have to work with at height. What to notice: carbon monoxide then takes away part of the blood's ability to carry what is left, so the two add together.

Question. Why does CO poisoning not make you feel breathless in the early stages, even though your tissues are short of oxygen?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: your breathing drive responds mainly to the carbon dioxide in your blood, and CO poisoning leaves that normal. The lungs also have plenty of oxygen. It is the blood that cannot carry it. So nothing triggers the feeling of breathlessness while the brain is being starved.

## Recognising carbon monoxide poisoning

The symptoms build roughly in a fixed order, as more of your haemoglobin is taken over. There are seven stages.

It starts with a headache, often throbbing, or a tight band across the forehead. Then dizziness, tiredness and sluggishness. Then nausea and vomiting. Then impaired judgement, confusion and poor vision. Then shortness of breath and a rapid pulse. Then cherry-red lips and skin. That is a late sign, and not a reliable one. And finally, convulsions, unconsciousness and death.

Think about the first few, and you will see the difficulty. Headache, tiredness and nausea are exactly what you would expect from a long flight, a late night, a bumpy ride, or ordinary hypoxia. Nothing about them says carbon monoxide.

The clue is timing and context. Symptoms that start soon after the heater goes on. Symptoms shared by more than one person on board. And symptoms that get worse the longer the heater runs.

That is why a CO detector is worth its weight. There are two kinds.

The first is a spot-card detector. It is a small card with a chemical patch that darkens in the presence of CO. It is cheap. But it must be within its date, it must be looked at to be any use, and it responds slowly.

The second is an electronic detector. It measures CO continuously, and sounds an alarm. That works even when you are not looking at it, and even when your judgement is already slipping. If you fly in winter, an electronic detector is the better choice.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. "You would smell carbon monoxide." That is false. CO is colourless, odourless and tasteless. An exhaust smell in the cabin means CO is probably present too, but the absence of a smell proves nothing. And another trap: cherry-red skin is a late sign, not an early warning.

## Treating carbon monoxide poisoning

The treatment follows from the mechanism. Stop the CO coming in. Dilute what is already in the cabin. Give the blood every chance to swap CO for oxygen. And get on the ground.

In flight, at the first suspicion, there are seven actions.

First, cabin heat off. That closes the most likely source.

Second, open all the fresh-air vents, and the windows if your type allows it in flight, to flush the cabin.

Third, use supplemental oxygen if it is carried. A high concentration of oxygen in the lungs helps push CO off the haemoglobin faster.

Fourth, no smoking.

Fifth, land as soon as possible. Do not press on to the planned destination with a poisoned pilot.

Sixth, get medical attention, and tell the doctor you suspect CO, so that they test for it.

And seventh, report the defect, and have the exhaust and heater system inspected before the aeroplane flies again.

Do the first two straight away, before you are sure. Turning the heater off and opening the vents costs you nothing but some warmth. Waiting for certainty means waiting while your judgement degrades.

Prevention comes back to the same mechanism. Have the heater muff and exhaust inspected at the intervals the maintenance schedule requires, as CASA's airworthiness bulletins on carbon monoxide advise. On the preflight, look for soot stains or exhaust streaks around the cowling joints and the exhaust stacks. After start, listen for a changed or dull exhaust note. Do not smoke, and do not let passengers smoke, in or near the aeroplane. And carry a detector.

So, the first actions once more: heater off, fresh air on, oxygen if carried, and land.

Question. You suspect CO, but you have no symptoms yet and no smell. Is it worth turning the heater off and opening the vents before you are sure?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: yes. The cost is a cold cabin. The cost of waiting is a growing dose of a gas that clears slowly, and first attacks the judgement you are using to decide. With CO, you act on suspicion, not certainty.

## Fuel vapour and other toxic aviation products

Carbon monoxide is the hazard the exam asks most about. But it is not the only thing in an aeroplane you should not breathe. We'll take fuel first, then smoke, then fluids, extinguishers and cargo, and then the response that covers them all.

Start with fuel, both AVGAS and Jet A-1. Fuel vapour gets into the cabin in several ways. A leaking fuel line or fitting. A leaking primer. A fuel selector valve weeping into the cabin. An overfilled tank venting. Or a spill during refuelling or fuel drains that soaks into clothing or carpet.

Fuel vapours are absorbed through the lungs, and act on the brain much like an anaesthetic. There are four effects. First, headache, dizziness and nausea, with irritated eyes and throat. Second, an intoxicated, drunk feeling, with impaired judgement and coordination. Third, drowsiness, and in high concentrations, unconsciousness. And fourth, a serious fire risk. The vapour that is making you dizzy is also an explosive mixture looking for a spark.

AVGAS also contains tetraethyl lead, which is toxic with repeated skin contact or inhalation. That is a reason for good habits on the ground. Avoid skin contact during fuel drains and refuelling, and wash your hands afterwards. Keep fuel-soaked rags out of the cabin. And never siphon fuel by mouth.

Next, smoke and the products of fire. Burning plastics, wiring insulation and cabin materials give off far more than smoke. The fumes include carbon monoxide and cyanide, among other toxic gases. That is why even a small electrical fire behind the panel is so dangerous in a closed cabin.

Smoke in the cockpit is an emergency. Follow the procedure in the flight manual, the POH, for your type. In broad terms, identify and cut the source, which means the master switch, or the affected electrical circuit. Ventilate the cabin, unless the fresh air would feed the fire. Use oxygen if it is carried. And land.

Then fluids, extinguishers and cargo. There are four to know.

First, hydraulic fluid, engine oil and de-icing fluid. Fumes and mists from a leak onto a hot exhaust irritate the eyes and lungs, and may cause nausea. Oil smoke from the vents after a leak is also a sign of an engine problem.

Second, fire extinguisher agents, such as halon. In a confined cockpit, they can cause dizziness, and reduce the oxygen available. Ventilate the cabin after using one.

Third, agricultural chemicals. That means pesticides and fertilisers in ag operations, and their residue on and in aircraft that have done ag work.

And fourth, cargo, batteries and dangerous goods that passengers bring on board. Solvents, gas bottles, a leaking battery. Your passenger briefing and your loading check are the defence.

Finally, what to do when fumes enter the cockpit. The response is the same shape, whatever the fumes. There are six steps.

First, identify and stop the source, if you can. Second, ventilate with fresh air. Third, use oxygen if it is available. Fourth, avoid anything that could ignite fuel vapour. If fuel is the suspected source, minimise electrical switching, and follow the POH. Fifth, land as soon as practicable. And sixth, report the defect, and have it fixed before further flight.

Question. Why is fuel vapour in the cabin more than just a breathing hazard?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the same vapour that causes headache, dizziness and an intoxicated feeling is flammable. So the cabin holds a fire risk as well as a toxic one. That is why the response includes avoiding ignition sources, as well as ventilating and landing.

## Bringing it together

It is a cold June departure from Narrogin for Jandakot, with a passenger. You preflight in the dark. Under the torch, you notice a grey sooty streak along the lower cowling joint near the exhaust.

You almost dismiss it. Then you remember what it means. Exhaust is escaping where it should not, and in winter the heater will be carrying it into the cabin. You report it, and the flight waits for an engineer. The engineer finds a cracked exhaust inside the heater muff.

A week later, in a different aeroplane, the electronic CO detector on the coaming chirps, fifteen minutes after you selected cabin heat over Brookton. You feel fine, and there is no smell. You do not wait to feel unwell. Heat off. All vents open. You tell your passenger, and the readings fall away.

You land at the nearest suitable aerodrome, rather than pressing on to Jandakot. You mention the exposure to a doctor, and you write up the defect. And the fuel smell that greeted you when you opened the door that morning gets the same treatment. Find the source before you fly.

## Key points

Let's recap.

Carbon monoxide first. CO is a colourless, odourless, tasteless product of incomplete combustion. It comes mostly from a cracked exhaust in the cabin heater muff. It also comes from exhaust leaks, exhaust re-entering the cabin, and smoking.

It binds to haemoglobin 200 to 250 times more readily than oxygen. That causes anaemic hypoxia, which clears slowly, and adds to altitude.

The symptoms run from headache, dizziness, tiredness and nausea, to impaired judgement, then breathlessness, then the late sign of cherry-red skin, then unconsciousness.

The treatment: heater off, vents open, oxygen if carried, no smoking, land as soon as possible, get medical attention, and have the system inspected.

Then the other products. Fuel vapour causes headache, dizziness, nausea, eye and throat irritation, an intoxicated feeling and drowsiness, and it is a fire risk. The lead in AVGAS is toxic on repeated contact. Burning materials release CO and cyanide. Hydraulic fluid, oil, de-icing fluid, extinguisher agents, ag chemicals and dangerous goods can also contaminate the cabin air. The response: stop the source, ventilate, use oxygen, avoid ignition, land, and report.

A final exam tip. The favourite trap is that you would smell carbon monoxide. You will not, because it is colourless and odourless. Know the classic source, the cabin heater muff around the exhaust. Know the mechanism: it binds to haemoglobin 200 to 250 times more readily than oxygen, causing anaemic hypoxia. And know the first actions on suspicion: heater off, fresh air on, oxygen if carried, and land. For fuel vapour, the answer combines intoxication-like effects with a fire risk. For burning wiring, it is toxic gases such as CO and cyanide.
