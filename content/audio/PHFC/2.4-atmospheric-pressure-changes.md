This is PHFC 2.4, Atmospheric pressure changes. By the end you will know what a change of height does to the gas trapped in your body, which parts hurt on the climb and which on the descent, how to prevent and treat it, and how long to wait before flying after a dive. There are calculations to try in your head and a few questions, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (PHFC 2.4)

The exam wants two things from you. First, trapped gases: what a change of pressure does to gas trapped in the body's cavities, how that affects normal bodily function, and how to prevent and treat the problems. And second, what can happen if you fly soon after underwater diving, and the precautions to take before flying after a dive.

## Why this matters

Your body is full of small pockets of gas. In an unpressurised aeroplane, every one of them feels each change of height. Most of the time you notice nothing more than your ears popping on the climb out of Jandakot. But with a head cold, the same descent back into the circuit can produce pain bad enough to make you abandon the approach.

And WA has some of the best diving in the country, at Rottnest, Busselton and Ningaloo. That tempts pilots and passengers to dive in the morning and fly home in the afternoon. It is the one combination in this lesson that can cripple or kill.

The physics behind both is simple. Once you have it, the exam's questions answer themselves. Which way the gas goes. Whether it hurts on the climb or the descent. And how long to wait after diving. Expect one calculation of gas expansion, and one question on the diving interval.

## Pressure falls as you climb

The air around you has weight. At sea level, the whole column of air above you presses down with a pressure of about {1013 hPa|ten thirteen hectopascals}. As you climb, there is less air above you, so the pressure falls.

It does not fall evenly. The air near the ground is compressed by the air above it. So the pressure drops quickly at first, and more slowly higher up. In the International Standard Atmosphere, the pressure is about 700 hPa at 10,000 ft. And it is about 500 hPa, half the sea-level value, at 18,000 ft.

[[diagram:atmosphere-pressure-oxygen-vs-altitude]]
Picture a graph with altitude running along the bottom and pressure going up the side. The total pressure curve starts high on the left, at {1013 hPa|ten thirteen hectopascals} at sea level. It falls as it runs to the right: about 700 at 10,000 ft, about 500 at 18,000 ft, and about 250 by 34,000 feet. Notice that it bends. The first few thousand feet take the most pressure away. The point to hold on to is that the pressure halves by about 18,000 ft.

In a light aeroplane the cabin is not pressurised. The air inside is the air outside. So the cabin altitude is the aircraft altitude, and your body feels the full pressure change of every climb and descent. An airliner keeps its cabin at a lower cabin altitude than it flies at. That softens the change, but it does not remove it. Passengers' ears still pop.

## Boyle's law: why trapped gas expands

Take a balloon up a mountain, partly inflated and tied off. It grows as you climb. And if you carry it back down, it shrinks. Nothing has been added or removed. The gas inside is simply pushing outwards against the air outside. When the outside pressure falls, the gas expands until the pressure inside matches it again.

That is Boyle's law. At constant temperature, the volume of a fixed amount of gas is inversely proportional to its pressure. Halve the pressure, and the volume doubles. Double the pressure, and the volume halves.

As an equation, call the starting condition 1 and the new condition 2. The first pressure times the first volume equals the second pressure times the second volume. Rearranged, the new volume equals the old volume, times the old pressure divided by the new pressure.

That ratio, old pressure over new pressure, is all you need. Climbing from sea level, the old pressure is {1013|ten thirteen} hectopascals, and the new one is smaller. So the ratio is bigger than one, and the gas grows. Descending, the ratio is smaller than one, and the gas shrinks.

Here are the figures for the standard atmosphere. At sea level, {1013 hPa|ten thirteen hectopascals}, the volume is 1. At 10,000 ft, about 700 hPa, the volume is about 1.5 times. At 18,000 ft, about 500 hPa, it is about 2 times. And at 25,000 ft, about 375 hPa, it is about 2.7 times.

[[diagram:boyles-law-gas-expansion]]
Picture those on a graph, with altitude along the bottom and the gas volume going up the side. The curve starts low on the left, at 1 at sea level, and climbs more and more steeply to the right: half as big again at 10,000 ft, twice the size at 18,000 ft, where the pressure is half, and about 2.7 times at 25,000 ft. Beside the graph are two boxes. The upper one is the climb: the gas expands, and the gut and teeth hurt. The lower one is the descent: the gas shrinks, air must get back in, and the ears and sinuses hurt if a cold or hay fever has blocked them.

Notice the shape. The first 10,000 ft adds half again to the volume. It takes another 8,000 ft to add the next half. Pressure falls fastest near the ground, so a given change of height near the ground produces the largest change of volume. That matters on descent. The last few thousand feet into the circuit squeeze trapped gas the hardest.

Let's work one. You have a 150 mL pocket of gas in your gut at sea level, at {1013 hPa|ten thirteen hectopascals}. You climb to cruise at 8,000 ft, where the pressure is about 753 hPa. What volume is the pocket now? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
Step one, the pressure ratio. {1013|ten thirteen} divided by 753 is 1.35. Step two, the new volume. 150 mL times 1.35 is 202 mL, about 200.

So the pocket has grown by about a third, at an ordinary PPL cruising level. A gut that was comfortable on the ground is now noticeably distended. That is why a fizzy drink before a long climb is a poor idea.

The same arithmetic works at a higher level. A 120 mL pocket at sea level expands to about 240 mL at 18,000 ft, where the pressure is roughly half. {1013|ten thirteen} over 500, times 120. And in an unpressurised aeroplane, where the cabin altitude is the aircraft altitude, the volume at 10,000 ft is about 1.5 times.

Here is one for you to try. Take the 150 mL pocket from the worked example on up to 18,000 ft, where the pressure is about 500 hPa. What volume does it occupy?
[[pause 6]]
The ratio: {1013|ten thirteen} divided by 500 is 2.03. The volume: 150 times 2.03 is 304. So about 300 mL, double the sea-level volume. Half the pressure means twice the volume. That is the one relationship to remember.

Question. A pocket of gas trapped at 10,000 ft is carried down to sea level. Does it grow or shrink, and roughly by how much?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: it shrinks, because the outside pressure rises from about 700 hPa to {1013 hPa|ten thirteen hectopascals}. The volume falls to about 700 over {1013|ten thirteen}, roughly two-thirds of what it was. That shrinking is what pulls the eardrum inwards on descent, if air cannot get back in.

## Where gas is trapped in the body

Gas in the body causes trouble only where it is trapped. That means enclosed in a space with rigid walls, or connected to the outside by a passage narrow enough to block. Where it can flow freely in and out, as in the lungs, the pressure equalises with every breath, and nothing happens. There are four places that matter.

The first is the middle ear. Behind the eardrum is a small air-filled cavity, the middle ear. It is connected to the back of the throat by the eustachian tube. On the climb, the air in the middle ear expands. The pressure inside rises above the outside pressure, and the excess pushes its way out down the tube easily. You feel the occasional pop.

On the descent, the opposite happens. The outside pressure rises, the air in the middle ear contracts, and the eardrum is pushed inwards. To equalise, air must travel up the tube, into the middle ear. But the lower end of the tube is a soft, slit-like opening. It tends to stay shut until you open it, by swallowing or yawning. And if a cold or hay fever has swollen it, it may not open at all. The result is pain, reduced hearing, sometimes vertigo, and in severe cases a burst eardrum. That is barotrauma: injury caused by a pressure difference. The ear has a lesson of its own, the lesson on the anatomy of the ear.

[[diagram:ear-pressure-equalisation]]
Picture two cross-sections of the ear, side by side. On the left, the climb. The air in the middle ear expands, and vents easily down the eustachian tube to the throat. On the right, the descent. The outside pressure pushes the eardrum in, and air has to get back up the tube. But the soft end of the tube stays closed, and swollen by a cold, it may not open at all. That is an ear block. The fix is written underneath: climb back, equalise, and descend more slowly.

The second place is the sinuses. The sinuses are air spaces in the bones of the face and forehead. Each one drains into the nose through a small opening. With healthy linings, the openings are clear, and you never notice them. With a cold, hay fever or a sinus infection, the openings swell shut. The sinus becomes a sealed box with rigid bony walls. On the descent, its air contracts, and the rising outside pressure cannot be relieved. The result is severe pain in the forehead, behind the eyes, or in the cheeks. And again, it usually comes on the descent.

The third place is the gut. The stomach and intestines always contain some gas, from swallowed air and from digestion. Their walls are soft, so the gas can expand. And as you climb, it does. You get bloating, discomfort and cramping pain. The gas can usually escape eventually, in either direction, but not always quickly. In extreme cases, at high altitude, a badly distended gut pushes up on the diaphragm, and makes breathing difficult.

And the fourth place is the teeth. A poor filling or a decaying tooth can hold a tiny bubble of gas against the nerve. As it expands on the climb, it presses on the nerve and causes toothache. That is called barodontalgia. The pain usually stops on descent.

Picture a head and torso, with the four places marked, and the page split in two. On the left, under the heading descent, are the sinuses and the ears. Their air contracts, and must get back in through a narrow passage that a cold or hay fever can block. On the right, under the heading climb, are the teeth and the gut. Their gas expands inside a space it cannot leave quickly.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. It likes to mix up the direction. Ears and sinuses hurt on the descent, because air has to get back in through a narrow passage that may be blocked. Gut and teeth hurt on the climb, because gas expands inside a space it cannot leave quickly. A question about a pilot with a cold who develops severe ear pain will usually be set on descent.

Question. Why does a cold cause ear pain on the descent rather than the climb?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: on the climb, the expanding air in the middle ear forces its way out down the eustachian tube, even if the tube is narrowed. On the descent, air must get back into the middle ear through the tube. Its soft lower end stays shut unless it is opened, and a cold-swollen tube may not open at all. So the outside pressure pushes the eardrum in.

## What trapped gas does to a pilot

The syllabus asks for the effect on normal bodily function. And the answer is wider than: it hurts. There are five effects.

The first is pain, the obvious one. And pain is a distraction. An ear or sinus block at the end of a flight arrives when you are busiest, on descent and in the circuit. Severe pain can make it very hard to fly an accurate approach.

The second is reduced hearing. An eardrum that has been pushed in cannot vibrate freely. So radio calls are muffled, just when you need to hear the CTAF, the common traffic advisory frequency, or the tower.

The third is vertigo. An unequal pressure in the two middle ears can disturb the balance organs next door. It produces a sudden sense of spinning, called alternobaric vertigo. With no visual horizon, that is a disorientation risk.

The fourth is injury. A burst eardrum or a damaged sinus lining may keep you out of the air for weeks.

And the fifth is breathing. A badly distended gut at altitude restricts the movement of the diaphragm.

Any of these can escalate from discomfort to incapacitation. In a single-pilot aeroplane, that is the real hazard.

## Prevention and treatment

Prevention follows from the mechanism. Keep the passages open, keep the gas down, and give the body time to equalise. There are five measures.

First, do not fly with a cold, hay fever or a sinus infection. This is the single most effective measure, because a blocked passage is the cause of almost every ear and sinus problem.

Second, avoid gas-producing food and fizzy drinks before a flight to higher levels.

Third, keep your dental work in good condition, and mention to your dentist that you fly.

Fourth, descend slowly, and equalise your ears often on the way down. Swallow, yawn, or move the jaw. Or use the Valsalva manoeuvre. That means pinch the nose, close the mouth, and blow gently, to push air up the eustachian tubes. Equalise before discomfort builds, not after.

And fifth, wake sleeping passengers before the descent. People swallow far less when they are asleep, so their ears do not equalise.

Now treatment in flight. For an ear or sinus block on descent, climb back to the level where it was comfortable. That reduces the outside pressure, relieves the pressure difference, and lets the passage open. Equalise, then descend again, more slowly. Continuing the descent through the pain is how eardrums burst.

For gut pain on the climb, stop climbing or descend. Loosen tight clothing, and let the gas escape.

And for pain that persists after landing, see a doctor or a DAME, a designated aviation medical examiner, before flying again.

Question. On descent, your passenger complains of sharp pain in one ear. What is the first thing to do with the aeroplane?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: stop the descent, and climb back to the level where the ear was comfortable. Lowering the outside pressure reduces the pressure difference on the eardrum, and lets the eustachian tube open. Then have the passenger equalise, and descend again more slowly.

## Flying after diving

Underwater, the pressure rises fast. Every 10 m of seawater adds roughly the pressure of the whole atmosphere above it. A scuba diver breathes air from the tank at the same pressure as the surrounding water. So at depth, they breathe air at two, three or more times the sea-level pressure.

That matters because of a second gas law. The amount of a gas that dissolves in a liquid is proportional to the pressure of that gas above it. Think of a bottle of soft drink. It is filled under pressure, so a large amount of carbon dioxide dissolves in the liquid, and you see nothing. Open the cap, and the pressure drops. The gas can no longer stay dissolved, and it comes out as bubbles. The analogy holds for the chemistry. Where it breaks down is that the body does not fizz at once, because the gas has to move out of the tissues into the blood first.

A diver breathing high-pressure air dissolves extra nitrogen into the blood and tissues. Nitrogen makes up most of the air. Back at the surface, the excess nitrogen leaves slowly, through the blood and out of the lungs, over many hours. During those hours, the diver's tissues hold more nitrogen than they would at sea-level pressure.

Now take that diver flying. The cabin pressure falls with height. That is exactly like opening the soft-drink bottle a second time. The nitrogen that was only just staying dissolved at sea level can come out of solution, as bubbles in the tissues and the blood. That is decompression sickness, known as the bends.

[[diagram:nitrogen-bubbles-after-diving]]
Picture three panels, left to right. On the left, the diver at depth, breathing air at two, three or more times sea-level pressure, with extra nitrogen dissolving into the blood and tissues. In the middle, back at the surface, the extra nitrogen leaking away slowly, over many hours. On the right, the same person flying soon after. The pressure has dropped again, and the nitrogen comes out as bubbles, even at 5,000 to 8,000 ft. The lesson of the picture: wait 12 or 24 hours.

The symptoms depend on where the bubbles form. Joint pain, usually in the shoulders, elbows or knees, is the bends. Itching or a skin rash is called the creeps. Chest pain and coughing, from bubbles in the lungs, is called the chokes. Bubbles in the nervous system bring tingling, numbness, paralysis and visual disturbance. And the end of the line is collapse.

After diving, decompression sickness can occur even at quite low cabin altitudes, from about 5,000 to 8,000 ft. That is ordinary cruising territory for a light aeroplane. Decompression sickness from altitude alone is rare in unpressurised light aircraft below about 18,000 ft. Diving is what makes it a real risk.

Now the precautions. These are commonly taught, so check the current CASA guidance. There are four of them.

First, wait at least 12 hours after a dive that did not need decompression stops.

Second, wait at least 24 hours after a dive that did need decompression stops, or after multiple dives over several days.

Third, snorkelling, which is breath-hold diving, does not load nitrogen, and needs no wait. You are only ever breathing surface air.

And fourth, if symptoms appear in flight: descend, use oxygen if it is available, land, and get medical help. Tell them you have been diving. The treatment is in a recompression chamber. It squeezes the bubbles back into solution, and then lets the nitrogen out slowly.

Let's work one. You finish a recreational scuba dive off Rottnest at {1000|ten a m}, with no decompression stops. You plan to fly back to Jandakot that afternoon, at 1,500 ft. When is the earliest you should go? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
Step one. Is this flying after diving, at such a low height? Yes. A low cabin altitude still lowers the pressure. Step two. The minimum wait for a no-decompression dive is 12 hours. Step three. {1000|ten a m} plus 12 hours is {2200|ten p m}. So wait until at least {2200|ten p m}. Realistically, plan to fly the next day.

Here is one for you to try. You spend four days diving at Ningaloo, two dives a day, all without decompression stops. Your last dive ends at {1500|three p m} on Sunday. When is the earliest you should fly home from Exmouth?
[[pause 6]]
The answer: multiple dives over several days fall under the longer interval, at least 24 hours. That holds even though no single dive needed decompression stops, because repeated dives build up more nitrogen in the slower tissues. {1500|three p m} on Sunday plus 24 hours is {1500|three p m} on Monday, at the earliest.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. The option says: a low-level flight is safe straight after diving. No. Decompression sickness after diving can start at cabin altitudes of about 5,000 to 8,000 ft. And any reduction in pressure moves the risk in the wrong direction. The wait applies whatever height you plan to fly.

Question. Why does snorkelling need no wait before flying, when scuba diving does?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: a snorkeller breathes only surface air, at sea-level pressure, so no extra nitrogen dissolves in the tissues. A scuba diver breathes air at the higher pressure of the depth. That loads the tissues with nitrogen, which later comes out as bubbles if the pressure falls.

## Bringing it together

You and a friend have flown up to Exmouth for a long weekend. Your friend dives on Saturday morning. You snorkel. On Saturday night, your friend develops a head cold. You had planned to fly home on Sunday, at 8,500 ft.

You sort it out, one element at a time. You snorkelled, so you have no nitrogen load, and no wait. Your friend dived once, without decompression stops, so the minimum is 12 hours. On that count, Sunday is fine.

But your friend now has a cold. So as a passenger, they risk an ear or sinus block on the long descent into Jandakot. You plan a slow descent. You have them equalise often, and you keep them awake for it. And you are ready to level off, or climb back, if they report pain. If the cold is bad, a day's delay costs far less than a burst eardrum.

## Key points

Let's recap.

By Boyle's law, trapped gas expands as the pressure falls, and contracts as it rises. It grows about 1.5 times at 10,000 ft, and doubles at 18,000 ft, where the pressure is half.

Ears and sinuses hurt on the descent, because air cannot get back in through a blocked passage. Gut and teeth hurt on the climb, because of the expanding gas. The effects are pain, reduced hearing, vertigo, injury, and in extreme cases impaired breathing.

To prevent it: do not fly with a cold, avoid gassy food and drinks, keep your teeth sound, and descend slowly while you equalise. To treat an ear or sinus block: climb back, equalise, then descend more slowly.

Scuba diving loads the tissues with nitrogen that leaves over many hours. Flying lowers the pressure, and the nitrogen forms bubbles. That is decompression sickness, and it is possible from about 5,000 to 8,000 ft. Wait at least 12 hours after a no-decompression dive. Wait at least 24 hours after a dive with decompression stops, or after multiple dives over several days. Snorkelling needs no wait.

A final exam tip. For the calculation, multiply the volume by {1013|ten thirteen} divided by the new pressure. Half the pressure means double the volume, at about 18,000 ft. For direction, remember: descent for ears and sinuses, climb for gut and teeth. For diving, have 12 hours and 24 hours by heart. 12 hours with no decompression stops. 24 hours with decompression stops, or multiple dives over several days. And remember that the danger is nitrogen coming out of solution, not the air in the ears.

That's the end of PHFC 2.4.
