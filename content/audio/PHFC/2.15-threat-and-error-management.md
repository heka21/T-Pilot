This is PHFC 2.15, Threat and error management. By the end you will be able to tell a threat from an error from an undesired aircraft state, run the single-pilot process on any phase of flight, explain why checklists work, and put your priorities in the right order when something goes wrong. There are worked examples and several questions along the way, so pause whenever you want a moment to think.

## What the exam expects (PHFC 2.15)

The exam wants nine things from you. First, the basic idea behind threat and error management, TEM for short. Second, how TEM works, and a process a pilot flying alone can use. Third, what a threat is, with examples. Fourth, an example of an error, and how the pilot could act to keep the flight safe. Fifth, how checklists and standard operating procedures stop errors. Sixth, how an unmanaged threat or error turns into an undesired aircraft state. Seventh, the resources a pilot can use to avoid or get out of one, such as being lost or flying into bad weather. Eighth, why prioritising tasks matters in that state. And ninth, how good working relationships with other people make a flight safer.

## Why this matters

Every flight you make has things working against it. A sea breeze swinging the wind across the runway at Jandakot. A NOTAM about a crane near your departure path. A passenger who is quietly airsick. A fuel gauge that reads a little optimistic.

And on every flight, you will make mistakes. A frequency dialled wrong. A check rushed. A heading misread.

None of these is unusual. And none is, on its own, dangerous. Accidents happen when they are not noticed and dealt with, and one leads to another.

Threat and error management is the framework the airlines use to turn that observation into a habit. And it scales down to a solo navigation exercise in a {Cessna 172|Cessna one seventy-two} without losing anything.

The exam asks you to tell a threat from an error from an undesired aircraft state. It asks for the single-pilot process, and why checklists work. And it asks you to apply aviate, navigate, communicate.

## The idea behind TEM (2.15.1)

Older approaches to safety tried to stop pilots making mistakes. More rules, more training, more discipline. They helped. But they never got the error rate to zero, because people make errors however well trained they are.

Research in the {1990s|nineteen nineties} watched airline crews on normal flights. It found that threats and errors turned up on almost every flight. And it found that the crews who flew safely were not the ones who made no errors. They were the ones who caught and managed them.

That is the premise of TEM. Threats are always present, and humans will make errors. Safety does not come from expecting perfection. It comes from anticipating threats, recognising threats and errors when they happen, and managing them before they lead to an accident. An error is not a moral failing to be hidden. It is an event to be trapped.

The model has three layers.

The first is threats. Those are things outside the pilot's control that increase complexity, and must be managed.

The second is errors. Those are pilot actions or inactions that lead away from what was intended or expected.

And the third is undesired aircraft states. That is the aircraft in a position, speed, attitude or configuration that reduces safety margins. It is the last stage before an incident or accident.

Each layer can be managed, leaving the flight safe. Or it can be mismanaged, leading to the next layer. A mismanaged threat often produces an error. A mismanaged threat or error produces an undesired aircraft state. And a mismanaged undesired aircraft state produces the incident or accident.

[[diagram:tem-model]]
Picture three boxes in a row across the top. On the left, threats, outside your control: weather, terrain, traffic, ATC changes, defects, passengers and time pressure. In the middle, errors, your own action or inaction: handling, procedural, or communication, such as the landing flap not set. On the right, the undesired aircraft state, where safety margins are reduced, such as fast and long on final. Arrows marked mismanaged run from left to right along the top, and from the last box down to incident or accident. Arrows marked managed, trapped and recovered drop from each box down to a safe flight. Underneath sit the three countermeasures: anticipate, recognise, recover.

The model's value is that it gives you three chances to stop the chain, at three different layers, instead of one.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. "Well-trained pilots do not make errors", and "errors should be hidden", are both the opposite of TEM. The premise is that errors are inevitable. The way to deal with them is to expect them, look for them, and report them.

## Threats: what the world throws at you (2.15.3)

A threat is an event or error that occurs outside the influence of the pilot, increases operational complexity, and must be managed to maintain safety margins. The key words are outside the influence of the pilot. You did not cause it, and you cannot make it go away. But you can plan for it, and fly around it.

Threats come in three kinds, by when you can see them.

Anticipated threats are known before you go. A forecast crosswind. A busy Saturday circuit. An unfamiliar strip.

Unexpected threats appear in flight without warning. A sudden traffic conflict. A bird strike. A rough-running engine.

And latent threats are hidden in the system until they bite. An ambiguous chart. A poorly designed fuel selector. A club culture of just going for a look.

Threats also fall into two families, by where they come from.

Environmental threats come from the world around the flight. Weather: low cloud, thunderstorms, crosswind, turbulence, and heat, which means density altitude. Terrain and obstacles, and unfamiliar aerodromes. Other traffic, and busy circuits, like Jandakot. ATC clearances and changes, and airspace. And wildlife, such as kangaroos on outback strips.

Organisational and other threats come from people and systems. Time pressure, and commercial or social pressure. Aircraft defects, like a rough engine or an unserviceable radio. Passengers, through distraction, illness, or pressure to continue. Poor or missing documentation, and NOTAMs. And fatigue imposed by schedules.

Notice that some of these come from other people's errors. An ATC clearance with a wrong level. A refueller who leaves a cap loose. From your seat, those are threats, because they arose outside your influence. Your own mistakes are errors.

Question. A passenger keeps talking during the pre-landing checks. Threat, or error?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: a threat. It comes from outside the pilot, and increases the workload and the chance of a missed item. If the pilot then misses the flap selection because of it, that omission is an error. Managing the threat prevents it, with a polite "quiet please until we're on the ground" in the briefing.

## Errors: what you do wrong (2.15.4)

An error is a pilot action or inaction that leads away from what was intended or expected. TEM sorts them into three groups.

First, handling errors. That is flying the aeroplane: poor speed control on final, a ballooned flare, an unbalanced turn.

Second, procedural errors. That is doing the wrong thing in the procedure, or skipping something: a missed check, a wrong frequency, a wrong altimeter setting.

And third, communication errors. Those are between you and anyone else: a misheard clearance, an incomplete readback, a passenger briefing that left out the door latch.

An error on its own often does nothing. What matters is what happens next. There are four outcomes. It may be trapped before it has any effect. It may be inconsequential. It may lead to another error. Or it may produce an undesired aircraft state.

Let's work one. You are departing Jandakot eastbound for Northam, over the Darling Scarp. The ATIS gives QNH {1012|ten twelve}. On the ground, you set {1022|ten twenty-two} on the subscale by mistake, a transposition error. And the pre-take-off check does not catch it. What does the error do to the altimeter, and by how much? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
First, what the error does. The altimeter now reads as if the pressure level of {1022 hPa|ten twenty-two hectopascals} were sea level. Use the rule of thumb of 30 ft per hPa. The error is 10 hectopascals times 30, which is 300 ft. And with the subscale set too high, the altimeter over-reads. At an indicated 3,500 ft, you are at about 3,200 ft.

Second, why it matters. Crossing the Scarp, you have 300 ft less clearance than you think from terrain and towers. In cloud or at night, that is the margin that matters.

Third, how it is caught. In the cruise check, you cross-check the subscale against the area QNH you noted on the flight log, and they disagree.

Fourth, the action. Reset {1012|ten twelve}. Confirm the aeroplane's actual height against terrain and your planned level. Check that no airspace limit has been infringed. And tell ATC if you are in or near controlled airspace.

The error was committed, and escaped the first trap. But because the cruise check exists, and you used it, it was caught before it produced an undesired aircraft state. The flight stayed safe. So the answer once more: the altimeter over-reads by 300 ft.

Question. The error in that example slipped past the pre-take-off check. Does that mean TEM failed?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: no. TEM expects some errors to escape the first defence, which is why it has several layers. The error was recognised at the next check, and managed before it created an undesired aircraft state. That is TEM working as intended.

## Checklists and SOPs: built-in error traps (2.15.5)

The lesson on human factors considerations showed how fragile short-term memory is, under workload, stress and interruption. A checklist is the answer to that fragility. And a standard operating procedure, an SOP, a fixed way of doing a task every time, is its partner.

Checklists and SOPs prevent errors in four ways.

First, they take the load off memory, which is unreliable under workload, stress and interruption.

Second, they make actions standard, so deviations stand out.

Third, they provide a structured cross-check, that traps errors before they matter.

And fourth, they make sure critical items are done in the right order, at the right time.

The second point is the subtle one. If you always set the flaps, then the trim, then the fuel pump, in the same order, a missed item leaves a gap in a familiar pattern. And the gap is noticeable. If you do things in a different order each time, nothing looks out of place.

How you use them depends on the phase. There are three ways.

Read-and-do, for normal checks on the ground. Read the item, do it, and move on.

Flow then verify, in flight. Do the items from memory, in a fixed sweep of the cockpit. Then read the checklist to confirm.

And memory items then the checklist, in emergencies. The first actions come from memory, because there is no time to read. Then the checklist confirms nothing was missed.

The trap with any checklist is reciting it without looking. "Fuel, on", said while your eyes are on the runway, is not a check. A checklist traps errors only when each item is confirmed by looking at, or touching, the thing it names.

Question. Why does flying a standard flow make a missed item easier to spot?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because a standard flow makes the cockpit look the same at the same point every time. A missed item leaves a switch or lever out of its usual position, in a familiar pattern, and the difference stands out. In a random sequence, nothing looks unusual.

## The single-pilot TEM process (2.15.2)

In an airliner, two pilots share the work of watching for threats, and catching each other's errors. Flying alone, you have to do both jobs yourself. The single-pilot process is three words, applied to every phase of flight.

First, anticipate. In planning, and before each phase, departure, cruise and arrival, ask: what threats are ahead, and what will I do about them? Brief yourself out loud.

Second, recognise. Monitor and cross-check, so that threats, errors and undesired aircraft states are spotted early.

And third, recover. Take action to restore safety margins. Trap the error, manage the threat, get out of the undesired state.

The countermeasures include planning, briefings, checklists and SOPs, monitoring and cross-checking, workload management, and personal minimums.

The briefing out loud is the heart of single-pilot TEM. Here is one. "The threats on this departure are the crosswind from the left, the traffic in the training area, and the trees at the end of the runway. If the engine fails below 500 ft, I land ahead." Saying that turns vague awareness into a plan. And it makes the plan easy to recall when the threat arrives. It is the same loop as the DECIDE model, run in advance.

[[diagram:decision-making-decide]]
Picture the six steps of DECIDE arranged in a circle. Detect at the top, then clockwise through estimate, choose, identify, do, and evaluate, and back to detect. Now lay TEM over it. Anticipate is DECIDE run on the ground. Recognise is the detect step, in flight. And recover is choose, identify and do, with evaluate to check that it worked.

Let's work one. You are 15 nm from Wagin on a hot, gusty afternoon, arriving for the first time. Run the three words over the arrival. Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
Anticipate the threats. An unfamiliar aerodrome. A crosswind from the north-west, gusting. A high density altitude for the go-around. Possible kangaroos near the strip late in the day. And other traffic on the CTAF.

Anticipate the plan. Overfly to inspect the strip and the windsock. Crosswind technique, with a gust margin on the approach speed. And a decision point at 300 ft: if not stable, go around early, knowing the climb will be sluggish.

Recognise. On final, check the speed and alignment against the brief. And watch the strip edges for movement.

Recover. At the decision point, the approach is unstable in the gusts. So you go around as briefed, and try again with more margin, or divert.

The go-around was not a surprise, because it was part of the plan before you joined the circuit. So the answer, in one line: brief the threats and a 300 ft decision point before you arrive, and the go-around becomes part of the plan.

## From threat or error to undesired aircraft state (2.15.6)

An undesired aircraft state is the result of a threat or error that was not managed. The aeroplane is somewhere, at a speed, in an attitude, or in a configuration, that reduces the safety margin. It is the last layer before an incident or accident, so recognising it early is critical.

Here are four examples, and each follows the same chain.

First, a density altitude threat, not planned for. It is a hot afternoon at a short country strip, with trees at the end. The pilot uses the sea-level take-off distance from memory. The aeroplane accelerates slowly, lifts off late, and climbs at a shallow angle towards the trees. It is low, slow, and near obstacles.

Second, a navigation error, not checked. A wrong heading is set on the heading indicator, unchecked against the compass. Twenty minutes later, the pilot is lost, low on fuel, or infringing controlled airspace.

Third, a gusty crosswind, not anticipated. There is no gust margin on the approach speed, and a sudden lull drops the airspeed on short final. The aeroplane is low and slow, sinking below the approach path.

And fourth, a distracting passenger, not briefed. The result is a missed checklist item, and a landing with the flap not set.

Recognise the undesired state early, and recover. Go around from an unstable approach. Turn back from weather. Follow the lost procedure. The recovery is almost always to restore a margin first, height, speed, or distance from cloud, before trying to diagnose what went wrong.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Do not confuse the layers. Low and slow on short final is not a threat, or an error. It is the undesired aircraft state that a threat or error produced. The exam describes the aeroplane's condition, and asks which layer it is.

## Resources: you have more than you think (2.15.7)

When things start to go wrong, pilots often act as if they are completely alone. You rarely are. A pilot has more resources than they often think. There are six groups.

First, ATC, and the Flight Information Service. Ask for help, or for a position fix. Declare PAN PAN or MAYDAY early. ATC radar and ADS-B surveillance can identify your position.

Second, the emergency frequency, {121.5 MHz|one two one decimal five megahertz}, and other aircraft on frequency, which can relay for you.

Third, navigation aids. GNSS, charts, the ERSA, the planned flight log, and the visual features around you.

Fourth, the aircraft. The POH procedures, the autopilot if one is fitted, and the fuel and endurance information.

Fifth, passengers. They can help look for landmarks, hold the chart, and watch for traffic.

And sixth, what you do before flight. Forecasts, NAIPS, your instructor, personal minimums, and an alternate.

The reluctance to use them is a human factor in itself. Pride, and the fear of looking foolish on the radio, from the lesson on human factors considerations. A call made early, while you still have fuel, height and daylight, gives the people helping you options. The same call made late gives them very few.

Two situations come up often. First, lost. Hold or orbit near a recognisable feature, or fly the planned heading. Conserve fuel. Check the heading indicator against the compass. Use the radio early. And consider a precautionary landing before the fuel or daylight runs out.

Second, adverse weather. Turn back early, with a 180 degree turn on instruments if needed. Descend only if the terrain allows. Tell ATC. And land at the nearest suitable aerodrome.

Question. Why is an early call to ATC or Flight Information Service better than a late one, when you are uncertain of your position?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because early, you still have the fuel, height and daylight that give everyone options. Surveillance can find you. A heading to a known feature can be given. A diversion can be planned. Late, with fuel and light running out, the options shrink to an emergency.

## Prioritising: aviate, navigate, communicate (2.15.8)

You are climbing out of Rottnest at 300 ft, when the door pops open with a bang and a roar of air. Your attention snaps to the door. This is the moment the prioritising rule is for.

When something goes wrong, workload jumps, and attention narrows. That is the right-hand side of the arousal curve, from the lesson on human factors considerations. Without priorities, pilots fixate on the problem, a door, a radio, a GPS, and lose control of the aircraft. Many accidents have begun with a minor fault, and ended with a perfectly serviceable aeroplane flown into the ground while the pilot dealt with it.

[[diagram:stress-performance-curve]]
Picture an upside-down U, with arousal along the bottom and performance up the side. The best performance is at the top of the hump, at moderate arousal. A sudden fault pushes you up and over, down the right-hand side, into anxiety, tunnelling and poor memory. Attention narrows onto the problem, and the aeroplane is the first thing to be forgotten.

The order is: aviate, navigate, communicate, and then manage the systems. Fly the aeroplane first, and keep it clear of terrain and cloud. Then work out where you are going. Then talk. And shed the lower-priority tasks.

Apply that to the open door.

Aviate. Hold the climb attitude and airspeed, keep it balanced, and ignore the door. In most light aeroplanes, an open door is noisy but not dangerous, but check your POH.

Navigate. Continue the circuit, and plan to land back on the runway you left.

Communicate. A short call to tell the traffic you are returning.

And the systems. Deal with the door on the ground.

Every one of those decisions is easy, once you refuse to let the problem set your priorities.

Question. In an undesired aircraft state, why does aviate always come before diagnosing the fault?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because the aeroplane's flight path is what keeps you alive, and attention narrows under stress. If you diagnose first, the attitude, speed and height drift unmonitored, and a minor fault can become a loss of control. Flying the aeroplane first preserves the margin you need to deal with everything else.

## People: relationships that keep you safe (2.15.9)

TEM is not only about you and the aeroplane. The people around a flight are among your best defences, if the relationships allow them to help. There are five to think about.

First, passengers. A clear, friendly passenger briefing turns passengers into extra eyes for traffic. It makes it easier for them to speak up. And it makes it easier for you to say, we're not going today.

Second, ATC. Respectful, clear radio use builds a good working relationship with ATC, who are then more able to help.

Third, your instructor or club. An open relationship there means you will ask questions, and report mistakes.

Fourth, other pilots. Talking with them shares local knowledge of strips, weather and hazards.

And fifth, maintenance engineers. Being assertive but courteous with them gets defects fixed properly.

The common thread is that safety information has to flow. And it only flows between people who trust each other. A passenger who has been made to feel part of the flight will tell you about the smell of fuel. One who feels a nuisance will sit in silence. A club where errors are discussed openly learns from each one. A club where they are mocked hides them, until one becomes an accident.

## Bringing it together

You are planning a Saturday flight from Jandakot to Busselton with two friends.

Anticipate. The threats are a forecast sea breeze at Busselton giving a crosswind, a busy Jandakot departure, two passengers who have not flown in a light aeroplane before, and a social plan for lunch that creates pressure to arrive. You brief the passengers on the doors and harnesses, on staying quiet during take-off and landing, and on helping to spot traffic. And you tell them that if the weather is wrong, you will turn back, and they will still get lunch.

Recognise. Abeam Mandurah, the cloud ahead is lower than forecast. And you notice you have drifted 200 ft below your planned level while looking at it.

Recover. You climb back to your level. That is an error trapped. You check the Busselton weather with Flight Information Service. That is a resource. And you decide on a decision point at Lake Clifton.

At the decision point, the cloud is still lowering. So you aviate first, and turn back. Then you tell your passengers and ATC.

A threat was managed. An error was trapped. And the undesired aircraft state never happened.

Question. In that flight, which items were threats, which was an error, and what would the undesired aircraft state have been?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the threats were the sea breeze crosswind, the busy traffic, the inexperienced passengers, the social pressure, and the lowering cloud. The error was drifting below the planned level. And the undesired aircraft state, if both were mismanaged, would have been the aeroplane low, close to cloud and terrain, in deteriorating weather.

## Key points

Let's recap.

The idea. Threats and errors are inevitable. Safety comes from anticipating, recognising and managing them. The model has three layers: threats, errors, and undesired aircraft states.

The process. Single-pilot TEM is anticipate, recognise, recover, on every phase, with a self-brief out loud before each.

Threats. A threat arises outside the pilot's influence, and adds complexity. Weather, terrain, traffic, defects, passengers, and pressure.

Errors. An error is the pilot's own action or inaction. One that escapes the first trap can still be caught at the next check, and corrected before it produces an undesired aircraft state.

Checklists and SOPs. They unload memory, standardise actions so deviations stand out, and provide a cross-check. But only if each item is actually looked at.

Undesired aircraft states. A wrong position, speed, attitude or configuration is what a mismanaged threat or error produces. Recover by restoring a margin first.

Resources. ATC and the Flight Information Service, {121.5 MHz|one two one decimal five megahertz}, other aircraft, GNSS and charts, the POH, passengers, and the planning done before flight. Use them early.

Priorities. Aviate, navigate, communicate. Fly the aeroplane first, then everything else.

And people. Good relationships with passengers, ATC, instructors, other pilots and engineers let safety information flow.

A final exam tip. A threat comes from outside: weather, traffic, defects. An error is the pilot's own action or inaction. An undesired aircraft state is what results when either is mismanaged. And in any undesired aircraft state, fly the aircraft first. Have these by heart. Anticipate, recognise, recover. Aviate, navigate, communicate. And the four things checklists and SOPs do to trap errors.

That's the end of PHFC 2.15.
