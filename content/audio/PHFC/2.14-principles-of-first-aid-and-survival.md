This is PHFC 2.14, Principles of first aid and survival. By the end you will know where the first aid and survival guidance lives in flight, the order of the first aid action plan, how to treat the common injuries of a forced landing, the survival priorities in the outback, and the signals that help searchers find you. There are a few questions along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (PHFC 2.14)

The exam wants one thing from you, in four parts. Know what first aid and survival guidance the ERSA carries, and be able to recall and apply it. That means the first aid action plan and the treatment of common injuries. The survival priorities after a forced landing. And the signals that help searchers find you.

## Why this matters

Western Australia is a big, empty place to have a forced landing. Between Kalgoorlie and the South Australian border, or north of Meekatharra, you can fly for an hour over station country and salt lakes, with no town, no road traffic, and no mobile coverage. And on a summer day the ground is over 40 degrees.

A forced landing there that injures nobody can still kill you, if you walk off into the heat. And a minor injury left untreated can become a serious one before help arrives.

This lesson teaches the first aid and survival principles in the ERSA's emergency section, and the reasoning behind each. That way you would know what to do, and in what order, with no one to ask.

The exam asks where the information lives, the order of the first aid action plan, and the CPR ratio. It asks the right survival action after a remote forced landing. And it asks the meaning of the ground-to-air signals.

## Where the information lives (2.14.1)

Every flight in Australia carries the ERSA, on paper or on a tablet. ERSA stands for En Route Supplement Australia. It is the book you use for aerodrome details and frequencies.

It has an EMERG section, short for emergency procedures. That section contains emergency procedures and signals, search and rescue information, and first aid and survival guidance. It is the reference the exam means whenever it asks where a pilot finds first aid, survival or ground-to-air signal information in flight.

Know where it is in your copy before you need it. On an outback flight, after a forced landing, it may be the only reference you have. And a tablet with a flat battery is no reference at all.

Content and layout change between editions. So check the current ERSA, and read the section through on the ground at least once. The time to learn what an arrow on the ground means is not when you are building one.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. It lists other documents as distractors: the maintenance release, the weight and balance schedule, the NOTAMs. None of them carries first aid or survival guidance. The answer is always the EMERG section of the ERSA.

## First aid: the action plan (2.14.1)

Picture the scene. You have made a forced landing in a paddock east of Merredin. The aeroplane has run through a fence, and stopped on its nose. You are shaken but unhurt. Your passenger is slumped, and not answering.

The instinct is to rush straight to them. The first aid action plan exists to stop that instinct from creating a second casualty. And it puts the life-saving steps in the order that saves the most lives.

That plan is {DRSABCD|D R S A B C D}. It has seven steps, one for each letter. Danger. Response. Send for help. Airway. Breathing. CPR. And defibrillation.

[[diagram:first-aid-drsabcd]]
Picture seven numbered tiles laid out in order, four along the top row and three along the bottom. The first tile, the one that comes before everything, is danger: fuel, fire, a running engine, and power lines. The sixth is CPR, 30 compressions to 2 breaths. Danger first, always. Fuel, fire and a running engine come before the casualty.

Each step has a reason for its place. Let's take them in turn.

First, danger. A crashed aeroplane is a hazardous place. Fuel may be leaking onto a hot exhaust. The propeller may still be turning, if the engine has not stopped. And a wire strike may have left live power lines on the ground. If you become a casualty too, nobody helps anyone. Check for danger to yourself, to bystanders and to the casualty. Turn the fuel and the magnetos off, if it is safe to reach them. And if there is a fire risk, getting the casualty away from the aeroplane comes before anything else.

Second, response. Is the person conscious? Talk to them, and gently squeeze their shoulders. A person who answers is conscious and breathing, and you can move on to their injuries. A person who does not respond needs the rest of the plan, and quickly.

Third, send for help. Help takes time to arrive, so start it early, before you are busy with the casualty. In a town, that is a phone call to {000|triple zero}, or {112|one one two} on a mobile. In the outback, it means activating the ELT, the aircraft's emergency locator transmitter, or your PLB, your personal locator beacon. Both alert the search and rescue system by satellite.

Fourth, airway. An unconscious person's tongue can fall back and block the throat. Vomit or blood can do the same. Open the airway by tilting the head back and lifting the chin, and clear anything visible from the mouth. Nothing else works if air cannot get in.

Fifth, breathing. Look at the chest. Listen at the mouth. And feel for breath on your cheek. Occasional gasps are not normal breathing.

Sixth, CPR. If the person is unresponsive and not breathing normally, their heart is probably not pumping effectively. The brain is starting to die for lack of oxygen. The lesson on the atmosphere explains why the brain is so vulnerable.

Chest compressions push blood round the body by hand. The breaths put oxygen into it. The ratio is 30 compressions to 2 breaths, at about 100 to 120 compressions per minute. Push hard and fast in the centre of the chest. Keep going until help arrives, the person recovers, or you physically cannot continue.

And seventh, defibrillation. An AED, an automated external defibrillator, can restart a heart in some kinds of cardiac arrest. It tells you by voice what to do. You will not find one in a paddock, but you will at many airports and clubs.

So, once more: danger, response, send for help, airway, breathing, CPR at 30 to 2, and defibrillation.

Question. Why does checking for danger come before checking the casualty?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because a rescuer who is injured or killed, by fire, a turning propeller, or live power lines, cannot help anyone, and becomes another casualty. Making the scene safe, or moving the casualty out of immediate danger, protects both of you. And it lets the rest of the plan be carried out.

## Treating common injuries (2.14.1)

Once the action plan has dealt with airway, breathing and circulation, look for the injuries a forced landing typically causes. The ERSA's first aid guidance covers the principles. The reasoning is what makes them stick. There are six to know.

First, bleeding. Blood loss reduces the haemoglobin available to carry oxygen. That is the anaemic hypoxia from the lesson on hypoxia. And enough of it causes shock. Apply firm direct pressure, with a pad on the wound. Raise the limb if you can. And keep the pressure on. Lifting the pad to look lets the clot break, and the bleeding restart.

Second, burns. Heat keeps damaging tissue after the source is removed. Cool the burn with clean running water for 20 minutes. That stops the damage, and eases the pain. Do not use ice, butter or creams. Ice damages tissue further, and grease traps the heat. Cover the burn loosely with something clean, such as cling film or a clean cloth, to keep out infection.

Third, fractures. A broken bone that moves damages the nerves, blood vessels and muscle around it. Immobilise it in the position you found it, with padding, and a splint if you have one. Do not try to straighten it.

Fourth, shock. Shock here is not fright. It is a circulation failure, where too little blood reaches the organs, from blood loss, burns or severe injury. The casualty is pale, cold and clammy, with a fast, weak pulse. Lie them down, keep them warm, and reassure them. Give nothing by mouth, in case they need surgery.

Fifth, spinal injury. The casualty may have hurt their neck or back. The signs are a heavy impact, neck pain, or numbness or tingling in the limbs. Moving them could cause permanent paralysis. So avoid moving them, unless there is immediate danger, such as fire, which outweighs the spinal risk.

And sixth, the recovery position. An unconscious casualty who is breathing should be rolled onto their side, in the recovery position, with the airway clear. That way vomit drains out of the mouth, rather than into the lungs. Keep checking their breathing.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. With a burn, the urge is to apply something cold and quick: ice, or a cream from the first aid kit. The ERSA principle is cool running water for 20 minutes, and nothing greasy or frozen. Likewise with a fracture, "straighten it to make the splint fit" is wrong. Splint it as found.

Question. An unconscious passenger is breathing normally after a forced landing. There is no sign of spinal injury, and no fire. What position do you put them in, and why?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the recovery position, on their side, with the airway open. An unconscious person cannot protect their own airway. On their side, the tongue falls forward, and any vomit drains out instead of being inhaled.

## Survival: what comes first (2.14.1)

Now change the scenario. You have made a successful forced landing on a claypan, two hours' flying from Kalgoorlie. Nobody is hurt. The aeroplane is intact, but cannot fly. It is {2 pm|two p m} in February. What do you do, and in what order?

The survival priorities, in order, are protection, location, water, and then food.

[[diagram:survival-priorities]]
Picture a numbered list running down the left side. One, protection: first aid, shelter and shade. Two, location: the beacon on, the radio, and signals. Three, water: cut sweat loss, and rest in shade. Four, food, last. On the right, the aeroplane sits on the ground, with people in the shade under its wing, a V laid out beside it, and a fire for smoke by day and flame by night. The message beside it: stay with the aircraft. It is far easier to find than a person walking.

Take the priorities one at a time.

Protection comes first, because the immediate threats are injury and exposure. Treat anyone who is hurt, using the action plan. Then get out of the sun or the cold. In the WA outback in summer, the heat can kill in a day. At night in winter, it can be close to freezing. The aircraft and its wing give you ready-made shade and shelter.

Location comes next. Help the rescuers find you. A search will start, but it can only end when they see you. So everything that makes you easier to see shortens your wait. There is more on that in the next section.

Water is third. A person can survive weeks without food, but only days without water, and in heat far less. In the WA outback heat, dehydration is the main killer.

Food is last. You will be hungry, but hunger will not harm you in the time a search is likely to take. Eating also uses water to digest. So if water is short, eat little.

Now the single most important survival decision. Stay with the aircraft. There are three reasons, and each one is strong on its own.

First, it is far easier to see. From the air, a person on foot in scrub is almost invisible. A light aeroplane, especially with its doors open and bright material spread around it, is a large, unnatural shape that searchers are trained to spot.

Second, it gives shelter. Shade under the wing, protection from wind, and materials for signals and warmth: seat cushions, cowlings, oil and fuel.

And third, the search starts where you said you would be. Your flight notification, the flight plan or SARTIME you lodged before departure, tells the search and rescue system your planned route. If you do not cancel your SARTIME, the search is triggered, and it begins along that route. Walk away from the aircraft, and you leave the place they will look.

Leave the aircraft only if you are certain help is close and reachable. A homestead in sight. A road with traffic visible. A shimmer on the horizon that might be a building is not certainty.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. "Walk to the nearest town for help", and "split up to cover more ground", are the exam's wrong answers. Both take people away from the aircraft, use up water, and make them harder to find. The right answer is to stay together, with the aircraft.

## Being found (2.14.1)

There are three ways to help them find you: the beacon, the radio, and visual signals.

First, the beacon. Activate the ELT or PLB, which transmits on {406 MHz|four oh six megahertz}, and keep it activated. The {406 MHz|four oh six megahertz} signal is picked up by satellites, and gives rescuers your position. Turning it off to save the battery stops the very thing that will bring help.

Second, the radio. If the aircraft's radio works, try the area frequency, and {121.5 MHz|one two one decimal five megahertz}, the emergency frequency. An aircraft passing high overhead may hear you, even when nobody on the ground can.

Third, visual signals. Searchers look for anything that does not belong in the landscape: smoke, flashes, straight lines, bright colours. Prepare them in advance, so they are ready the moment you hear an aircraft. There are four kinds.

A fire, with smoke by day, and flame by night. Add green leaves, oil or a tyre for smoke. Keep it well away from the aircraft, and any spilt fuel.

A signal mirror, or any shiny surface, to flash at an aircraft.

Bright material spread on the ground: clothing, cowlings, life jackets.

And the ground-to-air signal codes shown in the ERSA. Those are standard symbols that survivors lay out on the ground, to tell searchers what they need. Here are five of them. V means require assistance. X means require medical assistance. N means no. Y means yes. And an arrow means proceeding in this direction.

[[diagram:ground-to-air-survival-codes]]
Picture the five symbols laid out in a row, as they would look from the air. From left to right: a V, require assistance. An X, require medical assistance. An N, no. A Y, yes. And an arrow, proceeding in this direction. What to notice: V for assistance and X for medical assistance are the two to know. Make every symbol large and contrasting, and lay an arrow only if you leave.

Make them large and contrasting. Rocks on red sand. Dark branches on a pale claypan. Or trenches dug so that their shadows show. A symbol a few metres across is invisible from a search aircraft at height. So make each line as long and as wide as the materials allow.

The arrow is the one to use only if you really do leave. It points the way you have gone.

Question. Survivors decide to walk to a homestead they can see. Which symbol should they lay out, and why?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: an arrow pointing the direction they are going, meaning proceeding in this direction. Searchers who find the aircraft then know where to look, rather than starting a fresh search around the wreck.

## Water and heat: the WA survival problem (2.14.1)

In a WA summer, the survival problem is mostly a water problem. And the way to solve it is to cut the losses, rather than to ration the intake.

You lose water mainly through sweat. The harder you work, and the hotter you are, the more you lose. So rest in the shade by day. Do any work, such as building signals or collecting wood, in the cool of the evening and early morning. Stay clothed and covered. Skin exposed to the sun and the hot wind loses more water, not less. And sit off the hot ground if you can.

Drink what you need. Rationing water to a few mouthfuls a day while you work in the sun risks dehydration, with water still in the bottle. The water does you more good inside you than in the container. Conserve sweat, not water.

The don'ts follow from the same physiology. There are four.

First, do not walk away from the aircraft, unless you are certain help is close and reachable.

Second, do not ration water to the point of dehydration. Drink what you need, and reduce sweat loss instead.

Third, do not drink alcohol, urine or seawater. Each makes the body lose more water than it gains.

And fourth, do not smoke, or light fires, near spilt fuel.

Keep the group together, and give everyone a task. One on the signal fire. One watching and listening for aircraft. One looking after an injured passenger. Activity with a purpose keeps morale up. And morale keeps people doing the right things.

## Bringing it together

Let's work one. A pilot makes a forced landing on a station track, 120 nm east of Kalgoorlie, on a 40 degree day. The pilot is uninjured, with 4 litres of water. What happens in the first hour? Pause if you'd like to think it through.
[[pause 6]]
Here is how it goes.

Protection first. There are no injuries to treat, so the pilot moves into the wing's shade, out of the sun.

Then location. The pilot activates the PLB, and leaves it on. And makes a radio call on the area frequency, and on {121.5 MHz|one two one decimal five megahertz}.

Then signals. The pilot sets out a V, require assistance, near the aircraft, large and contrasting.

Then water. The pilot stays in the wing's shade during the day, to cut sweat loss, and drinks as needed.

And finally, getting ready for the searchers. The pilot prepares a smoky fire, away from the aircraft and the fuel, to light when an aircraft is heard.

Throughout, the pilot stays with the aircraft. The search will start along the notified route, and the aeroplane is what the searchers will see. So, in one sentence: shade, beacon on, radio call, a V on the ground, water drunk as needed, and a fire ready, all beside the aircraft.

Question. Why is reducing sweat loss a better strategy than strictly rationing water?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because the body loses water mainly through sweat, and dehydration harms you whether the water is lost or never drunk. Resting in shade, and working only in the cool, reduces the loss at its source. And drinking what you need keeps the water where it does good.

## Key points

Let's recap.

Where. First aid, survival and ground-to-air signal information is in the EMERG section of the ERSA. Read it before you need it, and check the current edition.

First aid. {DRSABCD|D R S A B C D}, with danger first. CPR is 30 compressions to 2 breaths, at about 100 to 120 a minute. Direct pressure for bleeding. 20 minutes of cool running water for burns. Immobilise fractures as found. Keep a shocked casualty lying down and warm. Do not move a suspected spinal injury, unless they are in danger. And put an unconscious, breathing casualty in the recovery position.

Survival. Protection, location, water, food. Stay with the aircraft, and stay together. It is easier to see, it gives shelter, and the search follows your notified route.

Being found. The ELT or PLB, on {406 MHz|four oh six megahertz}, switched on and left on. The radio, on the area frequency and {121.5 MHz|one two one decimal five megahertz}. Smoke by day, and flame by night. A mirror. Bright material. And the ground-to-air codes: V assistance, X medical assistance, N no, Y yes, and an arrow for proceeding this way.

Water. Dehydration is the main killer in the WA outback. Conserve sweat, not water.

A final exam tip. The standard survival answer is to stay with the aircraft, activate the ELT, and conserve water by reducing sweat loss. Searchers look for the aircraft along the notified route. Have these by heart. ERSA EMERG for where. {DRSABCD|D R S A B C D} for the order. 30 to 2 for CPR. Protection, location, water, food for survival. And V and X, for assistance and medical assistance.
