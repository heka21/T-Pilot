This is PAKA 3.1, Take-off and landing performance, for the private pilot licence. By the end you will be able to say what every factor does to the take-off, the landing and the climb, work a density height in your head, and pick the weight limit that governs today. There are worked examples and questions along the way, so pause whenever you want a moment to think.

## What the exam expects (PAKA 3.1)

The exam wants five things from you. First, whether each factor in a long list, from wind and temperature to slope, surface and frost, increases or decreases the take-off distance, the landing distance and the take-off climb. Second, the difference between pressure height and density height. Third, how to use the altimeter on the ground to find the local QNH, the pressure height and the elevation. Fourth, the structural take-off and landing weights, and the climb weight limit. And fifth, what is likely to happen when the weight limits are exceeded.

## Why this matters

As a private pilot, you will carry passengers, and use aerodromes your instructor never sent you to. A sloping farm strip near York after winter rain. Kalgoorlie on a 42 degree afternoon. A short coastal strip with a sea breeze swinging round. With four people, full fuel, and a hot or wet strip, the generous margins of the training circuit can disappear, without anything looking different from the cockpit.

This subtopic brings together the recreational licence lessons on these topics, and adds what the private licence needs. Seeing all the factors at once, knowing how they stack, and knowing which weight limit governs today.

CASA names density and pressure height, and the take-off and landing charts, as the most common errors in the PPLA exam. So the exam leans on exactly these skills.

## Three questions behind every factor

Here is the one picture that makes every factor predictable. Every take-off and landing comes down to three questions.

The first question. How fast must the aeroplane be going over the ground? The wing lifts off and touches down at a fixed indicated airspeed. But what the runway sees is the groundspeed at that moment. And the groundspeed depends on the wind, and on the air density, because thin air needs a higher true airspeed for the same indicated airspeed.

The second question. How hard can it speed up, or slow down? On the take-off roll, the accelerating force is thrust, minus drag, rolling resistance, and any slope. On the landing roll, the slowing force is braking, drag and rolling resistance, plus or minus the slope. The distance is the energy to be gained or lost, divided by that force. And because kinetic energy goes with the square of the groundspeed, a small change in groundspeed makes a big change in distance.

The third question. Once airborne, how much power is left over to climb? The take-off climb is set by the surplus of power available over power required. Thin air cuts the power available. Weight and drag raise the power required.

Groundspeed needed, force available, and power left over. Hold those three, and you can work out every factor rather than memorise it.

## Pressure height and density height

You met both for the recreational licence. Here they are in one place, because this exam asks you to state the difference, and then use it.

Pressure height is the height in the International Standard Atmosphere, the ISA, at which the pressure is the same as the pressure where you are. It is what the altimeter reads with {1013 hPa|ten thirteen hectopascals} set on the subscale. It is the right number for the airspace system, because flight levels use pressure.

Density height is the height in the ISA at which the air has the same density as the air where you are. You find it by correcting the pressure height for temperature. The correction is about 120 ft for every degree the actual temperature differs from the ISA temperature at that pressure height. Density is what the wing, the propeller and the engine respond to. So performance follows density height.

The syllabus lists density height in non-standard conditions as a factor of its own, and that is the key. Temperature, QNH and elevation are three routes to the same thing. A low QNH raises the pressure height. High elevation raises it further. And a hot day lifts the density height above it. The aeroplane does not know which route brought the thin air. It only knows the density height.

The ISA temperature is 15 degrees at sea level, and it falls 2 degrees for every 1,000 ft. So there are two formulas. Pressure height equals the elevation, plus {1013|ten thirteen} minus the QNH, times 30. And density height equals the pressure height, plus 120 times the actual temperature minus the ISA temperature.

[[widget:density-height]]
Picture the two corrections side by side. Low QNH and heat both push the density height up. But heat is the big one. Every degree above the ISA adds about 120 ft, while each hectopascal of low QNH adds only 30 ft.

Let's work one: density height at a wheatbelt strip in summer. The elevation is 1,000 ft, the QNH is {1008|ten oh eight}, and the temperature is 36 degrees. Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 8]]
Step one, the pressure height. {1013|ten thirteen} minus {1008|ten oh eight}, times 30, is 150 ft. So the pressure height is 1,000 plus 150, which is 1,150 ft.

Step two, the ISA temperature at 1,150 ft. 2 degrees per thousand feet, times 1.15, is 2.3 degrees. 15 minus 2.3 is 12.7 degrees.

Step three, the deviation. 36 minus 12.7 is 23.3 degrees above the ISA.

Step four, the density height. 23.3 times 120 is 2,796 ft. Add the 1,150 ft pressure height, and the density height is about 3,950 ft.

Of the 2,950 ft gained, the low QNH gave 150 ft, and the heat nearly 2,800 ft. At roughly 3 percent of power lost for every 1,000 ft of density height, the engine is giving about 88 percent of its sea-level power. So the answer: a density height of about 3,950 ft.

Now the other direction. Here is one for you to try. A frosty July morning, at a strip with an elevation of 400 ft. The QNH is {1026|ten twenty-six}, and the temperature is 2 degrees. Is the density height above or below the elevation, and by roughly how much?
[[pause 10]]
The pressure height: {1013|ten thirteen} minus {1026|ten twenty-six} is minus 13. Minus 13 times 30 is minus 390 ft. 400 minus 390 is 10 ft. The ISA temperature at 10 ft is 15 degrees. Today is 2, so the deviation is minus 13 degrees. Minus 13 times 120 is minus 1,560 ft. Add the 10 ft, and the density height is about minus 1,550 ft. That is nearly 2,000 ft below the elevation.

Cold, high-pressure air is denser than standard. So the aeroplane performs better than at sea level on a standard day, if the frost is off the wings.

[[diagram:power-loss-with-altitude]]
Picture full-throttle power sloping down with height, roughly 3 percent for every 1,000 ft, to about 75 percent by 7,000 to 8,000 ft. The climb, which lives on the surplus power, suffers most.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. The CASA workbook charts are entered with pressure height and temperature, and apply the density correction internally. If you work out the density height, and then enter the chart with it and the temperature, you have corrected for temperature twice. So for a chart, stop at the pressure height. For an increase or decrease question, think in density height.

Question. Two aerodromes have the same density height today. One is high and cool, and one is low and hot. Which aeroplane needs the longer take-off run, if everything else is equal?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: neither. They need the same. Performance follows density height, not elevation or temperature separately. So the wing, the propeller and the engine behave identically in both places.

## Reading the heights off the altimeter

The altimeter is a barometer with an adjustable datum, the subscale. It shows the height above the level where the pressure set on the subscale exists. So on the ground, it gives you three answers.

First, the local QNH. Wind the subscale until the needle shows the aerodrome elevation from ERSA. The subscale now holds the local QNH, even at a strip with no weather service.

Second, the pressure height. Set {1013|ten thirteen} on the subscale, and read the pressure height on the dial. That works because pressure height is, by definition, what the altimeter shows with standard pressure set.

Third, the elevation. Set the aerodrome QNH, and the dial should show the elevation. That doubles as the pre-flight altimeter check: within 100 ft of the elevation is acceptable for VFR. Check that figure against the current AIP.

[[widget:altimeter-subscale]]
Picture turning the subscale knob and watching the needle. Each hectopascal you wind on moves the reading about 30 ft. That is the same figure that turns the QNH into pressure height.

Question. You set {1013|ten thirteen} on the subscale at an aerodrome, and the altimeter reads 1,420 ft. The elevation is 1,300 ft. Is today's QNH above or below {1013|ten thirteen}?
[[pause 5]]
The answer: below. The pressure height is 120 ft above the elevation, so the air is thinner than standard. 120 ft, at 30 ft per hectopascal, means a QNH about 4 hectopascals below {1013|ten thirteen}. That is around {1009|ten oh nine}.

## Wind: the headwind and tailwind component

Only the part of the wind along the runway affects the distances. That is the headwind or tailwind component. The crosswind component is a control problem.

[[widget:crosswind-component]]
Picture the angle between the wind and the runway. Just 30 degrees off the runway already puts half the wind across it. And the headwind component, the part along the runway, is what the chart wants.

A headwind supplies part of the lift-off airspeed before the aeroplane has moved. So it lifts off at a lower groundspeed. Because the energy to be built up goes with the square of the groundspeed, the saving is larger than the wind speed suggests. On landing, the same lower groundspeed means less energy for the brakes. Both distances decrease.

A tailwind does the opposite. And because of that square, it does it more strongly. Both distances increase markedly. The usual rule of thumb is that a tailwind of 10 percent of the lift-off speed adds roughly 20 percent to the take-off distance.

For the climb, wind changes nothing about the rate shown on the vertical speed indicator. It changes how much ground you cover while climbing. Into a headwind, the climb gradient over the ground is steeper. With a tailwind, it is flatter, and the fence and the trees arrive sooner.

[[diagram:climb-gradient-wind]]
Picture the same aeroplane climbing at 600 ft per minute, three times over. In still air it gains 450 ft for every nautical mile. With a 20 kt headwind, 600. With a 20 kt tailwind, only 360. So a 300 ft obstacle 1 nautical mile ahead, cleared by about 150 ft in still air, is cleared by only about 60 ft with the tailwind.

When you take a wind to a chart, use the component, never the full wind speed, and check whether the chart wants it as a headwind or a tailwind. You'll find that in the lesson on performance charts.

## Slope and surface: what the ground does

Once the air has had its say, the runway itself changes the forces on the roll. Here is the mechanism in brief. First the slope.

On a slope, a part of the aeroplane's weight acts along the runway, very nearly the weight times the slope as a fraction. So on a 2 percent upslope, about 2 percent of the weight pulls the aeroplane back. That sounds small. But the net accelerating force is itself only a fraction of the weight. So, as a rule of thumb, a 2 percent upslope can add around 10 percent to the take-off distance.

An upslope lengthens the take-off, because gravity fights the thrust. And it shortens the landing, because gravity helps the brakes. A downslope does the reverse. It shortens the take-off, and lengthens the landing. And on a downslope the aeroplane tends to float, as the runway falls away under the flare.

The take-off climb through the air is the same either way, because once airborne, the slope no longer acts on the aeroplane. What changes is the ground beyond. Taking off uphill, you lift off later, and the terrain usually keeps rising. So the climb is reduced relative to the rising ground. Taking off downhill, the ground falls away.

[[diagram:runway-slope-take-off-and-landing]]
Picture two side views of a sloping runway. Taking off uphill, part of the thrust climbs the slope, so the run is longer. Landing uphill, the slope helps you stop. So with a light wind, land uphill and take off downhill.

Now the surface: dry, wet and slushy. The dry sealed runway is the baseline the charts are drawn for. Everything else is a correction to it.

A wet sealed runway changes grip far more than rolling resistance. A film of water lubricates the tyre on the bitumen. So braking is poorer, and the landing distance increases, often by 15 percent or more. The thin film adds little drag to a rolling wheel, so the take-off distance changes little. And standing water can lift the tyre clear of the surface. That is aquaplaning, with almost no braking or steering.

Wet grass hurts both. Soft, wet ground makes the wheels sink and push through it. So rolling resistance rises, and the take-off distance increases. And the slippery surface spoils braking, so the landing distance increases too.

Slush is the worst surface of all, and standing water or deep mud work by the same physics. Pushing it aside causes drag, and so does the spray hitting the airframe. Both grow with speed, while the thrust falls. So the take-off distance increases greatly, and the aeroplane may never reach lift-off speed. On landing, it aquaplanes, or barely grips, and the landing distance increases greatly. Most light-aeroplane flight manuals give no slush figures, and that silence is the answer: do not operate.

[[diagram:runway-slope-and-surface-effects]]
Picture slope and surface as one table. Slope works in opposite directions for take-off and landing. Wet and slushy surfaces lengthen both. And slush can stop you reaching lift-off speed.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Slope is the one factor that works opposite ways for take-off and landing. Uphill is bad is only half true. Uphill lengthens the take-off, but shortens the landing. And a wet sealed runway is not worse for everything. It hurts the landing much more than the take-off.

## Ground effect and wind shear

First, ground effect. Within about one wingspan of the ground, the ground squeezes the wingtip vortices and the downwash behind the wing. Induced drag falls, and the wing produces the same lift at a lower angle of attack. That is ground effect, and it cuts both ways.

On take-off, the aeroplane will fly in ground effect at a speed and angle of attack at which it cannot fly clear of it. Pulled off the runway early, it lifts to 10 or 15 ft and climbs out of the cushion. There it meets the full induced drag. And it either sinks back, or hangs there, unable to climb. So the aeroplane may lift off early but not climb, and the climb sags on leaving ground effect. The protection is to accelerate to the flight manual speed before rotating.

On landing, the reduced drag lets an aeroplane that arrives a few knots fast keep flying just above the runway. It floats, eating up landing distance.

Now wind shear. Wind shear is a change in wind speed or direction over a short distance. The danger for take-off and landing performance is a sudden loss of headwind. The aeroplane's inertia keeps its groundspeed for a moment. So the airspeed falls at once, by the amount of headwind lost, and lift falls with it.

On the take-off roll, a dying headwind means a higher groundspeed is needed at lift-off, so the take-off is longer. Just after lift-off, it is worse: a dangerous loss of airspeed and climb, close to the ground and the stall. On approach, the effect is variable. A loss of headwind makes the aeroplane sink and undershoot. A gain makes it float and land long. Either way, the landing distance is no longer what the chart assumed.

[[diagram:wind-gradient-on-approach]]
Picture an aeroplane on final into a strong headwind. Friction slows the wind close to the ground, so the headwind is strongest a few hundred feet up, and weakest at the surface. As the aeroplane descends, the headwind dies away and the airspeed decays. It sinks below the path, with no change in attitude or power, unless the pilot adds power.

In WA, you meet wind shear at the sea breeze front on the coastal plain, and under thunderstorm outflow. The defences are a speed margin in gusty conditions, a hand on the throttle, and no take-off or landing with a storm nearby.

Question. Why can ground effect make an aeroplane lift off, and then fail to climb?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: in ground effect, induced drag is lower, so the wing can support the aeroplane at a speed too low to fly clear of it. As the aeroplane rises out of the cushion, the full induced drag returns. At that low speed there is not enough surplus power to climb. So it sinks back, or hangs just above the ground.

## Frost on the aeroplane

Frost looks harmless, a thin, sandpaper-like layer on a winter morning at Narrogin. But the first few per cent of the wing's chord, near the leading edge, are where the airflow is most sensitive to roughness. Frost there makes the boundary layer turbulent, and lets it separate earlier. So the maximum lift coefficient falls, and the stall speed rises. The same roughness adds drag.

The result: the aeroplane needs a higher speed to lift off, and accelerates less well towards it. So the take-off distance increases, and it may not lift off at all at the normal rotation speed. Once airborne, less lift and more drag reduce the climb. For landing, frost is not applicable: it forms on a parked aeroplane, and you will have removed it. All frost comes off before flight, however thin.

## The complete factor table

You have now met every factor, and they fall into a pattern.

Only a headwind decreases both distances. Higher temperature, lower QNH, higher elevation and higher density height from any cause all increase both, and reduce the climb. Slope works opposite ways. No surface changes the climb itself, only where it starts. A loss of headwind lengthens the take-off and makes the landing variable. And frost has no landing entry, because it is gone before you fly.

[[diagram:takeoff-landing-factors]]
Picture that as a table of arrows. Only one factor shortens both distances: a headwind. Everything that lowers density, adds weight, adds a tailwind or slows the roll lengthens them.

The factors stack. A hot afternoon at a high, sloping, wet grass strip with a tailwind applies every penalty at once. And each correction multiplies the distance that the previous one produced. So two 10 percent penalties are not 20 percent. They are 1.1 times 1.1, which is 1.21. That is 21 percent.

[[diagram:stacked-performance-factors]]
Picture the take-off distance as a percentage of the chart's level, nil-wind figure. A 2 percent upslope adds about 10 percent, making 110. Then a tailwind of 10 percent of the lift-off speed adds about 20 percent of that already longer distance. That is 22 more, for 132, not 130. And density height and a wet grass surface multiply the result again, by amounts read from the flight manual.

## Weight limits: structural and performance

Weight appears in all three questions. A heavier aeroplane needs a higher lift-off speed, accelerates more slowly towards it, and has less surplus power to climb. Its weight limits are of two different kinds.

The first kind is structural. The maximum structural take-off weight, MTOW, is the heaviest at which the airframe is certified to begin a take-off. The maximum structural landing weight, MLW, is the heaviest at which it may land, when the undercarriage takes the touchdown loads. Both are fixed by the strength of the structure, published in section 2 of the flight manual, and never change with the weather. In many light trainers, the MLW equals the MTOW. In larger aeroplanes, the MLW is lower, so the aeroplane must burn fuel before it lands.

The second kind is performance. The climb weight limit is the heaviest weight at which the aeroplane can still meet the required take-off climb gradient, at the density height of the day. On a cool morning at a sea-level aerodrome, it is usually above the MTOW, and you never notice it. On a hot afternoon inland, it can fall well below the MTOW. And then it is the limit that applies. The workbook take-off weight chart gives it, read for the pressure height and temperature, without the runway length correction.

[[workbook:fig5]]
Picture the take-off chart from the workbook, printed sideways on the page. In the small panel at its bottom left, lines for 4,000, 5,000 and 6,000 ft of pressure height, together with the temperature, turn into a climb limit weight. You read it on the panel's own scale, which runs up to the 1,055 kg maximum.

So the weight you may actually take off at is the lowest of four numbers. First, the structural MTOW. Second, the climb weight limit for today's pressure height and temperature. Third, the runway-length-limited weight from the take-off chart. And fourth, the weight that will keep the landing weight within the MLW after the planned fuel burn.

Let's work one: which limit governs. These are made-up, illustrative figures, not from any chart. The structural MTOW is 1,150 kg. The structural MLW is 1,100 kg. Today's climb weight limit is 1,120 kg. The runway-limited take-off weight is 1,180 kg. And the planned fuel burn to the destination is 70 kg. Pause if you'd like to find the governing limit.
[[pause 8]]
Step one, the landing-limited take-off weight. 1,100 plus the 70 kg you will burn is 1,170 kg.

Step two, line up the four limits. 1,150 structural. 1,120 for the climb. 1,180 for the runway. And 1,170 for the landing.

Step three, take the lowest. That is 1,120 kg, the climb weight limit.

The runway and the structure would allow more, but the climb would not. If the loading sheet says 1,140 kg, 20 kg has to come off, or you wait for cooler air. So the answer: 1,120 kg, the climb weight limit, governs.

[[diagram:weight-limits-lowest]]
Picture those four limits lined up, from the runway limit at 1,180 kg down to the climb limit at 1,120. On a hot day the climb limit can sit below the structural MTOW, and the lowest governs.

The exam supplies the workbook charts, and the flight manual figures for your own aeroplane always take precedence over any rule of thumb.

Question. Why can the climb weight limit change during the day, when the structural MTOW cannot?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the structural MTOW depends only on the airframe's strength. The climb weight limit depends on the surplus power available to climb, which falls as the density height rises. So it is lowest in the heat of the afternoon, and highest on a cool morning.

## What happens when the limits are exceeded

An overweight aeroplane does not fail at a sharp boundary. Everything gets worse at once. There are three kinds of harm.

First, performance. The wing must make more lift at every speed, so the stall speed rises with the square root of the weight, and so do the lift-off, approach and touchdown speeds. A higher lift-off speed, reached with less acceleration, means a longer take-off. A faster touchdown, with more mass to stop, means a longer landing. With less surplus power, the rate and gradient of climb fall, perhaps below what the terrain beyond the runway needs. Cruise speed, range and manoeuvrability all fall.

Second, the structure. The airframe's limit load factor, 3.8 g for a normal category aeroplane, is a multiple of the certified weight. Above the MTOW, the same structure carries a bigger load at every g. So turbulence or a steep turn can take it past what it was certified for. Above the MLW, a firm touchdown can damage the undercarriage and its attachments, and tyre and brake limits can be exceeded.

And third, legality. Flying above a published limit breaches the regulations.

And if the load is also in the wrong place, a centre of gravity out of limits adds control and stability problems. A forward CG may leave too little elevator to flare. An aft CG reduces stability, and may make stall and spin recovery impossible.

Let's work one: what 8 percent overweight costs. The flight manual stall speed is 50 kt at the MTOW. The aeroplane is loaded 8 percent above the MTOW. And the normal category limit load factor is 3.8 g. Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 8]]
Step one, the stall speed. 50 times the square root of 1.08. The square root of 1.08 is 1.039. So the stall speed is 52 kt.

Step two, the ground roll. As a rule of thumb, it is roughly proportional to the weight squared, because the lift-off speed squared rises with weight, and the acceleration falls with it. 1.08 squared is 1.166. So the roll is about 17 percent longer.

Step three, the g at which the structure meets its design load. 3.8 divided by 1.08 is 3.5 g.

So the answer: a stall at 52 kt instead of 50, a run about 17 percent longer, and a structure that meets its design load at 3.5 g. Eight percent does not sound much, but every margin has shrunk at once. And the flight manual has no figures for this weight, so the charts can no longer tell you whether the runway is long enough.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. It still flew, so it was fine. That is the trap. The charts and the structural limits only describe the aeroplane up to its limits. Above them, you have no data. And the extra take-off distance grows faster than the extra weight.

Question. Why is exceeding the maximum landing weight mostly a structural problem, rather than a performance one?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: because the MLW is set by the loads the undercarriage and its attachments can take at touchdown. Landing above it lengthens the landing a little. But the real risk is damage to the undercarriage and the structure, in a firm landing.

## Bringing it together

It is late January. You plan to fly three friends out of a 900 m grass strip near Northam, at three in the afternoon. The strip slopes up 1 percent towards the west. The AWIS at a nearby aerodrome gives 40 degrees, and a QNH of {1007|ten oh seven}. The wind is a light easterly.

The air first. The low QNH lifts the pressure height a little, and the heat puts the density height thousands of feet higher. The easterly is a headwind towards the east, which is downhill, so wind and slope agree. And dry grass needs a grass correction, but not a wet one.

And the weights. Four people and full tanks put you close to the MTOW. And at 40 degrees, the chart's climb weight limit is below your loaded weight. The runway would do. The climb would not.

The fix is weight, not technique. Take less fuel, and redo the fuel plan. Take one person fewer. Or leave early tomorrow, when 18 degrees takes thousands of feet off the density height.

Question. On a hot day, you take off downhill with a light tailwind, rather than uphill into a light headwind. What does each choice do to the take-off distance, and to obstacle clearance?
[[pause 5]]
The answer: downhill helps the acceleration, and the ground falls away. But the tailwind lengthens the run, and flattens the climb gradient over the ground. Uphill into wind, the headwind shortens the run and steepens the gradient. But the slope lengthens the run, and the ground keeps rising. So work both through the chart. The light-wind convention is to take off downhill, but only the numbers decide.

## Key points

Let's recap.

The factors. Only a headwind shortens both distances. A tailwind lengthens both, markedly. Heat, low QNH and high elevation all act through density height: longer distances, and a poorer climb. Upslope lengthens the take-off and shortens the landing, and downslope the reverse. Wet sealed hurts the landing, wet grass both, and slush both greatly. Ground effect lets you lift off before you can climb. Frost may prevent lift-off. And the factors multiply.

The heights. Pressure height is the ISA height with the same pressure, the altimeter reading with {1013|ten thirteen} set. Density height is the ISA height with the same density: the pressure height, plus 120 ft for every degree above the ISA. Performance follows density height.

The altimeter on the ground. Set the elevation, and read the QNH. Set {1013|ten thirteen}, and read the pressure height. Set the QNH, and read the elevation, within 100 ft for VFR.

The limits. The structural MTOW and MLW are fixed by the airframe. The climb weight limit is the heaviest weight that still meets the required climb gradient today. Take off at no more than the lowest of the structural, climb, runway and landing-limited weights.

And overweight. Higher stall speeds, longer distances, a poorer climb, a structure loaded beyond certification, and it is illegal.

A final exam tip. Questions here are usually increase or decrease tables, and the density height calculation. So practise the density height method until it is automatic, then work the workbook charts in the lesson on performance charts. Have these by heart. 30 ft per hectopascal. 120 ft per degree. The ISA, 15 degrees at sea level, falling 2 for every 1,000 ft. And slope is the only factor that works opposite ways for take-off and landing.

That's the end of PAKA 3.1.
