This is PAKA 2.1, Propellers. By the end you will know why the RPM of a fixed-pitch propeller follows the airspeed, what makes it overspeed, and what you do with your hands when it does. You will also see how a constant-speed propeller's governor changes the picture. There are worked examples and a few questions along the way, so pause whenever you want a moment to think.

## What the exam expects (PAKA 2.1)

The exam wants one thing from you, in two parts. First, the reasons a fixed-pitch propeller can overspeed. And second, the action you take when it does.

## Why this matters

Most training aeroplanes in WA drive a fixed-pitch propeller. That goes for the Cessna {152s|one fifty-twos} and {172s|one seventy-twos} at Jandakot, and for the Jabirus and Tecnams on farm strips. In one of them, the tachometer needle does not stay where you put it.

Picture yourself descending off the top of the Darling Scarp, towards the Jandakot inbound points. You lower the nose without touching the throttle, and the RPM creeps up. Sometimes it goes past the red line, while you are busy with the radio and the lookout.

Nothing has broken. The propeller is doing exactly what its geometry says it must. Once you see why, overspeed stops being a fact to memorise. It becomes something you prevent with your left hand, before you move the control column.

The exam asks for the causes, the cure, and why a fine-pitch propeller reaches the limit sooner. And it likes to offer adding power, or steepening the descent, as distractors. So this lesson builds the propeller up from the blade. Then it explains overspeed and the remedy. And, for when you move on to a constant-speed aeroplane, it shows how a governor changes the picture.

## A propeller blade is a rotating wing

You met the idea in the lesson on piston engine aircraft. The propeller turns engine torque into thrust, and each blade is a small wing whose lift points forward. Take that literally, because everything about overspeed follows from it.

Cut a blade across, a little way out from the hub. You see an aerofoil section with a chord line, just like a slice through the wing. Now picture the plane of rotation. That is the flat disc the propeller sweeps, at right angles to the crankshaft. The angle between the chord line and the plane of rotation is the blade angle.

On a fixed-pitch propeller, the blade angle is carved or forged in at manufacture. It never changes in flight. The word pitch is the same idea, expressed as a distance. It is the distance the propeller would move forward in one turn if it screwed through the air like a bolt through a nut, with no slip. A large blade angle is coarse pitch. A small one is fine pitch.

A wing's angle of attack is measured against the relative airflow, the air coming at it. The same is true of a blade. But a blade section is doing two things at once. It is going round, at a speed set by the RPM and by how far out from the hub it is. And it is going forward, at the aeroplane's true airspeed. Put the two together, and each blade section traces a corkscrew through the air, a helical path. The relative airflow comes from exactly opposite to that path.

The angle between the helical path and the plane of rotation is the helix angle. It is sometimes called the angle of advance. And the blade's angle of attack is what is left over. The blade angle of attack equals the blade angle minus the helix angle.

This is exactly the subtraction you met for the wing, where the angle of attack is the pitch attitude less the angle of the flight path. The blade angle plays the part of the attitude. And the helix angle plays the part of the flight path.

[[diagram:angle-of-attack-vs-attitude]]
Picture the wing version first, seen from the side. The aeroplane's nose is 10 degrees above the horizon, but its flight path is climbing at 6 degrees. So the relative airflow comes from 6 degrees above the horizontal, ahead, and the angle of attack is 10 minus 6, about 4 degrees. Now swap the words. For a blade, read blade angle where it says attitude, and helix angle where it says flight path.

Next, picture the two speeds as the sides of a triangle. The rotational speed is the long side, pointing along the plane of rotation. The forward speed is the short side, pointing along the line of flight. And the helical path is the diagonal. Make the forward speed bigger, with the rotational speed unchanged, and the diagonal swings forward. The helix angle grows. The blade angle is fixed, so the angle of attack must shrink. Make the forward speed smaller, and the opposite happens.

So here is the key fact of the whole lesson. On a fixed-pitch propeller, the angle of attack of the blades falls as the airspeed rises, and rises as the airspeed falls.

One more piece of geometry. A point near the tip travels a much bigger circle each turn than a point near the hub. So it moves round much faster, and its helix angle is smaller. To keep every part of the blade working at a sensible angle of attack, the blade angle is made large near the hub and small near the tip. That is the twist you can see if you sight along a propeller blade on the pre-flight. The blade angle quoted for a propeller is the angle at a reference station, often about three-quarters of the way out.

[[diagram:propeller-blade-twist]]
Picture three sections of one blade, side by side, all moving forward at the same 80 kt. Near the hub, the section moves round slowly, so its helical path is steep, and the blade angle is large. Three-quarters of the way out, it moves round at about 345 kt at 2,400 RPM. There the helix angle is 13 degrees, the blade angle 21 degrees, and the angle of attack 8. Near the tip, the section moves fastest, the helical path is flattest, and the blade angle is smallest. The twist keeps each section at a sensible angle of attack.

Let's work one: what a change of airspeed does to the blade's angle of attack. Take that point three-quarters of the way out along a typical trainer's blade, moving round at about 345 kt at 2,400 RPM. Suppose its blade angle there is 21 degrees. Compare 80 kt, a climb, with 120 kt, a fast descent. Look at the instant the speed changes, before the RPM has had time to respond. Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
At 80 kt, the helix angle is the angle whose tangent is 80 over 345. That is 13 degrees. So the angle of attack is 21 minus 13, which is 8 degrees.

At 120 kt, the helix angle is the angle whose tangent is 120 over 345. That is 19 degrees. So the angle of attack is 21 minus 19, which is only 2 degrees.

The same blade, at the same RPM, has gone from working hard at 8 degrees to barely working at 2 degrees. The blade angle of 21 degrees is only illustrative. The point is the direction and the size of the change, and that is the same for any fixed-pitch propeller. So the answer: 8 degrees at 80 kt, and 2 degrees at 120 kt.

Question. On a fixed-pitch propeller, what happens to the blade's angle of attack when the aeroplane accelerates at constant RPM, and why?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: it decreases. The forward speed is one side of the triangle that sets the helix angle. More forward speed makes the helix angle bigger. And with a fixed blade angle, the angle of attack is the blade angle less the helix angle.

## Why the RPM follows the airspeed

The engine and the propeller are bolted together, so they always turn at the same speed. That speed settles wherever two twisting forces balance.

On one side is the engine. It pushes the crankshaft round with its torque, set mainly by the throttle. On the other side is the propeller. It resists being turned, because each blade makes drag as well as thrust. That drag, acting at a radius, is a resisting torque, called the propeller torque, or the load.

When the engine torque equals the propeller torque, the RPM is steady. When the engine's torque is bigger, the combination speeds up. When the propeller's is bigger, it slows down.

The propeller's resisting torque depends on how hard its blades are working. In other words, on their angle of attack. A blade at a high angle of attack makes plenty of thrust and plenty of drag, and takes a lot of torque to turn. A blade at a low angle of attack makes little of either, and turns easily.

Now put that together with the triangle from the last section. There are two cases.

First, lower the nose and accelerate, with the throttle where it was. The helix angle grows, and the blade angle of attack falls. The propeller absorbs less torque. And the engine, now meeting less resistance, speeds up. The RPM climbs until the higher rotational speed has steepened the triangle back enough to restore the balance. So it settles at a higher RPM than before.

Second, raise the nose and slow down, with the throttle untouched. The helix angle shrinks, and the blade bites harder. The propeller absorbs more torque, and the RPM falls.

That is why, in an aeroplane with a fixed-pitch propeller, the RPM rises in a dive and falls in a climb, with the throttle untouched. Push the speed high enough, and the airflow starts to drive the propeller, rather than the propeller driving the air. The blades go to a zero or negative angle of attack, and the propeller windmills the engine. That is what it does in a glide with the throttle closed.

It is also why the tachometer is only a true power gauge at a steady airspeed. The lesson on the piston engine uses that to read mixture and carburettor ice off the tachometer in a steady cruise. This lesson is the other side of the same coin, where the airspeed changes and the RPM changes with it.

Now, fine pitch and coarse pitch, and the compromise between them. Because the blade angle cannot change, the manufacturer chooses one angle for the whole flight. And it is always a compromise.

A fine-pitch propeller, often sold as a climb propeller, has a small blade angle. At low speed, on the take-off run and in the climb, its blades are at an efficient angle of attack, and they do not overload the engine. So the engine can reach its full rated RPM and full power. That means a good take-off and climb. But at cruise speed, the helix angle has grown. The blades are at a very small angle of attack, and the propeller absorbs little torque. The engine runs up to high RPM at a modest throttle opening, and the red line arrives quickly as the speed builds.

A coarse-pitch propeller, the cruise propeller, has a larger blade angle. In the cruise, its blades are at a good angle of attack. So it turns the engine's power into thrust efficiently, at a lower RPM. That gives a faster, quieter, more economical cruise. But on the take-off run and in the climb, its blades are at a high angle of attack, and the load is heavy. The engine cannot reach full RPM, and it cannot develop full power. That means a longer take-off and a poorer climb.

The point for this subtopic is this. A fine-pitch propeller sits closer to the red line at any given airspeed. So it overspeeds sooner as the airspeed rises.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. The claim is that the propeller overspeeds because the engine is making too much power. That is only half the story, and the exam's distractors build on it. With the throttle untouched, the engine's torque is about the same. What has changed is that the propeller, at a higher airspeed, is absorbing less of it. That is why raising the nose, to reduce the airspeed, is part of the cure, and not only closing the throttle.

Question. Why does a fine-pitch propeller reach its RPM limit sooner in a descent than a coarse-pitch one?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: its blade angle is smaller. So at any airspeed, its blades are at a lower angle of attack, and they absorb less torque. The engine already runs at a higher RPM for a given throttle setting. And as the airspeed rises, the angle of attack falls further, so the red line is reached at a lower airspeed.

## What an overspeed does to the engine and the propeller

The red line on the tachometer is the maximum RPM, from the limitations section of the flight manual. It protects two things.

First, the engine. Every piston, connecting rod and valve is stopped and started twice a revolution. The force needed to do that rises with the square of the RPM. So 10 percent more RPM is about 21 percent more load. You met that in the lesson on engine handling. Above the red line, those loads can overstress the crankshaft, the connecting rods and the valve gear. And the valves may fail to close in time.

Second, the propeller. Its tips are the fastest-moving part of the aeroplane. The tip's speed through the air combines its rotational speed with the aeroplane's forward speed. So it rises with RPM, and it rises with airspeed. Those are exactly the two things that rise together in a dive.

As the tip speed approaches the speed of sound, shock waves form on the blade. Its efficiency drops, the noise rises, and the vibration and the stresses grow. The centrifugal load, trying to pull each blade out of the hub, also rises with the square of the RPM. A propeller is designed with a margin for all of this at the red line, not beyond it.

So an overspeed is not free power. It is damage accumulating in parts you cannot see. That is why an exceedance has to be recorded, and may need an inspection.

## The causes of a fixed-pitch overspeed

Every cause on the list is a version of one idea. High power combined with high airspeed. Or a propeller that is absorbing less torque than it should. There are five causes.

The first is descending or diving with high power. It is the commonest cause by far. You start a cruise descent, and lower the nose without first reducing power. The airspeed rises, the blades unload, and the RPM follows.

The second is high power at high airspeed in level flight. Full throttle at low altitude in a fast aeroplane can take the RPM past the red line without any descent at all, particularly with a fine-pitch climb propeller. Many flight manuals limit full throttle at low level for this reason.

The third is recovering from a spiral dive or an unusual attitude with the power still on. The nose is low, and the airspeed is high and rising. If the throttle has not been closed, the RPM goes with it. This is one reason the spiral dive recovery begins with the throttle closed. You'll find that recovery in the lesson on stalling, spinning and spiral dives.

The fourth is a fine-pitch propeller, fitted for climb performance. It reaches red-line RPM sooner as the speed builds. That is the compromise you have just heard about.

And the fifth is the loss of a propeller blade tip, or other propeller damage. A damaged blade absorbs less torque, so the RPM rises. And it is out of balance, so there is severe vibration as well. This one is a mechanical failure, not a handling error. And the vibration is the clue.

So there are five causes. Descending with high power. High power at high speed in level flight. Recovering from a dive or an unusual attitude with power on. A fine-pitch propeller. And propeller damage, with its vibration.

Question. You are in a steady cruise, and the RPM rises sharply with severe vibration, though the airspeed has not changed. What is the likely cause, and how is it different from the usual overspeed?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: propeller damage, such as a lost blade tip. The damaged propeller absorbs less torque, and it is out of balance. The usual overspeed comes from the airspeed rising with power on, and it has no vibration. Here the airspeed is steady, and the vibration says something has broken. Reduce power at once, and plan an early landing.

## Remedial action

The cure follows directly from the cause. If the RPM is high because the engine's torque is not being absorbed, either reduce the torque, or make the propeller absorb more of it. In practice you do both. There are four steps.

First, reduce power. Close the throttle as far as needed to bring the RPM back below the red line. This is the first and fastest action, and it works at any airspeed.

Second, reduce airspeed. Raise the nose, smoothly, and without overstressing the aeroplane, to slow down. Lower airspeed means a smaller helix angle, a higher blade angle of attack, and more load on the propeller. So the RPM falls further.

Third, re-establish a normal attitude and power. And in future, reduce power before descending, so the RPM stays within the green arc.

And fourth, record it. If the red line was exceeded, record the exceedance on the maintenance release. The flight manual or the maintenance manual may require an inspection, depending on how far, and for how long, the limit was exceeded.

Two things to notice about the order. Power comes off first, because it is immediate and safe. The nose comes up second, and gently. A sharp pull at high speed raises the load factor, and it can overstress the airframe. Or, if the speed is in the yellow arc, it can put you outside the structural limits. And recording the exceedance is not optional good manners. The next pilot is entitled to know.

The best remedy is the one you never need. Throttle back, then lower the nose. That is the habit that prevents almost every fixed-pitch overspeed.

Let's work one: starting a cruise descent. You are cruising at 2,350 RPM, and the red line is 2,700 RPM. You want a 500 ft per minute descent. Pause if you'd like to think about how you would do it.
[[pause 5]]
First, the wrong way. Lower the nose without touching the throttle. As the speed increases from 100 kt to 120 kt, the RPM climbs towards 2,700. You are at the red line before you have finished your radio call.

Now the right way. Reduce power first, to about 2,100 RPM. Then lower the nose. As the speed increases, the RPM rises again, but from the lower starting point. It settles back near the cruise value, instead of at the red line.

So you descend at the speed you wanted, and the RPM stays in the green arc throughout. The answer, once more: power first, to about 2,100 RPM, then the nose. These figures are for illustration. Your flight manual gives the cruise and descent power settings for your type.

Here is one for you to try. You are recovering from a spiral dive in a fixed-pitch aeroplane. The airspeed indicator is high and rising, and the tachometer is just past the red line. What do you do, in what order, and what do you write down after landing?
[[pause 8]]
Close the throttle first. That stops the RPM rising, and it stops the power adding to the speed. Then roll the wings level, with aileron and coordinated rudder. Only then ease out of the dive, gently. That also reduces the airspeed and loads the propeller. Restore power once the speed is under control.

After landing, record the RPM exceedance on the maintenance release: how far over, and for how long, as well as you can tell. Then it can be assessed against the flight manual or the maintenance manual. So the order is throttle closed, wings level, ease out, and then write it up.

Here's a second trap the exam likes to set. The distractors are to increase power to recover, and to steepen the descent to get through it. Both make an overspeed worse. The answer always contains less power and less airspeed.

## A constant-speed propeller: how a governor changes the picture

Sooner or later, you will fly an aeroplane with a constant-speed propeller. It has a blue propeller lever beside the throttle, and a manifold pressure gauge on the panel. The element only asks about fixed pitch. But seeing what the governor does makes the fixed-pitch behaviour clearer, and the exam occasionally contrasts the two.

A constant-speed propeller has blades that can rotate in the hub, to change the blade angle in flight. The pilot does not set the blade angle directly. Instead, the propeller lever sets an RPM. And a governor on the engine adjusts the blade angle continuously, to hold that RPM, whatever the airspeed and the throttle are doing.

The governor works on a simple balance, with three parts. First, a pair of flyweights, spun by the engine. The faster the engine turns, the further the flyweights swing outwards. Second, a speeder spring, pushing down on the flyweights. The propeller lever sets how hard it pushes. Lever forward means more spring force, and a higher RPM selected. And third, between them, a pilot valve. It directs engine oil, boosted to a higher pressure by the governor's own pump, into or out of the propeller hub. There the oil moves a piston that turns the blades.

Now repeat the descent. You lower the nose, and the airspeed rises. The blade angle of attack starts to fall, just as on a fixed-pitch propeller, and the RPM starts to rise. But as soon as it does, the flyweights swing outwards against the spring. The pilot valve moves, and oil flows to turn the blades to a coarser angle. The coarser blade angle restores the angle of attack. The propeller absorbs more torque again, and the RPM comes back to the setting.

Slow down, and the reverse happens. The RPM starts to sag, the flyweights fall in, the blades go finer, and the RPM recovers. The pilot sees a steady tachometer, and reads power from the manifold pressure gauge instead.

[[diagram:constant-speed-governor]]
Picture the governor as a schematic, reacting to a rise in RPM as the aeroplane speeds up in a descent. Follow it in four steps. One, the engine-driven flyweights swing outwards. Two, they lift the pilot valve against the speeder spring, whose force the propeller lever sets. Three, oil, boosted by the governor pump, flows to the piston in the propeller hub. And four, the blades turn to a coarser angle, the load is restored, and the RPM returns to the setting.

So an increase in airspeed is met by a coarser blade angle, not by more RPM. But a constant-speed propeller can still overspeed, in three ways.

The first is that the governor fails, or its oil supply is lost. On many single-engine aeroplanes, oil pressure drives the blades towards coarse pitch. A spring, and the blades' own aerodynamic and centrifugal twisting forces, drive them towards fine. Lose the oil pressure, and the blades go to fine pitch. And fine pitch is where a fixed-pitch propeller overspeeds. Check your flight manual, because the design varies. On many twins the arrangement is reversed, so that a failed propeller goes coarse and feathers.

The second is that the blades reach a pitch stop. The blade angle can only travel between a fine stop and a coarse stop. In a high-speed dive, the governor may have coarsened the blades all the way to the coarse stop. Beyond that, it has nothing left to give. The propeller behaves like a fixed-pitch one, and the RPM rises with the airspeed.

And the third is a rapid throttle opening. That can momentarily outrun the governor, giving a brief overspeed until the blades catch up.

The remedial actions rest on the same principle. Throttle back, slow down, and pull the propeller lever back, to select a lower RPM. If the oil pressure is falling, the overspeed is a symptom of a bigger problem, and the emergency procedure in the flight manual for your type applies.

Question. In a constant-speed aeroplane, what does the governor do when you lower the nose, and why does the RPM stay steady?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the rising airspeed starts to unload the blades, and the RPM starts to rise. The flyweights swing out, and the pilot valve sends oil to coarsen the blades. The angle of attack and the load are restored, and the RPM returns to the setting. The governor turns a would-be RPM change into a blade angle change.

## Bringing it together

You are returning to Jandakot from a navigation exercise to York, in a Cessna {172|one seventy-two} with a fixed-pitch propeller. You are cruising at 4,500 ft, at 2,350 RPM. Approaching the scarp, you need to be down to the inbound altitude before the reporting point. And the ATIS, the frequency change and a traffic call all arrive at once.

This is the moment the RPM gets away. The easy thing is to push the nose down and deal with the radio. Trace what happens. The airspeed builds, the helix angle grows, the blades unload, and the RPM rises with every knot. If a little turbulence over the scarp finds you holding a speed in the yellow arc as well, you are close to the red line, and above the speed for rough air.

The answer is in the order of your hands. Throttle back to the descent power first. Then lower the nose. Then the radio. And if you do find the needle past the red line, power off, and nose gently up. The exceedance goes on the maintenance release when you shut down.

## Key points

Let's recap.

A fixed-pitch blade's angle of attack is its fixed blade angle less the helix angle. More airspeed means a bigger helix angle, a smaller angle of attack, less torque absorbed, and a higher RPM.

Overspeed comes from high power with high airspeed. Diving or descending with cruise power. Full throttle at high speed, especially with a fine-pitch propeller. Recovering from a spiral dive or an unusual attitude with power on. Or propeller damage, and that one comes with severe vibration.

The remedy. Reduce power. Reduce airspeed, by raising the nose smoothly. In future, reduce power before descending. And record any exceedance on the maintenance release.

A constant-speed propeller's governor coarsens the blades instead. It overspeeds only if the governor or its oil fails, if the blades reach a stop, or if the throttle outruns it.

A final exam tip. In a fixed-pitch aeroplane, RPM follows airspeed. If the RPM rises towards the red line in a descent, the answer is to throttle back and raise the nose. Never more power, and never a steeper dive. Expect a question on why a fine-pitch propeller overspeeds sooner: a smaller blade angle, a lower angle of attack, and less load at any speed. And expect one on what to do after an exceedance: record it, because an inspection may be required.
