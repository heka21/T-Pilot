This is PAKA 2.2, Aircraft systems: stall warning and autopilot. By the end you will know how each kind of stall warning senses the approaching stall, why it is a warning and not a guarantee, what an autopilot is made of, what its modes hold, what its trim indicator is telling you, and how to switch it off. You will also know why you never fight an autopilot. There are a few questions along the way, with a pause to think before each answer.

## What the exam expects (PAKA 2.2)

The exam wants you to say what two typical aeroplane systems do and how they work. For each, it wants to know how the pilot can overpower it, and the precautions to take. The first system is the stall warning device. The second is the autopilot, and for the autopilot there are three parts: the roll, attitude, heading and pitch controls, the trim indicator, and the cut-out mechanisms.

## Why this matters

Two systems in this subtopic sit between you and the aeroplane. And both can lull you.

The first is the stall warning. It is the horn in a Cessna, or the light in a Piper, and it is the last clear message before the wing lets go. On the pre-flight, at Jandakot or on a farm strip in the wheatbelt, it is easy to skip the check that proves it works. That is especially true after a dusty week, or a night out on the tie-downs.

The second is the autopilot. Once you fly a touring aeroplane on a long leg to Kalbarri or Esperance, it does the flying for hours at a time. The danger is not that it fails. The danger is that it does something you did not expect, and you fight it instead of switching it off.

Both systems share the theme of the element. Each can be overpowered, or can mislead. And the precautions are about knowing exactly how it works.

The exam asks how each stall warning type senses the stall, and which one needs electrical power. It asks what the autopilot modes do, what a trim annunciator means, how you disconnect, and why fighting an autopilot is dangerous.

## How a stall warning knows the wing is near the stall

A wing stalls at its critical angle of attack. On a light aeroplane, that is about 15 to 18 degrees, at any speed and any attitude. You'll find that in the lesson on stalling, spinning and spiral dives. So a useful stall warning cannot simply watch the airspeed. In a steep turn, or at a heavy weight, the wing stalls well above the book speed. The warning has to sense angle of attack. And a light aeroplane does that without any instrument at all, by watching where the air divides at the leading edge.

Picture the air arriving at the front of the wing. Some goes over the top, and some goes underneath. At one point on the leading edge, it does neither. It comes to rest against the wing. That point is the stagnation point.

At a small angle of attack, the stagnation point sits right at the nose of the aerofoil. Tilt the wing to a higher angle, and the oncoming air meets it from further below. So the stagnation point moves down and back, under the leading edge. And the air just above it now has to whip round the sharp curve of the nose to get to the upper surface. That fast-moving air around the nose is at a very low pressure. The lower the stagnation point goes, the stronger that suction becomes.

Both light-aircraft stall warning devices exploit this. One senses where the stagnation point is. The other senses the suction above it. Both are set so that they trigger about 5 to 10 kt above the stall in level flight. That gives you an aural warning, and sometimes a visual one, while there is still time to act.

[[diagram:stall-sequence]]
Picture three wing sections in a row, each at a higher angle of attack than the last. On the first, the airflow is attached over the whole upper surface. On the second, it is starting to separate near the trailing edge. On the third, past the critical angle, the flow over the top is separated and turbulent. Below them run the warning signs, in their usual order. First, a low and decreasing airspeed with a high nose attitude. Then sloppy controls and a quieter airflow note. Then the stall warning, 5 to 10 kt above the stall. Then buffet. And then the stall itself. So the warning comes after the sloppy controls, and before the buffet.

Now the two types. First, the reed type, which is pneumatic.

Many Cessnas use a reed stall warning. A small slot or opening in the leading edge of one wing connects through a tube to a reed horn. That is a reed that sounds when air is drawn across it, like a harmonica. At normal angles of attack, the opening is near the stagnation point, and nothing much happens. As the angle of attack increases, the stagnation point moves below the opening. And the low pressure over the leading edge sucks air through the reed, from the cabin end of the tube out to the wing. The reed sounds a wailing note, which grows louder as you get closer to the stall.

The great advantage is that it is purely pneumatic. No electrical power is needed. It still works with the master switch off, or after a complete electrical failure.

Its weaknesses are all things that can block a small hole. Insects, dirt, a mud wasp, ice, or a blanking cover left in place. The pre-flight check is to gently suck on the opening, through a clean cloth, and listen for the horn in the cabin. If you hear nothing, it is not working.

Second, the vane type, which is electric.

Many Pipers use a vane stall warning. A small hinged tab sits just below the leading edge. At normal angles of attack, the stagnation point is above the vane. So the air at the vane is flowing down and back along the lower surface, and it holds the vane down. As the angle of attack increases, the stagnation point moves back below the vane. Now the air at the vane is flowing forwards and up, around the leading edge. It pushes the vane up, closing a microswitch that sounds a horn or lights a warning light.

Because it uses a switch, it needs electrical power, with the master switch on. An electrical failure disables it. And ice can jam the vane in either position. The pre-flight check: master on, lift the vane gently, and confirm the warning sounds or lights.

So compare the two. They sense the same thing, the stagnation point moving down and back. The reed responds to the suction over the leading edge, and sounds a horn with air. It needs no power, and a blocked opening disables it. You check it by sucking gently through a clean cloth. The vane responds to the stagnation point moving below it, and closes a switch. It needs the master on, and an electrical failure or ice disables it. You check it with the master on, by lifting the vane.

[[diagram:stall-warning-reed-and-vane]]
Picture the leading edge of a wing twice, once at a normal angle of attack and once near the critical angle. In the second picture, the stagnation point, where the oncoming air stops against the wing, has moved down and back under the leading edge. And fast, low-pressure air whips round the nose. On the reed type, that suction over the opening draws air through the reed horn in the cabin, with no electrical power needed. On the vane type, air flowing forward round the leading edge pushes the vane up, closing the microswitch. That one needs the master switch on.

Bigger aeroplanes do it differently. Larger aircraft use dedicated angle-of-attack sensors. Each is a vane on the side of the fuselage that aligns itself with the airflow. They drive a stick shaker, which shakes the control column to imitate the buffet. And sometimes they drive a stick pusher, which physically pushes the column forward to lower the angle of attack, if the pilot does not.

Question. After a complete electrical failure, which stall warning type still works, and why?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the reed type. It is driven by the suction over the leading edge drawing air through the horn, and it needs no electricity. The vane type uses a microswitch, and it stops working when there is no electrical power.

## Why the warning is not a guarantee

A stall warning advises. It does not prevent. Nothing in a light aeroplane's stall warning stops you pulling the wing past the critical angle. And that is the first sense in which this element talks about overpowering a system. You can fly straight through the horn into a stall. Pilots do, usually when their attention is elsewhere.

Even a working warning may give you less than you expect. There are three reasons.

The first: it warns at an angle, not a speed. That is its strength. And it catches pilots who have memorised that the horn goes at 55 kt. In a turn, at a heavier weight, or with ice on the wing, the stall speed rises. And the warning speed rises with it.

Let's work one: where the horn sounds in a steep turn. The level-flight stall speed is 48 kt, flaps up. The stall warning sounds at about 7 kt above that, at 55 kt, in level flight. Now you fly a level 60 degree turn. At what speed does the horn sound? Pause if you'd like to try it.
[[pause 6]]
Step one, the load factor. It is one over the cosine of 60 degrees. The cosine of 60 degrees is a half, so the load factor is 2.0.

Step two, the square root of the load factor. The square root of 2.0 is 1.41.

Step three, the stall speed in the turn. 48 times 1.41 is 68 kt.

Step four, the warning speed in the turn. 55 times 1.41 is 78 kt.

So the horn now sounds at 78 kt, not 55. Because the vane or the reed senses angle of attack, it still warns before the stall. But a pilot who reads the airspeed indicator and thinks there is plenty of speed will be wrong. Both speeds scale by the square root of the load factor, just as in the lesson on stalling. So the answer once more: in a 60 degree turn, the stall is at 68 kt, and the horn at 78 kt.

The second reason: the margin can be small. In an accelerated stall, a hard pull-up or a pull out of a dive, the angle of attack rises so quickly that the horn and the stall arrive almost together. And on an iced wing, the critical angle itself is lower than the angle the warning was set for. So the wing can stall before the warning sounds.

The third reason: it can be inoperative. It can be blocked, iced, unpowered, or simply broken. And nothing in the cockpit tells you so until you need it.

On larger aircraft, a stick pusher can be overpowered by a determined pilot, or switched off. Accidents have followed both. The flight manual says when it may be disabled.

The precautions follow, and there are four. First, test it on every pre-flight, by the method for your type. Second, do not rely on it alone. Know the other symptoms of the approaching stall, in order: low and decreasing airspeed, sloppy controls, the warning, and then buffet. Third, treat it as the last warning, not the first. In a steep turn, a pull-up, or with any suspicion of ice, the margin is smaller. And fourth, respond to it. The horn means reduce the angle of attack. It does not mean nearly there.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. It says the stall warning sounds at a fixed airspeed. That is wrong. It senses angle of attack, so the speed at which it sounds rises in a turn and at a higher weight, exactly as the stall speed does. And on an iced wing, it may not sound before the stall at all.

Question. Why can an iced wing stall before the stall warning sounds?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: ice reduces the critical angle of attack, so the wing stalls at a smaller angle than the clean wing. The warning is set to trigger a little below the clean wing's critical angle. So the iced wing can reach its lower stalling angle first.

## What an autopilot is made of

An autopilot does what you do when you fly by hand. It watches the aeroplane's attitude and heading, compares them with what you asked for, and moves the controls to remove the difference. It just does it continuously, without tiring.

A light-aircraft autopilot has four parts.

First, the sensors. These are the attitude gyro, the turn coordinator or an attitude and heading reference system, the heading indicator, and navigation inputs from a VOR or a GNSS receiver. They tell the autopilot what the aeroplane is doing.

Second, a computer. It compares what the sensors say with what the pilot has selected, and works out what control movement will correct the difference.

Third, the servos. These are electric motors connected to the control cables, which move the ailerons, the elevator, and sometimes the trim. Each drives its control through a clutch, and that clutch matters later.

And fourth, a mode controller. That is the panel of buttons and knobs where you tell the autopilot what to hold.

[[diagram:gyro-instruments-panel]]
Picture three gyro instruments in a row, the same ones an autopilot can read. The attitude indicator gives roll and pitch. It is a gyro spinning about a vertical axis, rigid in space, normally vacuum driven. The directional indicator gives the heading. Its gyro spins about a horizontal axis, it has no magnetic sense, and you reset it to the compass about every 10 to 15 minutes. And the turn coordinator, normally electric, gives the rate of turn, with a ball showing balance. An autopilot reads the same quantities you do.

The simplest autopilots act in one axis only, roll. They are little more than a wing leveller. Two-axis autopilots add pitch. And the exam's element lists the controls you need to recognise.

[[diagram:autopilot-control-loop]]
Picture the autopilot as a loop. On the input side, the sensors tell the computer what the aeroplane is doing, and the mode controller tells it what to hold. The computer works out the correction. The servos, electric motors driving the control cables through slip clutches, move the ailerons, the elevator, and sometimes the trim. The aeroplane responds, and the sensors see the result, closing the loop. And notice what is missing. No mode moves the throttle.

## The modes: roll, attitude, heading and pitch

The modes are easiest to understand as answers to one question. What should the autopilot hold? There are six common answers.

The first is roll, or wing leveller, marked {ROL|R O L}. It holds the wings level. On some systems, it holds the bank angle at engagement instead. A single-axis autopilot may do only this, using the turn coordinator as its sensor. It is useful when you reach for a chart. And it is a recovery aid if you start to lose control in cloud.

The second is heading, marked {HDG|H D G}. It turns the aeroplane to the heading bug on the heading indicator, and holds that heading. Move the bug, and the autopilot turns to follow it. Heading mode holds a heading, not a track. So it does not correct for drift. If the wind changes, you drift off your track.

The third is navigation, marked NAV or GPS. It tracks a VOR radial or a GNSS course, correcting for wind to stay on the selected track.

The fourth is pitch, or attitude, marked {PIT|P I T}. It holds the pitch attitude at engagement.

The fifth is altitude hold, marked {ALT|alt}. It holds the altitude at engagement, by moving the elevator.

And the sixth is vertical speed, marked {VS|V S}. It holds a selected rate of climb or descent.

On many controllers, a pitch trim wheel, or an up and down rocker, adjusts the attitude or the vertical speed being held.

Now notice what a pitch mode does not control. Power. Engage altitude hold and reduce power, and the autopilot will raise the nose to hold the height. It keeps raising it as the speed falls, all the way to the stall, unless the system has its own protection. In vertical speed mode, with a climb rate set too high for the aeroplane, the same thing happens in the climb. The autopilot holds what you asked for. You manage the speed.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. Heading mode and nav mode are not the same. Heading mode holds a heading, and lets the wind blow you off track. Nav mode tracks the course you selected, and corrects for drift. So an exam question that asks which mode follows a GNSS track wants NAV, or GPS. Not heading.

Question. The autopilot is in altitude hold. You bring the power back for a long descent planning check, without changing the mode. What will happen, and why?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the aeroplane will hold its altitude by raising the nose, and the speed will decay, possibly towards the stall. Altitude hold moves the elevator, not the throttle. So it trades airspeed for height, without telling you.

## The trim indicator

When you fly by hand, you trim the aeroplane so that it holds its attitude with no force on the control column. An autopilot holding the pitch with its elevator servo faces the same loads. If the aeroplane is out of trim, the servo is pushing or pulling continuously to hold the attitude. And you do not feel it, because you are not holding the controls.

Autopilots deal with this in one of two ways. Many have an automatic pitch trim servo. It moves the trim tab to take the load off the elevator servo, just as you would. Others, without auto-trim, have a trim indicator, or annunciator. It shows when the elevator servo is holding a load. Typically it reads {TRIM UP|trim up} or {TRIM DN|trim down}, meaning nose-up trim, or nose-down trim, is needed.

[[diagram:trim-tab]]
Picture the tailplane from the side, with the airflow coming from the left. Behind the tailplane hangs the elevator, and on the back of the elevator is the little trim tab. For nose-up trim, the tab is deflected down. The air pushes up on it, and that force holds the elevator's trailing edge up, without any force from the pilot. A servo holding the elevator instead is a hidden force.

The reason this matters is what happens at disconnect. The moment the autopilot lets go, whatever force its servo was holding is suddenly yours. If it was holding a large load, the aeroplane will pitch suddenly, in the direction the load was pushing. So an out-of-trim indication is a warning of a pitch upset waiting to happen.

There are three precautions. First, where the autopilot has no auto-trim, retrim manually as the annunciator directs, so the servo is not holding a load. Second, look at the trim indicator before you disconnect, and hold the controls firmly, ready for a pitch change. And third, engage the autopilot only with the aeroplane in trim.

Question. Why can disconnecting an autopilot that has been holding an out-of-trim load cause a sudden pitch change?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: the elevator servo has been holding the aeroplane against the out-of-trim force. At disconnect, nothing opposes that force until the pilot takes up the load. So the nose moves in the direction the trim was pushing it.

## Cut-out mechanisms

An autopilot is only safe if you can always switch it off, quickly, and in more than one way. The systems give you several, from the fastest to the most drastic. There are six to know.

First, the autopilot disconnect button on the control wheel, marked {A/P DISC|A P disc}. It is the primary means. It sits under your thumb, so you can disconnect without letting go of the controls.

Second, the {AP|A P} button on the mode controller.

Third, the autopilot master switch, or the autopilot circuit breaker.

Fourth, the avionics master. It removes power from the autopilot, along with the radios.

Fifth, trim interrupt or control wheel steering switches. They disengage the servos while you hold them, so you can manoeuvre by hand, and then let go to resume.

And sixth, automatic disconnects. The autopilot disconnects itself on a system failure, or at an excessive attitude. On some systems, it also disconnects when the pilot overpowers the servos. An automatic disconnect is usually announced with an aural tone or a flashing annunciator. And the aeroplane may not be in trim when it happens.

Know every means for your aeroplane, and practise them. And check the disconnect during the pre-flight. The autopilot supplement, in section 9 of the flight manual, lists the test.

Question. The {A/P DISC|A P disc} button does not disconnect the autopilot. What do you do next?
[[pause 4]]
The answer: use the next means. The {AP|A P} button on the mode controller. Then the autopilot master switch or circuit breaker, or the avionics master. For a trim runaway, follow the flight manual procedure. The point is to remove power from the servos, not to fight them.

## Overpowering the autopilot

Here is the second sense of overpowering in the element. The servos drive the controls through slip clutches. A slip clutch slips when the force on it exceeds a set value. That is a safety feature. It means a pilot can physically overpower an engaged autopilot, if it jams or runs away. But overpowering it is not a way of flying it. And doing so deliberately is hazardous.

Trace what happens with an autopilot that has auto-trim. It is holding altitude. You pull back on the control column, against the servo, to climb. The autopilot senses that the aeroplane is trying to depart from the altitude, and that its elevator servo is under load. So it does what it is designed to do. It runs the trim to relieve the load, nose down, against you.

You pull harder. It trims further. Now you are holding a large nose-up force against a large nose-down trim. Then the autopilot finally disconnects. Perhaps you press the button, or perhaps it gives up and disconnects itself. Either way, the trim is still wound fully nose-down, and your pull is suddenly the only thing opposing it. The result is a sharp nose-down pitch upset, towards the ground.

[[diagram:autopilot-overpowering-trim]]
Picture three traces running left to right against time. The autopilot holds altitude. The pilot pulls back on the column, against the servo, and the auto-trim runs nose-down to relieve the servo load. The pilot pulls harder, and the trim runs further. All the while, the pitch hardly changes, because the servo is holding it. Then, at disconnect, the full nose-down trim and the pilot's pull are suddenly unopposed, and the aeroplane pitches sharply, possibly towards the ground.

A trim runaway, where the trim motor keeps running on its own, feels the same. A steadily growing force on the controls.

Here's a trap the exam likes to set. The distractor says to hold the controls against the autopilot until it settles. That sounds sensible. But with auto-trim, the harder you hold against the servo, the further it trims against you, and the bigger the upset when it lets go. The answer is always to disconnect, not to overpower.

So the precautions for the autopilot. There are four.

First, never fight the autopilot. If it does something you did not expect, disconnect it with the {A/P DISC|A P disc} button, then fly by hand.

Second, hold the controls firmly at disconnect, expecting an out-of-trim force. Check the trim indicator first.

Third, if the disconnect fails, pull the autopilot circuit breaker, or switch off the autopilot master or the avionics master. For a trim runaway, follow the flight manual procedure.

And fourth, keep monitoring. Engage the autopilot only in trim, and within the altitude and speed limits in the flight manual supplement. And keep watching the attitude, the altitude, the airspeed and the mode annunciations while it flies.

## Bringing it together

You are flying a Piper Archer with a two-axis autopilot, from Jandakot to Albany on a hot afternoon. On the pre-flight, you lift the stall warning vane with the master on, and hear the horn. The autopilot test in the flight manual supplement passes, including the disconnect.

In the cruise at 6,500 ft, the autopilot is in NAV and {ALT|alt}, tracking the GNSS course. Approaching Mount Barker, you start planning the descent. You bring the power back a little to slow down while you brief the approach, without changing modes. The autopilot, still holding altitude, raises the nose, and the speed decays. The trim annunciator lights. Then a thermal off the hills pitches the nose up further. And you instinctively pull against the autopilot to stop it.

Every part of this lesson is in play. The pitch mode was holding height at the expense of speed. The annunciator was warning of an out-of-trim load. Pulling against an auto-trim system makes it trim against you. And if the speed had kept falling, the vane would have sounded the horn about 5 to 10 kt above a stall speed already raised by the turbulence.

The right sequence goes like this. Thumb on the {A/P DISC|A P disc} button. Hold the controls firmly, expecting a pitch change. Fly by hand, and retrim. Set the descent power and attitude. Then re-engage in a vertical speed mode, if you want the autopilot back.

## Key points

Let's recap.

The stall warning senses angle of attack, from the stagnation point moving down and back under the leading edge. It warns about 5 to 10 kt above the stall.

The reed type works by suction drawing air through a horn. It needs no electrical power. Insects, dirt, ice or a cover can block it, and you check it by sucking gently through a clean cloth. The vane type is pushed up, and closes a microswitch. It needs electrical power, and ice can jam it. You check it with the master on, by lifting the vane. Either way, the warning advises, but it cannot stop you stalling. And an iced wing or an accelerated stall can beat it.

An autopilot is sensors, a computer, servos with clutches, and a mode controller. Roll mode holds the wings level. Heading mode flies the heading bug. Nav mode tracks a VOR or GNSS course. Pitch, altitude and vertical speed modes hold an attitude, an altitude or a rate, with the elevator. No pitch mode controls power.

A trim annunciator, trim up or trim down, means the elevator servo is holding a load. Retrim, and expect a pitch change at disconnect.

Disconnect with the {A/P DISC|A P disc} button first. Then the {AP|A P} button, the autopilot master or circuit breaker, and the avionics master. Autopilots also disconnect themselves. And never overpower one. With auto-trim, fighting the servo trims the aeroplane further against you, and the upset comes at disconnect.

A final exam tip. The reed stall warning works without electrical power. The vane type needs it. If an autopilot misbehaves, disconnect, do not overpower. Overpowering an auto-trim autopilot trims the aeroplane further out of balance. Expect one question on how each stall warning senses the stall, and one on a mode: heading flies the bug, and nav tracks a course. Expect one on the trim annunciator, and one on the means of disconnecting.

That's the end of PAKA 2.2.
