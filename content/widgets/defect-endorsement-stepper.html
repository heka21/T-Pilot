Who may release the lock?
Part 2 shows the next pilot

What to notice. The endorsement is a lock. Any pilot who finds a defect places it, in Part 2 with a description, the date and time in service, a signature and licence number, and only a licensed engineer releases it, by rectifying the defect and signing it off or by endorsing it as a permissible unserviceability with any conditions. Telling someone places no lock at all, and the release still looks clean to the next pilot. Clock times, hours, the licence number and the day VFR condition are illustrative.

Try it: your pre-flight finds a cracked exhaust pipe and no engineer is in until tomorrow. May the aeroplane fly this afternoon if the next pilot is happy to take it?

No. Press Not dealt with: Part 2 holds an open defect, so the lock stays shut and the aeroplane is grounded. Only a licensed engineer can release it, by rectifying it or endorsing it as a permissible unserviceability; neither you nor the next pilot can decide alone that it is probably fine.

Try it: the alternator light flickered in flight and you told the instructor as you walked in. Why is that not enough?

Press Told, not endorsed and step through: Part 2 stays blank, so when the next pilot checks the maintenance release there is nothing to see, and a light that flickers in flight cannot be found on a walk-around. They fly it unknowingly. Endorse it in Part 2 after landing; that is the only record every future pilot is required to check.
