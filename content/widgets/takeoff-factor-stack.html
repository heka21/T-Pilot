What to notice. Each factor multiplies the distance the one before it produced, so penalties compound: 2% upslope (×1.10) and a tailwind of 10% of the lift-off speed (×1.21) make 133%, not 130%. Only a headwind shortens both distances. The wind row is the lessons' square law, $(V \mp w)^2 / V^2$; slope (2% up ≈ +10% take-off), wet sealed (landing +15% or more) and weight (take-off ∝ weight²) are their rules of thumb. Where a lesson gives a direction but no figure (grass, landing slope), the arrow shows which way and the POH supplies the number. The density height row is illustrative: the lesson's three losses (3% engine power per 1,000 ft, thinner air for the propeller, a higher TAS for the wing) multiplied together.

Try it: a 10 kt wind on a 60 kt lift-off. Why does the tailwind cost more than the headwind saves?

Press 10 kt headwind: the groundspeed at lift-off is 50 kt and $(50/60)^2 = 0.69$, about 30% shorter. Press 10 kt tailwind: 70 kt and $(70/60)^2 = 1.36$, about 36% longer. Energy goes with the square of groundspeed, so the same 10 kt is worth more against you than for you (BAKC 6.2).

Try it: the chart gives 520 m and the farm strip, 600 m long, slopes 2% up the only way you can go. Is it on?

Press Farm strip, 2% up: 520 × 1.10 = 572 m, leaving 28 m, under 5% of the strip. Legal, but too thin to accept: wait for cooler air, take less weight, or use the other direction if the wind allows (RBKA 4.1). Now switch the surface to wet grass: the arrow says it gets longer still, by a figure only the POH can give.
