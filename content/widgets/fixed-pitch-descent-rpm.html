What to notice. In a fixed-pitch aeroplane the RPM follows the airspeed: nobody touches the throttle, yet lowering the nose from 100 to 120 kt takes 2,350 RPM to the 2,700 red line, because the faster airflow unloads the blades. Throttle back first and the same descent ends near the cruise value. The cure is always less power and less airspeed, in that order, then a note on the maintenance release. The 2,350, 2,100 and 2,700 RPM and 100 to 120 kt are the lesson's illustrative figures; the straight-line link between them and the recovery RPMs are illustrative too.

Try it: why does the RPM rise when nobody touched the throttle?

More airspeed swings the helical path forward, so the fixed blade meets the air at a smaller angle of attack and absorbs less torque. The engine's torque is about the same, so it speeds up until the higher RPM restores the balance.

Try it: in what order do you fix an overspeed, and why that order?

Choose Recover: power off first, because it is immediate and works at any speed; then raise the nose smoothly, because a sharp pull at high speed overstresses the airframe; then normal attitude and power; then record the exceedance on the maintenance release.
