What to notice. Lift comes from two things the pilot sets: the angle of attack (through CL) and the speed, squared. Double the speed at the same angle and lift is four times as great, so to stay level the angle must come down until CL is a quarter. CL rises in a straight line until the critical angle, about 16°, then the flow separates and lift falls. Flap lifts the whole curve: more CL at every angle, a higher CL max, so a lower stall speed and the same lift at a smaller angle, with a slightly lower critical angle. The curve shapes and the 50 kt stall speed are an illustrative example aeroplane.

Try it: level at 60 kt, you accelerate to 120 kt without moving the elevator. What happens to lift, and what CL do you need to stay level?

Press Level at 60 kt, then Double the speed: lift jumps from 1.00 to 4.00 times the weight (2² = 4), so the aeroplane would climb. Press Level at 120 kt: CL falls from 1.04 to 0.26, a quarter, at well under 1° instead of 8.4°. That is why the nose sits low in a fast cruise.

Try it: at half the sea-level density, what true airspeed gives the same lift at the same angle, and what does the ASI read?

Press Half the density: ρV² must stay the same, so V rises by √2 to about 141 kt TAS, but the dynamic pressure is unchanged, so the ASI still reads 100 kt. The wing needs the same IAS at any height.
