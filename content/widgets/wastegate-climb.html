What to notice. The wastegate controls boost through the exhaust, not the intake: open sends the exhaust round the turbine, closed drives the turbine flat out. Climbing with an automatic controller it closes bit by bit and the manifold pressure stays put while a normally aspirated engine's falls about an inch per 1,000 ft; at the critical altitude it is fully closed and the reserve is gone. The model is illustrative: a turbo-normalised engine rated at 30 in, a critical altitude of 16,000 ft (typically 12,000 to 20,000 ft), a pop-off set 5 in above the red line; your POH has the real figures.

Try it: in a climb with an automatic controller, which way does the wastegate move, and what has happened when it reaches its stop?

Press Take-off and drag the altitude up: the wastegate closes progressively (nearly open on the ground, about half open by 8,000 ft), sending more exhaust through the turbine so the manifold pressure holds 30 in. At 16,000 ft it is fully closed: the critical altitude. Keep climbing and the needle falls, just as the dashed normally aspirated needle has done all along.

Try it: on a cold Perth winter morning, with a fixed wastegate, the manifold pressure passes the red line on take-off. What do you do, and why was the cold the problem?

Press Cold morning, fixed: the same throttle setting that gives just under 30 in on a warm day (untick the cold morning to see it) gives about 30.7 in, because cold air is dense and gives the compressor more to work with. Reduce the throttle smoothly until it is within limits, keep the mixture rich and the RPM high, watch the engine temperatures and record the overboost in the maintenance release. Reducing the RPM is the trap: high manifold pressure at low RPM invites detonation.
