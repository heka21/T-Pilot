What to notice. The oxygen percentage never changes; the pressure falls, and the oxygen partial pressure falls with it, to half by 18,000 ft. Haemoglobin hardly notices up to about 10,000 ft (night vision does, from about 5,000), then its loading falls away, the stages arrive and the time of useful consciousness shrinks to seconds. Stage heights and times are approximate textbook values; the loading curve is a sketch of the shape, not a measurement.

Try it: at 18,000 ft, which has halved: the oxygen percentage or the oxygen partial pressure?

Press 18,000: the pressure is about 500 hPa and the oxygen partial pressure about 105 hPa, half the sea-level 213. The lungful holds half as many molecules, but one in five is still oxygen: 21 percent.

Try it: a pressurised twin at 30,000 ft loses its cabin pressure suddenly. How long does the pilot have?

Set 30,000 ft: the table gives 1 to 2 minutes for a resting person. Tick rapid decompression and it roughly halves to 30 s to 1 min. Mask first, then descend.
