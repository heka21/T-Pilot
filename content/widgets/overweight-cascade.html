What to notice. One overload hits speed, distance and strength at once: the stall speed rises with the square root of the weight, the take-off distance with about the square, and the 3.8 g limit, set at the MTOW, is reached at 3.8 divided by the weight ratio. The square-root and 3.8 g figures are the lessons'; the distance law is their rule of thumb, and the climb is shown as a direction only. Above the limits the POH has no data at all.

Try it: stall speed 48 kt at the 1,150 kg MTOW, loaded to 1,265 kg. What has changed?

Press 10% over: 48 × √1.10 = 50.3 kt; a TOSS meant to be 1.2 times the stall speed is now only about 1.14 times; the take-off is about 21 percent longer (1.1² = 1.21); and the structure reaches its design load at about 3.45 g.

Try it: why is 10 percent overweight about 20 percent more runway, not 10?

The heavier aeroplane must reach a higher lift-off speed (its square rises with the weight) and it accelerates more slowly (the same thrust on more mass). Two 10 percent effects multiply: 1.1 × 1.1 = 1.21.
