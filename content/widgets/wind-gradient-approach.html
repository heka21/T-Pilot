What to notice. The wing flies on airspeed, and inertia holds the groundspeed for a moment, so a loss of headwind (or a gain of tailwind) on the way down comes straight off the airspeed: lift falls and the aeroplane sinks below the path with no change of attitude or power. The opposite shear makes it balloon and float. Like the lesson's worked example, the widget holds the groundspeed all the way down; a real aeroplane partly recovers its speed in a gradual gradient, and the sink and touchdown point here are illustrative.

Try it: 70 kt on final, 25 kt headwind at 500 ft, 10 kt at the surface. How much margin over the 48 kt stall is left near the ground?

Press 500 ft, then Near the ground: the groundspeed stays 45 kt, the headwind drops to 10 kt, so the airspeed is 45 + 10 = 55 kt, only 7 kt above the stall instead of 22. Turn on Add power to see why you expect to need power late on final.

Try it: what does a 10 kt tailwind that dies away near the ground do?

Press Tailwind dies: the groundspeed is held at 80 kt, so as the tailwind goes the airspeed rises from 70 to 80 kt. The aeroplane balloons above the path and floats: a long landing, the performance-increasing case.
