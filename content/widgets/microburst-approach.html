What to notice. A microburst on approach comes in three parts: a headwind that makes the aeroplane fast and high, a downdraught that pushes it down, then a tailwind that takes airspeed and lift away just as it is low and close to the ground. Each change of headwind comes straight off (or onto) the airspeed for a moment, because inertia holds the groundspeed. Reducing power in the float makes the rest worse, and the total change of wind can exceed what a light aeroplane can out-climb: the only defence is avoidance. Every strength, speed and height here except the 70 kt approach speed is illustrative.

Try it: on final the airspeed suddenly rises and you float above the path, with a dark-based storm just beyond the aerodrome. What is happening, and what do you do?

You have entered a microburst’s outflow: a growing headwind, performance-increasing shear. Do not reduce power to get back on the path; what comes next is the downdraught and then a tailwind. Go around at once, and keep away from the storm. Press Headwind, then tick Pilot reduces power and play on to see why.

Try it: why is the tailwind side the dangerous one?

On the far side the outflow is a tailwind, so the airspeed drops by the gain in tailwind: lift falls just when the downdraught has already pushed the aeroplane low and there is little height left to recover. Press Tailwind and watch the airspeed strip dip below 70 kt.
