What to notice. The slope part of the weight always points down the slope, so it works opposite ways for the two rolls: uphill it fights the thrust on take-off but helps the brakes on landing; downhill it helps the take-off and fights the brakes. It is only about the slope's percentage of the weight, but the net force on a take-off roll is itself a modest fraction of the weight, so 2% takes a real share: about +10% take-off distance (the lesson's rule of thumb). The lesson gives no figure for a downhill take-off or for landing on a slope, only the direction. The force sizes in the bars are illustrative.

Try it: the chart says 520 m, the farm strip is 600 m and slopes 2% up the only way you can go. Is it on?

Press Take-off, 2% up: 520 × 1.10 = 572 m, leaving 28 m, about 5% of the strip. The lesson calls that too thin to accept: wait for a cooler part of the day, reduce the weight, or use the other direction if the wind allows.

Try it: light wind, a sloping strip. Which way do you land, and which way do you take off?

Compare Landing, 2% up with Landing, 2% down, then the two take-offs: uphill the slope adds to the stopping force, downhill it adds to the accelerating force. So land uphill and take off downhill. Once airborne the slope no longer acts on the aeroplane: its climb through the air is the same either way.
