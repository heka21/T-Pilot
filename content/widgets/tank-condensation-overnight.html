What to notice. The water comes from the air space, not the fuel: the more air above the fuel, the more vapour there is to condense on the cold walls each night, and it all sinks to the drain. A full tank has almost no air, so almost no water. The amounts and timings here are illustrative; the lesson gives no figures, only that one night makes little and a week with half tanks can make a lot.

Try it: the aeroplane will sit for a week of cold nights. Half tanks or full?

Compare A week, half tank with A week, full tank: half the tank is air, which fills the low point with water by the end of the week; the full tank has almost no air space and collects only a trace. That is the advantage of fuelling before parking (as long as the next flight's weight allows it).

Try it: where does the water end up, and why does that matter for your pre-flight?

Play one night: the droplets sink through the fuel and slide down the floor to the lowest point, beside the drain, because water is heavier than fuel. So the first fuel out of the drain is exactly where the water is, and a sample shows it before it reaches the engine.
