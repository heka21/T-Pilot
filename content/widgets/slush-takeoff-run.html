What to notice. What accelerates you is the gap between thrust and the surface's drag. Ordinary rolling resistance barely changes with speed, so on dry or wet bitumen the gap stays open to lift-off, and wet grass only narrows it. Slush drag grows with speed while the thrust falls, so the gap closes before lift-off speed: the aeroplane stops accelerating however much runway is left. Most light-aircraft POHs give no slush figures, and that absence means do not operate. The curves follow the lesson's diagram; every magnitude here (forces, distances, the 700 m runway) is illustrative, only the directions are the lesson's.

Try it: a long runway would fix the slush problem, wouldn't it?

Press Slush and play on: once slush drag equals thrust, at about 44 kt here, the net force is zero and the speed line goes flat. More runway only gives more distance at the same speed. The fix is not to take off from slush at all.

Try it: why does a wet sealed runway barely change the take-off but wet grass does?

Compare Wet sealed and Wet grass with the dashed dry lines: a film of water on bitumen adds almost no rolling resistance, so lift-off comes within a few metres of the dry figure. Soft wet grass makes the wheels push through the surface all the way along, so the gap between thrust and drag is smaller and the run is noticeably longer.
