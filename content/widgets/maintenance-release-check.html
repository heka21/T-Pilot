Verdict

What to notice. The release runs out on hours or on the date, whichever comes first (commonly 100 hours or 12 months for a private or training aeroplane; check the aircraft's schedule), and maintenance due inside it, such as an AD at a lower hour count, comes first of all. The question is not "is it current now?" but "will it still be current when I land?", so add the flight time before you compare. A permissible unserviceability lets you fly only within its conditions, and an open defect grounds the aeroplane until an engineer deals with it. "Runs out first" projects the hours at the rate the aeroplane has been flown since the release was issued.

Try it: two weeks later the aeroplane shows 3,511.0 hours and you plan a 2-hour flight to Northam and back. Can you go?

Press Two weeks later: the release expires at 3,512.5 hours, so it is current now, but you would land at 3,513.0, half an hour past it. Not for this flight; one of under 1.5 hours would do.

Try it: on the POPC release the brake has been fixed. How long a trip can you plan before the AD falls due?

Press AD before landing: at 1,944.2 hours a 6.5-hour trip would end at 1,950.7, 0.7 hours past the AD at 1,950.0. Drag the flight time down to 5.8 hours and the last question turns green: that is the most you can fly before the inspection.
