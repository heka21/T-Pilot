Ice in the carburettor

What to notice. With a fixed-pitch propeller, ice shows first as a slow, unexplained RPM drop. Full carburettor heat always costs RPM at first; ice is proven by what comes next: rough running, a rise with the heat still on, and a reading on cold higher than before. No ice: a small drop that stays, then back to the original. Opening the throttle instead only hides the ice until there is no throttle left. The RPMs are the lessons' worked examples; the timings are illustrative.

Try it: RPM normally 2400, now 2300. Full heat gives 2200 and rough running, then 2350; on cold, 2400. Was there ice?

Play the first story: yes. The rise with the heat still on was the passage clearing, and 2400 on cold is higher than the 2300 before the heat, because the ice had been stealing power (RBKA 2.1).

Try it: why is "open the throttle to get the RPM back" the wrong answer?

Choose the chase story: each nudge brings the RPM back while the ice keeps growing behind a more open butterfly. At full throttle there is nothing left, and the engine stops with little heat left to melt the ice (BAKC 3.4). Any unexplained RPM loss is carburettor ice: full heat, and leave it on.
