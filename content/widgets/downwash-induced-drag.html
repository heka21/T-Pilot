What to notice. Induced drag is the price of lift: the harder the wing works (slow, heavy, high angle of attack), the stronger the tip vortices and the downwash, the further the lift leans back and the bigger its backward part. It falls with the square of the speed, so double the speed and it is a quarter. A longer, slender wing of the same area makes much less (half, in this simple model). The 600 N at 70 kt is the lesson's illustrative figure; the angles of attack come from a simple model, and the tilts and vortex sizes are exaggerated.

Try it: the lesson's example has 600 N of induced drag at 70 kt. What is it at 100 kt and at 55 kt?

Press 100 kt: 600 × (70 ÷ 100)² = 294 N. Press 55 kt: 600 × (70 ÷ 55)² = 972 N. Slowing down makes the wing work harder, so induced drag climbs steeply as you approach the stall.

Try it: why are the wake vortices behind a heavy aircraft worst just after take-off?

Press 55 kt and drag the weight to 100%: slow and heavy is the combination that makes the wing work hardest, so the vortices are strongest. Speed up or lighten the aeroplane and they shrink (BAKC 4.4).
