What to notice. Each range puts you on a sphere around its satellite. Two spheres meet in a circle, a third cuts the circle at two points, one of them deep in the Earth or out in space, so three ranges would fix a 3D position if the receiver's clock were perfect. It is not, and its error makes every pseudorange wrong by the same amount, so the spheres miss: a fourth satellite lets the receiver solve for its own clock and gives the 3D fix. A fifth lets RAIM compare four-satellite solutions and detect a faulty satellite (fault detection); a sixth lets it identify and exclude the culprit (FDE). Bunch the satellites together and the spheres cross at shallow angles: high DOP, every range error magnified, however many satellites are in view.

Try this: with the clock error on, add the fourth satellite. What changed, and why could the receiver not do it with three?

The spheres shrink to their true ranges and meet at the receiver. With latitude, longitude, height and clock error there are four unknowns, and four unknowns need four measurements; with three the receiver can at best assume a height and give a 2D fix.

Try this: six satellites bunched together against five spread out. Which gives the better fix?

The five spread out. Accuracy depends on geometry as well as number: bunched satellites give a high DOP, so the same range errors produce a long thin area of uncertainty instead of a small one.
