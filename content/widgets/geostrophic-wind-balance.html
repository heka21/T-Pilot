What to notice. The pressure gradient force starts the air towards the low; Coriolis, to the left in the Southern Hemisphere and growing with speed, turns it until the two balance and the wind blows along the isobars with the low on its right. Close isobars mean a bigger push and a faster balanced wind. Near the ground friction slows the air, Coriolis weakens, and the wind turns across the isobars towards the low: veered and lighter. The path is an illustrative model.

Try it: with the high to the north and the low to the south, what wind do you get, and which way would an overseas textbook have it turn?

Press Release the air: Coriolis turns it left until it blows east, a westerly, the westerlies of the Southern Ocean. In the Northern Hemisphere Coriolis acts to the right, so the same pressure pattern there would give an easterly.

Try it: why is the surface wind veered from the 2,000 ft wind?

Tick friction: the slower air feels less Coriolis, the unchanged pressure gradient force wins a little and the wind turns towards the low. With the low on your right, turning towards it is turning clockwise: veered, here from 270 to about 300.
