What to notice. Three bands by the height of the base: high above about 20,000 ft (Ci, Cc, Cs, all ice crystals, thin and white), middle about 6,500 to 20,000 ft (Ac, As, Ns, water droplets often supercooled), low below about 6,500 ft (Sc, St), plus Cu and Cb, which start low and grow tall, the Cb spreading into an anvil at the tropopause. Nimbostratus is classed as middle cloud but its base often lowers to near the ground. Only the halo tells cirrostratus from altostratus. The bands are approximate for Australian latitudes and higher in the tropics; each cloud's drawn base and top are representative, not figures from the lesson.

Try this: two featureless grey-white sheets, the sun visible through both. One has a halo round it. Which is which, and which could ice the aeroplane?

The halo is cirrostratus: ice crystals above 20,000 ft, which bend the light into a halo and do not stick to an airframe. The other is altostratus, water droplets in the middle band, which shows the sun as if through ground glass and can give light to moderate icing and light continuous rain.

Try this: a TAF says SCT040CB. What air, what hazards, and why is this cloud named when stratus is not?

Unstable air: cumulonimbus grows upwards on its own buoyancy. Expect severe turbulence, hail, heavy showers, lightning, severe icing and microburst wind shear. Only CB and TCU are named in a TAF or METAR because only they are a direct hazard; every other cloud is reported as amount and base alone.
