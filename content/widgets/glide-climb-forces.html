What to notice. In every steady case the forces balance: lift holds the part of the weight across the path, and thrust pays for drag plus the part of the weight along it. Climbing, that part of the weight acts backwards like extra drag, so thrust must exceed drag. With the engine at idle there is no thrust, so the path tilts down until the weight's forward share pays for the drag: the glide angle depends only on the lift-to-drag ratio. The 5° climb and 3° descent are illustrative angles; weight 9,810 N is the lesson's 1,000 kg aeroplane.

Try it: in a glide at L/D 10, what are lift and drag, and what is the glide angle?

Press Glide, L/D 10: lift ≈ 9,761 N and drag ≈ 976 N, the lesson's figures. The path is 1 in 10, about 5.7°. A better L/D gives a flatter glide; slide it up and watch γ shrink.

Try it: why does a climb need more thrust than level flight at the same lift-to-drag ratio?

Press Level, then Climb 5°: drag barely changes, but W sin γ, about 855 N here, now acts back along the path, so thrust rises from about 981 N to about 1,832 N. That extra is the work of lifting the weight.
