What to notice. Wind moves the air, not the glide through it: rate of descent, time to the ground and still-air distance never change, but a headwind cuts the groundspeed and shortens the distance over the ground while a tailwind lengthens it. Still-air distance is height × L/D, and only the best glide speed gives the best L/D (some textbooks add: fly slightly faster than best glide in a strong headwind, slightly slower in a tailwind).

Try this: 2000 ft, 65 kt, 9.8 : 1. How far in still air, into a 20 kt headwind and with a 20 kt tailwind?

Rate of descent $= 65 \times 6076 \div 60 \div 9.8 \approx 670$ fpm, so the time is $2000 \div 672 \approx 2.98$ min. Still air: $2.98 \times 65 \div 60 \approx 3.23$ NM. Headwind (GS 45 kt): $3.226 \times 45 \div 65 \approx 2.23$ NM. Tailwind (GS 85 kt): $3.226 \times 85 \div 65 \approx 4.22$ NM. The note rounds the time to 3 minutes and gets 3.25, 2.25 and 4.25 NM: same answer, rounder numbers.

Try this: 3000 ft with a best L/D of 9 : 1, then mishandled at a speed giving only 7 : 1. How much distance is lost?

$3000 \times 9 = 27{,}000$ ft $\div 6076 \approx 4.4$ NM. At 7 : 1: $3000 \times 7 = 21{,}000$ ft $\approx 3.5$ NM. Almost a nautical mile gone, in still air, just from flying the wrong speed.

Try this: with a 20 kt headwind, does the rate of descent change?

No. It stays about 670 fpm because the aeroplane glides through the air exactly as before; only the groundspeed (45 kt) and so the path over the ground change. The glide over the ground is steeper and shorter.
