What to notice. The great circle is the shortest path, but because the meridians converge it cuts each one at a different angle, so its true track keeps changing. The rhumb line holds one track and is longer, by most on long east–west legs at high latitude, where it lies on the equator side of the great circle. On a VFR-length leg the two are almost identical, and the line you rule on a WAC is close to a great circle. Routes are illustrative, on a spherical earth with 60 NM to the degree.

Try it: why do you measure a long track at a meridian near its middle?

Look at the three arrows: a straight line on the chart, like the great circle, leaves on one track and arrives on another because the meridians converge. At the middle it reads the average, which is the track you fly.

Try it: an exam option says "parallels of latitude are great circles". True?

Only the equator is. Press Along the equator: the two lines coincide. Any other parallel's plane misses the earth's centre, so it is a small circle, and the great circle between two points on it swings towards the pole.
