What to notice. The wake begins where the heavy rotates and lies along and below its climb path: the two counter-rotating vortices (left clockwise, right anticlockwise, seen from behind) sink at a few hundred feet per minute and level off 500 to 1,000 ft below its path, but near the ground they stop a short height up and spread outward at a few knots. Your path lifts off before its rotation point, climbs above its path and turns away upwind. Add a light crosswind and the downwind vortex leaves twice as fast while the upwind vortex's outward drift is cancelled: it parks over the runway. Calm, stable air keeps the vortices strongest and longest; wind and turbulence break them up. Sink, spread and decay rates are the lesson's typical values, not limits, and the heavy's climb is schematic.

Try this: set a 2 kt crosswind from the right and let the clock run. Which vortex is still over the runway after 30 s, and why is a stronger crosswind safer here?

The right (upwind) vortex. It spreads outward at about the same few knots that the wind blows it back, so it sits almost stationary near the touchdown zone. A stronger crosswind, say 8 kt, overpowers the outward drift and carries both vortices clear of the runway.

Try this: why does your path lift off before the heavy's rotation point rather than after it?

Because the wake only exists from the rotation point onwards. Airborne before it, and climbing above the heavy's path, you never enter the volume that contains the vortices; if the runway is too short for that, the answer is to wait for the wake to decay.
