What to notice. Flap is lift as well as drag. Take it all away while slow and low and, at the same attitude, the lift goes with it: the aeroplane sinks towards the runway and its stall speed jumps to the clean figure. The first, high-drag stage costs little lift; the rest comes up once you are climbing well above the next stall speed. Hold the attitude, too: let the nose drop and the speed runs past VFO (85 kt), and the flap is stuck out until you slow again. VFO and VFE are the lesson's hypothetical aeroplane; the stall speeds are the RBKA example aeroplane's (55 kt clean as in RBKA 3.6, 50 kt with full flap so that 1.3 × 50 gives the 65 kt approach, about 52 kt at the first stage); the 75 kt climb, the heights, timings and the lift bar are illustrative.

Try it: on the go-around you raise all the flap at once at 65 kt. What happens to the stall speed and to the flight path?

Choose All at once: the stall speed jumps from 50 kt to the clean 55 kt while you are still slow, and with the flap's lift gone the aeroplane sinks to a few tens of feet above the runway. Raising the nose to stop the sink brings the margin down to a few knots. In stages, the margin never drops below about 13 kt.

Try it: you let the nose drop and reach 100 kt with the first stage still out (VFO 85, VFE 105 and 95). Within limits? May you raise the flap?

Press Nose low, 100 kt: within limits, because 100 kt is below the first-stage VFE of 105. But 100 kt is above VFO 85, so the flap may not move: raise the nose and slow to 85 kt or below first, then retract it.
