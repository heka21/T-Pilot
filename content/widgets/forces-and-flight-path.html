What to notice. In steady flight the forces always balance. Level, thrust equals drag and lift equals weight; climbing, part of the weight acts back along the path, so thrust must equal drag plus that share, and the surplus thrust sets how steep the path is. Lift stays at right angles to the path and is a little less than the weight in a climb. The nose attitude is the path angle plus the angle of attack, so it always looks steeper than the climb. An illustrative trainer at maximum weight, sea level, still air, tuned to the climb figures: its power-off glide comes out flatter (about 1 in 13 at 70 kt) than the 1 in 10 of BAKC 2.5's example and the 9:1 of RBKA 3.4, so use the lesson's ratio for glide arithmetic.

Try it: level at 100 kt, you raise the nose to 80 kt without touching the throttle. What happens?

Press Level, 100 kt, then Nose up, same power: at 80 kt drag is lower and the propeller gives a little more thrust, so about 3% of the weight is left over and the aeroplane climbs at roughly 230 fpm. The elevator chose the speed; the surplus thrust at that speed made the climb.

Try it: why is the climb steeper at Vx than at Vy, although the rate is lower?

Press Full power, Vy (75 kt): thrust − drag is about 7.4% of the weight, a 4.2° path and 560 fpm. Press Full power, Vx (60 kt): the surplus is bigger (8.2%), so the path is steeper, about 500 ft/NM against 450, but the aeroplane moves along it more slowly, so the rate falls to about 500 fpm. Vx about 60 kt and Vy about 75 kt are BAKC 4.3's typical trainer; the climb rates come from this illustrative model.
