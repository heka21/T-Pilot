What to notice. The gradient wind at about 2,000 ft hardly changes; what changes is how well the air below is mixed with it. By day thermals bring its speed and direction down, so the surface wind is stronger, backed towards the gradient and gusty, peaking in the early to mid afternoon. At night a radiation inversion cuts the surface off: the wind goes lighter and more veered, often calm by dawn, with the low-level jet just above. Cloud or a strong gradient keeps the swing small. The lesson's land rule of thumb (about half to two-thirds, veered about 30°) is the average; the percentages and timings either side of it are illustrative.

Try it: which gives the calmest dawn at Northam, clear with a weak gradient or overcast with a strong one?

Press Calm dawn: clear and weak lets a strong inversion form, the surface is cut off and the windsock goes limp. Now set Overcast and Strong: the graph flattens, there is no inversion, and the dawn wind stays near its average of about half to two-thirds of the gradient wind.

Try it: you depart Jandakot at 0600 on the lesson's January day. What happens a few hundred feet up?

Press January 0600: the windsock is limp, but just above the inversion the low-level jet blows at about 25 kt. Expect a sudden increase in headwind or crosswind, and a bump, as you climb through the top of the inversion.
