What to notice. Vapour lock is worst after a hot engine is shut down and restarted: no cooling air, so heat soaks into the fuel lines and pump and the fuel boils, and a pump made for liquid cannot move bubbles. The symptoms are what an intermittent supply looks like: fluctuating fuel pressure and flow, rough running, surging and hard starting. The booster pump's extra pressure pushes the vapour through or collapses it back to liquid. This aeroplane has an engine-driven pump, an electric booster pump and a fuel pressure gauge, as low-wing and fuel-injected types usually do; a gravity-fed high-wing trainer such as the Cessna 152 has none of them. Timings and needle positions are illustrative; follow your POH hot-start procedure.

Try it: play the restart with the booster pump off. When the needles start to flicker, what do you do?

Tick the booster pump: the pressure rises and steadies within a few moments as the vapour is pushed through or collapses back to liquid, and the engine smooths out. Then enrich if needed and follow the POH hot-start procedure.

Try it: why is vapour lock more likely after a short stop on a hot day than in cruise?

In cruise, air through the cowling carries the engine's heat away. Once the engine stops, the cooling air stops too, so heat soaks from the hot engine into the fuel lines and pump, and the fuel in them boils into vapour.
