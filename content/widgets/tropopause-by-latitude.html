What to notice. The tropopause is a lid, and the hotter the surface below it, the higher it sits: about 8 km (26,000 ft) over the poles, about 11 km (36,000 ft) over Perth, 16 to 18 km (52,000 to 59,000 ft) over the equator, and higher in summer than in winter. A storm grows until it reaches the lid, then spreads into its anvil. The lid steps down in breaks, where the jet streams and clear air turbulence live. The latitudes of the breaks and the size of the seasonal shift are illustrative; the lesson gives no figures for them.

Try it: why is the tropopause higher over Darwin than over Hobart?

Move from Hobart to Darwin: near the equator the surface is heated far more strongly, and the vigorous convection carries warm air higher and lifts the top of the troposphere, to 16 to 18 km against about 11 km over Hobart.

Try it: which storm grows taller, one over the Kimberley in the wet season or one off Albany in winter?

Press each preset: the Kimberley storm can climb to 16 to 18 km before the tropical tropopause, raised further by the summer heating, stops it; the winter storm off Albany is capped near 11 km, a little lower in winter.
