What to notice. The governor turns a would-be RPM change into a blade-angle change: the rising RPM swings the flyweights out, they lift the pilot valve, oil goes to the hub and the blades go coarser until the angle of attack, the load and the RPM are back. Without oil the blades run to fine pitch and the propeller overspeeds like a fixed-pitch one. The blade and helix angles follow the lesson's triangle (345 kt round at 2,400 RPM); the RPM overshoot, timings, fine stop and 2,700 red line are illustrative.

Try it: why is the tachometer steady when you lower the nose in a constant-speed aeroplane?

Play it: a brief rise in RPM swings the flyweights out, the valve sends oil to the hub and the blades coarsen from 21 to about 27 degrees, which restores the 8 degree angle of attack and the load. The pilot reads power from the manifold pressure gauge instead.

Try it: why does a constant-speed propeller still overspeed if it loses oil pressure?

Choose the oil story: on many singles oil drives the blades coarse, so without it the spring and the blades' twisting forces take them to the fine stop, where they absorb little torque and the RPM follows the airspeed. Throttle back, slow down, pull the propeller lever back and follow the POH.
