## What the exam expects (PAKA 2.2)

- **2.2.1** Say what typical aeroplane systems do and how they work, including how the pilot can "overpower" them and the precautions to take, for: (a) stall warning devices; (b) autopilot components: (i) the roll, attitude, heading and pitch controls, (ii) the trim indicator, (iii) the cut-out mechanisms.

## Why this matters

Two systems in this subtopic sit between you and the aeroplane, and both can lull you. The stall warning horn in a Cessna or the light in a Piper is the last clear message before the wing lets go, and on the pre-flight at Jandakot or a farm strip in the wheatbelt it is easy to skip the check that proves it works, especially after a dusty week or a night out on the tie-downs. The autopilot, once you fly a touring aeroplane on a long leg to Kalbarri or Esperance, does the flying for hours at a time, and the danger is not that it fails but that it does something you did not expect and you fight it instead of switching it off. Both systems share the theme of the element: each can be overpowered or can mislead, and the precautions are about knowing exactly how it works. The exam asks how each stall warning type senses the stall, which one needs electrical power, what the autopilot modes do, what a trim annunciator means, how you disconnect, and why fighting an autopilot is dangerous.

## How a stall warning knows the wing is near the stall

A wing stalls at its **critical angle of attack**, about 15 to 18 degrees on a light aeroplane, at any speed and any attitude ([RBKA 3.6](/notes/RBKA/3.6)). So a useful stall warning cannot simply watch the airspeed: in a steep turn or at a heavy weight the wing stalls well above the book speed. It has to sense **angle of attack**. A light aeroplane does that without any instrument at all, by watching where the air divides at the leading edge.

Picture the air arriving at the front of the wing. Some goes over the top, some goes underneath, and at one point on the leading edge it does neither: it comes to rest against the wing. That point is the **stagnation point**. At a small angle of attack it sits right at the nose of the aerofoil. Tilt the wing to a higher angle and the oncoming air meets it from further below, so the stagnation point moves **down and back** under the leading edge, and the air just above it now has to whip round the sharp curve of the nose to get to the upper surface. That fast-moving air around the nose is at a **very low pressure**, and the lower the stagnation point goes, the stronger that suction becomes.

Both light-aircraft stall warning devices exploit this. One senses where the stagnation point is; the other senses the suction above it. Both are set so that they trigger about **5 to 10 kt** above the stall in level flight, giving you an aural (and sometimes visual) warning while there is still time to act.

![Where does the stall warning fit in the sequence of stall symptoms?](diagram:stall-sequence "The warning arrives after the low airspeed and sloppy controls and before the buffet: it is set about 5 to 10 kt above the stall.")

### The reed (pneumatic) type

Many Cessnas use a **reed** stall warning. A small slot or opening in the leading edge of one wing connects through a tube to a **reed horn**, a reed that sounds when air is drawn across it, like a harmonica. At normal angles of attack the opening is near the stagnation point and nothing much happens. As the angle of attack increases, the stagnation point moves below the opening and the low pressure over the leading edge **sucks air through the reed**, from the cabin end of the tube out to the wing. The reed sounds a wailing note that grows louder as you get closer to the stall.

The great advantage is that it is purely pneumatic: **no electrical power is needed**. It still works with the master switch off or after a complete electrical failure.

Its weaknesses are all things that can block a small hole: insects, dirt, a mud wasp, ice, or a blanking cover left in place. The **pre-flight check** is to gently suck on the opening through a clean cloth and listen for the horn in the cabin. If you hear nothing, it is not working.

### The vane (electric) type

Many Pipers use a **vane** stall warning. A small hinged tab sits just below the leading edge. At normal angles of attack the stagnation point is above the vane, so the air at the vane is flowing down and back along the lower surface, and it holds the vane **down**. As the angle of attack increases, the stagnation point moves back **below** the vane. Now the air at the vane is flowing forwards and up around the leading edge, and it pushes the vane **up**, closing a **microswitch** that sounds a horn or lights a warning light.

Because it uses a switch, it **needs electrical power** (the master switch on). An electrical failure disables it, and ice can jam the vane in either position. The **pre-flight check**: master on, lift the vane gently and confirm the warning sounds or lights.

| | Reed (pneumatic) | Vane (electric) |
|---|---|---|
| Senses | Suction over the leading edge | Stagnation point moving below the vane |
| How it warns | Air sucked through a reed horn | Vane lifts, closes a microswitch, horn or light |
| Electrical power | Not needed | Needed (master on) |
| Disabled by | Blocked opening: insects, dirt, ice, a cover | Electrical failure, ice jamming the vane |
| Pre-flight check | Suck gently through a clean cloth, listen | Master on, lift the vane gently, confirm warning |

![How do the reed and vane stall warnings sense the stall?](diagram:stall-warning-reed-and-vane "What to notice: both watch the stagnation point move down and back under the leading edge; the reed is sounded by suction and needs no power, the vane closes a switch and needs the master on.")

### Bigger aeroplanes: angle-of-attack sensors, stick shakers and pushers

Larger aircraft use dedicated **angle-of-attack sensors**, a vane on the fuselage side that aligns itself with the airflow, and drive a **stick shaker**, which shakes the control column to imitate the buffet, and sometimes a **stick pusher**, which physically pushes the column forward to lower the angle of attack if the pilot does not.

??? check "Check yourself: after a complete electrical failure, which stall warning type still works, and why?"
    The reed type, because it is driven by the suction over the leading edge drawing air through the horn and needs no electricity. The vane type uses a microswitch and stops working when there is no electrical power.

## Why the warning is not a guarantee

A stall warning **advises**; it does not prevent. Nothing in a light aeroplane's stall warning stops you pulling the wing past the critical angle, and that is the first sense in which this element talks about "overpowering" a system. You can fly straight through the horn into a stall, and pilots do, usually when their attention is elsewhere.

Even a working warning may give you less than you expect.

**It warns at an angle, not a speed.** That is its strength, and it catches pilots who have memorised "the horn goes at 55 kt". In a turn, at a heavier weight or with ice on the wing, the stall speed rises, and the warning speed rises with it.

!!! example "Worked example: where the horn sounds in a steep turn"
    Given: level-flight stall speed 48 kt, flaps up. The stall warning sounds at about 7 kt above that, 55 kt, in level flight. You fly a level 60 degree turn.

    1. Load factor: $n = 1/\cos 60° = \mathbf{2.0}$.
    2. Square root: $\sqrt{2.0} = \mathbf{1.41}$.
    3. Stall speed in the turn: $48 \times 1.41 = \mathbf{68\ kt}$.
    4. Warning speed in the turn: $55 \times 1.41 = \mathbf{78\ kt}$.

    The horn now sounds at 78 kt, not 55. Because the vane or reed senses angle of attack, it still warns before the stall, but a pilot who reads the ASI and thinks "plenty of speed" will be wrong. Both speeds scale by the square root of the load factor, as in [RBKA 3.6](/notes/RBKA/3.6).

**The margin can be small.** In an **accelerated stall**, a hard pull-up or a pull out of a dive, the angle of attack rises so quickly that the horn and the stall arrive almost together. On an iced wing the critical angle itself is lower than the angle the warning was set for, so the wing can stall **before** the warning sounds.

**It can be inoperative.** Blocked, iced, unpowered, or simply broken, and nothing in the cockpit tells you so until you need it.

On larger aircraft, a **stick pusher** can be overpowered by a determined pilot, or switched off, and accidents have followed both. The flight manual says when it may be disabled.

The **precautions** follow:

1. **Test it on every pre-flight**, by the method for your type.
2. **Do not rely on it alone.** Know the other symptoms of the approaching stall, in order: low and decreasing airspeed, sloppy controls, the warning, buffet ([RBKA 3.6](/notes/RBKA/3.6)).
3. **Treat it as the last warning, not the first.** In a steep turn, a pull-up or with any suspicion of ice, the margin is smaller.
4. **Respond to it.** The horn means reduce the angle of attack, not "nearly there".

!!! warning "Common trap"
    "The stall warning sounds at a fixed airspeed" is wrong. It senses angle of attack, so the speed at which it sounds rises in a turn and at higher weight, exactly as the stall speed does. And on an iced wing it may not sound before the stall at all.

??? check "Check yourself: why can an iced wing stall before the stall warning sounds?"
    Ice reduces the critical angle of attack, so the wing stalls at a smaller angle than the clean wing. The warning is set to trigger a little below the clean wing's critical angle, so the iced wing can reach its lower stalling angle first.

## What an autopilot is made of

An autopilot does what you do when you fly by hand: it watches the aeroplane's attitude and heading, compares them with what you asked for, and moves the controls to remove the difference. It just does it continuously, without tiring. A light-aircraft autopilot has four parts:

- **Sensors**: the attitude gyro, turn coordinator or an attitude and heading reference system (AHRS), the heading indicator, and navigation inputs from a VOR or GNSS receiver. They tell the autopilot what the aeroplane is doing.
- A **computer** that compares what the sensors say with what the pilot has selected and works out what control movement will correct the difference.
- **Servos**: electric motors connected to the control cables, which move the ailerons, the elevator and sometimes the trim. Each drives its control through a **clutch**, which matters later.
- A **mode controller**: the panel of buttons and knobs where you tell the autopilot what to hold.

![Which instruments might feed an autopilot's sensors?](diagram:gyro-instruments-panel "The attitude indicator gives roll and pitch, the turn coordinator the rate of turn, the directional indicator the heading: an autopilot reads the same quantities.")

The simplest autopilots act in one axis only, roll, and are little more than a **wing leveller**. Two-axis autopilots add pitch. The exam's element lists the controls you need to recognise.

![How do the parts of an autopilot work together?](diagram:autopilot-control-loop "What to notice: sensors and the mode controller feed the computer, the servos move the controls through slip clutches, and the sensors see the result; nothing moves the throttle.")

## The modes: roll, attitude, heading and pitch

The modes are easiest to understand as answers to the question "what should the autopilot hold?"

- **Roll, or wing leveller (ROL).** Holds the wings level (or holds the bank angle at engagement on some systems). A single-axis autopilot may do only this, using the turn coordinator as its sensor. Useful for reaching for a chart, and as a recovery aid if you start to lose control in cloud.
- **Heading (HDG).** Turns the aeroplane to the **heading bug** on the heading indicator and holds that heading. Move the bug and the autopilot turns to follow it. Heading mode holds a heading, not a track, so it does not correct for drift: if the wind changes, you drift off your track.
- **Navigation (NAV or GPS).** Tracks a VOR radial or a GNSS course, correcting for wind to stay on the selected track.
- **Pitch or attitude (PIT).** Holds the pitch attitude at engagement.
- **Altitude hold (ALT).** Holds the altitude at engagement by moving the elevator.
- **Vertical speed (VS).** Holds a selected rate of climb or descent.

On many controllers a **pitch trim wheel** or an **up/down rocker** adjusts the attitude or vertical speed being held.

Notice what a pitch mode does not control: **power**. If you engage altitude hold and reduce power, the autopilot will raise the nose to hold the height, and keep raising it as the speed falls, all the way to the stall unless the system has its own protection. In vertical speed mode with a climb rate set too high for the aeroplane, the same thing happens in the climb. The autopilot holds what you asked for; you manage the speed.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Heading mode and nav mode are not the same. Heading mode holds a heading and lets the wind blow you off track; nav mode tracks the course you selected and corrects for drift. An exam stem that asks which mode follows a GNSS track wants NAV (or GPS), not HDG.

??? check "Check yourself: the autopilot is in altitude hold and you bring the power back for a long descent planning check without changing the mode. What will happen, and why?"
    The aeroplane will hold its altitude by raising the nose, and the speed will decay, possibly towards the stall. Altitude hold moves the elevator, not the throttle, so it trades airspeed for height without telling you.

## The trim indicator

When you fly by hand you trim the aeroplane so that it holds its attitude with no force on the control column. An autopilot holding the pitch with its elevator servo faces the same loads: if the aeroplane is out of trim, the servo is pushing or pulling continuously to hold the attitude, and you do not feel it because you are not holding the controls.

Many autopilots deal with this with an **automatic pitch trim** servo that moves the trim tab to take the load off the elevator servo, just as you would. Others, without auto-trim, have a **trim indicator or annunciator** that shows when the elevator servo is holding a load, typically "TRIM UP" or "TRIM DN", meaning "trim nose up (or down) is needed".

![How does the trim tab hold the elevator without any force from the pilot?](diagram:trim-tab "The tab deflects the opposite way to the elevator; the air on it holds the elevator where it is. A servo holding the elevator instead is a hidden force.")

The reason this matters is what happens at **disconnect**. The moment the autopilot lets go, whatever force its servo was holding is suddenly yours. If it was holding a large load, the aeroplane will **pitch suddenly** in the direction the load was pushing. An out-of-trim indication is therefore a warning of a pitch upset waiting to happen.

The precautions:

- Where the autopilot has no auto-trim, **retrim manually** as the annunciator directs, so the servo is not holding a load.
- **Look at the trim indicator before you disconnect**, and hold the controls firmly, ready for a pitch change.
- Engage the autopilot only with the aeroplane **in trim**.

??? check "Check yourself: why can disconnecting an autopilot that has been holding an out-of-trim load cause a sudden pitch change?"
    The elevator servo has been holding the aeroplane against the out-of-trim force. At disconnect that force is no longer opposed by anything until the pilot takes up the load, so the nose moves in the direction the trim was pushing it.

## Cut-out mechanisms

An autopilot is only safe if you can always switch it off, quickly, and in more than one way. The systems give you several, from the fastest to the most drastic:

- **Autopilot disconnect (A/P DISC)** button on the control wheel: the **primary means**, under your thumb, so you can disconnect without letting go of the controls.
- The **AP button** on the mode controller.
- The **autopilot master switch** or the autopilot **circuit breaker**.
- The **avionics master**, which removes power from the autopilot along with the radios.
- **Trim interrupt** or **control wheel steering** switches, which disengage the servos while held, so you can manoeuvre by hand and let go to resume.
- **Automatic disconnects**: the autopilot disconnects itself on a system failure, at an excessive attitude, or, on some systems, when the pilot overpowers the servos.

An automatic disconnect is usually announced with an aural tone or a flashing annunciator, and the aeroplane may not be in trim when it happens.

Know every means for your aeroplane, practise them, and **check the disconnect during the pre-flight**: the POH autopilot supplement in section 9 lists the test.

??? check "Check yourself: the A/P DISC button does not disconnect the autopilot. What do you do next?"
    Use the next means: the AP button on the mode controller, then the autopilot master switch or circuit breaker, or the avionics master. For a trim runaway, follow the POH procedure. The point is to remove power from the servos, not to fight them.

## Overpowering the autopilot

Here is the second sense of "overpowering" in the element. The servos drive the controls through **slip clutches**, which slip when the force on them exceeds a set value. That is a safety feature: it means a pilot can physically overpower an engaged autopilot if it jams or runs away. But overpowering it is not a way of flying it, and doing so deliberately is hazardous.

Trace what happens with an autopilot that has **auto-trim**. It is holding altitude. You pull back on the control column, against the servo, to climb. The autopilot senses that the aeroplane is trying to depart from the altitude and that its elevator servo is under load, so it does what it is designed to do: it runs the trim to relieve the load, **nose down, against you**. You pull harder; it trims further. You are now holding a large nose-up force against a large nose-down trim. When the autopilot finally disconnects, whether you press the button or it gives up and disconnects itself, the trim is still wound fully nose-down and your pull is suddenly the only thing opposing it. The result is a sharp nose-down **pitch upset**, towards the ground.

![Why does fighting an auto-trim autopilot end in a pitch upset?](diagram:autopilot-overpowering-trim "What to notice: every pull is answered by more nose-down trim while the attitude looks normal; the out-of-trim force all arrives at once at disconnect.")

A **trim runaway**, where the trim motor keeps running on its own, feels the same: a steadily growing force on the controls.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    "Hold the controls against the autopilot until it settles" sounds sensible and is the distractor. With auto-trim, the harder you hold against the servo, the further it trims against you, and the bigger the upset when it lets go. The answer is always to **disconnect, not overpower**.

The **precautions** for the autopilot:

1. **Never fight the autopilot.** If it does something you did not expect, **disconnect it** with the A/P DISC button, then fly by hand.
2. **Hold the controls firmly at disconnect**, expecting an out-of-trim force; check the trim indicator first.
3. **If disconnect fails**, pull the autopilot circuit breaker or switch off the autopilot or avionics master; for a trim runaway, follow the POH procedure.
4. **Keep monitoring.** Engage the autopilot only in trim and within the altitude and speed limits in the POH supplement, and keep watching the attitude, altitude, airspeed and mode annunciations while it flies.

## Bringing it together

You are flying a Piper Archer with a two-axis autopilot from Jandakot to Albany on a hot afternoon. On the pre-flight you lift the stall warning vane with the master on and hear the horn; the POH supplement's autopilot test passes, including the disconnect. In the cruise at 6,500 ft the autopilot is in NAV and ALT, tracking the GNSS course. Approaching Mount Barker you start planning the descent and bring the power back a little to slow down while you brief the approach, without changing modes. The autopilot, still holding altitude, raises the nose and the speed decays; the trim annunciator lights. Then a thermal off the hills pitches the nose up further, and you instinctively pull against the autopilot to stop it. Every part of this lesson is in play. The pitch mode was holding height at the expense of speed. The annunciator was warning of an out-of-trim load. Pulling against an auto-trim system makes it trim against you. And if the speed had kept falling, the vane would have sounded the horn about 5 to 10 kt above a stall speed already raised by the turbulence. The right sequence: thumb on A/P DISC, hold the controls firmly expecting a pitch change, fly by hand, retrim, set descent power and attitude, then re-engage in a vertical speed mode if you want the autopilot back.

## Key points

- **2.2.1 (a)** The stall warning senses angle of attack from the stagnation point moving down and back under the leading edge, and warns about 5 to 10 kt above the stall. Reed type: suction draws air through a horn, no electrical power needed, blocked by insects, dirt, ice or a cover; check by sucking gently through a clean cloth. Vane type: the vane is pushed up and closes a microswitch, needs electrical power, jammed by ice; check with the master on by lifting the vane. It advises but cannot stop you stalling, and an iced wing or an accelerated stall can beat it.
- **2.2.1 (b)(i)** An autopilot is sensors, a computer, servos with clutches and a mode controller. ROL holds the wings level, HDG flies the heading bug, NAV tracks a VOR or GNSS course, PIT, ALT and VS hold an attitude, an altitude or a rate with the elevator. No pitch mode controls power.
- **2.2.1 (b)(ii)** A trim annunciator (TRIM UP or DN) means the elevator servo is holding a load: retrim, and expect a pitch change at disconnect.
- **2.2.1 (b)(iii)** Disconnect with A/P DISC first; then the AP button, the autopilot master or circuit breaker, the avionics master. Autopilots also disconnect themselves. Never overpower: with auto-trim, fighting the servo trims the aeroplane further against you and the upset comes at disconnect.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    The reed stall warning works without electrical power; the vane type needs it. If an autopilot misbehaves, disconnect, do not overpower: overpowering an auto-trim autopilot trims the aeroplane further out of balance. Expect one question on how each stall warning senses the stall, one on a mode (HDG flies the bug, NAV tracks a course), one on the trim annunciator and one on the means of disconnecting.
