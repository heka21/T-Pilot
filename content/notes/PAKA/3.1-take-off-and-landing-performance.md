## What the exam expects (PAKA 3.1)

- **3.1.1** Say whether each of these increases or decreases the take-off distance, the landing distance and the take-off climb: the headwind or tailwind component, air temperature, QNH, density height in non-standard conditions, aerodrome elevation, runway slope, the surface (wet, dry or slushy), ground effect and wind shear, and frost on the aeroplane.
- **3.1.2** Tell pressure height and density height apart.
- **3.1.3** Use the altimeter on the ground to find the local QNH, the aerodrome's pressure height and its elevation.
- **3.1.4** Explain the maximum structural take-off and landing weights and the climb weight limit.
- **3.1.5** Say what is likely to happen when the aeroplane's weight limits are exceeded.

## Why this matters

As a private pilot you will carry passengers and bags, and you will use aerodromes your instructor never sent you to: a sloping farm strip near York after winter rain, Kalgoorlie on a 42 degree afternoon, a short coastal strip with a sea breeze swinging round. With four people, full fuel and a hot or wet strip, the generous margins of the training circuit can disappear without anything looking different from the cockpit. This subtopic brings together the RPL material in [BAKC 6.2](/notes/BAKC/6.2) (density height, altimeter use, weight limits), [RBKA 4.1](/notes/RBKA/4.1) (slope and surface) and [BAKC 6.4](/notes/BAKC/6.4) (the effects of overweight), and adds what the PPL needs: seeing all the factors at once, knowing how they stack, and knowing which weight limit governs today. CASA names density and pressure height, and the take-off and landing charts, as the most common PPLA errors, so the exam leans on exactly these skills.

## Three questions behind every factor

Before the list of factors, the one picture that makes all of them predictable. Every take-off and landing comes down to three questions.

**How fast must the aeroplane be going over the ground?** The wing lifts off and touches down at a fixed *indicated* airspeed. What the runway sees is the **groundspeed** at that moment, and the groundspeed depends on the wind and on the air density (thin air needs a higher true airspeed for the same indicated airspeed).

**How hard can it speed up or slow down?** On the take-off roll the accelerating force is propeller thrust minus drag minus rolling resistance, minus any part of the weight pulling back down a slope. On the landing roll the decelerating force is braking, drag and rolling resistance, plus or minus the slope. The **distance** is the energy to be gained or lost divided by that force, and because kinetic energy goes with the **square** of the groundspeed, a small change in groundspeed makes a big change in distance.

**Once airborne, how much power is left over to climb?** The **take-off climb** is set by the surplus of power available over power required. Thin air cuts power available; weight and drag raise power required.

Hold those three questions and every row of the factor table later is something you can work out rather than memorise.

## Pressure height and density height

You met both in [BAKC 6.2](/notes/BAKC/6.2); here is the distinction in one place, because the PPL exam asks you to state it and then use it.

**Pressure height** is the height in the International Standard Atmosphere (ISA) at which the pressure is the same as the pressure where you are. It is what the altimeter reads with **1013 hPa** set on the subscale. It is the right number for the airspace system: flight levels use pressure.

**Density height** is the height in the ISA at which the air has the same *density* as the air where you are. It is found by correcting the pressure height for temperature, at about **120 ft for every degree** the actual temperature differs from the ISA temperature at that pressure height. Density is what the wing, the propeller and the engine respond to, so **performance follows density height**.

The syllabus lists "density height (non-standard conditions)" as a factor of its own, and that is the key to the PPL view: temperature, QNH and elevation are three routes to the same thing. A low QNH raises the pressure height, high elevation raises it further, and a hot day lifts the density height above it. The aeroplane does not know which route brought the thin air. It only knows the density height.

The ISA temperature is 15 degrees at sea level and falls 2 degrees per 1,000 ft, so the two formulas are:

$$\text{pressure height} = \text{elevation} + (1013 - \text{QNH}) \times 30$$

$$\text{density height} = \text{pressure height} + 120 \times (T_\text{actual} - T_\text{ISA})$$

![Work out pressure height and density height for any aerodrome and day](widget:density-height "Heat is the big one: every degree above ISA adds about 120 ft of density height, while each hectopascal of low QNH adds only 30 ft.")

!!! example "Worked example: density height at a wheatbelt strip in summer"
    Given: elevation 1,000 ft, QNH 1008 hPa, temperature 36 degrees.

    1. Pressure height: $1000 + (1013 - 1008) \times 30 = 1000 + 150 = \mathbf{1{,}150\ ft}$.
    2. ISA temperature at 1,150 ft: $15 - 2 \times 1.15 = 15 - 2.3 = \mathbf{12.7}$ degrees.
    3. Deviation from ISA: $36 - 12.7 = \mathbf{23.3}$ degrees above.
    4. Density height: $1150 + 120 \times 23.3 = 1150 + 2796 = \mathbf{3{,}950\ ft}$ (3,946).

    Of the 2,950 ft between the elevation and the density height, the low QNH contributed 150 ft and the heat nearly 2,800 ft. At roughly 3 percent of power lost per 1,000 ft of density height, the engine is giving about 88 percent of its sea-level power.

Now the other direction. Try it before opening the solution: a frosty July morning at a strip with elevation 400 ft, QNH 1026 hPa, temperature 2 degrees. Is the density height above or below the elevation, and by roughly how much?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    Pressure height $= 400 + (1013 - 1026) \times 30 = 400 - 390 = 10$ ft. ISA temperature at 10 ft is 15 degrees. Deviation $= 2 - 15 = -13$ degrees. Density height $= 10 + 120 \times (-13) = 10 - 1560 =$ **about −1,550 ft**, nearly 2,000 ft *below* the elevation. Cold, high-pressure air is denser than standard, so the aeroplane performs better than it would at sea level on a standard day (if the frost is off the wings, which comes later in this lesson).

![How much power does a normally aspirated engine lose with height?](diagram:power-loss-with-altitude "Roughly 3 percent per 1,000 ft of density height; the climb, which lives on the surplus power, suffers most.")

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The performance charts in the CASA workbook are entered with **pressure height and temperature** as separate inputs, and they apply the density correction internally. If you work out the density height and then enter the chart with it *and* the temperature, you have corrected for temperature twice. For a chart, stop at the pressure height; for an "increase or decrease" question, think in density height.

??? check "Check yourself: two aerodromes have the same density height today, one high and cool, one low and hot. Which aeroplane needs the longer take-off run, if everything else is equal?"
    Neither: they need the same. Performance follows density height, not elevation or temperature separately, so the wing, propeller and engine behave identically in both places.

## Reading the heights off the altimeter

The altimeter is a barometer with an adjustable datum, the subscale. Whatever pressure is set on the subscale, the needle shows height above the level where that pressure exists. That lets one instrument give you three answers on the ground, which is the content of 3.1.3:

| To obtain | Set the subscale to | Read |
|---|---|---|
| Local QNH | The known aerodrome elevation on the dial | The QNH on the subscale |
| Pressure height | 1013 hPa | The pressure height on the dial |
| Elevation (and an accuracy check) | The aerodrome QNH | The elevation on the dial, within 100 ft for VFR |

The first row gives you a QNH at a strip with no weather service: wind the subscale until the needle shows the ERSA elevation, and the subscale holds the sea-level pressure that makes the altimeter tell the truth. The second row works because pressure height is, by definition, what the altimeter shows with standard pressure set. The third row is the reverse of the first and doubles as the pre-flight altimeter check: with an accurate aerodrome QNH set, a reading within **100 ft** of the elevation is acceptable for VFR (AIP ENR 1.7, as in [BAKC 6.2](/notes/BAKC/6.2); verify the current figure).

![Explore what the subscale setting does to the reading](widget:altimeter-subscale "About 30 ft per hectopascal: the same figure that turns QNH into pressure height.")

??? check "Check yourself: you set 1013 on the subscale at an aerodrome and the altimeter reads 1,420 ft. The elevation is 1,300 ft. Is today's QNH above or below 1013?"
    Below. The pressure height is 120 ft above the elevation, so the air is thinner than standard; 120 ft at 30 ft per hectopascal means a QNH about 4 hPa below 1013, around 1009.

## Wind: the headwind and tailwind component

Only the part of the wind along the runway, the **headwind or tailwind component**, affects the distances; the crosswind component is a control problem.

![Split any wind into headwind and crosswind components for a runway](widget:crosswind-component "Thirty degrees off the runway already puts half the wind across; the headwind component is what the chart wants.")

A **headwind** supplies part of the lift-off airspeed before the aeroplane has moved, so it lifts off at a lower groundspeed. Because the energy to be built up goes with the square of groundspeed, the saving is larger than the wind speed suggests. On landing, the same lower groundspeed means less energy for the brakes. Both distances **decrease**. A **tailwind** does the opposite and, because of that square, does it more strongly: both distances **increase markedly**. The usual rule of thumb is that a tailwind of 10 percent of the lift-off speed adds roughly 20 percent to the take-off distance.

For the climb, wind changes nothing about the rate shown on the VSI. It changes how much ground you cover while climbing. Into a headwind the **climb gradient over the ground is steeper**; with a tailwind it is **flatter**, and the fence and trees arrive sooner.

![Does wind change how fast or how steeply you climb?](diagram:climb-gradient-wind "Same rate of climb in every case; the headwind steepens the path over the ground, the tailwind flattens it.")

When you take a wind component to a chart, take it from the chart's own wind component table or your own calculation, never the full wind speed, and check whether the chart wants it entered as a headwind or a tailwind ([POPA 2.4](/notes/POPA/2.4)).

## Slope and surface: what the ground does

Once the air has had its say, the runway itself changes the forces on the roll. [RBKA 4.1](/notes/RBKA/4.1) teaches this in detail; here is the mechanism in brief.

### Runway slope

On a slope, a component of the aeroplane's weight acts along the runway: for real runway slopes, very nearly the weight times the slope as a fraction. On a **2 percent upslope**, about 2 percent of the weight pulls the aeroplane back. That sounds small, but the net accelerating force is itself only a fraction of the weight, so as a rule of thumb a 2 percent upslope can add **around 10 percent** to the take-off distance.

- **Upslope** lengthens the take-off (gravity fights the thrust) and **shortens** the landing (gravity helps the brakes).
- **Downslope** shortens the take-off and **lengthens** the landing, and the aeroplane tends to float as the runway falls away under the flare.

The take-off climb through the air is the same either way, because once airborne the slope no longer acts on the aeroplane. What changes is the ground beyond: taking off uphill you lift off later and the terrain usually keeps rising, so the climb is **reduced relative to the rising ground**; taking off downhill the ground falls away.

![Uphill or downhill?](diagram:runway-slope-take-off-and-landing "Taking off uphill, part of the thrust climbs the slope; landing uphill, the slope helps you stop.")

### Surface: dry, wet and slushy

The **dry sealed runway** is the baseline the charts are drawn for. Everything else is a correction to it.

A **wet sealed runway** changes grip far more than rolling resistance. A film of water lubricates the tyre on the bitumen, so braking is poorer and the **landing distance increases**, often by 15 percent or more. The thin film adds little drag to a rolling wheel, so the **take-off distance changes little**. Standing water can lift the tyre clear of the surface: **aquaplaning**, with almost no braking or steering.

**Wet grass** hurts both. Soft, wet ground makes the wheels sink and push through it, so rolling resistance rises and the **take-off distance increases**; the slippery surface spoils braking, so the **landing distance increases** too.

**Slush**, and by the same physics standing water or deep mud, is the worst surface of all. Pushing it aside and the spray hitting the airframe both cause drag that **grows with speed** while thrust falls, so the take-off distance **increases greatly** and the aeroplane may never reach lift-off speed. On landing it aquaplanes or barely grips: the landing distance **increases greatly**. Most light-aeroplane POHs give no slush figures, and that silence is the answer: do not operate.

![Slope and surface: longer or shorter?](diagram:runway-slope-and-surface-effects "Slope works in opposite directions for take-off and landing; wet and slushy surfaces lengthen both, and slush can stop you reaching lift-off speed.")

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Slope is the one factor that works **opposite ways** for take-off and landing. "Uphill is bad" is only half true: uphill lengthens the take-off but shortens the landing. And a wet sealed runway is not "worse for everything": it hurts the landing much more than the take-off.

## Ground effect and wind shear

### Ground effect

Within about one wingspan of the ground, the ground squeezes the wingtip vortices and the downwash behind the wing. **Induced drag** falls, and the wing produces the same lift at a lower angle of attack. That is **ground effect**, and it cuts both ways.

On take-off, the aeroplane will fly in ground effect at a speed and angle of attack at which it cannot fly clear of it. Pulled off the runway early, it lifts to 10 or 15 ft, climbs out of the cushion, meets the full induced drag, and either sinks back or hangs there unable to climb. That is why the aeroplane **may lift off early but not climb**, and why the **climb sags on leaving ground effect**. The protection is to accelerate to the POH speed before rotating, so that the aeroplane has the speed to keep climbing when the cushion is gone.

On landing, the reduced drag lets an aeroplane that arrives a few knots fast keep flying just above the runway: it **floats**, eating up landing distance.

### Wind shear

**Wind shear** is a change in wind speed or direction over a short distance. The danger for take-off and landing performance is a **sudden loss of headwind**. The aeroplane's inertia keeps its groundspeed for a moment, so the airspeed falls at once by the amount of headwind lost, and lift falls with it.

On the take-off roll, a dying headwind means a higher groundspeed is needed at lift-off: the take-off is **longer**. Just after lift-off it is worse: a **dangerous loss of airspeed and climb**, close to the ground and close to the stall, with the attitude unchanged. On approach the effect is **variable**: a loss of headwind makes the aeroplane sink and undershoot, a gain makes it float and land long. Either way the landing distance is no longer what the chart assumed.

![What happens on final when the headwind dies away near the ground?](diagram:wind-gradient-on-approach "The airspeed decays and the aeroplane sinks below the path with no change in attitude or power, unless the pilot adds power.")

In WA you meet it at the sea breeze front on the coastal plain and under thunderstorm outflow. The defences are a speed margin in gusty conditions, a hand on the throttle, and no take-off or landing with a storm nearby.

??? check "Check yourself: why can ground effect make an aeroplane lift off and then fail to climb?"
    In ground effect induced drag is lower, so the wing can support the aeroplane at a speed too low to fly clear of it. As the aeroplane rises out of the cushion the full induced drag returns, and at that low speed there is not enough surplus power to climb, so it sinks back or hangs just above the ground.

## Frost on the aeroplane

Frost looks harmless: a thin, sandpaper-like layer on a winter morning at Narrogin. But the first few per cent of the wing's chord, near the leading edge, are where the airflow is most sensitive to roughness. Frost there makes the boundary layer turbulent and lets it separate earlier, so the **maximum lift coefficient falls** and the **stall speed rises** ([RBKA 3.6](/notes/RBKA/3.6)). The same roughness adds drag.

The result: the aeroplane needs a higher speed to lift off and accelerates less well towards it, so the **take-off distance increases**, and it **may not lift off at all** at the normal rotation speed. Once airborne, less lift and more drag **reduce the climb**. For landing, frost is treated as not applicable: it forms on a parked aeroplane, and you will have removed it before you go. All frost comes off before flight, however thin.

The factors **stack**. A hot afternoon at a high, sloping, wet grass strip with a tailwind applies every penalty at once, and each correction multiplies the distance that the previous one produced. Two 10 percent penalties are not 20 percent; they are $1.1 \times 1.1 = 1.21$, 21 percent.

## Weight limits: structural and performance

Weight appears in all three questions: a heavier aeroplane needs a higher lift-off speed, accelerates more slowly towards it, and has less surplus power to climb. Its weight limits are of two different kinds.

**Maximum structural take-off weight** (MTOW) is the heaviest weight at which the airframe is certified to begin a take-off. **Maximum structural landing weight** (MLW) is the heaviest at which it is certified to land, where the undercarriage and its attachments take the touchdown loads. Both are fixed by the **strength of the structure**, published in the POH (section 2, limitations), and never change with the weather. In many light trainers MLW equals MTOW; in larger aeroplanes MLW is lower, so the aeroplane must burn fuel before it lands.

The **climb weight limit** is a **performance** limit. It is the heaviest weight at which the aeroplane can still meet the required take-off climb gradient at the density height of the day. On a cool morning at a sea-level aerodrome it is usually above MTOW and you never notice it. On a hot afternoon inland it can fall well below MTOW, and then it is the limit that applies. The workbook take-off weight chart gives it, read for the pressure height and temperature without the runway length correction.

![Where does a real chart give the climb weight limit?](workbook:fig5 "What to notice: the small panel at bottom left of the sideways page turns pressure height (lines for 4,000, 5,000 and 6,000 ft) and temperature into a climb limit weight, read on its own scale up to the 1,055 kg maximum.")

The weight you may actually take off at is the **lowest** of four numbers:

1. the structural MTOW;
2. the climb weight limit for today's pressure height and temperature;
3. the runway-length-limited weight from the take-off chart;
4. the weight that will keep the landing weight within MLW after the planned fuel burn.

!!! example "Worked example: which limit governs"
    Given, for a made-up aeroplane (illustrative figures, not from any chart): structural MTOW 1,150 kg; structural MLW 1,100 kg; today's climb weight limit 1,120 kg; runway-limited take-off weight 1,180 kg; planned fuel burn to the destination 70 kg.

    1. Landing-limited take-off weight: $1100 + 70 = \mathbf{1{,}170\ kg}$.
    2. The four limits: 1,150 (structural), 1,120 (climb), 1,180 (runway), 1,170 (landing).
    3. The lowest: **1,120 kg**, the climb weight limit.

    The runway is long enough and the structure could take more, but on this day the aeroplane could not make the required climb gradient any heavier. If the loading sheet says 1,140 kg, 20 kg has to come off, or you wait for cooler air.

![Which weight limit governs today?](diagram:weight-limits-lowest "What to notice: the take-off weight is the lowest of the four limits; on a hot day the climb weight limit, 1,120 kg here, can sit below the structural MTOW.")

How you find the chart figures is the subject of [POPA 2.4](/notes/POPA/2.4); the exam supplies the workbook charts, and the POH figures for your own aeroplane always take precedence over any rule of thumb.

??? check "Check yourself: why can the climb weight limit change during the day when the structural MTOW cannot?"
    The structural MTOW depends only on the airframe's strength. The climb weight limit depends on the surplus power available to climb, which falls as the density height rises, so it is lowest in the heat of the afternoon and highest on a cool morning.

## What happens when the limits are exceeded

An overweight aeroplane does not fail at a sharp boundary; everything gets worse at once.

**Performance.** The wing must make more lift at every speed, so the **stall speed rises** with the square root of the weight, and with it the lift-off, approach and touchdown speeds. A higher lift-off speed reached with less acceleration means a **longer take-off distance**; a faster touchdown with more mass to stop means a **longer landing distance**. With less surplus power, the **rate and gradient of climb fall**, perhaps below what the terrain beyond the runway needs. Cruise speed, range and manoeuvrability all fall.

**Structure.** The airframe's limit load factor (3.8 g for a normal category aeroplane) is a multiple of the certified weight. Above MTOW, the same structure carries a bigger load at every g, so turbulence or a steep turn can take it past what it was certified for. Above MLW, a firm touchdown can damage the **undercarriage** and its attachments, and **tyre and brake** limits can be exceeded.

**Legality.** Flying above a published limit breaches the regulations.

And if the load is in the wrong place as well as too heavy, an **out-of-limits CG** adds control and stability problems on top: a forward CG may leave too little elevator to flare; an aft CG reduces stability and may make stall and spin recovery impossible ([BAKC 6.4](/notes/BAKC/6.4)).

!!! example "Worked example: what 8 percent overweight costs"
    Given: POH stall speed 50 kt at MTOW; the aeroplane is loaded 8 percent above MTOW; normal category limit load factor 3.8 g.

    1. Stall speed: $50 \times \sqrt{1.08} = 50 \times 1.039 = \mathbf{52\ kt}$.
    2. Ground roll (rule of thumb: roughly proportional to weight squared, because the lift-off speed squared rises with weight and the acceleration falls with it): $1.08^2 = 1.166$, about **17 percent longer**.
    3. The g at which the structure meets its design load: $3.8 \div 1.08 = \mathbf{3.5\ g}$.

    Eight percent does not sound much, but every margin has shrunk at once: a longer run, a faster stall, a weaker climb and a structure that reaches its design load in less turbulence. The POH has no figures for this weight, so the charts can no longer tell you whether the runway is long enough.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    "It still flew, so it was fine" is the trap. The charts and structural limits only describe the aeroplane up to its limits; above them you have no data, and the extra take-off distance grows faster than the extra weight.

??? check "Check yourself: why is exceeding the maximum landing weight mostly a structural problem rather than a performance one?"
    Because MLW is set by the loads the undercarriage and its attachments can take at touchdown. Landing above it lengthens the landing a little, but the real risk is damage to the undercarriage and structure in a firm landing.

## Bringing it together

Late January, and you plan to fly three friends out of a 900 m grass strip near Northam at three in the afternoon. The strip slopes up 1 percent towards the west, the AWIS at a nearby aerodrome gives 40 degrees and QNH 1007, and the wind is a light easterly. The air first: the low QNH lifts the pressure height a little above the elevation, and the heat puts the density height thousands of feet higher. The wind: the easterly is a headwind towards the east, which is downhill, so wind and slope agree. The surface: dry grass, a grass correction but not a wet one. The weights: four people and full tanks put you close to MTOW, and at 40 degrees the chart's climb weight limit is below your loaded weight. The runway would do; the climb would not. The fix is weight, not technique: less fuel (with the fuel plan redone), one person fewer, or leave early tomorrow, when 18 degrees takes thousands of feet off the density height.

??? check "Check yourself: on a hot day you take off downhill with a light tailwind rather than uphill into a light headwind. What does each choice do to the take-off distance and to obstacle clearance?"
    Downhill helps the acceleration and the ground falls away, but the tailwind lengthens the run and flattens the climb gradient over the ground. Uphill into wind, the headwind shortens the run and steepens the gradient, but the slope lengthens the run and the ground keeps rising. Work both through the chart: the light-wind convention is take off downhill, but only the numbers decide.

## Key points

- **3.1.1** Headwind shortens both distances and steepens the climb over the ground; tailwind lengthens both markedly. Heat, low QNH and high elevation all act through density height: longer distances, poorer climb. Upslope lengthens the take-off and shortens the landing; downslope the reverse. Wet sealed hurts the landing, wet grass both, slush both greatly. Ground effect lets you lift off before you can climb and makes you float. Loss of headwind drops the airspeed. Frost raises the stall speed and may prevent lift-off. The factors multiply.
- **3.1.2** Pressure height: ISA height with the same pressure, the altimeter reading at 1013. Density height: ISA height with the same density, pressure height plus 120 ft per degree above ISA. Performance follows density height.
- **3.1.3** Altimeter on the ground: set elevation, read QNH; set 1013, read pressure height; set QNH, read elevation, within 100 ft for VFR (verify).
- **3.1.4** Structural MTOW and MLW are fixed by the airframe. The climb weight limit is the heaviest weight that still meets the required climb gradient today. Take off at no more than the lowest of structural, climb, runway and landing-limited weights.
- **3.1.5** Overweight: higher stall and approach speeds, longer take-off and landing, poorer climb, reduced cruise, range and manoeuvrability, structure and undercarriage loaded beyond certification, tyre and brake limits exceeded, and illegal; an out-of-limits CG adds control problems.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    PPL questions here are usually "increase or decrease" tables and the density height calculation. CASA names density and pressure height, and the take-off and landing charts, as the most common PPLA errors, so practise the [BAKC 6.2](/notes/BAKC/6.2) method until it is automatic and then work the workbook charts in [POPA 2.4](/notes/POPA/2.4). Have by heart: 30 ft per hectopascal, 120 ft per degree, ISA 15 degrees falling 2 per 1,000 ft, and that slope is the only factor that works opposite ways for take-off and landing.
