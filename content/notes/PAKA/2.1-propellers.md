## What the exam expects (PAKA 2.1)

- **2.1.1** Give the reasons a fixed-pitch propeller can overspeed, and the action you take when it does.

## Why this matters

Most training aeroplanes in WA, from the Cessna 152s and 172s at Jandakot to the Jabirus and Tecnams on farm strips, drive a fixed-pitch propeller. In one of them the tachometer needle does not stay where you put it: lower the nose to descend off the top of the Darling Scarp towards the Jandakot inbound points without touching the throttle and the RPM creeps up, sometimes past the red line, while you are busy with the radio and the lookout. Nothing has broken; the propeller is doing exactly what its geometry says it must. Once you see why, overspeed stops being a fact to memorise and becomes something you prevent with your left hand before you move the control column. The exam asks for the causes, the cure and why a fine-pitch propeller reaches the limit sooner, and it likes to offer "add power" or "steepen the descent" as distractors. This lesson builds the propeller up from the blade, then explains overspeed, the remedy and, for when you move on to a constant-speed aeroplane, how a governor changes the picture.

## A propeller blade is a rotating wing

You already know from [BAKC 3.1](/notes/BAKC/3.1) that the propeller turns engine torque into thrust and that each blade is a small wing whose "lift" points forward. Take that literally, because everything about overspeed follows from it.

Cut a blade across, a little way out from the hub, and you see an aerofoil section with a chord line, just like a slice through the wing. The angle between that chord line and the **plane of rotation** (the flat disc the propeller sweeps, at right angles to the crankshaft) is the **blade angle**. On a **fixed-pitch propeller** the blade angle is carved or forged in at manufacture and never changes in flight. The word **pitch** is the same idea expressed as a distance: the distance the propeller would move forward in one turn if it screwed through the air like a bolt through a nut, with no slip. A large blade angle is **coarse pitch**; a small one is **fine pitch**.

A wing's angle of attack is measured against the **relative airflow**, the air coming at it, and the same is true of a blade. But a blade section is doing two things at once. It is going **round**, at a speed set by the RPM and how far out from the hub it is, and it is going **forward**, at the aeroplane's true airspeed. Put the two together and each blade section traces a corkscrew through the air, a **helical path**. The relative airflow comes from exactly opposite to that path. The angle between the helical path and the plane of rotation is the **helix angle** (sometimes called the angle of advance), and the blade's **angle of attack** is what is left over:

$$\text{blade angle of attack} = \text{blade angle} - \text{helix angle}$$

This is exactly the subtraction you met for the wing, where the angle of attack is the pitch attitude less the angle of the flight path. The blade angle plays the part of the attitude, and the helix angle plays the part of the flight path.

![Is angle of attack the same as attitude? The blade works the same way](diagram:angle-of-attack-vs-attitude "Angle of attack is the attitude less the flight path angle; for a blade, read blade angle for attitude and helix angle for the flight path.")

Now picture the two speeds as the sides of a triangle. The rotational speed is the long side, pointing along the plane of rotation; the forward speed is the short side, pointing along the line of flight. The helical path is the diagonal. Make the forward speed bigger, with the rotational speed unchanged, and the diagonal swings forward: the helix angle grows. The blade angle is fixed, so the angle of attack must shrink. Make the forward speed smaller and the opposite happens. **On a fixed-pitch propeller, the angle of attack of the blades falls as airspeed rises and rises as airspeed falls.**

One more piece of geometry. A point near the tip travels a much bigger circle each turn than a point near the hub, so it moves round much faster, and its helix angle is smaller. To keep every part of the blade working at a sensible angle of attack, the blade angle is made large near the hub and small near the tip. That is the **twist** you can see if you sight along a propeller blade on the pre-flight. The blade angle quoted for a propeller is the angle at a reference station, often about three-quarters of the way out.

![Why is a propeller blade twisted?](diagram:propeller-blade-twist "What to notice: every section moves forward at the same speed but round faster towards the tip, so the helical path flattens and the blade angle is made smaller to keep the angle of attack about the same.")

!!! example "Worked example: what a change of airspeed does to the blade's angle of attack"
    Given: a point three-quarters of the way out along a typical trainer propeller blade is moving round at about 345 kt at 2,400 RPM. Suppose its blade angle there is 21 degrees. Compare 80 kt (a climb) and 120 kt (a fast descent), at the instant the speed changes and before the RPM has had time to respond.

    1. Helix angle at 80 kt: $\tan^{-1}(80 / 345) = \mathbf{13°}$.
    2. Angle of attack at 80 kt: $21° - 13° = \mathbf{8°}$.
    3. Helix angle at 120 kt: $\tan^{-1}(120 / 345) = \mathbf{19°}$.
    4. Angle of attack at 120 kt: $21° - 19° = \mathbf{2°}$.

    The same blade, at the same RPM, has gone from working hard at 8 degrees to barely working at 2 degrees. The blade angle of 21 degrees is illustrative; the point is the direction and the size of the change, which is the same for any fixed-pitch propeller.

![What happens to the blade's angle of attack as the airspeed changes?](widget:propeller-angle-of-attack "What to notice: the blade angle is fixed, so as the helical path swings forward with airspeed the angle of attack shrinks: 8° at 80 kt, 2° at 120 kt.")

??? check "Check yourself: on a fixed-pitch propeller, what happens to the blade's angle of attack when the aeroplane accelerates at constant RPM, and why?"
    It decreases. The forward speed is one side of the triangle that sets the helix angle; more forward speed makes the helix angle bigger, and with a fixed blade angle the angle of attack is the blade angle less the helix angle.

## Why the RPM follows the airspeed

The engine and the propeller are bolted together, so they always turn at the same speed. That speed settles wherever two twisting forces balance. The engine pushes the crankshaft round with its **torque**, set mainly by the throttle. The propeller resists being turned, because each blade makes drag as well as thrust, and that drag acting at a radius is a resisting torque, the **propeller torque** or **load**. When engine torque equals propeller torque the RPM is steady. When the engine's torque is the bigger, the combination speeds up; when the propeller's is bigger, it slows down.

The propeller's resisting torque depends on how hard its blades are working, which is to say on their angle of attack. A blade at a high angle of attack makes plenty of thrust and plenty of drag, and takes a lot of torque to turn. A blade at a low angle of attack makes little of either and turns easily.

Put that together with the triangle from the last section:

- **Lower the nose and accelerate** with the throttle where it was. The helix angle grows, the blade angle of attack falls, the propeller absorbs less torque, and the engine, now meeting less resistance, speeds up. The RPM climbs until the higher rotational speed has steepened the triangle back enough to restore the balance, at a higher RPM than before.
- **Raise the nose and slow down**, throttle untouched. The helix angle shrinks, the blade bites harder, the propeller absorbs more torque and the RPM falls.

That is why, in an aeroplane with a fixed-pitch propeller, **the RPM rises in a dive and falls in a climb with the throttle untouched**. Push the speed high enough and the airflow starts to drive the propeller rather than the propeller driving the air: the blades go to a zero or negative angle of attack and the propeller **windmills** the engine, as it does in a glide with the throttle closed.

It is also why the tachometer is only a true power gauge at a steady airspeed. [RBKA 2.1](/notes/RBKA/2.1) uses that to read mixture and carburettor ice off the tachometer in steady cruise; this lesson is the other side of the same coin, where the airspeed changes and the RPM changes with it.

### Fine pitch and coarse pitch: the compromise

Because the blade angle cannot change, the manufacturer chooses one angle for the whole flight, and it is always a compromise.

A **fine-pitch** propeller (often sold as a "climb" propeller) has a small blade angle. At low speed, on the take-off run and in the climb, its blades are at an efficient angle of attack and do not overload the engine, so the engine can reach its full rated RPM and full power: good take-off and climb. But at cruise speed the helix angle has grown, the blades are at a very small angle of attack, and the propeller absorbs little torque. The engine runs up to high RPM at a modest throttle opening, and the red line arrives quickly as the speed builds.

A **coarse-pitch** ("cruise") propeller has a larger blade angle. In the cruise its blades are at a good angle of attack, so it turns the engine's power into thrust efficiently at a lower RPM: a faster, quieter, more economical cruise. But on the take-off run and in the climb its blades are at a high angle of attack, the load is heavy, the engine cannot reach full RPM, and it cannot develop full power: a longer take-off and a poorer climb.

The point for this subtopic: a fine-pitch propeller sits closer to the red line at any given airspeed, so it **overspeeds sooner** as the airspeed rises.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    "The propeller overspeeds because the engine is making too much power" is only half the story, and the exam's distractors build on it. With the throttle untouched the engine's torque is about the same; what has changed is that the propeller, at a higher airspeed, is absorbing less of it. That is why raising the nose (reducing airspeed) is part of the cure, not only closing the throttle.

??? check "Check yourself: why does a fine-pitch propeller reach its RPM limit sooner in a descent than a coarse-pitch one?"
    Its blade angle is smaller, so at any airspeed its blades are at a lower angle of attack and absorb less torque. The engine already runs at a higher RPM for a given throttle setting, and as the airspeed rises the angle of attack falls further, so the red line is reached at a lower airspeed.

## What an overspeed does to the engine and the propeller

The **red line** on the tachometer is the maximum RPM in the POH limitations section, and it protects two things.

The engine. Every piston, connecting rod and valve is stopped and started twice a revolution, and the force needed to do that rises with the **square of the RPM**: 10 percent more RPM is about 21 percent more load ([BAKC 3.3](/notes/BAKC/3.3)). Above the red line those loads can overstress the **crankshaft, connecting rods and valve gear**, and the valves may fail to close in time.

The propeller. Its tips are the fastest-moving part of the aeroplane. The tip's speed through the air combines its rotational speed with the aeroplane's forward speed, so it rises both with RPM and with airspeed, exactly the two things that rise together in a dive. As the tip speed approaches the **speed of sound**, shock waves form on the blade, its efficiency drops, the noise rises and the vibration and stresses grow. The **centrifugal load** trying to pull each blade out of the hub also rises with the square of the RPM. A propeller is designed with a margin for all of this at the red line, not beyond it.

An overspeed is therefore not "free power". It is damage accumulating in parts you cannot see, which is why an exceedance has to be recorded and may need an inspection.

## The causes of a fixed-pitch overspeed

Every cause on the list is a version of one idea: **high power combined with high airspeed**, or a propeller that is absorbing less torque than it should.

1. **Descending or diving with high power.** The commonest cause by far: you start a cruise descent and lower the nose without first reducing power. The airspeed rises, the blades unload, and the RPM follows.
2. **High power at high airspeed in level flight.** Full throttle at low altitude in a fast aeroplane, particularly with a fine-pitch ("climb") propeller, can take the RPM past the red line without any descent at all. Many POHs limit full throttle at low level for this reason.
3. **Recovering from a spiral dive or an unusual attitude with power still on.** The nose is low, the airspeed is high and rising, and if the throttle has not been closed the RPM goes with it. This is one reason the spiral dive recovery in [RBKA 3.6](/notes/RBKA/3.6) begins with the throttle closed.
4. **A fine-pitch propeller** fitted for climb performance, which reaches red-line RPM sooner as the speed builds (the compromise above).
5. **Loss of a propeller blade tip or other propeller damage.** A damaged blade absorbs less torque, so the RPM rises, and it is out of balance, so there is **severe vibration** as well. This one is a mechanical failure, not a handling error, and the vibration is the clue.

??? check "Check yourself: you are in a steady cruise and the RPM rises sharply with severe vibration, though the airspeed has not changed. What is the likely cause, and how is it different from the usual overspeed?"
    Propeller damage such as a lost blade tip. The damaged propeller absorbs less torque and is out of balance. The usual overspeed comes from airspeed rising with power on and has no vibration. Here the airspeed is steady, and the vibration says something has broken: reduce power at once and plan an early landing.

## Remedial action

The cure follows directly from the cause. If the RPM is high because the engine's torque is not being absorbed, either reduce the torque or make the propeller absorb more of it. In practice you do both.

1. **Reduce power.** Close the throttle as far as needed to bring the RPM back below the red line. This is the first and fastest action, and it works at any airspeed.
2. **Reduce airspeed.** Raise the nose, smoothly and without overstressing the aeroplane, to slow down. Lower airspeed means a smaller helix angle, a higher blade angle of attack and more load on the propeller, so the RPM falls further.
3. **Re-establish a normal attitude and power**, and in future **reduce power before descending** so the RPM stays within the green arc.
4. **Record it.** If the red line was exceeded, record the exceedance on the maintenance release. The POH or maintenance manual may require an inspection, depending on how far and for how long the limit was exceeded.

Two things to notice about the order. Power comes off first because it is immediate and safe. The nose comes up second and gently, because a sharp pull at high speed raises the load factor and can overstress the airframe or, if the speed is in the yellow arc, put you outside the structural limits. And recording the exceedance is not optional good manners: the next pilot is entitled to know.

The best remedy is the one you never need. **Throttle back, then lower the nose** is the habit that prevents almost every fixed-pitch overspeed.

!!! example "Worked example: starting a cruise descent"
    Given: cruising at 2,350 RPM; red line 2,700 RPM. You want a 500 ft/min descent.

    1. The wrong way: lower the nose without touching the throttle. As the speed increases from **100 kt to 120 kt**, the RPM climbs towards **2,700 RPM**, and you are at the red line before you have finished your radio call.
    2. The right way: **reduce power first to about 2,100 RPM**, then lower the nose.
    3. As the speed increases, the RPM rises again, but from the lower starting point: it settles back **near the cruise value** instead of at the red line.

    You descend at the speed you wanted and the RPM stays in the green arc throughout. The figures here are for illustration; your POH gives the cruise and descent power settings for your type.

Now try one before opening the solution. You are recovering from a spiral dive in a fixed-pitch aeroplane. The ASI is high and rising and the tachometer is just past the red line. What do you do, in what order, and what do you write down after landing?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    Close the throttle first: it stops the RPM rising and stops the power adding to the speed. Then roll the wings level with aileron and coordinated rudder, and only then ease out of the dive gently, which also reduces the airspeed and loads the propeller. Restore power once the speed is under control. After landing, record the RPM exceedance (how far over and for how long, as well as you can tell) on the maintenance release so it can be assessed against the POH or maintenance manual.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    "Increase power to recover" and "steepen the descent to get through it" are the distractors. Both make an overspeed worse. The answer always contains **less power and less airspeed**.

## A constant-speed propeller: how a governor changes the picture

Sooner or later you will fly an aeroplane with a **constant-speed propeller**, with a blue propeller lever beside the throttle and a manifold pressure gauge on the panel. The element only asks about fixed pitch, but seeing what the governor does makes the fixed-pitch behaviour clearer, and the exam occasionally contrasts the two.

A constant-speed propeller has blades that can rotate in the hub to change the blade angle in flight. The pilot does not set the blade angle directly. Instead the propeller lever sets an **RPM**, and a **governor** on the engine adjusts the blade angle continuously to hold that RPM whatever the airspeed and throttle are doing.

The governor works on a simple balance:

- A pair of **flyweights** is spun by the engine. The faster the engine turns, the further the flyweights swing outwards.
- A **speeder spring** pushes down on the flyweights. The propeller lever sets how hard it pushes: lever forward, more spring force, higher RPM selected.
- Between them sits a **pilot valve** that directs engine oil, boosted to a higher pressure by the governor's own pump, into or out of the propeller hub, where it moves a piston that turns the blades.

Now repeat the descent. You lower the nose and the airspeed rises. The blade angle of attack starts to fall, just as on a fixed-pitch propeller, and the RPM starts to rise. But as soon as it does, the flyweights swing outwards against the spring, the pilot valve moves, and oil flows to turn the blades to a **coarser** angle. The coarser blade angle restores the angle of attack, the propeller absorbs more torque again, and the RPM comes back to the setting. Slow down and the reverse happens: the RPM starts to sag, the flyweights fall in, the blades go **finer**, and the RPM recovers. The pilot sees a steady tachometer and reads power from the **manifold pressure** gauge instead.

![How does the governor hold the RPM when the airspeed rises?](diagram:constant-speed-governor "What to notice: a rising RPM swings the flyweights out, lifts the pilot valve and sends oil to the hub, turning the blades coarser until the RPM is back at the setting.")

So an increase in airspeed is met by a coarser blade angle, not more RPM. A constant-speed propeller can still overspeed, in three ways:

- **The governor fails** or its oil supply is lost. On many single-engine aeroplanes oil pressure drives the blades towards **coarse** pitch, and a spring and the blades' own aerodynamic and centrifugal twisting forces drive them towards **fine**. Lose the oil pressure and the blades go to fine pitch, which is where a fixed-pitch propeller overspeeds. Check your POH, because the design varies: on many twins the arrangement is reversed so that a failed propeller goes coarse and feathers.
- **The blades reach a pitch stop.** The blade angle can only travel between a fine stop and a coarse stop. In a high-speed dive the governor may have coarsened the blades all the way to the **coarse stop**; beyond that it has nothing left to give, the propeller behaves like a fixed-pitch one, and the RPM rises with the airspeed.
- **A rapid throttle opening** can momentarily outrun the governor, giving a brief overspeed until the blades catch up.

The remedial actions rest on the same principle: **throttle back, slow down, and pull the propeller lever back** to select a lower RPM. If oil pressure is falling, the overspeed is a symptom of a bigger problem, and the POH emergency procedure for your type applies.

??? check "Check yourself: in a constant-speed aeroplane, what does the governor do when you lower the nose, and why does the RPM stay steady?"
    The rising airspeed starts to unload the blades and the RPM starts to rise; the flyweights swing out, the pilot valve sends oil to coarsen the blades, the angle of attack and the load are restored, and the RPM returns to the setting. The governor turns a would-be RPM change into a blade angle change.

## Bringing it together

You are returning to Jandakot from a navigation exercise to York in a Cessna 172 with a fixed-pitch propeller, cruising at 4,500 ft at 2,350 RPM. Approaching the scarp you need to be down to the inbound altitude before the reporting point, and the ATIS, the frequency change and a traffic call all arrive at once. This is the moment the RPM gets away: the easy thing is to push the nose down and deal with the radio. Trace what happens. The airspeed builds, the helix angle grows, the blades unload, and the RPM rises with every knot. If a little turbulence over the scarp has you holding the speed in the yellow arc as well, you are close to the red line and above the speed for rough air. The lesson's answer is in the order of your hands: throttle back to the descent power first, then lower the nose, then the radio. If you do find the needle past the red line, power off and nose gently up, and the exceedance goes on the maintenance release when you shut down.

## Key points

- **2.1.1** A fixed-pitch blade's angle of attack is its fixed blade angle less the helix angle; more airspeed means a bigger helix angle, a smaller angle of attack, less torque absorbed, and higher RPM. Overspeed comes from high power with high airspeed: diving or descending with cruise power, full throttle at high speed (especially with a fine-pitch propeller), recovering from a spiral dive or unusual attitude with power on, or propeller damage (with severe vibration). The remedy: reduce power, reduce airspeed by raising the nose smoothly, then reduce power before descending in future, and record any exceedance on the maintenance release. A constant-speed propeller's governor coarsens the blades instead, and overspeeds only if the governor or its oil fails, the blades reach a stop, or the throttle outruns it.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    In a fixed-pitch aeroplane, RPM follows airspeed. If the RPM rises towards the red line in a descent, the answer is throttle back and raise the nose, never more power or a steeper dive. Expect a question on why a fine-pitch propeller overspeeds sooner (smaller blade angle, lower angle of attack, less load at any speed) and one on what to do after an exceedance (record it; an inspection may be required).
