## What the exam expects (PNVC 2.5)

- **2.5.1** Explain the principles of map reading: how to orient the chart, when to read from map to ground and when from ground to map, which position lines give groundspeed, track error and a fix, and which ground features to use at low level (500 ft AGL), between about 2,000 and 10,000 ft, and over mountainous, coastal, densely populated and sparsely populated country.
- **2.5.2** Prepare a chart: draw tracks, track error lines and time and distance marks, and select the WACs and AIP visual charts for a given route.
- **2.5.3** Work from a planned track and in-flight data: find the track made good, calculate drift, alter heading to parallel track, to intercept track at a chosen point and to hold it once regained, revise ETAs from the latest groundspeed or by time and distance proportion, establish a DR position, and solve problems mentally (the 1 in 60 rule, revised estimates, and the track and time interval to a diversion point).

## Why this matters

Out past Northam the wheatbelt's paddocks, salt lakes and gravel roads all look alike from 4,500 ft, and the forecast wind is rarely the wind you get. This subtopic teaches you to notice early that you have drifted from the plan, by how much, and to correct it with mental arithmetic. CASA names time and distance work and the revision of estimates as weak areas in the PPLA exam, and the 1 in 60 rule is examined with numbers every time. It is all one idea, small angles and simple proportion, used over and over.

## Holding the chart and reading it (2.5.1 a and b)

You are tracking east from Northam with the chart north-up on your knee. A road appears ahead and right; on the chart "ahead" is to the right of the page and "right" is towards the bottom, so every feature must be rotated in your head, and under workload that is where mistakes creep in.

So turn the chart. **Orienting the chart** means holding it track-up, so the track line points the way the aeroplane is going over the ground. Now a feature ahead and right of the aeroplane is ahead and right of your position on the chart. Rotate the chart with you at each turn; upside-down printing on a southbound leg does not matter, the shapes do.

**Map to ground** starts on the chart. You know where you are, so you pick the next feature the chart says you should see, decide when (from groundspeed and distance), where (left, right or ahead) and what it should look like, then look outside for it. Looking for one thing in one place at one time is fast and reliable. It is the normal method: after every fix to choose the next checkpoint, whenever you are confident of your position, and when approaching a turning point or the destination.

**Ground to map** starts outside: you see a feature and search the chart for it. This is slower and error-prone, because one dam, bend or silo looks very like the next. Use it only when you need to:

- when you are uncertain of your position or lost;
- when map-to-ground has failed and the expected feature has not appeared;
- after a period of distraction, a diversion around weather, or a turn you did not time;
- when an unexpectedly prominent feature appears and you want to identify it.

Even then, start from your **DR position** (your best estimate from heading, groundspeed and time, explained later), search around it, and confirm any match with a second feature.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The classic error is "making the ground fit the map": you expect a town with a railway, a town with a railway appears, and you accept it without checking the shape of the lake beside it or the angle of the road. Confirm every fix with at least two independent features, and be most suspicious when a fix is exactly what you hoped to see.

??? check "Check yourself: you were busy with a radio call and a fuel tank change for ten minutes and now nothing outside looks familiar. Which method of map reading do you use, and where on the chart do you start?"
    Ground to map, because you are no longer sure of your position. Start from your DR position (last fix plus groundspeed times the time elapsed, along the track you were making good) and search outward from there, confirming any match with a second feature.

## Position lines: groundspeed, track error and a fix (2.5.1 c)

A **position line** is any line on which you know you are, even if you do not yet know where along it: the railway you are crossing, the line at right angles to your heading through a homestead off the wingtip, the coast directly below. The art is choosing the right line for the question you are asking.

**For groundspeed you want a line across the track.** Groundspeed is distance along track divided by time, so you need the exact moment you reached two points a known distance apart. A road, railway or river crossing the track at about **90 degrees** gives a sharp moment of crossing that does not move if you are a little left or right. A line crossing at a shallow angle is poor: a small error left or right moves the crossing point a long way along track.

!!! example "Worked example: groundspeed from two crossing features"
    Given: you cross a railway at right angles to track at 1012 and a highway, also across track, at 1024. The chart shows them 21 nm apart along the track.

    1. Time between them: $1024 - 1012 = \mathbf{12\ min}$.
    2. Groundspeed: $\dfrac{21\ \text{nm}}{12\ \text{min}} \times 60 = \mathbf{105\ kt}$.

    If you planned 115 kt, you now know the wind is stronger on the nose than forecast, and every estimate further down the leg will be late.

**For track error you want a line along the track.** A railway, long straight road or coastline roughly **parallel to the track** shows at a glance how far left or right you are and whether that is growing. It says nothing about groundspeed, because you could be anywhere along it.

**For a fix you want lines that cross.** A **fix** is a definite position: two position lines crossing near 90 degrees, so a small error in either moves the fix only a little. Lines crossing at 20 degrees give a long thin region of uncertainty instead of a point. Three lines rarely meet exactly; they form a small triangle, the **cocked hat**, and you take its centre. The quickest fix is a single unmistakable **point feature**: a distinctive town, a lake with an island, a road and railway junction.

??? check "Check yourself: a railway runs 5 degrees off your track for 30 nm. What can it tell you, and what can it not?"
    Being almost parallel to track, it is an excellent track-error line: you can see how far off track you are and whether the error is growing. It cannot give a groundspeed, because the moment of "reaching" a line you are flying alongside is not defined.

## Choosing features for height and terrain (2.5.1 d)

Two things change with height: how far you can see, and how much detail survives.

At **low level (500 ft AGL)** the view is close and fast: at 100 kt a feature 2 nm ahead arrives in just over a minute, and a town cannot be seen whole. Choose small, close features before flight, at short intervals. Minimum-height rules apply (see [RFRC 2.3](/notes/RFRC/2.3)).

Between **2,000 and 10,000 ft** you see far but lose small detail in haze, so large features work best; in WA, salt lakes, whose shapes are a signature.

Terrain and population change the picture again:

| Situation | Use | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Low level (500 ft AGL) | Small, close features: roads, creeks, fence lines, homesteads, single hills, pre-selected at short intervals | Large features you cannot see whole |
| 2,000 to 10,000 ft | Large features: towns, lakes, coastline, major roads and railways, ranges, salt lakes | Small features and anything hidden by haze |
| Mountainous terrain | Valleys, river systems, prominent peaks, passes, the shape of ridges | Individual hills that all look the same; the apparent height of terrain is misleading |
| Coastal | The shape of the coast, river mouths, headlands, islands, lighthouses, coastal towns | Flying out of sight of the coast |
| Densely populated | Major highways, railways, large water bodies, airports | Individual suburbs and minor roads |
| Sparsely populated (the WA interior) | Salt lakes, creek lines, the few roads, the vermin fence, mines, ranges; DR and time | Uncharted tracks and bores |

The exam asks for the reasons. In **mountains** peaks look alike from different angles, but the pattern of valleys and rivers does not change. Along the **coast** the shoreline is a ready-made track-error line, which is why a route such as Jandakot to Lancelin keeps it in view. Over **Perth's suburbs** minor detail merges into one grey sheet and only the river, freeway, railways and airports stand out. In the **interior** the chart shows few features and some on the ground (new tracks, bores) are not charted at all, so you lean on DR and the clock.

![Which chart features can you rely on?](diagram:chart-legend-symbols "Contours, lakes, railways with cross ticks and towns are the symbols you will match most often; intermittent rivers are drawn dashed because they are often dry.")

??? check "Check yourself: why do salt lakes make better checkpoints than homesteads when you are at 5,500 ft over the eastern wheatbelt?"
    At that height small features such as homesteads are lost in detail and haze, and many look alike. Salt lakes are large, distinctive in outline and in their relationship to each other, and do not change, so their shape identifies them uniquely.

## Preparing the chart (2.5.2)

A good chart answers "how far off track?" and "how far have I come?" at a glance.

**The track.** Each leg as a bold straight line between turning points, magnetic track and distance written beside it clear of features.

**Track error lines.** From the departure point (and each turning point) draw two thin lines at **10 degrees** either side of track, and two more at 10 degrees into the destination (and the next turning point). These **fan lines** are a built-in protractor: a fix's place between the departure lines shows the **track error** by eye (halfway to the line is about 5 degrees), and its place between the destination lines shows the **closing angle**. Both are explained below.

**Distance and time marks.** Mark every **10 nm** from departure and the **halfway point**. Some pilots add time marks every **6 minutes** (a tenth of an hour).

**Climb, descent and safety.** Mark the **TOPC and TOPD** (from [PNVC 2.4](/notes/PNVC/2.4)), the leg's **lowest safe altitude** (highest terrain or obstacle plus a margin), and nearby CTA steps and PRD areas.

**Selecting the charts.** Each chart in the AIP visual chart series answers a different question:

- The **WAC** (World Aeronautical Chart, 1:1,000,000) is the main cross-country chart: topography, towns, roads, railways and aerodromes. The WAC index on the PCA shows which sheets the route crosses; near a sheet edge you need both.
- The **VNC** (Visual Navigation Chart, 1:500,000) adds controlled airspace, PRD areas and frequencies: needed wherever the route passes near controlled airspace.
- The **VTC** (Visual Terminal Chart, 1:250,000) covers terminal areas such as Perth: VFR routes, approach points and CTA steps.
- **ERSA** goes with every chart, for every aerodrome on the route and for the PRD areas.

![Which chart shows what?](diagram:chart-features-panel "The WAC has the topography but no airspace; the VNC and VTC add airspace at larger scales; the ERC-L has airspace and navigation aids but no topography.")

Jandakot to Merredin, for example, needs the Perth VTC for the departure, the VNC for the airspace east of Perth, the wheatbelt WAC sheet or sheets, and ERSA.

## Track made good, track error and drift (2.5.3 a and b)

You set a heading that, with the forecast wind, should have kept you on track. Twenty minutes later a fix shows you are not where the plan said. Three terms describe what happened.

The **track made good (TMG)** is the line from your departure point (or last fix) to your present fix: the path actually flown over the ground. The **track error (TE)** is the angle between the planned track and the TMG. The **drift** is the angle between the heading flown and the TMG: what the real wind did to you. Heading 085 and TMG 084 means 1 degree of left drift.

![What is the difference between heading, track and drift?](diagram:heading-track-drift "The nose points along the heading but the wind carries the aeroplane sideways; the line over the ground is the track, and the angle between them is the drift.")

Keep track error and drift apart by what they compare: track error compares **plan with reality** (planned track against TMG); drift compares **nose with ground** (heading against TMG).

## The 1 in 60 rule: why it works

You need the track error in degrees, in the air, without a protractor. The **1 in 60 rule** does it: **an error of 1 nm in 60 nm subtends an angle of 1 degree**.

Picture a circle of radius 60 nm around your departure point. Its circumference, $2\pi \times 60 \approx 377$ nm, is shared among 360 degrees, so each degree is

$$\frac{377\ \text{nm}}{360^\circ} \approx 1.05\ \text{nm per degree}$$

So at 60 nm a 1 degree difference puts you about 1 nm sideways; at 30 nm, half a nautical mile; at 120 nm, 2 nm. Turn that around for the angle:

$$\text{track error}\ (^\circ) = \frac{\text{distance off track} \times 60}{\text{distance flown}}$$

Looking forward, the angle between the planned track and the line from you to the destination is the **closing angle (CA)**:

$$\text{closing angle}\ (^\circ) = \frac{\text{distance off track} \times 60}{\text{distance to go}}$$

It is a small-angle rule of thumb, treating a short arc as a straight line: accurate to a few percent for navigation angles, rough beyond about 20 degrees.

![How do the track error and the closing angle add up?](widget:one-in-sixty "Move the fix off track: the track error grows as you move it closer to departure for the same offset, and the closing angle grows as the destination gets closer.")

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Track error divides by the distance **flown**; closing angle divides by the distance **to go**. Swapping them is the most common wrong answer, and the exam's distractors include it. Ask yourself which end of the leg the angle is measured at.

## Getting back on track (2.5.3 c)

Knowing the two angles, the syllabus names three choices.

**Parallel the track.** To stay offset, alter heading towards track by the **track error**; the old heading was wrong by exactly that amount, so the new TMG runs parallel to the plan.

**Intercept the track at a nominated point.** To reach a point on the track, alter by the **track error plus the closing angle**. The track error part stops the drift; the closing angle part points you at the target. Use the distance to the nominated point, not to the destination, in the closing angle.

**Maintain the track once intercepted.** Back on track, take off the closing angle and keep the track error correction, which is the real fix for the wind. Leaving the closing angle on would carry you across to the other side.

!!! example "Worked example: back to the destination"
    Given: planned track 090° M, heading 085° M flown for 30 nm. The fix shows you 3 nm left of track, with 45 nm to go.

    1. Track error: $\dfrac{3 \times 60}{30} = \mathbf{6^\circ}$ left of track.
    2. Closing angle: $\dfrac{3 \times 60}{45} = \mathbf{4^\circ}$.
    3. Alteration to reach the destination: $6 + 4 = \mathbf{10^\circ}$ right, so the new heading is $085 + 10 = \mathbf{095^\circ\ M}$.
    4. On regaining track, take off the closing angle: $095 - 4 = \mathbf{091^\circ\ M}$ to hold it.

    The TMG was $090 - 6 = 084^\circ$ M, so the actual drift was 1 degree left, where you had allowed 5 degrees: the wind had more southerly in it than forecast. The holding heading of 091° M is also the heading that would have paralleled the track in the first place.

Suppose instead a restricted area lies just north of track and you want to regain it at a town 15 nm ahead.

!!! example "Worked example: intercepting at a nominated point"
    Given: the same fix, 3 nm left of track after 30 nm, track error 6 degrees. The nominated point is 15 nm ahead along the track.

    1. Closing angle to the nominated point: $\dfrac{3 \times 60}{15} = \mathbf{12^\circ}$.
    2. Alteration: $6 + 12 = \mathbf{18^\circ}$ right, so the new heading is $085 + 18 = \mathbf{103^\circ\ M}$.
    3. Over the town, take off the closing angle: $103 - 12 = \mathbf{091^\circ\ M}$.

    The closer the nominated point, the bigger the closing angle and the steeper the intercept; the holding heading at the end is 091° M either way, because the wind has not changed.

Now try one with the error on the other side. Planned track 240° M, heading 247° M (you allowed 7 degrees of right drift correction). After 45 nm the fix shows you **3 nm right** of track with **90 nm** to go. Find the heading to the destination and the heading to hold track once regained.

??? solution "Show the solution"
    Track error $= \dfrac{3 \times 60}{45} = 4^\circ$ right. Closing angle $= \dfrac{3 \times 60}{90} = 2^\circ$. You are right of track, so turn **left** $4 + 2 = 6^\circ$: heading $247 - 6 =$ **241° M** to the destination. On regaining track, turn right by the closing angle: $241 + 2 =$ **243° M** to hold it. The TMG was 244° M against a heading of 247° M, so the actual drift was 3 degrees left where you had allowed 7: the wind from the right was weaker than forecast.

A useful special case: at the **halfway point** the closing angle equals the track error, so the correction to the destination is **double the track error**. The halfway mark is worth a careful fix.

??? check "Check yourself: why do you take off only the closing angle, and not the whole alteration, when you regain track?"
    The closing angle was added only to converge on the track; the track error correction compensates for the wind that the original heading did not allow for. Removing the closing angle stops you crossing the track, and keeping the track error correction keeps you on it.

## Revising the ETA (2.5.3 d)

Your plan's ETAs rest on a forecast groundspeed. Two timed fixes give the *actual* groundspeed, and every later estimate should be revised. Two methods give the same answer.

![Why is the groundspeed different from the plan?](diagram:groundspeed-headwind-tailwind "The TAS is the same in both bars; only the wind component changes the groundspeed, and with it every ETA on the leg.")

**By groundspeed.** Find the groundspeed between two fixes, then the time to go:

$$\text{GS} = \frac{\text{distance}}{\text{time (min)}} \times 60 \qquad \text{time to go (min)} = \frac{\text{distance to go}}{\text{GS}} \times 60$$

**By proportion.** Skip the groundspeed: the remaining distance takes time in the same ratio, often quicker in the air because prepared legs have round numbers:

$$\text{time to go} = \text{distance to go} \times \frac{\text{time taken}}{\text{distance flown}}$$

!!! example "Worked example: revising the estimate"
    Given: the first 30 nm of a 75 nm leg took 18 minutes (planned 16). The fix was at 0942 WST.

    1. By proportion, time for the remaining 45 nm: $45 \times \dfrac{18}{30} = \mathbf{27\ min}$ (planned $45 \times \dfrac{16}{30} = 24$ min).
    2. Check by groundspeed: $\dfrac{30}{18} \times 60 = \mathbf{100\ kt}$, and $\dfrac{45}{100} \times 60 = \mathbf{27\ min}$.
    3. Revised ETA: $0942 + 27 = \mathbf{1009\ WST}$, 3 minutes later than planned for this part of the leg.

    Pass any significant change to ATS if you are on a flight plan, and revise the fuel and the SARTIME along with it.

Now try the companion to the 240° M problem above. The first 45 nm of that leg took **25 minutes** against a plan of 23. You fixed at **1043 WST**, with 90 nm to go. What is the revised ETA, and what is the groundspeed?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    By proportion, the remaining 90 nm will take $90 \times \dfrac{25}{45} = 50$ min (the plan, $90 \times \dfrac{23}{45}$, was 46 min). Revised ETA $= 1043 + 50 =$ **1133 WST**, 4 minutes later than the plan for that stretch. Groundspeed $= \dfrac{45}{25} \times 60 =$ **108 kt**, and $\dfrac{90}{108} \times 60 = 50$ min confirms it.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Mixing hours and minutes. Groundspeed is in nautical miles per **hour**, so a distance divided by a time in minutes must be multiplied by 60. A "groundspeed" of 1.67 or an ETA 6,000 minutes away is the sign you skipped that step. Write the units next to every number.

## Dead reckoning: where am I now? (2.5.3 e)

Sometimes there is no fix: haze over featureless country, or ground that will not match the chart. You still know your last fix, the track made good since, and the groundspeed. **Dead reckoning (DR)** combines them:

$$\text{DR position} = \text{last fix} + (\text{GS} \times \text{time since the fix})\ \text{along the TMG}$$

Use the latest **TMG** and **groundspeed**, not the planned ones.

!!! example "Worked example: a DR position"
    Given: last fix at 1015 WST over a railway siding, TMG since then 075° M, groundspeed 105 kt. The time is now 1029 WST and there is nothing recognisable below.

    1. Time since the fix: $1029 - 1015 = \mathbf{14\ min}$.
    2. Distance per minute: $\dfrac{105}{60} = \mathbf{1.75\ nm/min}$.
    3. Distance flown: $14 \times 1.75 = \mathbf{24.5\ nm}$.
    4. DR position: **24.5 nm along 075° M from the siding** (plot it on the chart in true, by applying the variation).

    Now read ground to map *from that point*: search the features within a few miles of the DR position rather than the whole sheet.

A DR position's error grows with time since the fix. DR tells you where to look; map reading confirms it and starts the next DR.

??? check "Check yourself: why do you use the latest TMG and groundspeed for a DR position rather than the planned track and groundspeed?"
    The planned values assume the forecast wind. The TMG and groundspeed measured from your last fixes include the wind you are actually experiencing, so a DR position built from them is much more likely to be close to where you really are.

## Navigating in your head: mental DR and diversions (2.5.3 f)

In the air you rarely have room for the full computer. The 1 in 60 rule and proportion are already mental arithmetic; a few habits make them quick:

- **Per-minute speeds.** Know your groundspeed per minute: 90 kt is 1.5 nm a minute, 105 kt is 1.75, 120 kt is 2.
- **The 6-minute rule.** Six minutes is a tenth of an hour, so in 6 minutes you cover a tenth of your groundspeed: 10.5 nm at 105 kt.
- **Halfway check.** At halfway, the correction to the destination is double the track error.
- **Rounding.** Round freely; the next fix will show any error.

The biggest mental task is a **diversion**: weather ahead, a sick passenger, a rough engine. Four parts:

1. **Mark and measure.** Mark the diversion point. Estimate its track with the protractor or by eye against the graticule (WAC meridians point to true north), and its distance with the scale or the latitude scale (one minute of latitude is one nautical mile).
2. **Heading.** Apply variation and a drift re-estimated for the new track: the wind is the same, but it meets the new track at a different angle.
3. **Time.** Estimate the groundspeed on the new track and from it the estimated time interval (ETI).
4. **Check and tell.** Check fuel against the ETI plus reserve, terrain, airspace and daylight, and tell ATS or your SARTIME holder. Fly the heading, start the clock, refine at the first fix.

!!! example "Worked example: a mental diversion"
    Given: TAS 105 kt, wind from your last leg about 270° T at 20 kt, variation 2° W. On the chart the diversion aerodrome lies about **040° T, 34 nm** from your present position.

    1. Wind angle: the wind blows from 270, which is $40 + 90 = \mathbf{130^\circ}$ left of the new track, so it comes from behind on the left: a tailwind with left crosswind (right drift).
    2. Crosswind: $20 \times \sin 50^\circ \approx 20 \times 0.77 = \mathbf{15\ kt}$ (the angle from the tail is 50 degrees).
    3. Drift by 1 in 60: $\dfrac{15}{105} \times 60 \approx \mathbf{9^\circ}$ right, so steer 9 degrees left of track: $040 - 9 = \mathbf{031^\circ\ T}$.
    4. Magnetic heading: west variation is added, $031 + 2 = \mathbf{033^\circ\ M}$.
    5. Tailwind: $20 \times \cos 50^\circ \approx 20 \times 0.64 = \mathbf{13\ kt}$, so GS $\approx 105 + 13 = \mathbf{118\ kt}$, about 2 nm a minute.
    6. ETI: $\dfrac{34}{118} \times 60 \approx \mathbf{17\ min}$.

    Turn onto 033° M, note the time, and work out the fuel for 17 minutes plus reserve. If the first fix shows a track error, correct it with the 1 in 60 rule just as on a planned leg.

??? check "Check yourself: on a diversion, why is it not enough to use the same drift correction you were using on the original leg?"
    Drift depends on the angle between the wind and the track. The wind has not changed, but the new track meets it at a different angle, so both the drift and the groundspeed change and must be re-estimated for the new track.

## Bringing it together

You depart Northam for Merredin, about 75 nm, planning 110 kt with fan lines and 10 nm marks on the WAC. You cross the Great Eastern Highway at right angles to track, then a railway 18 nm further on 10 minutes later: groundspeed 108 kt. Near halfway a salt lake fix puts you 2 nm left of track: track error $2 \times 60 \div 37 \approx 3$ degrees, so you turn right about 6 degrees, and take off 3 when the railway paralleling the last part of the leg slides under the nose. By proportion the remaining 38 nm take about 21 minutes. Then the Merredin AWIS reports a thunderstorm overhead, and the diversion procedure gives you a heading, time and fuel check for the alternate inside two minutes: small angles, proportion and the clock.

## Key points

- **2.5.1** Chart track-up. Map to ground normally; ground to map only when uncertain, from the DR position. Across-track line for groundspeed, along-track line for track error, two lines near 90 degrees for a fix. Small close features low, large features high.
- **2.5.2** Bold track, 10 degree fan lines from both ends, 10 nm and halfway marks, TOPC, TOPD, lowest safe altitude. WAC en route, VNC near controlled airspace, VTC for terminal areas, ERSA always.
- **2.5.3** Track error = off track × 60 ÷ distance flown; closing angle = off track × 60 ÷ distance to go. Parallel: alter by the track error; intercept: add the closing angle; regained: take the closing angle off. ETA by groundspeed or proportion. DR = last fix plus GS × time along the TMG. Diversion: track, distance, drift, heading, ETI, fuel, tell someone.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    The 1 in 60 rule is examined with numbers every time. Track error uses the distance flown, closing angle uses the distance to go, and the correction to the destination is the sum; on regaining track you remove only the closing angle. Revised estimates come from groundspeed between two timed fixes or by straight proportion: write the units down and remember the factor of 60 between minutes and hours. Expect map-reading questions on which position line gives groundspeed (across track) and which gives track error (along track), and on when to read ground to map.
