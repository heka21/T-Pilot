## What the exam expects (PNVC 2.4)

- **2.4.1(a)** Estimate a time interval in your head from groundspeed and distance, using rules of thumb such as 120 kt = 2 NM a minute.
- **2.4.1(b)** Estimate endurance in your head from fuel flow and the fuel available, not counting the reserve.
- **2.4.2** Apply magnetic variation to get a magnetic direction.
- **2.4.3** Convert feet and metres, nautical miles and kilometres, pounds and kilograms, and US gallons, litres and kilograms of avgas.
- **2.4.4** Work out headwind, tailwind and crosswind components from a wind velocity and a heading, (a) on the navigation computer and (b) with the ERSA conversion and wind component tables.
- **2.4.5(a)** Find CAS and TAS, given the air temperature and pressure height.
- **2.4.5(b)** Find heading, groundspeed and drift, given TAS, wind velocity and track.
- **2.4.5(c)** Find the track, given heading, TAS and wind velocity.
- **2.4.5(d)** Work out climb and descent rates and gradients.
- **2.4.5(e)** Find the top of climb and top of descent positions from average airspeed, wind velocity and rates of climb and descent.

## Why this matters

Every number on your navigation log comes out of this lesson. The flight from Jandakot to Kalgoorlie starts as a line on a chart and a forecast wind; it becomes a heading, a groundspeed, a time for each leg, a fuel figure, and the points where the climb ends and the descent begins. Some of that you do on the ground with the flight computer, the rest in your head in the air when the wind is not what was forecast. CASA names "time/distance and revision of estimates" as a weak area in the PPLA exam. The exam allows the computer, but rewards the pilot who checks every answer with a mental estimate, which catches the factor of two or the wrong sign at once.

## Time and distance in your head

You are 36 NM from Northam at a groundspeed of 120 kt. How long until you arrive? The formula is easy enough:

$$\text{time (min)} = \frac{\text{distance (NM)} \times 60}{\text{GS (kt)}}$$

but in the air you want the answer without a pencil. Think in nautical miles per **minute** instead of per hour: divide the groundspeed by 60. Learn these by heart:

| Groundspeed | NM per minute | NM per 10 minutes |
|---|---|---|
| 60 kt | 1 | 10 |
| 90 kt | 1.5 | 15 |
| 105 kt | 1.75 | 17.5 |
| **120 kt** | **2** | 20 |
| 150 kt | 2.5 | 25 |
| 180 kt | 3 | 30 |

The time is then the distance divided by the miles per minute. Between the round numbers, use the 10-minute column (at 90 kt, 15 NM every 10 minutes) or scale from the nearest value.

!!! example "Worked example: time from distance and groundspeed"
    Given: (a) 36 NM at 120 kt; (b) 42 NM at 105 kt.

    1. (a) Miles per minute: $120 \div 60 = 2$ NM/min. Time: $36 \div 2 = $ **18 min**.
    2. (b) Miles per minute: $105 \div 60 = 1.75$ NM/min. Time: $42 \div 1.75 = $ **24 min**.
    3. Check (b) the other way: at 105 kt you cover 17.5 NM in 10 minutes, so 42 NM is a little under $2.5 \times 10 = 25$ min. It agrees.

Now try one before opening the solution: your groundspeed is 96 kt and the next town is 40 NM away. How long?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    $96 \div 60 = 1.6$ NM per minute. $40 \div 1.6 =$ **25 minutes**. Check: at 90 kt it would be about 27 minutes, at 120 kt 20 minutes; 96 kt sits between, a little closer to 90, so 25 is right.

## Endurance in your head

**Endurance** is how long the aeroplane can fly on its fuel. For planning, set aside the **fixed reserve** first (30 minutes for day VFR; verify the current figure in the Part 91 MOS and CASA's fuel guidance), plus any holding or alternate fuel. What is left is the fuel **available**:

$$\text{endurance (h)} = \frac{\text{fuel available (L)}}{\text{fuel flow (L/h)}}$$

The reserve is a time, so it becomes a quantity at the fuel flow you will be using: 30 minutes at 30 L/h is 15 L.

!!! example "Worked example: endurance excluding the reserve"
    Given: 120 L on board, fuel flow 30 L/h, reserve 15 L.

    1. Fuel available: $120 - 15 = $ **105 L**.
    2. Endurance: $105 \div 30 = $ **3.5 h**.
    3. In minutes: $3.5 \times 60 = $ **210 min**.

    Three and a half hours of flying before you touch the reserve.

!!! example "Worked example: reserve worked out from the fuel flow"
    Given: 150 L on board, fuel flow 25 L/h, a 30-minute fixed reserve.

    1. Reserve: $0.5\ \text{h} \times 25\ \text{L/h} = $ **12.5 L**.
    2. Fuel available: $150 - 12.5 = $ **137.5 L**.
    3. Endurance: $137.5 \div 25 = $ **5.5 h**, which is **330 min**.

    Mental check: 25 L/h is 100 L every 4 hours, so 137.5 L is a little under $4 \times 1.4 = 5.6$ h.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The exam's distractors are the endurance on all the fuel (forgetting the reserve), and an answer in hours given as if it were hours and minutes: 3.5 h is 3 h **30** min, not 3 h 50 min.

## Applying variation

You learned the rule in [PNVC 2.1](/notes/PNVC/2.1): **variation east, magnetic least; variation west, magnetic best**. With 3 degrees east, a track of 125°T becomes $125 - 3 = 122$°M. To plot a magnetic direction on the chart, reverse it: a heading of 300°M with 2 degrees west is $300 - 2 = 298$°T.

What this lesson adds is **where** the variation goes in a calculation. Forecast winds are true, so a triangle of velocities is solved entirely in true, and only the heading that comes out is converted to magnetic. Mixing a true wind with a magnetic track gives the wrong drift.

## Conversions

Aviation mixes units: runways in metres and altitudes in feet, weights in pounds in an American POH and kilograms on the load sheet, fuel in US gallons on the gauge, litres from the bowser and kilograms on the weight and balance. [BAKC 2.4](/notes/BAKC/2.4) introduced the units; here you convert between them.

One idea stops most errors: **converting to a smaller unit gives a bigger number**. A metre is longer than a foot, so a length in feet is the bigger number (1,500 m is about 4,920 ft). A kilogram is heavier than a pound, so the weight in pounds is the bigger number. Decide which way the answer should move before you multiply or divide, and the factor's direction follows.

| Conversion | Factor | Quick check |
|---|---|---|
| Feet to metres | × 0.3048 (1 m = 3.28 ft) | 1,000 ft ≈ 305 m; 1,500 m runway ≈ 4,920 ft |
| Nautical miles to kilometres | × 1.852 (1 km = 0.54 NM) | 100 NM ≈ 185 km |
| Statute miles to nautical miles | × 0.87 | |
| Pounds to kilograms | × 0.4536 (1 kg = 2.205 lb) | 2,200 lb ≈ 1,000 kg |
| US gallons to litres | × 3.785 | 37 US gal ≈ 140 L |
| Imperial gallons to litres | × 4.546 | |
| Litres of avgas to kilograms | × 0.72 (SG given in the question) | 100 L = 72 kg |
| US gallons of avgas to kilograms | × 2.72 (6 lb per US gal) | 10 US gal ≈ 27 kg |
| Kilograms of avgas to litres | ÷ 0.72 | 72 kg = 100 L |

![How do the units you will meet convert?](diagram:units-conversion-ladder "Read straight across each scale: 1 NM is 1.852 km, 110 kt is 204 km/h, and 100 L of avgas weighs about 72 kg.")

Fuel needs more thought, because it changes from volume (litres, gallons) to weight (kilograms, pounds). The bridge is the **specific gravity** (SG), the weight in kilograms of a litre of fuel: about **0.72** for avgas, and the exam gives the figure to use. A US gallon of avgas weighs about 6 lb.

!!! example "Worked example: fuel from US gallons to kilograms, two ways"
    Given: 48 US gallons of avgas; SG 0.72; or 6 lb per US gallon and 1 kg = 2.205 lb.

    1. Via litres: $48 \times 3.785 = $ **181.7 L**; then $181.7 \times 0.72 = $ **130.8 kg**.
    2. Via pounds: $48 \times 6 = $ **288 lb**; then $288 \div 2.205 = $ **130.6 kg**.

    Both routes give about 131 kg for the weight and balance. The small difference is rounding in the 6 lb per gallon figure.

!!! example "Worked example: from a weight to litres and a length to feet"
    Given: the load sheet allows 95 kg of fuel; a runway is 1,500 m long.

    1. Fuel: weight to volume, divide by the SG: $95 \div 0.72 = $ **131.9 L**, about 132 L to ask for.
    2. Runway: metres to feet, a bigger number: $1500 \times 3.28 = $ **4,920 ft**.

The computer has conversion scales and ERSA GEN has tables. The common errors are multiplying when you should divide and using the wrong gallon: the imperial gallon is a fifth larger than the US one.

??? check "Check yourself: you have 60 kg of avgas. Is that more or less than 60 litres, and why?"
    More: about 83 L. A litre of avgas weighs only 0.72 kg, so it takes more litres than kilograms to make the same amount of fuel; divide the weight by 0.72.

## Wind components

Lined up on runway 24 with the tower wind 290 degrees at 20 kt, how much of that wind helps you get airborne and how much pushes you sideways? Any wind splits into two parts: one along the runway (or your heading) and one across it. The part along it is the **headwind component**, or a **tailwind component** if it blows from behind; the part across it is the **crosswind component**.

The split depends on the **wind angle** θ, the difference between the wind direction and the runway direction. Both must be in the same reference: runway directions and tower winds are **magnetic**; forecast winds are **true**. With a wind speed W:

$$\text{headwind} = W \cos\theta \qquad \text{crosswind} = W \sin\theta$$

When the angle is more than 90 degrees the cosine is negative: the headwind component has become a tailwind.

![Split any wind into headwind and crosswind components for a runway](widget:crosswind-component "Thirty degrees off the runway already puts half the wind across it; move the wind round and watch the headwind turn into a tailwind past 90 degrees.")

You do not need a calculator for the sine. The **clock rule** treats the wind angle as minutes on a clock face and the crosswind as the fraction of an hour: 15 degrees gives a quarter (0.25) of the wind across, 30 degrees a half (0.5), 45 degrees about 0.7, 60 degrees about 0.9, and 90 degrees all of it. The headwind is the same table read from the other end: at 60 degrees off, half the wind is still along the runway.

!!! example "Worked example: components for runway 24"
    Given: runway 24 (240°M); tower wind 290°M at 20 kt.

    1. Wind angle: $290 - 240 = $ **50°**, from the right of the runway.
    2. Crosswind: $20 \times \sin 50° = 20 \times 0.77 = $ **15 kt** from the right.
    3. Headwind: $20 \times \cos 50° = 20 \times 0.64 = $ **13 kt**.
    4. Clock rule check: 50 degrees is between 45 (0.7) and 60 (0.9), about 0.8, so $20 \times 0.8 = 16$ kt across. Close enough.

    If the aeroplane's maximum demonstrated crosswind is 15 kt, this is at the limit.

!!! example "Worked example: components for runway 06"
    Given: runway 06 (060°M); tower wind 100°M at 18 kt.

    1. Wind angle: $100 - 060 = $ **40°**, from the right.
    2. Crosswind: $18 \times \sin 40° = 18 \times 0.64 = $ **11.6 kt**, about 12 kt from the right.
    3. Headwind: $18 \times \cos 40° = 18 \times 0.77 = $ **13.8 kt**, about 14 kt.

    Comfortably inside a typical trainer's crosswind figure, with a useful headwind.

**On the navigation computer**, set the wind direction under the index on the wind side and mark the wind speed up from the centre; rotate the runway direction (or heading) under the index and read the components off the grid: the dot's offset across the centre line is the crosswind, above or below the centre the headwind or tailwind.

**With the ERSA table** (ERSA GEN; verify its layout in the current issue), enter with the wind angle and the wind speed, read both components, and interpolate when your values fall between the printed ones.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The wind angle comes from the wind and the runway in the **same** reference. A forecast wind is true; a runway number is magnetic. Near Perth the difference is a degree or two, but the exam may set a larger variation. And past 90 degrees off, the "headwind" is a tailwind: a wind from behind the wing shortens nothing.

??? check "Check yourself: why does the crosswind component grow so quickly at small wind angles and then level off?"
    Because the crosswind is $W \sin\theta$, and the sine climbs steeply from zero (0.5 already at 30 degrees) and then flattens towards 1 at 90 degrees. Thirty degrees off the runway already puts half the wind across it.

## True airspeed from temperature and pressure height

Correct the **IAS** for instrument and position error with the POH calibration table (section 5) and you have **CAS**, usually within a few knots. **TAS** is CAS corrected for air **density**, which depends on pressure height and temperature (see [BAKC 2.2](/notes/BAKC/2.2)): thinner air, higher TAS for the same CAS.

![How do you get from indicated airspeed to groundspeed?](diagram:airspeed-chain-ictg "Always I, C, T, G: instrument and position error, then density, then wind.")

**On the computer**, the airspeed window of the computer side does the density correction for you: set the **pressure height** against the **outside air temperature**, then read the **TAS** on the outer scale opposite the **CAS** on the inner scale.

**By rule of thumb**, TAS exceeds CAS by about **2 percent per 1,000 ft of density height**. Find the density height as in [BAKC 6.2](/notes/BAKC/6.2): the ISA temperature at the pressure height is $15 - 2 \times$ (pressure height in thousands of feet), and each degree warmer than ISA adds about 120 ft.

!!! example "Worked example: TAS at 8,000 ft on a warm day"
    Given: pressure height 8,000 ft, OAT 5 degrees, CAS 100 kt.

    1. ISA temperature at 8,000 ft: $15 - 2 \times 8 = $ **−1 degree**.
    2. Deviation: $5 - (-1) = $ **6 degrees warm**.
    3. Density height: $8000 + 120 \times 6 = 8720$, **about 8,700 ft**.
    4. Rule of thumb: $8.7 \times 2\% \approx 17\%$, so TAS $\approx 100 \times 1.17 = $ **117 kt**.

    The computer, which works the density out exactly, gives a little less, about 114 kt: the 2 percent rule overestimates slightly at these heights. In the exam, use the method the question names.

!!! example "Worked example: TAS at 6,000 ft on a hot day"
    Given: pressure height 6,000 ft, OAT 15 degrees, CAS 105 kt.

    1. ISA temperature at 6,000 ft: $15 - 2 \times 6 = $ **3 degrees**.
    2. Deviation: $15 - 3 = $ **+12 degrees**.
    3. Density height: $6000 + 120 \times 12 = $ **7,440 ft**.
    4. Rule of thumb: $7.44 \times 2\% = 14.9\%$, so TAS $\approx 105 \times 1.149 = $ **121 kt** (the computer gives about 117 kt).

![Work out the density height for any pressure height and temperature](widget:density-height "Heat is the big one: every degree above ISA adds about 120 ft of density height, and with it about a quarter of a percent of TAS.")

??? check "Check yourself: you hold the same CAS while climbing. Does the TAS go up or down, and why?"
    Up. The air thins as you climb, so to produce the same dynamic pressure, which is what the airspeed indicator measures, the aeroplane must move faster through it.

## The triangle of velocities

You want to fly a track of 070 degrees true at 110 kt TAS, with the wind from 020 degrees at 25 kt. Point the nose along 070 and the wind carries you sideways. How far do you point into the wind, and how fast will you cover the ground?

Three motions add, each with a direction and a speed, so each is a **vector**: the **air vector**, **heading (HDG)** and **TAS**, the aeroplane through the air; the **wind vector**, the **wind velocity (W/V)**, the air over the ground; and the **ground vector**, **track (TR)** and **groundspeed (GS)**, the aeroplane over the ground. Put the air and wind vectors end to end and the ground vector closes the triangle. The angle between the heading and the track is the **drift**. If the wind blows from the left of the aeroplane it carries you to the right: **right drift**, so the track lies to the right of the heading. To fly a given track you therefore point the nose **into the wind**, to the left of the track by the drift angle.

![What is the difference between heading, track and drift?](diagram:heading-track-drift "The nose points along the heading, but a wind from the left carries the aeroplane sideways; the line over the ground is the track, and the angle between them is the drift.")

### Heading, groundspeed and drift from track, TAS and wind

**On the navigation computer**, the wind side solves the triangle: set the wind direction under the index and mark the wind speed up from the centre; set the true track under the index; slide the TAS arc under the wind dot; read the **drift** from the dot's offset and the **groundspeed** under the centre. Heading is the track corrected by the drift, towards the wind; expect 5 to 10 degrees in typical winds. Work in true and convert the heading to magnetic at the end.

**In your head**, split the wind into components along and across the track, as for a runway:

- **Drift** in degrees is about the crosswind component times 60, divided by the TAS: the 1 in 60 rule (see [PNVC 2.5](/notes/PNVC/2.5)).
- **Groundspeed** is about the TAS minus the headwind component (or plus the tailwind).

!!! example "Worked example: heading and groundspeed, wind from the left"
    Given: track 070°T, TAS 110 kt, wind 020°T at 25 kt, variation 2° W.

    1. Wind angle: the wind is from $070 - 020 = $ **50°** left of the track, ahead of the wing.
    2. Crosswind: $25 \times \sin 50° = 25 \times 0.77 \approx $ **19 kt** from the left.
    3. Drift: $19 \times 60 \div 110 \approx $ **10°**, and it is **right** drift, because the wind is from the left.
    4. Heading: point into the wind, left of track: $070 - 10 = $ **060°T**; with 2° W, $060 + 2 = $ **062°M**.
    5. Headwind: $25 \times \cos 50° = 25 \times 0.64 \approx $ **16 kt**; GS $\approx 110 - 16 = $ **94 kt**. The computer gives about **92 kt** (the quick method ignores drift).

    Steer 062 magnetic and expect a groundspeed in the low nineties.

![Solve the triangle of velocities for any track and wind](widget:wind-triangle "What to notice: the heading always points upwind of the track; solve in true, then apply the variation.")

!!! example "Worked example: heading and groundspeed, wind from the right"
    Given: track 160°T, TAS 100 kt, wind 230°T at 20 kt.

    1. Wind angle: the wind is from $230 - 160 = $ **70°** right of the track.
    2. Crosswind: $20 \times \sin 70° = 20 \times 0.94 = $ **18.8 kt** from the right.
    3. Drift: $\sin^{-1}(18.8 \div 100) \approx $ **11°**, **left** drift, because the wind is from the right. (The 1 in 60 rule gives $18.8 \times 60 \div 100 = 11.3$°.)
    4. Heading: point into the wind, right of track: $160 + 11 = $ **171°T**.
    5. Headwind: $20 \times \cos 70° = 20 \times 0.34 = $ **6.8 kt**; GS $\approx 100 \times \cos 11° - 6.8 = 98.2 - 6.8 \approx $ **91 kt**.

    With a large drift the aeroplane's own speed along the track is a little less than its TAS (here $100 \times \cos 11° = 98$ kt), so subtract the headwind from that.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Get the direction of the heading correction from the picture, not from memory: the nose always points **into** the wind. Wind from the left gives right drift and a heading **left** of track; wind from the right gives left drift and a heading **right** of track. If your heading is on the downwind side of the track, the sign is wrong.

### Track from heading, TAS and wind

The reverse problem: you have flown a heading for a while, and you want to know what track you actually made good and how fast, to plot a dead-reckoning position. On the computer, set the wind, set the **heading** under the index, place the TAS under the wind dot, and read the drift and groundspeed; the track made good is the heading corrected by the drift, **downwind**.

!!! example "Worked example: track made good from a heading"
    Given: heading 300°T, TAS 95 kt, wind 250°T at 15 kt.

    1. Wind angle: the wind is from $300 - 250 = $ **50°** left of the nose.
    2. Crosswind: $15 \times \sin 50° = $ **11.5 kt** from the left, so **right** drift.
    3. Drift: $11.5 \times 60 \div 95 \approx $ **7 to 8°**.
    4. Track: downwind of the heading: $300 + 8 \approx $ **308°T**.
    5. Headwind: $15 \times \cos 50° = 9.6$ kt; GS $\approx 95 - 9.6 \approx $ **86 kt**.

    You have been drifting 8 degrees right of where the nose points, at 86 kt over the ground.

??? check "Check yourself: the wind is exactly on the nose. What are the drift and the groundspeed?"
    No drift, because there is no crosswind component: heading and track are the same. The groundspeed is the TAS minus the full wind speed.

## Climb and descent rates and gradients

The VSI shows feet per **minute**; whether you clear the trees depends on feet per **nautical mile** over the ground, the **gradient**. Groundspeed links the two.

$$\text{gradient (ft/NM)} = \frac{\text{rate (ft/min)}}{\text{GS (NM/min)}} = \frac{\text{rate (ft/min)}}{\text{GS (kt)} \div 60}$$

As a percentage, the height gained per hundred feet travelled: a nautical mile is 6,076 ft, so a knot is $6076 \div 60 \approx 101.3$ ft per minute, and

$$\text{gradient (\%)} = \frac{\text{rate (ft/min)}}{\text{GS (kt)} \times 1.013}$$

!!! example "Worked example: climb gradient"
    Given: (a) rate of climb 500 ft/min at a groundspeed of 80 kt; (b) 600 ft/min at 90 kt.

    1. (a) GS: $80 \div 60 = 1.33$ NM/min. Gradient: $500 \div 1.33 = $ **375 ft/NM**.
    2. (a) As a percentage: $500 \div (80 \times 1.013) = 500 \div 81 = $ **6.2%** (or $375 \div 6076 \times 100$).
    3. (b) GS: $90 \div 60 = 1.5$ NM/min. Gradient: $600 \div 1.5 = $ **400 ft/NM**, about **6.6%**.

    A higher rate of climb at a higher groundspeed has given only a slightly steeper path.

![Does wind change how fast or how steeply you climb?](diagram:climb-gradient-wind "Same 600 ft per minute in every case; the headwind steepens the path over the ground, the tailwind flattens it.")

Wind changes the gradient but not the rate: a headwind lowers the groundspeed and steepens the path.

For descent the same arithmetic runs backwards. A standard **3 degree** approach path is about 300 ft per nautical mile, which gives a handy rule: **rate of descent (ft/min) ≈ GS × 5**. At 100 kt, that is about 500 ft/min.

??? check "Check yourself: you climb at a steady 500 ft/min. Does a headwind make the gradient steeper or shallower, and why?"
    Steeper. The rate is the same, but the headwind lowers the groundspeed, so you cover fewer nautical miles in each minute of climb and gain more height per mile.

## Top of climb and top of descent

The **top of climb (TOPC)** is the point along the track where you reach cruising level; the **top of descent (TOPD)** is where you must start down to arrive at circuit height. Both go on the chart, because only between them do the cruise groundspeed and fuel flow apply.

The method is the same for both:

1. **Height to change**: cruising level minus the starting height (or minus the circuit height for a descent).
2. **Time**: height to change divided by the rate of climb or descent.
3. **Groundspeed** during the climb or descent: the average TAS for that phase, corrected for the wind component, as in the triangle above.
4. **Distance**: time multiplied by groundspeed in NM per minute.

!!! example "Worked example: TOPC and TOPD for a Wheatbelt flight"
    Given: departure elevation 100 ft, cruise 5,500 ft, climb 500 ft/min at a climb groundspeed of 75 kt; descent to a circuit height of 1,100 ft at 500 ft/min and 110 kt groundspeed.

    1. Climb height: $5500 - 100 = $ **5,400 ft**. Time: $5400 \div 500 = $ **10.8 min**.
    2. Climb GS: $75 \div 60 = 1.25$ NM/min. Distance: $10.8 \times 1.25 = $ **13.5 NM** from departure to TOPC.
    3. Descent height: $5500 - 1100 = $ **4,400 ft**. Time: $4400 \div 500 = $ **8.8 min**.
    4. Descent GS: $110 \div 60 = 1.83$ NM/min. Distance: $8.8 \times 1.83 = $ **16 NM** before the destination for TOPD.

    Mark TOPC 13.5 NM along the track from departure and TOPD 16 NM back from the destination.

!!! example "Worked example: TOPC and TOPD with the wind"
    Given: climb from 1,500 ft to 7,500 ft at 600 ft/min, climb TAS 80 kt into a 10 kt headwind component; descent from 8,500 ft to 1,500 ft at 500 ft/min, descent TAS 135 kt into a 15 kt headwind component.

    1. Climb time: $(7500 - 1500) \div 600 = 6000 \div 600 = $ **10 min**.
    2. Climb GS: $80 - 10 = $ **70 kt**, $70 \div 60 = 1.17$ NM/min. Distance: $10 \times 1.17 = $ **11.7 NM** to TOPC.
    3. Descent time: $(8500 - 1500) \div 500 = 7000 \div 500 = $ **14 min**.
    4. Descent GS: $135 - 15 = $ **120 kt**, 2 NM/min. Distance: $14 \times 2 = $ **28 NM** before the destination for TOPD.

    A headwind pulls TOPC closer to departure; a tailwind would push TOPD further out.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Use the **groundspeed** for the phase, not the cruise TAS and not the still-air climb speed. And subtract the starting height: a climb from a 1,500 ft aerodrome to 7,500 ft is 6,000 ft of climbing, not 7,500.

## Bringing it together

You plan Jandakot to a Wheatbelt strip, 96 NM on a track of 070°T at 6,500 ft, forecast wind 020°T at 25 kt, temperature 10 degrees. ISA there is 2 degrees, so density height is about 7,500 ft and your 100 kt CAS is about 115 kt TAS. The wind gives right drift of about 10 degrees and a groundspeed of about 97 kt: steer 060°T, 062°M. The 6,400 ft climb at 500 ft/min takes about 13 minutes and, at 70 kt over the ground, ends 15 NM out; a 5,000 ft descent at 500 ft/min and 90 kt starts 15 NM before the strip. The 66 NM cruise at about 1.6 NM a minute takes roughly 40 minutes. The ERSA table gives the crosswind on arrival, and the fuel gauge's US gallons become kilograms on the load sheet.

## Key points

- **2.4.1(a)** Time = distance ÷ (GS ÷ 60). 60 kt is 1 NM a minute, 90 kt 1.5, 120 kt 2, 150 kt 2.5.
- **2.4.1(b)** Endurance = (fuel on board − reserve) ÷ fuel flow; convert hours to minutes carefully.
- **2.4.2** Variation east, magnetic least; west, magnetic best. Solve wind triangles in true and convert the heading at the end.
- **2.4.3** To a smaller unit, a bigger number: 1 m = 3.28 ft, 1 NM = 1.852 km, 1 kg = 2.205 lb, 1 US gal = 3.785 L, avgas 0.72 kg per litre.
- **2.4.4** Headwind = W cos θ, crosswind = W sin θ, with wind and runway in the same reference; clock rule 15, 30, 45, 60 degrees gives a quarter, a half, 0.7, 0.9. Computer wind side or the ERSA table, interpolating.
- **2.4.5(a)** CAS from the POH table; TAS on the computer from pressure height and OAT, or about 2 percent per 1,000 ft of density height.
- **2.4.5(b)** Drift ≈ crosswind × 60 ÷ TAS; GS ≈ TAS − headwind. The nose points into the wind: wind from the left, right drift, heading left of track.
- **2.4.5(c)** Track made good = heading corrected by the drift, downwind.
- **2.4.5(d)** Gradient (ft/NM) = rate ÷ (GS ÷ 60); a 3 degree descent needs about GS × 5 ft/min.
- **2.4.5(e)** TOPC and TOPD: height to change ÷ rate = time; time × phase groundspeed = distance.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Practise the per-minute speeds until they are automatic, keep every wind problem in one reference (true for forecast winds, magnetic for the tower), and show the arithmetic step by step on the scribble paper so a slip is easy to find. For every computer answer, run the mental check: drift from the 1 in 60 rule, groundspeed from the headwind component, and a heading that points into the wind.
