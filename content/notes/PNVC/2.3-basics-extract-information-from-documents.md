## What the exam expects (PNVC 2.3)

- **2.3.1(a)** On the WAC and the visual charts for your area, recognise without the legend: the major map-reading features such as roads, rivers and lakes; obstacles and spot heights, with elevation and height above terrain; and controlled airspace, PRD areas and aerodrome data on the VTC or ERC.
- **2.3.1(b)** Decode every other symbol with the help of the chart legend.
- **2.3.1(c)** Judge the general height of the terrain from the hypsometric tints and contours.
- **2.3.1(d)** Estimate a track and a distance from the chart.
- **2.3.1(e)** Show how to orient the chart in flight, and explain why.
- **2.3.2** Use the legend to identify airspace boundaries and their symbols on the visual AIP charts.
- **2.3.3(a)** Extract runway data from ERSA.
- **2.3.3(b)** Extract the data for prohibited, restricted and danger areas from ERSA.

## Why this matters

In the RPL you met the charts and ERSA as documents: what each chart shows, how an ERSA entry is laid out (see [BAKC 5.1](/notes/BAKC/5.1) and [BAKC 5.2](/notes/BAKC/5.2)). The PPL turns that into a working skill. Planning Jandakot to Kalgoorlie, you will draw a line across two WACs, measure it, read the high ground along it, pick out the railway and the Great Eastern Highway as features to follow, check the Perth steps and the restricted areas around RAAF Pearce on the VNC, and then go to ERSA for the runway at your destination and the hours of every restricted area your track touches. In the air you will hold the chart so the country below matches it. The exam gives you an extract of a chart or an ERSA page and asks you to read it quickly and correctly: an obstacle's two figures, a distance on a stated scale, the meaning of a boundary line, whether an area is active at your ETA.

## Charts at a glance

You already know the family. The **WAC** (1:1,000,000) shows the ground and no airspace; the **VNC** (1:500,000) and **VTC** (1:250,000) show the ground and the airspace together; the **ERC-L** shows airspace and air routes but no topography. The PPL asks you to read some symbols at sight, because in the air you have no time to look up a railway, and the rest with the legend while planning.

![What does each visual chart show?](diagram:chart-features-panel "The WAC has topography but no airspace; the VNC and VTC add airspace and frequencies; the ERC-L has airspace but no topography.")

## Features you should know without the legend

Picture the view from 3,500 ft east of the Darling Scarp: wheat paddocks, a highway and a railway side by side, a string of salt lakes, a town every 20 or 30 miles. Each has a symbol you should recognise at sight. On the WAC (verify each against the legend of your current chart):

- **Roads** are red for major sealed roads and brown or dashed for minor and unsealed ones.
- **Railways** are black lines with **cross ticks**, a single line for a single track. Railways and highways often run together out of Wheatbelt towns, and the ticks tell you which line is which.
- **Power lines** are red lines carrying their own symbol.
- **Rivers** are blue: **solid** for perennial, **dashed** for intermittent, which is most rivers in WA. A dry riverbed shows as a line of trees, not water.
- **Lakes** have a blue outline. **Salt lakes** have a distinctive fill and are often the best landmarks in the Wheatbelt: white, oddly shaped and visible from a long way off.
- The **coastline** is shown with the sea in blue.
- **Towns** are yellow shapes or circles, sized by the town.
- In the outback, where they may be the only features for miles, **homesteads**, **mines**, **tanks**, **windmills** and **vermin fences** each have a symbol.

![Which symbols should you be able to pick out on a visual chart?](diagram:chart-legend-symbols "Railways have cross ticks, intermittent rivers are dashed, salt lakes have their own fill, and the obstacle carries two figures.")

Learn the symbols so that your attention goes on the real question: will this feature be unmistakable from the air? A line feature crossing your track or a distinctive lake beats a homestead that looks like every other homestead.

### Spot heights and obstacles

A **spot height** is a dot with a number beside it: the surveyed elevation of that point in feet above mean sea level, usually a hilltop.

An **obstacle** is a tall structure: a mast, tower, chimney or wind turbine. Its symbol carries **two figures**, and they answer two different questions. The larger, unbracketed figure is the **elevation of the top above mean sea level**: that is what you compare with your altimeter set to QNH, because the altimeter reads height above sea level. The **bracketed** figure is the **height of the structure above the ground** it stands on: it tells you how much it sticks up from the land around it, and so how visible it will be. Lit obstacles carry a flash symbol, and a group of obstacles has its own symbol. (Confirm which figure is which on your chart's legend; the convention given here is the one the exam uses.)

!!! example "Worked example: decoding an obstacle"
    Given: an obstacle on the WAC marked **1,250 (400)**.

    1. The unbracketed figure is the elevation of the top: **1,250 ft AMSL**.
    2. The bracketed figure is the height of the structure above the ground: **400 ft**.
    3. The ground at its base: $1250 - 400 = $ **850 ft AMSL**.

    On a QNH altimeter the top of the mast is at 1,250 ft. Flying at 1,500 ft you would pass only 250 ft above it.

Now try one before opening the solution. A mast near your track is marked 2,180 (520). What is the elevation of the ground it stands on, and what altimeter reading on QNH puts you 500 ft above its top?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    Ground: $2180 - 520 =$ **1,660 ft AMSL**. Top of the mast: 2,180 ft, so 500 ft above it is $2180 + 500 =$ **2,680 ft** on QNH. The 520 ft only tells you how tall the mast looks from the ground; the altimeter cares about the 2,180.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The exam offers the bracketed figure as the "height to clear". It is not: the altimeter reads above sea level, so the figure to compare with it is the elevation of the top. Adding the two figures together is the other distractor; the elevation already includes the structure.

### Airspace, PRD areas and aerodromes on the VTC and ERC-L

On the VTC, and en route on the ERC-L, there is a third set of things you should read without reaching for the legend, because you will be looking at them in the air in the busiest part of the flight:

- **Controlled airspace**: the control zone around a towered aerodrome and the **CTA steps** around it, each step labelled with its lower and upper limits.
- **PRD areas**, each with its identifier (the letter P, R or D and a number) and its vertical limits.
- **VFR routes and reporting points**, the paths and named places you fly and report at in the terminal area.
- **Aerodromes**, with their elevation, runway alignment and frequency, and **navigation aids**.

The VTC also prints a **maximum elevation figure** in each grid square: the highest terrain or obstacle in that square, rounded up with a margin, so that you can pick a safe height for a whole area at a glance.

??? check "Check yourself: an obstacle reads 940 (380). You are cruising at 1,200 ft on the area QNH. Which figure do you compare with your altimeter, and by how much do you clear the top?"
    The 940, because it is the elevation of the top above sea level, which is what a QNH altimeter measures against. You clear it by $1200 - 940 = 260$ ft. The 380 is the structure's height above its own ground.

## Using the legend for everything else

No pilot remembers every symbol: the kinds of aerodrome, heliports and ALAs, the navigation aid roses, glider and parachute activity. For anything outside the core set you decode it from the **legend** printed on the chart, and the exam allows you to use it. The skill is reading it carefully rather than guessing.

### Airspace boundaries and their limits

Airspace is where the legend earns its keep, and the syllabus names it separately. Controlled airspace is layered, like an upside-down wedding cake: a control zone from the surface around the aerodrome, then steps whose lower limits rise as you move away from it, with Class G underneath each step. A chart is flat, so it has to show that three-dimensional shape with **line styles, colours and labels**.

![Where does each class of airspace sit?](diagram:airspace-cross-section "Control zones start at the surface and the CTA steps above them rise with distance; the chart shows each step as a boundary line with its lower and upper limits printed beside it.")

With the legend, decode on the VTC and VNC:

- the **line styles and colours** for Class C, D and E boundaries and for the control zone;
- the **vertical limits**, printed as a pair: the upper limit over the lower limit, with **SFC** for the surface and **FL** for a flight level, and the abbreviations **LL** (lower limit) and **UL** (upper limit) where they are used;
- **restricted and danger area hatching** and identifiers;
- **VFR route** lines, lanes of entry and their altitude limits;
- **reporting points**, as named triangles or circles, with the compulsory and on-request kinds drawn differently;
- **CTAF** and ATC **frequencies**;
- the **aerodrome** categories, **navigation aid** symbols (VOR rose, NDB, DME), and the obstacle, power line and glider or parachute activity symbols.

Read a step label as a band. A step labelled with an upper limit of FL125 over a lower limit of 2,500 ft means controlled airspace from 2,500 ft up to FL125: below 2,500 ft you are in Class G and need no clearance, at 2,500 ft or above you are inside the step. (An illustration of the format; read the real limits from the current chart.)

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The bottom number of a step label is the base of the step, not a height you may climb to. You stay clear by staying **below** the base. And the WAC shows none of this airspace: a question that asks where to find a step base or a restricted area's limits has the VNC, VTC or ERC-L as its answer.

## Judging the height of the terrain

Before you choose a cruising altitude you need to know how high the ground under the route is. The chart shows height in three ways, and each answers a different question.

**Contour lines** join points of equal elevation, drawn at set intervals. Where they crowd together the ground is steep; where they spread out it is gentle. **Hypsometric tints** are the colour bands between contours, running from green or white lowlands through yellow and brown to higher ground, so that you can see where the high country is without reading a number. **Spot heights** give the exact height of particular points, usually the tops of hills, which stand above the general level of their tint band.

To assess a route, follow the line and note the **highest tint band** it crosses. The top of that band is the general height of the terrain you must clear. Then look along the line for spot heights and obstacles that stand above the band, because the tint shows the general level, not every hilltop. A rule of thumb for a comfortable cruising height is to add about **1,000 ft** to the highest ground, and then to check that against the minimum height rules (see [RFRC 2.3](/notes/RFRC/2.3)) and the VFR cruising levels.

!!! example "Worked example: a safe altitude from the tints"
    Given: along a leg, the highest tint band is the one whose upper limit is 2,000 ft. A spot height on a small hill within that area reads 2,140 ft (tints show general terrain; isolated tops can stand above the band), and there is an obstacle marked 2,310 (290) a mile off track.

    1. General terrain: the band tops out at **2,000 ft**.
    2. Highest point near the line: the obstacle at **2,310 ft** AMSL beats the spot height at 2,140 ft.
    3. Rule-of-thumb minimum cruising height: $2310 + 1000 = $ **3,310 ft**.
    4. Choose the next suitable VFR cruising level above that for your track.

    The tint told you the country is up to about 2,000 ft; the spot height and the obstacle told you what pokes above it.

![How do tints, spot heights and obstacles give a cruising height?](diagram:terrain-safe-altitude "What to notice: the tint band tops out at 2,000 ft, but the obstacle at 2,310 ft is the highest point, so 1,000 ft above it gives 3,310 ft.")

??? check "Check yourself: why is the highest tint band along a route not, on its own, enough to set a safe altitude?"
    Because the tint shows the general level of the ground between two contours. Hilltops (spot heights) and obstacles stand above that level, so you must scan the route for them and add a margin above the highest one.

## Estimating track and distance

Measuring a track is the first job of every navigation plan, and the exam expects you to do it on a WAC extract.

**Draw the line.** Join the departure and the destination with a straight line. On a WAC that line is close to a great circle, the shortest route (see [PNVC 2.1](/notes/PNVC/2.1)).

**Measure the true track.** Lay the protractor's centre on a **meridian near the middle of the track**, with its north line along the meridian. The meridians converge towards the pole, so the angle your line makes with them changes slightly along its length; the mid-meridian gives the average. Read the direction **from the departure end**, clockwise from true north. The result is a **true** track, because the meridians point to true north.

**Measure the distance.** Use dividers or a scale ruler against the **latitude scale** on a meridian near the track, where **1 minute of latitude is 1 nautical mile**, or against the chart's scale bar. Never use the longitude scale: a minute of longitude shrinks towards the poles.

**Convert to magnetic.** Apply the variation from the isogonal nearest the track: variation west, magnetic best; variation east, magnetic least.

![How does a true track become the magnetic track you fly?](diagram:true-magnetic-compass "Measure true on the chart against a meridian, then apply the variation from the nearest isogonal to get magnetic.")

### Scale arithmetic

When a question gives you a length on the chart and its scale, the scale is the conversion. **1:1,000,000** means 1 mm on the chart is 1,000,000 mm on the ground, which is 1 km. So on a WAC a length in millimetres is the ground distance in kilometres, and dividing by 1.852 turns kilometres into nautical miles. As a rule of thumb, **1 cm on a WAC is about 5.4 NM**. On a **1:500,000** VNC, 1 mm is half a kilometre, so 1 cm is 5 km, about 2.7 NM.

!!! example "Worked example: track and distance on a WAC"
    Given: on a 1:1,000,000 WAC, a leg eastward from Jandakot measures 251 mm. The protractor at the mid-meridian reads 067°T, and the nearest isogonal shows 1.5° W.

    1. Ground distance: $251\ \text{mm} \times 1{,}000{,}000 = 251{,}000{,}000\ \text{mm} = $ **251 km**.
    2. In nautical miles: $251 \div 1.852 = 135.5$, **about 136 NM**.
    3. Rule-of-thumb check: $25.1\ \text{cm} \times 5.4 = 135.5$ NM. It agrees.
    4. Magnetic track: variation west, magnetic best: $067 + 1.5 = 068.5$, **about 068°M**.

    That is a leg of roughly 136 NM, which at 110 kt is about an hour and a quarter.

!!! example "Worked example: distance on a VNC"
    Given: on a 1:500,000 VNC, a leg measures 120 mm.

    1. Ground distance: at 1:500,000, 1 mm is 0.5 km, so $120 \times 0.5 = $ **60 km**.
    2. In nautical miles: $60 \div 1.852 = 32.4$, **about 32 NM**.

    The same 120 mm on a WAC would be twice as far, 120 km or 65 NM: always check which chart and which scale before you convert.

On the latitude scale the arithmetic disappears: open the dividers along the track, then lay them on a meridian beside it, and if they span 42 minutes of latitude the leg is 42 NM.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Three slips are common. Measuring distance on the **longitude** scale, which overstates it everywhere except at the equator. Measuring the track at one end of a long line instead of at the mid-meridian. And forgetting that the protractor gives **true**: the track you fly is magnetic, after the variation.

??? check "Check yourself: why do you measure the track at a meridian near the middle of the line?"
    Because the meridians converge, so the angle between a straight line and the meridians changes along the line. Measuring at the middle gives the average track for the whole leg.

## Orienting the chart in flight

On the ground you read a chart north-up. In the air that becomes a trap: fly south with the chart north-up and a lake on your left in the windscreen appears on the right of the chart. Every feature has to be mentally turned round before you can match it, and under workload that is the step that goes wrong.

The answer is to turn the chart so that the **track line points in the direction of flight**: **track up**. Ground features then appear on the chart in the same relative positions as they appear outside. A lake to the right of the track line on the chart is to the right of the nose; a road that crosses the line ahead on the chart crosses your path ahead. You can glance from the chart to the ground and back without any mental rotation, which is the whole point.

Two habits go with it. **Fold the chart** to the area of the current leg, so it is manageable in the cockpit and the part you need is on top. And keep your **thumb or a mark at the last fix**, so you can find your place instantly after a radio call or a look-out scan. Read from the **map to the ground**: pick the next feature on the chart and look for it, rather than seeing something outside and hunting for it on the chart. [PNVC 2.5](/notes/PNVC/2.5) builds the whole technique of map reading on this.

??? check "Check yourself: you are flying a track of 200°M with the chart held north-up. A railway on the chart lies to the right of your track line. Where is it outside?"
    On your left. Flying roughly south with the chart north-up, the chart's right is your left. Turn the chart track-up and the railway appears on the same side as outside, which is why you orient the chart that way.

## Reading ERSA for runways and restricted areas

The charts tell you where things are; ERSA tells you how to use them. [BAKC 5.2](/notes/BAKC/5.2) walks through an entry field by field. At PPL level the exam gives you a route and expects you to pull out the runway data for the destination and the PRD data for every area the track touches, and to combine the hours with your ETA.

### Runway data

The ERSA **FAC** entry for an aerodrome gives the runway **designators**, the **length and width in metres**, the **surface**, the **slope**, the **lighting** and the **pavement strength**, together with the aerodrome **elevation** (in feet), its frequencies and local procedures. Each piece goes somewhere in your plan: length and surface into the performance charts, slope to tell you which way is downhill, designators to compare with the wind, lighting for use after dark, and remarks for how you join.

![What is in each block of an ERSA aerodrome entry?](diagram:ersa-entry-anatomy "Runway length and width are in metres in the runway table; elevation, variation and the time zone are in the header.")

!!! example "Worked example: runway data at Example Creek"
    Given: the invented ERSA entry in the diagram: runway 06/24, 1,200 × 30, sealed, 0.5% up E, lit; elevation 1,050 ft; right-hand circuits for runway 24. Your POH's take-off chart works in feet.

    1. Length in feet: $1200 \times 3.28 = $ **3,936 ft**, to enter or compare with the chart.
    2. Slope: the runway rises to the east, so runway 24 (pointing roughly 240 degrees magnetic, west-south-west) slopes **downhill** for take-off and runway 06 uphill.
    3. Elevation for pressure and density height: **1,050 ft**.
    4. Lighting: 06/24 is lit, so it is usable for a night landing by a suitably rated pilot.
    5. Circuit: joining for runway 24, fly **right-hand** circuits.

    One line of the table and one remark fed the performance chart, the runway choice and the join.

### PRD data

For each prohibited, restricted or danger area, the ERSA **PRD** section gives its **identifier and name**, its **lateral and vertical limits**, its **hours of activity**, its **controlling authority** and, for a restricted area, its **conditional status**: whether a clearance is normally available (RA1), not to be planned on (RA2) or not available at all (RA3). Specified hours are in **UTC**, so for a WA flight you add 8 hours and compare with your ETA in WST (see [PNVC 2.2](/notes/PNVC/2.2) and [BAKC 2.3](/notes/BAKC/2.3)). An area shown as activated by NOTAM is inactive unless a NOTAM has activated it, and a NOTAM can change any area's hours or limits, so the check always ends with the NOTAMs.

![When is an area listed in UTC active by your watch?](diagram:utc-to-wst-activity "Add 8 hours: an area listed active 2200 to 0600 UTC is active 0600 to 1400 WST.")

!!! example "Worked example: is the area active at your ETA?"
    Given: a restricted area beside your track is listed active 2200-0600 UTC, RA2. You plan to pass it at 1340 WST.

    1. Start of activity: $2200 + 0800 = 3000$, minus 24: **0600 WST**.
    2. End of activity: $0600 + 0800 = $ **1400 WST**.
    3. Compare: 1340 WST is inside 0600 to 1400, so the area is **active** when you get there.
    4. Status RA2: do not plan through it. Either route around it or plan to pass after 1400 WST, and check the NOTAMs either way.

    Twenty minutes either side decided the route.

![Is that airspace active? The pre-flight check](diagram:airspace-active-check "Chart for the boundaries and limits, ERSA PRD for the hours and controlling authority, NOTAM for changes, then ask the controlling authority.")

??? check "Check yourself: the chart shows a restricted area across your track and its limits. Why is that not enough to plan the flight?"
    The chart gives where the area is and how high it goes, but not when it is active, who controls it or whether a clearance is likely. Those come from the ERSA PRD section, and NOTAMs can change them on the day.

## Bringing it together

You are planning a flight from Jandakot to a Wheatbelt strip. On the WAC you draw the line, measure the true track at the mid-meridian and the distance on the latitude scale, and apply the variation. Along the line the scarp's tint band, a spot height and a mast's two figures set your cruising height 1,000 ft above the highest of them. You mark the features you will use: the highway and the ticked railway together, a salt lake right of track, the town where the railway branches. On the VNC you decode the Perth step bases with the legend and plan to stay under them, and you spot a restricted area beside the track. ERSA gives its hours in UTC; adding 8 shows it active when you will pass, so you route around it. The FAC entry gives the strip's runway: length in metres, gravel, slope, unlit. In the air you hold the chart track-up, thumb on the last fix, and read from map to ground.

## Key points

- **2.3.1(a)** Know at sight: red roads, black ticked railways, blue rivers (dashed when intermittent), lakes and salt lakes, yellow towns; spot heights in feet AMSL; obstacles as elevation of the top AMSL with the height above ground in brackets; on the VTC and ERC-L, CTA steps, PRD areas, VFR routes, aerodromes and navaids.
- **2.3.1(b)** Everything else, decode from the chart legend: knowing where to look beats guessing.
- **2.3.1(c)** Tints show the general height, contours the steepness, spot heights and obstacles what stands above; add about 1,000 ft to the highest point for a comfortable height.
- **2.3.1(d)** True track at the mid-meridian, distance on the latitude scale (1 minute = 1 NM), then apply variation. On a WAC 1 mm is 1 km and 1 cm about 5.4 NM; on a VNC 1 cm is 5 km.
- **2.3.1(e)** Fly with the chart track-up so features appear on the same side as outside; fold it to the leg and keep a thumb on the last fix.
- **2.3.2** Airspace boundaries are line styles and colours with the limits printed as upper over lower; you stay clear of a step by staying below its base. The WAC shows none of it.
- **2.3.3(a)** ERSA FAC: designators, length and width in metres, surface, slope, lighting, strength, elevation in feet, frequencies and remarks.
- **2.3.3(b)** ERSA PRD: identifier, limits, hours in UTC (add 8 for WST), controlling authority and conditional status; always finish with the NOTAMs.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Obstacle figures: the big number is elevation AMSL, the bracketed number is height AGL. Measure distance on the latitude scale and track at the mid-meridian, and on a WAC read millimetres as kilometres before dividing by 1.852. Orient the chart track-up and read map to ground. For any PRD question, convert the UTC hours to WST before comparing with the ETA.
