## What the exam expects (PNVC 2.1)

- **2.1.1(a)** Describe the shape of the earth and the way it rotates.
- **2.1.1(b)** Explain latitude and longitude, and how a position is written with them.
- **2.1.1(c)** Tell true north from magnetic north.
- **2.1.1(d)** Explain how distance and direction are measured on the chart and used in navigation.
- **2.1.1(e)** Explain magnetic variation and compass deviation, and apply them.
- **2.1.1(f)** Relate magnetic heading, relative bearing and magnetic bearing to one another.

## Why this matters

Every line you draw on a WAC rests on a few facts about the planet underneath it. The grid you measure your track against is a set of circles drawn on a spinning ball; the distance scale you lay your dividers on exists because a minute of latitude happens to be a nautical mile; and the compass you steer by points somewhere other than the grid's north. In the RPL you learned to quote directions and convert true to magnetic (see [BAKC 2.1](/notes/BAKC/2.1)). The PPL takes you off the local chart and across the state, from Jandakot to Kalgoorlie or up the coast to Geraldton, where those facts start to show: variation changes along the route, the longitude scale lies about distance, and a town off your wingtip has to be turned into a bearing you can plot. The exam asks short descriptive questions on the earth and its grid, a true to magnetic conversion, and almost always one MH plus RB question. This lesson builds each from the globe outwards.

## The shape and rotation of the earth

Picture the earth from space. It looks like a perfect ball, and for almost all of your navigation you can treat it as one. It is in fact very slightly squashed: the spin flings the equator outwards, so the diameter across the equator is about **43 km greater** than the diameter from pole to pole. A ball flattened at the poles in this way is an **oblate spheroid**. Forty-three kilometres out of nearly 13,000 is about a third of one percent, which is why the charts and the nautical mile can treat the earth as a sphere without you ever noticing.

The earth spins about an axis through its centre. The two places where that axis comes out of the surface are the **geographic poles**, also called the **true** north and south poles. Everything on the chart's grid is measured from them, so they are the fixed reference for the whole of navigation.

The spin is from **west to east**, once a day. Stand in Perth and the ground carries you eastward towards the sun each morning, so the sun appears to rise in the east and travel west across the sky. The same rotation means places further east see the sun first: sunrise reaches Kalgoorlie before it reaches Perth, and Sydney well before either. That is the root of time zones and of the arc-to-time arithmetic in [PNVC 2.2](/notes/PNVC/2.2). The earth turns 360 degrees in 24 hours, which is 15 degrees of longitude every hour; hold on to that number.

??? check "Check yourself: which way does the earth rotate, and why does that make the sun rise earlier in Kalgoorlie than in Perth?"
    From west to east. Kalgoorlie is east of Perth, so the rotation carries it round to face the sun first; every place further east sees sunrise, noon and sunset earlier by about 4 minutes for each degree of longitude.

## Latitude and longitude: a grid on a ball

To say where something is on a ball you need two numbers, the way a street directory needs a letter and a number. The earth's grid uses two sets of circles tied to the spin axis.

### Latitude

Halfway between the poles, a circle runs round the earth's middle: the **equator**. **Latitude** is how far north or south of the equator a place is, measured as an angle at the earth's centre, from **0 degrees** at the equator to **90 degrees** at each pole. Every place at the same latitude lies on a circle parallel to the equator, so these circles are called **parallels of latitude**. They shrink as you go towards the poles, until the parallel of 90 degrees is a single point. Perth is at about **32 degrees south**.

### Longitude

Now draw half-circles from pole to pole, like the segments of a peeled orange. Each one is a **meridian**. One of them, through Greenwich in England, was chosen as the starting line: the **prime meridian**, longitude 0. **Longitude** is the angle east or west of the prime meridian, from **0** to **180 degrees** each way; 180 east and 180 west are the same meridian on the far side of the world. Perth is at about **116 degrees east**. Every meridian runs exactly north and south, which is why you measure true direction against a meridian on the chart.

### Writing a position

Each degree is divided into **60 minutes** ('), and minutes into decimals or seconds. A position is always written **latitude first, then longitude**, each with its hemisphere letter. Perth Airport is **31 56.5S 115 58.0E**: 31 degrees 56.5 minutes south, 115 degrees 58 minutes east. ERSA gives every aerodrome's position in this form, and on the WAC you plot it against the **graticule**, the printed grid of parallels and meridians, which on the WAC is marked every 30 minutes with a tick for each minute.

![Which symbols should you be able to pick out on a visual chart?](diagram:chart-legend-symbols "The graticule is the latitude and longitude grid; the minute ticks along a meridian are a ready-made distance scale.")

??? check "Check yourself: a position is given as 30 47S 121 28E. Which number is the latitude, and which way would you go from it to reach the equator?"
    The first, 30 degrees 47 minutes south, is the latitude: latitude is always written first. South of the equator, so you would go north, about 30 degrees, to reach it.

### Great circles and rhumb lines

Slice an orange straight through its centre and the cut traces the biggest circle the skin can carry. On the earth, any circle whose plane passes through the centre is a **great circle**: the equator is one, and every meridian together with its partner on the far side makes one. Parallels other than the equator are **small circles**, because their planes miss the centre. The useful fact is this: **the shortest distance between two points on the earth lies along the great circle through them**. Stretch a string tight between two pins on a globe and it lies along a great circle.

A great circle has an awkward property for a pilot steering a compass heading: except along a meridian or the equator, it crosses each meridian at a slightly different angle. The meridians converge towards the pole, so the "straight" path keeps changing its direction measured from north.

The alternative is a **rhumb line**, a line that crosses every meridian at the **same angle**. Fly a constant true track and you follow a rhumb line. It is easy to steer but slightly longer than the great circle, and on a long east-west leg at high latitude it bows towards the equator.

For a VFR flight the difference is negligible. Your legs are short, and the WAC is drawn on a **Lambert conformal conic** projection, on which a straight line is very close to a great circle. So the straight line you rule on the WAC is, for practical purposes, the shortest path. Because the meridians on that chart converge, the line crosses each one at a slightly different angle, which is why you measure its direction at the middle of the leg (next section).

![Why is the shortest route not a constant direction?](diagram:great-circle-vs-rhumb-line "The great circle is the shortest path but cuts each meridian at a changing angle; the rhumb line keeps one angle and is a little longer.")

!!! warning "Common trap"
    "Shortest distance" is the **great circle**, not the rhumb line. The rhumb line is the **constant direction** line. Exam distractors swap the two, or describe parallels of latitude as great circles: only the equator is.

## Measuring distance

The unit of distance is the **nautical mile**, and the reason for it is on the chart's edge. One degree of latitude is 60 minutes, and the nautical mile was chosen as **one minute of latitude**. Today it is defined as **1,852 m**, which matches a minute of latitude closely enough for any navigation you will do. Speed follows: a **knot** is one nautical mile per hour (see [BAKC 2.2](/notes/BAKC/2.2)).

That makes the latitude scale a ruler. Open your dividers across the leg, carry them to a **meridian** near the leg, and count the minutes of latitude: the count is the distance in nautical miles. It works on any chart at any scale, because a minute of latitude is the same length everywhere on the earth.

The longitude scale is not a ruler. Meridians meet at the poles, so the distance between two of them shrinks as you go towards the pole. At the equator a minute of longitude is a nautical mile; at Perth's latitude it is only about 0.85 NM; at 60 degrees it is half a nautical mile. **Never measure distance against the longitude scale** along a parallel.

!!! example "Worked example: why the longitude scale lies"
    Given: Perth Airport 31 56.5S 115 58.0E; Kalgoorlie 30 47S 121 28E.

    1. Difference of latitude: $31^\circ 56.5' - 30^\circ 47' = 1^\circ 09.5' = \mathbf{69.5'}$, so Kalgoorlie is **69.5 NM** further north.
    2. Difference of longitude: $121^\circ 28' - 115^\circ 58' = 5^\circ 30' = \mathbf{330'}$.
    3. At about 31.4 degrees south a minute of longitude is $\cos 31.4^\circ \approx 0.85$ NM, so the east-west distance is $330 \times 0.85 \approx \mathbf{282}$ NM, not 330.
    4. Combining the two by Pythagoras: $\sqrt{69.5^2 + 282^2} \approx \mathbf{290}$ NM.

    Reading 330 minutes of longitude as 330 NM would have overstated the distance by about 40 NM. Measured on the chart against the latitude scale, the leg comes out at about 290 NM.

On a WAC you can also measure against the chart's **scale bar**, or by the scale itself: at 1:1,000,000 a centimetre is 10 km, about 5.4 NM. [PNVC 2.3](/notes/PNVC/2.3) works through chart measurement.

??? check "Check yourself: you measure a leg with dividers. Do you read the distance against the latitude scale or the longitude scale, and why?"
    The latitude scale, on a meridian near the leg. A minute of latitude is one nautical mile everywhere, but meridians converge towards the poles, so a minute of longitude is shorter than a nautical mile everywhere except the equator: about 0.85 NM at Perth.

## Measuring direction

A direction is an angle measured **clockwise from north**, from 001 to 360, written as a three-figure group (see [BAKC 2.1](/notes/BAKC/2.1)). On the chart the north you measure from is the **meridian**, so a direction measured on the chart is **true**.

Because the meridians converge on the WAC, a long straight line meets the meridian at its start at a slightly different angle from the meridian at its end. Measure with the protractor aligned to a **meridian near the middle of the track** and you get the mean of the two, which is the track you will fly. Read the direction from the departure end, so that you do not end up with the reciprocal.

![How do 3-figure directions and runway numbers work?](diagram:compass-rose-headings "Clockwise from north, always three figures; the reciprocal is 180 degrees away.")

Three directions matter once you are airborne. The **track** is the path over the ground, the line you drew. The **heading** is where the nose points. The **drift** is the angle between them, caused by the wind carrying the aeroplane sideways. You plan the track, then work out the heading that will make it good in the day's wind; [PNVC 2.4](/notes/PNVC/2.4) does that arithmetic.

![What is the difference between heading and track?](diagram:heading-track-drift "The nose keeps pointing along the heading while the wind carries the aeroplane sideways along the track; the angle between them is drift.")

## True north and magnetic north

The chart's north is the geographic pole. The compass's north is somewhere else.

The earth behaves as if it had a huge bar magnet inside it, and a freely suspended magnet lines up with that field. The field points towards **magnetic north**, a region in the Canadian Arctic hundreds of kilometres from the geographic pole, which wanders slowly from year to year. So there are two norths: **true north**, along the meridian, fixed by the spin axis; and **magnetic north**, along the field, which the compass needle follows. A direction measured from true north is written °T, one measured from magnetic north °M.

| | True north | Magnetic north |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | The geographic pole, end of the spin axis | Where the earth's magnetic field points |
| Where you find it | The meridians on every chart | The compass needle |
| Does it move? | No | Slowly, year by year |
| Used for | Chart tracks, forecast winds | Runways, ATC headings and winds, what you steer |

??? check "Check yourself: why can you not simply steer the true track you measured on the chart?"
    Because the compass points to magnetic north, not true north, and the two differ by the local variation. The true track has to be converted to magnetic before it can be compared with the compass or the direction indicator.

## Variation and deviation

### Variation: the place

Stand anywhere on the earth and look along the meridian to true north, then at the compass needle. The angle between the two is the **variation** at that place. It is named **east** if magnetic north lies east of true north, **west** if it lies west. The chart joins places of equal variation with dashed **isogonal lines**, or **isogonals**, each labelled with its value.

Variation depends on **where you are**, not on the aeroplane. It changes as you move across the country, and slowly over the years as magnetic north wanders. Around Perth it is small, about **1 to 2 degrees west**; in Sydney it is about **12 degrees east** (verify both against the current chart, because they change). A trip from Perth to the eastern states therefore passes through zero variation and into easterly values, and a habit formed at Jandakot, where a forgotten variation costs you only a couple of degrees, can cost you a dozen there.

### Deviation: the aeroplane

The compass in your aeroplane sits among steel, an engine, radios and wiring, each with its own magnetic field. They pull the needle away from magnetic north. The error is the **deviation**, and the direction the compass shows is a **compass** direction, °C. Deviation depends on **the aeroplane and its heading**: turn the aeroplane and its own magnetism turns with it, sitting at a new angle to the earth's field. Each aeroplane's deviation is measured in a compass swing and written on the **compass correction card** near the compass, often in the form "for 240 steer 237".

![How does a true track become a compass heading?](diagram:true-magnetic-compass "Variation east is subtracted, deviation west is added: 120 degrees true becomes 117 magnetic and 119 compass.")

### Applying them

The chain runs from the chart to the cockpit: **true, then magnetic, then compass**. Variation turns true into magnetic; deviation turns magnetic into compass. One rule covers both steps:

- **Variation east, magnetic least**: magnetic = true − easterly variation.
- **Variation west, magnetic best**: magnetic = true + westerly variation.
- **Deviation east, compass least**; **deviation west, compass best**, in the same way.

Why subtract an easterly variation? If magnetic north lies east of true north, the compass's zero is already turned towards the east, so every direction measured from it is smaller by that amount. Going the other way, from compass back to true, reverse every sign.

!!! example "Worked example: chart track to compass heading in WA"
    Given: track measured on the chart 245°T; variation 2°W; deviation on that heading 3°E (the card says "for 240 steer 237").

    1. Magnetic: variation west is best, $245 + 2 = \mathbf{247}$°M.
    2. Compass, ignoring wind: deviation east is least, $247 - 3 = \mathbf{244}$°C.

    The card's "for 240 steer 237" is the same 3 degrees east of deviation written as an instruction.

!!! example "Worked example: the same idea in the east"
    Given: a track of 038°T near Sydney; variation 12°E; deviation 2°W on that heading.

    1. Magnetic: east is least, $038 - 12 = \mathbf{026}$°M.
    2. Compass: west is best, $026 + 2 = \mathbf{028}$°C.

    Twelve degrees of variation is big enough that ignoring it would put you about 1 NM off track for every 5 NM flown.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Variation belongs to the **place** and is read from the chart; deviation belongs to the **aeroplane and its heading** and is read from the card. And the memory aid works from true towards compass: run it backwards without reversing the signs and your answer is wrong by twice the correction.

??? check "Check yourself: on the way from Perth to Kalgoorlie you hold one heading the whole way. Which changes, variation or deviation?"
    Variation, because it depends on where you are and you cross isogonals as you go east. Deviation stays the same, because it depends on the aeroplane's heading, which has not changed.

## Magnetic heading, relative bearing and magnetic bearing

Cruising north-east over the Wheatbelt, you see a salt lake well out to the left. To use it as a position line, you need its direction from you as a magnetic bearing you can plot. The only thing you can measure directly is its angle from the nose.

That angle, measured **clockwise from the nose** from 000 to 360, is the **relative bearing (RB)**. An object straight ahead is at 000 relative, one on the right wingtip at 090, one behind at 180 and one on the left wingtip at 270. It is the clock code of [BAKC 2.1](/notes/BAKC/2.1) in degrees: each hour is 30 degrees.

The nose points along the **magnetic heading (MH)**, which is the angle from magnetic north to the nose. The relative bearing is the angle from the nose to the object. Add the two angles and you have the angle from magnetic north to the object, its **magnetic bearing (MB)** from the aircraft:

$$\text{MB} = \text{MH} + \text{RB} \quad (\text{subtract } 360 \text{ if the result exceeds } 360)$$

![Where is traffic reported at two o'clock?](diagram:clock-code-traffic "Twelve o'clock is the nose: an angle measured from the nose is added to the heading to give a bearing from north.")

The bearing you have found is **from you to the object**. To plot a position line on the chart, you need the bearing **from the object to you**, its **reciprocal**: MB ± 180. Draw that line out from the lake and you are somewhere on it.

![How do heading and relative bearing add up to a magnetic bearing?](diagram:mh-rb-mb-bearings "The heading is measured from magnetic north to the nose, the relative bearing from the nose to the object; together they make the bearing from north.")

!!! example "Worked example: a lake off the left side"
    Given: heading 070°M; a lake lies 30 degrees left of the nose; variation 2°W.

    1. Relative bearing: 30 degrees left of the nose is $360 - 30 = \mathbf{330}$ relative.
    2. Magnetic bearing to the lake: $070 + 330 = 400$; $400 - 360 = \mathbf{040}$°M.
    3. True bearing to plot: variation west, so true = magnetic − 2: $040 - 2 = \mathbf{038}$°T.
    4. Bearing from the lake to you: $040 + 180 = \mathbf{220}$°M.

    On the chart you draw a line from the lake on 218°T (220 magnetic converted to true), and you are on it.

The formula rearranges for the other questions the exam asks. Knowing the bearing of a landmark and your heading, **RB = MB − MH** (add 360 if negative) tells you where to look: a landmark bearing 200°M while you head 110°M is at $200 - 110 = 090$ relative, on the right wingtip. Knowing the bearing and where it appears, **MH = MB − RB** gives the heading.

This is the arithmetic an ADF uses too: its needle shows the relative bearing of a beacon, and MH + RB gives the magnetic bearing to it ([PNVC 2.6](/notes/PNVC/2.6)).

Now try one before opening the solution. You are heading 155°M and a dam lies exactly off your left wingtip. What is its magnetic bearing from you, and what is your bearing from the dam?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    Left wingtip is 270 relative. MB $= 155 + 270 = 425$; $425 - 360 =$ **065°M** to the dam. The reciprocal, from the dam to you, is $065 + 180 =$ **245°M**. Sanity check: heading roughly south-south-east, the left wingtip points roughly east-north-east, which is 065.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    "Left of the nose" is not a negative relative bearing to add; it is 360 minus the angle. And MB = MH + RB gives the bearing **to** the object. If the question asks for the bearing **from** the object (or the radial you are on), add or subtract 180 at the end.

??? check "Check yourself: why does MB = MH + RB work?"
    Because both are angles measured clockwise and they join end to end: the heading is from magnetic north to the nose, the relative bearing from the nose to the object, so together they span from magnetic north to the object.

## Bringing it together

You are planning Jandakot to Kalgoorlie. ERSA gives you both positions in latitude and longitude, and you plot them on the WAC against the graticule. The ruled line is close to a great circle, so it is the shortest route; you measure its true track against a meridian near the middle, because the meridians converge and the angle changes along the line. You walk the dividers along the latitude scale, not the longitude scale, and get the distance in nautical miles. Along the line the isogonals change, so you note the variation for each leg and convert each true track to magnetic, then to compass with the card. Four hours later, with the sun in the west earlier than at home because Kalgoorlie lies further east, you see a mine headframe 60 degrees right of the nose while heading 085°M: MB $= 085 + 060 = 145$°M, from it to you 325°M, and a position line goes on the chart.

## Key points

- **2.1.1(a)** The earth is very nearly a sphere, slightly flattened at the poles (an oblate spheroid, about 43 km wider across the equator); it spins west to east once a day, 15 degrees an hour, about the axis through the true poles.
- **2.1.1(b)** Latitude is 0 to 90 degrees north or south of the equator along parallels; longitude 0 to 180 degrees east or west of Greenwich along meridians; positions are written latitude first, in degrees and minutes. A great circle is the shortest path; a rhumb line keeps a constant direction.
- **2.1.1(c)** True north is the geographic pole and the meridians point to it; magnetic north is where the compass points, and it wanders.
- **2.1.1(d)** Distance in nautical miles, one minute of latitude each, measured on the latitude scale; direction clockwise from north, measured on the chart against a meridian near the middle of the track, so it is true.
- **2.1.1(e)** Variation (place, from the isogonals) turns true into magnetic; deviation (aeroplane and heading, from the card) turns magnetic into compass. East is least, west is best; reverse the signs going back.
- **2.1.1(f)** MB = MH + RB, wrapping at 360; the reciprocal (± 180) is the bearing from the object to you.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Expect a descriptive question on the shape and rotation of the earth or on what latitude and longitude are, a true to magnetic conversion, and an MH plus RB question. Measure distance on the latitude scale and direction at the mid-meridian, apply "variation east, magnetic least" and the same for deviation, and remember that MB = MH + RB gives the bearing to the object: the question may want its reciprocal.
