## What the exam expects (PNVC 2.7)

- **2.7.1** Name the two kinds of area navigation system that use external sensors: VOR/DME and GNSS.
- **2.7.2** Explain the general principles: the inputs a system needs (air data and other inputs) and the outputs it produces, and what those outputs are used for.
- **2.7.3** Explain VOR/DME area navigation: how it works, its advantages and disadvantages, its limitations (errors, accuracy, reliability, coverage and range), and a typical control panel.
- **2.7.5** Explain satellite navigation: how GNSS works and its elements (GPS, GLONASS and others), its advantages and disadvantages, its limitations, a typical control panel, the approvals needed for IFR navigation, and the enhancements (DGNSS, GLS, WAAS).
- **2.7.6** Explain why an area navigation system's position must be updated, how it is updated (manual entry, automatic updating, and inhibiting updates), and what you see when it updates.

(Element 2.7.4 is reserved.)

## Why this matters

On a flight from Jandakot to Kalbarri there is not a ground beacon on the direct track for most of the way, yet the GNSS on the panel shows you a straight line to the destination, your groundspeed, the wind and the time to go. That is area navigation, and nearly every aeroplane you fly will carry some form of it. The exam asks what such a system needs to work, what it gives you, how the older VOR/DME version differs from GNSS, why each can be wrong, and what keeps its position honest. Knowing the answers is also what stops you following a confident display into the wrong valley. This lesson builds on the radio aids of [PNVC 2.6](/notes/PNVC/2.6) and on the GNSS principles taught in full in [GNSSC 2.1](/notes/GNSSC/2.1).

## What area navigation is (2.7.1)

Before area navigation, an instrument route ran from beacon to beacon. You tracked outbound from one VOR or NDB, then inbound to the next, and the route went wherever the beacons were, not where you wanted to go. A dogleg of 20 nm to pass over a beacon was normal.

**Area navigation (RNAV)** removes that constraint. An RNAV system works out the aeroplane's position continuously, as a latitude and longitude, and so can steer you along a straight track between any two points you choose. Those points are **waypoints**: positions defined by coordinates, or by a bearing and distance from a known point, that need not have anything on the ground beneath them.

To know where it is, a system needs a source of position. The syllabus covers the two that use **external sensors**, signals from outside the aeroplane:

- **VOR/DME RNAV**, which computes position from a VOR radial and a DME distance;
- **GNSS**, which computes position from satellite signals.

There are also **self-contained** systems, such as inertial navigation, that measure the aeroplane's own accelerations and need no outside signal. They appear in this lesson only because they show most clearly why position must be updated (2.7.6).

??? check "Check yourself: what is the essential difference between tracking from one VOR to the next and flying an RNAV route?"
    Beacon-to-beacon navigation can only follow lines to and from the stations, so the route goes where the aids are. RNAV computes your position continuously and can therefore fly a straight track between any two waypoints, whether or not there is an aid at either.

## What goes in and what comes out (2.7.2)

Think of an RNAV system as a navigator with a calculator who never tires. Like a human navigator it needs information to start with, and what it produces is the same set of answers you would work out by hand in [PNVC 2.5](/notes/PNVC/2.5), only continuously and more precisely.

**Inputs.** There are two groups.

- **Air data inputs**: true airspeed (from the air data computer), pressure altitude and outside air temperature. These describe how the aeroplane is moving through the air.
- **Other inputs**: heading from the compass system; the **sensor data**, either a VOR radial and DME distance or the satellite ranges (pseudoranges); the **navigation database** of waypoints, aids, aerodromes and airspace; and the **flight plan** the pilot enters.

**Outputs.**

- **Present position** as latitude and longitude.
- **Track and groundspeed**, from successive positions.
- **Bearing and distance to the active waypoint**, **time to go** and **ETA**.
- **Cross-track error**: how far left or right of the desired track you are, shown on a CDI or a map display.
- **Wind**, from comparing what the aeroplane is doing through the air (heading and TAS) with what it is doing over the ground (track and groundspeed).
- **Fuel and range estimates**, where fuel flow is an input.

The wind output shows why both groups of inputs are needed. Position alone gives track and groundspeed, but not the wind; for that the system also needs the heading and TAS. The difference between the two is the drift, exactly the angle in the diagram below.

![Where does the wind output come from?](diagram:heading-track-drift "Heading and TAS describe the motion through the air; track and groundspeed describe the motion over the ground. The gap between them is the wind's doing, and the computer solves for it.")

!!! example "Worked example: checking the wind the system shows"
    Given: heading 090° M, TAS 110 kt; the RNAV shows track 080° M and groundspeed 110 kt.

    1. Drift: track is $10^\circ$ left of heading, so the drift is **10 degrees left**: the wind is from the right.
    2. Crosswind by the 1 in 60 rule: $\dfrac{10 \times 110}{60} \approx \mathbf{18\ kt}$ from the right.
    3. Groundspeed equals TAS, so the head or tail component is about **zero**.

    So the wind is roughly from the south (on a heading of 090, "from the right" is from about 180) at about 18 kt. The system solves the same triangle exactly; your rough check tells you whether its answer is sensible.

The **uses** of the outputs are route navigation along the planned track, situational awareness relative to airspace and terrain, diversion planning (a direct track and time to any aerodrome) and, on approved equipment, instrument approaches.

??? check "Check yourself: a GNSS receiver with no heading input can still show track and groundspeed. Why can it not show the wind?"
    Track and groundspeed come from successive positions alone. The wind is the difference between motion through the air and motion over the ground, so the system also needs the heading and TAS (the air data and heading inputs) to compute it.

## VOR/DME RNAV (2.7.3)

### The principle

From [PNVC 2.6](/notes/PNVC/2.6), a VOR gives you the radial you are on and a co-located DME gives your distance from the station. Radial plus distance is a fix, called a **rho-theta** fix (rho for range, theta for angle). If you know where you are relative to the station, and you know where a waypoint is relative to the same station, the rest is geometry.

So the RNAV computer lets you define a waypoint as a **radial and distance** from the station: "the 070 radial at 25 nm", for example. That waypoint is often called a **phantom station**, because the computer then presents steering to it as if a VOR were sitting there. The CDI shows your deviation from the track to the waypoint, and the distance readout shows the distance to the waypoint, not to the real station. As you fly, the computer keeps solving the triangle between you, the station and the waypoint.

![How is a waypoint on the 070 radial described?](diagram:compass-rose-headings "Radials are numbered clockwise from magnetic north like the compass rose; the waypoint is a direction from the station plus a distance along it.")

### Advantages

- **Direct tracks** between waypoints, off the airway structure.
- **Simple** in principle, and the display is the familiar CDI.
- Uses **existing ground stations**; no satellites needed.

### Disadvantages and limitations

**Coverage and range.** The system only works **within the rated coverage** of the VOR/DME in use. The signal is line of sight, so range increases with altitude and is lost behind terrain: the 60 nm and 90 nm figures of [PNVC 2.6](/notes/PNVC/2.6) apply. A waypoint based on a station hidden behind high ground is unusable.

**Errors and accuracy.** The VOR bearing itself has an error, typically ±1 to 2 degrees and up to ±5 degrees (verify against your reference text). An angular error turns into a sideways error that grows with distance from the station, by exactly the 1 in 60 geometry of [PNVC 2.5](/notes/PNVC/2.5). So accuracy falls off the further you are from the station.

!!! example "Worked example: how big is the position uncertainty?"
    Given: a waypoint 50 nm from the VOR. VOR bearing error between ±1 and ±5 degrees.

    1. Sideways error for 1 degree at 50 nm: $\dfrac{1 \times 50}{60} \approx \mathbf{0.8\ nm}$.
    2. Sideways error for 5 degrees at 50 nm: $\dfrac{5 \times 50}{60} \approx \mathbf{4.2\ nm}$.

    So a waypoint 50 nm out may be uncertain by roughly **1 to 4 nm** across the radial, before any DME error is added. The same bearing error close to the station costs far less.

![Why does VOR/DME RNAV get less accurate away from the station?](diagram:vor-dme-rnav-error "What to notice: the bearing error is an angle, so its sideways cost grows with distance: about 0.8 nm for 1 degree and 4.2 nm for 5 degrees at 50 nm.")

Try it nearer the station. With a VOR bearing error of ±2 degrees, how far across the radial could a waypoint **20 nm** from the station be in error?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    $\dfrac{2 \times 20}{60} \approx$ **0.7 nm** either side. At 20 nm the same 2 degree error that would cost about 1.7 nm at 50 nm costs well under a mile, which is why VOR/DME RNAV is most accurate near the station.

**Slant range.** DME measures slant range, not ground distance. High and close to the station the difference matters; a few miles out it does not.

**Reliability** depends on the ground station and on the receiver: a station off the air for maintenance, a sector listed as unusable in ERSA, or a receiver fault all remove the fix.

Few light aircraft now carry a dedicated VOR/DME RNAV unit; the function lives on inside the flight management systems of larger aircraft.

### A typical control panel

A typical VOR/DME RNAV panel has:

- a **mode selector**: VOR (ordinary VOR operation), RNAV en route, RNAV approach;
- a **frequency window** for the VOR/DME station;
- a **waypoint definition** for each stored waypoint, entered as a radial and a distance from the tuned station;
- output on the normal **CDI or HSI**, with a **distance readout** to the waypoint.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Assuming RNAV accuracy is the same everywhere. VOR/DME RNAV is good near the station and progressively worse away from it, and it fails completely outside rated coverage or behind terrain. A distractor that says its accuracy is "independent of distance" is wrong.

??? check "Check yourself: why does a VOR/DME RNAV waypoint 60 nm from the station carry a larger position error than one 15 nm away, when the equipment is the same?"
    The VOR's error is an angle. The same angle produces a sideways error in proportion to distance (1 in 60), so at four times the distance the cross-radial error is about four times as large.

## Satellite navigation (2.7.5)

### The principle and the elements

GNSS fixes position by measuring the distance to several satellites at once, from the time each signal takes to arrive. [GNSSC 2.1](/notes/GNSSC/2.1) teaches this in full; the essentials for this subtopic are:

- **Four satellites** give a 3D position (three for latitude, longitude and height, one more to solve the receiver's clock error). Three give only a 2D position.
- **Five** allow **RAIM** (receiver autonomous integrity monitoring) to detect a faulty satellite; **six** allow it to be excluded.

A GNSS has three **segments**:

- the **space segment**, the satellite constellations. **GPS** (United States) has about 31 satellites in six orbital planes at about 20,200 km. **GLONASS** is the Russian system, **Galileo** the European, **BeiDou** the Chinese; modern receivers use several;
- the **control segment**, ground stations that track the satellites and upload corrections to their clocks and orbits;
- the **user segment**, the receiver in the aeroplane, which measures each signal's travel time and computes the pseudoranges.

### Advantages

- **Worldwide coverage**, independent of ground stations.
- **High accuracy**, typically 5 to 15 m, the same over the Nullarbor as over Perth.
- Continuous position with **track, groundspeed, distance and time to go**.
- Cheap receivers and tablet applications, with **moving-map** situational awareness.
- The basis of RNP and GNSS instrument approaches.

Compare the VOR/DME worked example above: a VOR/DME waypoint 50 nm from the station may be uncertain by miles; a GNSS position is good to tens of metres wherever you are.

### Disadvantages and limitations

**Errors.** Satellite clock and ephemeris errors, ionospheric and tropospheric delay, **multipath** reflections, receiver noise, and poor satellite geometry (a high **dilution of precision**) all degrade the fix. [GNSSC 2.1](/notes/GNSSC/2.1) explains each.

**Reliability.** The signals are weak, so GNSS is vulnerable to **jamming, spoofing and interference**. The antenna can lose satellites under the wing in a steep turn or in a valley. The **database** must be current, or the waypoints and airspace it shows may be wrong.

**Coverage and range.** Coverage is global and range unlimited, but **availability** depends on the satellites in view at that moment. RAIM may be unavailable for periods, which is why IFR receivers **predict RAIM** before flight.

**The pilot.** The greatest hazard is **over-reliance**: a VFR pilot who stops map reading because the screen is so convincing, and has no idea where they are when it fails or when a mistyped waypoint leads them astray.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    "GNSS is accurate to 15 m, so I am where it says." Accuracy is not integrity. A faulty satellite, a jammed signal or a wrong waypoint gives a confident, precise-looking and wrong answer. RAIM exists to catch the first; only your cross-check against the ground catches the last.

### A typical control panel

A panel-mounted GNSS unit typically has:

- a **direct-to** key, to fly straight to any waypoint;
- **flight plan** pages, to enter and check the route;
- a **nearest** function, listing aerodromes with bearing and distance;
- a **CDI** output with selectable **scale** (how many nautical miles full-scale deflection represents);
- an **OBS** mode, to fly a selected course to or from a waypoint;
- a **map** page;
- **message** and **RAIM** annunciators.

Hand-held and tablet applications offer the same functions without the IFR approval.

The cross-track error on the CDI is in nautical miles, not degrees. To turn it into a heading correction, use the 1 in 60 rule exactly as in [PNVC 2.5](/notes/PNVC/2.5).

!!! example "Worked example: from cross-track error to a heading correction"
    Given: 30 nm along a leg, the GNSS shows a cross-track error of 1.5 nm right of track, with 45 nm to go.

    1. Track error: $\dfrac{1.5 \times 60}{30} = \mathbf{3^\circ}$ right.
    2. Closing angle: $\dfrac{1.5 \times 60}{45} = \mathbf{2^\circ}$.
    3. Alter heading left $3 + 2 = \mathbf{5^\circ}$ to reach the destination; on regaining track, turn back right $\mathbf{2^\circ}$.

    The GNSS gives you the offset precisely; the correction is still your arithmetic, and a glance at the map confirms the offset is real.

### Approvals for IFR navigation

GNSS may be used as the sole means of navigation under IFR only when all of these hold (verify the current requirements in AIP GEN 1.5):

- the receiver is **certified** to the relevant standard (TSO C129, C145/C146 or C196 classes);
- it is **installed and approved** under the aircraft's flight manual supplement;
- the **navigation database is current**;
- **integrity** is provided by RAIM or SBAS;
- the **pilot** holds the GNSS endorsement and has been trained on that unit.

For VFR, GNSS is an aid. Below 2,000 ft AGL you must navigate by visual reference, and above that visual navigation remains the primary method, with GNSS used to confirm it (check the current Part 91 MOS and VFRG wording; [GNSSC 2.1](/notes/GNSSC/2.1) covers the rules).

### Enhancements

Plain GNSS errors can be measured at a point whose position is known exactly, and that is the idea behind every enhancement.

- **DGNSS** (differential GNSS): a ground station at a surveyed position measures the error in each satellite's signal and broadcasts corrections to nearby receivers.
- **SBAS** (satellite-based augmentation), such as **WAAS** in the United States and EGNOS in Europe: a network of ground stations computes corrections and integrity information and broadcasts them from geostationary satellites, enabling approaches with vertical guidance. Australia's **SouthPAN** SBAS is being introduced; check its current aviation status before relying on it.
- **GLS** (GBAS landing system): a ground-based augmentation station at the aerodrome provides corrections for precision approaches there.

??? check "Check yourself: what do DGNSS, SBAS and GBAS have in common, and what is the main difference between them?"
    All three measure GNSS errors at a known position and broadcast corrections (and integrity data). DGNSS and GBAS broadcast from a ground station to nearby users, GBAS specifically for approaches at one aerodrome; SBAS broadcasts from geostationary satellites over a whole region.

## Updating area navigation systems (2.7.6)

### Why position must be updated

Picture dead reckoning from [PNVC 2.5](/notes/PNVC/2.5): your DR position gets steadily worse the longer you go without a fix. A self-contained system such as inertial navigation does exactly the same. It integrates its measured accelerations, so small errors accumulate and the position **drifts with time**. And any RNAV system's position is only as good as its last sensor input. So the position has to be **updated** from external references.

### Three ways to update

- **Manual updating.** The pilot enters a known position, for example when overhead a fix, or corrects the displayed position.
- **Automatic updating.** The system continuously corrects itself from VOR/DME, DME/DME or GNSS.
- **Inhibiting updating.** The pilot stops automatic updates when the sensor data is known to be unreliable: a DME behind terrain, an area of GNSS jamming, a station known to be in error. A bad update would replace a good position with a wrong one, so it is better to keep the last good position and let it drift slowly than to accept a sudden wrong jump.

### What an update looks like

When a system updates, you may see:

- a **jump** in the displayed position;
- a **sudden change** in the computed track, groundspeed or wind;
- an **annunciator** showing the sensor in use (GPS, DME/DME, VOR/DME) or a message such as "position updated";
- a change in the **ETA** and **cross-track error** afterwards.

A jump is not always a fault: a system that had drifted and has just been corrected will jump back to the truth. That is why you judge an update by checking it against an independent source. A VFR pilot's equivalent is comparing the GNSS position with a visual fix after every leg.

??? check "Check yourself: during an exercise that NOTAMs warn will jam GNSS, why might a pilot of an aeroplane with inertial and GNSS sensors inhibit GNSS updating?"
    A jammed or spoofed GNSS could feed the system a wrong position, and an automatic update would then replace a good inertial position with a bad one. Inhibiting updates keeps the last good position, which drifts only slowly, until reliable updates are available again.

## Bringing it together

You are flying VFR at 6,500 ft from Jandakot towards Kalbarri on a direct GNSS track. The receiver shows groundspeed 118 kt, a wind of about 20 kt from the south-east, and a cross-track error of 0.4 nm; your map shows the same offset from the highway, so you accept it. Approaching an area that NOTAMs warn of GNSS interference, a RAIM message appears and the position jumps 3 nm. You do not follow it. The map still agrees with your DR, and the aeroplane's VOR/DME, tuned to a station 30 nm behind you and well inside the 90 nm coverage for your level, gives a radial and distance that match the map too. GNSS is now an untrusted sensor, so you navigate by map and clock, using the VOR/DME as a cross-check that is accurate this close to the station, until RAIM is restored and the GNSS position agrees with the ground again.

??? check "Check yourself: in that scenario, which outputs, inputs and limitations of each system came into play?"
    The GNSS gave position, groundspeed, wind and cross-track error (outputs), computed from satellite ranges plus heading and air data (inputs). Its limitations were integrity and interference, flagged by RAIM. The VOR/DME was usable because it was within rated coverage and close to the station, where its angular error costs little distance.

## Key points

- **2.7.1** Area navigation flies straight tracks between waypoints rather than beacon to beacon. The two external-sensor types are VOR/DME and GNSS.
- **2.7.2** Inputs: air data (TAS, pressure altitude, temperature) and others (heading, sensor data, database, flight plan). Outputs: position, track, groundspeed, bearing and distance to waypoint, time to go and ETA, cross-track error, wind, fuel. Wind needs both heading/TAS and track/GS.
- **2.7.3** VOR/DME RNAV is a rho-theta fix with waypoints as phantom stations defined by radial and distance. Simple and ground-based, but limited to rated coverage and line of sight, with accuracy falling with distance from the station (1 to 4 nm at 50 nm) and DME slant range close in.
- **2.7.5** GNSS: space, control and user segments; four satellites for 3D, five for RAIM, six to exclude a fault. Accurate (5 to 15 m) and global, but vulnerable to errors, jamming and over-reliance. IFR use needs a certified, approved installation, a current database, integrity and a trained pilot. Enhancements: DGNSS, SBAS (WAAS, SouthPAN), GLS.
- **2.7.6** Position drifts and is only as good as its last sensor, so it is updated: manually, automatically, or inhibited when the sensor is suspect. Updates show as position jumps, changes in track, groundspeed and wind, and sensor annunciations.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Four satellites for a 3D fix, five for RAIM, six to exclude a faulty one. VOR/DME RNAV accuracy falls off with distance from the station and is limited by line of sight and rated coverage. Expect "which is an output" and "which is a limitation" questions, so know the lists. GNSS for VFR is an aid, never a substitute for map reading, and IFR use needs a certified installation, a current database and a trained pilot. You inhibit updating when the sensor is suspect, not when it is working.
