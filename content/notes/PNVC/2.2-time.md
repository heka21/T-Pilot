## What the exam expects (PNVC 2.2)

- **2.2.1** Explain what UTC, local mean time, local standard time and local summer time are, and how they differ.
- **2.2.2** Read the beginning and end of civil twilight from the AIP daylight and darkness graphs, to within 5 minutes.
- **2.2.3** Convert between local mean time, UTC, local standard time and local summer time.
- **2.2.4** List the conditions that can make useful daylight end before the time the darkness graph gives.

## Why this matters

In the RPL you learned to turn Western Standard Time into UTC and back (see [BAKC 2.3](/notes/BAKC/2.3)). The PPL takes you far enough across the state for a second kind of time to matter: time by the sun. Leave Jandakot for Kalgoorlie at three in the afternoon in June and you are flying towards an earlier sunset, because Kalgoorlie sits more than 5 degrees of longitude east of Perth. Last light at your destination, not at your departure point, decides when you must be on the ground. The AIP gives first and last light as graphs in local mean time, so every reading has to be converted before it means anything on your watch, and a flight to Melbourne or Adelaide in summer adds daylight saving on top. The exam supplies a graph or a reading and asks you to carry it through to WST without losing a date, and it asks what can steal daylight before the graph says it is gone.

## Four clocks for one moment

Picture yourself on the apron at Jandakot at the instant the sun is due south, highest in the sky. Four different clocks could describe that moment, and each exists for a reason.

### UTC: one clock for the whole world

**UTC (Coordinated Universal Time)** is the time at the Greenwich meridian, longitude 0, kept by atomic clocks and never moved for summer. It is the single time used in aviation: flight notifications, forecasts, NOTAM, SARTIMEs and ATC logs. It is written as a 4-figure group, often with a **Z** for "Zulu": 0330Z. BAKC 2.3 covers it in full. In this lesson UTC is the hub: every conversion passes through it.

### Local mean time: time by the sun

Before railways and telegraphs, every town kept its own time from the sun. Noon was the moment the sun crossed the town's meridian, so a town a little east of yours had its noon a little earlier. That is **local mean time (LMT)**: time based on the sun's mean position relative to your own meridian, with noon LMT the moment the mean sun crosses it. ("Mean" because the real sun runs a few minutes fast or slow through the year; LMT uses an average sun that keeps perfect time.)

LMT is tied to longitude, which is exactly why it matters to you. Sunrise and sunset sweep across the country from east to west with the earth's rotation. The earth turns 360 degrees in 24 hours, so the sun appears to move **15 degrees of longitude every hour** (see [PNVC 2.1](/notes/PNVC/2.1)). Every place has its own LMT, and two places one degree of longitude apart differ by 4 minutes. The daylight and darkness graphs give their times in LMT because that lets one graph serve every longitude: the sun rises at much the same LMT at Perth and at Kalgoorlie on the same latitude and date, even though the clock times are different.

### Local standard time: one clock per zone

A country cannot run trains and phone calls on a different time in every town, so it divides itself into **zones**, each keeping one legal clock time a whole or half number of hours from UTC. That clock is **local standard time**. The zone covering Western Australia is set on the meridian of 120 degrees east, and $120 \div 15 = 8$, so **Western Standard Time (WST) is UTC + 8**. Central Standard Time (CST) is **UTC + 9½** and Eastern Standard Time (EST) is **UTC + 10**; the AIP uses the abbreviations WST, CST and EST.

Because Perth lies a little west of 120 degrees east, the sun crosses Perth's meridian about a quarter of an hour after noon WST. Standard time is a compromise that keeps a whole zone on one clock; LMT is the sun's own time at your spot.

![Where do UTC and WST sit against each other?](diagram:utc-and-wst-strip "The same moment sits in the same column: WST is always 8 hours ahead of UTC, so the WST date changes at 1600 UTC.")

### Local summer time: an extra hour in some states

**Local summer time** (daylight saving) moves the clocks one hour ahead of standard time in summer so that daylight falls later in the evening. **Eastern Daylight Time (EDT) is UTC + 11** and **Central Daylight Time (CDT) is UTC + 10½**. Summer time runs from the **first Sunday in October to the first Sunday in April** in New South Wales, Victoria, the ACT, Tasmania and South Australia. **Western Australia does not observe daylight saving**, and neither do Queensland and the Northern Territory. In summer the difference between Perth and Sydney is therefore 3 hours, not 2 (verify the current arrangements in AIP GEN 2.1).

| Time | Based on | Offset from UTC |
|---|---|---|
| UTC | Greenwich meridian, atomic clocks | 0 |
| LMT | The mean sun crossing your own meridian | Your longitude converted to time |
| Standard time | The zone's meridian | WST + 8, CST + 9½, EST + 10 |
| Summer time | Standard time plus one hour | CDT + 10½, EDT + 11; none in WA, Qld or NT |

??? check "Check yourself: why do the daylight graphs give times in LMT instead of in WST?"
    Because sunrise and sunset follow the sun's position relative to your own meridian, which is exactly what LMT measures. In LMT the times depend only on date and latitude, so one graph serves every longitude; converting to a zone time afterwards brings in the longitude.

## Arc to time: turning longitude into hours and minutes

The bridge between LMT and UTC is the **arc-to-time** conversion: how far the earth turns, measured in time, between Greenwich and your meridian. It comes straight from the 15 degrees an hour:

- 15 degrees of longitude = **1 hour**
- 1 degree = $60 \div 15$ = **4 minutes**
- 1 minute of arc = $60 \div 15$ = **4 seconds**

So divide the degrees by 15 for whole hours, multiply the degrees left over by 4 for minutes, then multiply the minutes of arc by 4 for seconds, and round to the nearest minute at the end.

!!! example "Worked example: arc to time for Perth"
    Given: Perth Airport, longitude 115° 58' E.

    1. Hours: $115 \div 15 = 7$ with $115 - 105 = 10°$ left over, so **7 h**.
    2. Remaining degrees: $10 \times 4 = $ **40 min**.
    3. Minutes of arc: $58 \times 4 = 232$ s $=$ **3 min 52 s**.
    4. Total: $7\ \text{h}\ 40\ \text{min} + 3\ \text{min}\ 52\ \text{s} = 7\ \text{h}\ 43\ \text{min}\ 52\ \text{s} \approx$ **7 h 44 min**.

    Perth's LMT is 7 hours 44 minutes ahead of UTC.

!!! example "Worked example: arc to time for Kalgoorlie"
    Given: Kalgoorlie, 30° 47' S 121° 28' E. Only the longitude matters for arc to time.

    1. Hours: $121 \div 15 = 8$ with $121 - 120 = 1°$ left over, so **8 h**.
    2. Remaining degree: $1 \times 4 = $ **4 min**.
    3. Minutes of arc: $28 \times 4 = 112$ s $=$ **1 min 52 s**.
    4. Total: $8\ \text{h}\ 05\ \text{min}\ 52\ \text{s} \approx$ **8 h 06 min**.

    Kalgoorlie's LMT is 22 minutes further ahead of UTC than Perth's: its sun does everything 22 minutes earlier by the clock.

Now try two before opening the solution: Bunbury at 115° 40' E and Geraldton at 114° 42' E.

??? solution "Show the solution"
    Bunbury: $115 \div 15 = 7$ h, remainder 10 degrees, $10 \times 4 = 40$ min; $40' \times 4 = 160$ s $= 2$ min 40 s. Total 7 h 42 min 40 s, **about 7 h 43 min**.

    Geraldton: $114 \div 15 = 7$ h, remainder $114 - 105 = 9$ degrees, $9 \times 4 = 36$ min; $42' \times 4 = 168$ s $= 2$ min 48 s. Total 7 h 38 min 48 s, **about 7 h 39 min**.

The direction follows from the rotation. The earth spins west to east, so places **east** of Greenwich see the sun earlier and their LMT is **ahead** of UTC. For an east longitude, LMT = UTC + arc to time, so **UTC = LMT − arc to time**. For a west longitude it is the other way round, but in Australia every longitude is east.

### A shortcut from LMT straight to WST

WST is the LMT of the 120 degrees east meridian. So the gap between your LMT and WST is just your longitude's distance from 120 degrees, at 4 minutes a degree. Perth Airport is $120° - 115° 58' = 4° 02'$ west of it: $4 \times 4 = 16$ min plus $2 \times 4 = 8$ s, so **Perth LMT runs about 16 minutes behind WST**. Kalgoorlie, 1° 28' east of 120, runs about 6 minutes ahead. West of 120, add the difference to LMT to get WST; east of it, subtract. Use this as a cross-check on the full conversion through UTC, which is the method the exam expects you to show.

![How far is each WA town's sun time from WST?](diagram:lmt-vs-wst-longitude "What to notice: WST is the sun's time at 120°E, so Perth's LMT runs about 16 minutes behind it and Kalgoorlie's about 6 ahead, 4 minutes per degree.")

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Two slips cost marks here. The first is the remainder: after dividing by 15, the leftover **degrees** are multiplied by 4 to give minutes, and the minutes of arc by 4 to give **seconds**, not minutes. The second is the sign: east of Greenwich, LMT is **ahead** of UTC, so you **subtract** the arc to time to get UTC. Adding it puts you 15 to 16 hours out, which the date usually gives away.

??? check "Check yourself: two places are 3 degrees of longitude apart on the same parallel. How far apart are their LMTs, and which is ahead?"
    12 minutes, because each degree is 4 minutes of the earth's turn. The eastern one is ahead: the rotation carries it to face the sun first.

## Reading the daylight and darkness graphs

The AIP tells you when daylight begins and ends with a set of graphs in GEN 2.7. Before you can read them you need to know what they mean by "daylight".

### What the graphs measure

At sunset the sun's upper edge dips below the horizon, but the sky stays bright for a while because the atmosphere scatters sunlight down from above. That glow fades steadily. **Civil twilight** is the period while the centre of the sun is between the horizon and **6 degrees below it**; at the end of it there is still enough light to see the ground and the horizon in clear weather, and shortly after it there is not. The AIP calls the start of morning civil twilight the **beginning of daylight** (first light) and the end of evening civil twilight the **end of daylight** (last light). They are not sunrise and sunset: first light is before sunrise and last light is after sunset.

### How the graphs are built and read

There are separate graphs for the **beginning** and the **end** of daylight, and for different latitude bands. Each is entered with the **date** along one axis, with a family of curves, one for each printed **latitude**. Read off the time on the other axis, and it is in **LMT**. Tables of first and last light for specific aerodromes are also published. (Verify the current layout in AIP GEN 2.7; the format has changed over the years.)

The procedure has five steps:

1. Pick the right graph: beginning or end of daylight, and the band that covers your latitude.
2. Find the date on the date axis and draw a line up (or across) from it.
3. Find where that line meets the curve for your latitude. Your latitude will usually fall between two printed curves, so estimate the point between them in proportion: a place at 31° 30' S sits halfway between curves for 31 and 32 degrees.
4. Read the time off the time axis to the **nearest 5 minutes**. That is the precision the syllabus asks for, and the best the graph can give.
5. Convert the LMT to UTC with your longitude, then to WST or whatever zone you need.

The exam supplies the graph or a reading from it. The skill it tests is interpolating honestly between curves and then not mangling the conversion.

??? check "Check yourself: is last light before or after sunset, and why?"
    After. Last light is the end of evening civil twilight, when the centre of the sun is 6 degrees below the horizon. The sky keeps scattering light down for some time after the sun itself has set.

## Carrying a graph reading through to your watch

Every conversion in this subtopic follows one chain, and it never skips a link:

$$\text{LMT} \xrightarrow{\ -\ \text{arc to time}\ } \text{UTC} \xrightarrow{\ +\ \text{zone offset}\ } \text{local time}$$

Write the UTC line down every time. Mixing LMT, zone time and summer time in one step is where the marks go.

!!! example "Worked example: end of daylight at Perth"
    Given: the end-of-daylight graph gives 1815 LMT at Perth on the day of your flight; arc to time 7 h 44 min.

    1. LMT to UTC: $1815 - 0744 = $ **1031 UTC**.
    2. UTC to WST: $1031 + 0800 = $ **1831 WST**.
    3. Cross-check with the shortcut: $1815 + 16$ min $= 1831$ WST. It agrees.
    4. A day VFR flight must be on the ground **10 minutes before** the end of daylight: $1831 - 0010 = $ **1821 WST** (see [RFRC 2.3](/notes/RFRC/2.3); verify the rule in the current Part 91 MOS).

    1821 WST is the latest landing time you can plan on at Perth that evening.

!!! example "Worked example: beginning of daylight at Kalgoorlie"
    Given: the beginning-of-daylight graph gives 0535 LMT at Kalgoorlie; arc to time 8 h 06 min.

    1. LMT to UTC: $0535 - 0806$ is negative, so you have gone back past midnight. Add 24 hours and subtract: $2935 - 0806 = $ **2129 UTC on the previous day**.
    2. UTC to WST: $2129 + 0800 = 2929$, which is past 2400, so subtract 24: **0529 WST**, and the date moves forward again, back to the day you started on.
    3. Cross-check: Kalgoorlie is 6 minutes east of the zone meridian, so $0535 - 6$ min $= 0529$ WST. It agrees.

    First light at Kalgoorlie is 0529 WST. In UTC it falls on the evening before, which matters for anything you file.

Now try two before opening the solutions. First: the end-of-daylight graph gives 1820 LMT at Bunbury (arc to time 7 h 43 min); by when must a day VFR flight be on the ground there, in WST? Second: the beginning-of-daylight graph gives 0550 LMT at Geraldton (arc to time 7 h 39 min); what is first light in UTC and in WST?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    Bunbury: $1820 - 0743 = 1037$ UTC; $1037 + 0800 = 1837$ WST. On the ground by $1837 - 10$ min $=$ **1827 WST**. Shortcut check: Bunbury is 4° 20' west of 120 degrees, about 17 minutes, and $1820 + 17$ min $= 1837$.

    Geraldton: $0550 - 0739$ is negative, so $2950 - 0739 =$ **2211 UTC on the previous day**; $2211 + 0800 = 3011$, minus 24 gives **0611 WST**, back on the day you started. Shortcut check: Geraldton is 5° 18' west of 120 degrees, about 21 minutes, and $0550 + 21$ min $= 0611$.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Last light at your **destination** governs, not at the aerodrome you leave. Flying east, the destination's LMT is further ahead of UTC, so its last light comes earlier by the clock: on the same evening Kalgoorlie's end of daylight is roughly 20 minutes before Perth's, before any difference in latitude is counted. And the 10-minute margin comes off the end of daylight, not off sunset.

## Summer time and the other zones

Summer time only adds one more link to the chain. Convert to UTC first, then apply the offset for the zone and season you need. UTC itself never changes for summer, which is why it makes such a reliable middle step.

!!! example "Worked example: Melbourne to Perth in summer"
    Given: departure from Melbourne at 0900 Eastern Daylight Time (UTC + 11); flight time 4 h 30 min to Perth.

    1. Departure in UTC: $0900 - 1100$ is negative, so $3300 - 1100 = $ **2200 UTC on the previous day**.
    2. Arrival in UTC: $2200 + 0430 = 2630$, minus 24 gives **0230 UTC**, back on the departure date.
    3. Arrival in WST: $0230 + 0800 = $ **1030 WST**.

    You leave Melbourne at 9 in the morning and arrive in Perth at half past 10 the same morning, after four and a half hours in the air: the 3-hour summer difference has eaten most of the flight.

!!! example "Worked example: Perth to Adelaide in summer"
    Given: departure from Perth at 1400 WST in January; flight time 3 h 10 min; Adelaide on Central Daylight Time (UTC + 10½).

    1. Departure in UTC: $1400 - 0800 = $ **0600 UTC**.
    2. Arrival in UTC: $0600 + 0310 = $ **0910 UTC**.
    3. Arrival in CDT: $0910 + 1030 = $ **1940 CDT**, same date.

    The clocks in Adelaide say 1940 when you land, five and a half hours after you left Perth at 1400: three hours ten in the air and two and a half hours of time zone.

The same chain converts between two LMTs. If you want the LMT at Kalgoorlie at the moment it is 1200 LMT at Perth, go through UTC: $1200 - 0744 = 0416$ UTC, then $0416 + 0806 = 1222$ LMT at Kalgoorlie. Or take the difference in longitude directly: $121° 28' - 115° 58' = 5° 30'$, which is $5 \times 4 + 30 \times 4 \div 60 = 22$ minutes, and Kalgoorlie is east, so it is ahead: 1222 again.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The exam may give a summer-time departure and ask for a WST arrival, or the reverse. Never subtract "Perth is 2 hours behind Sydney" from a summer time: in summer the gap is 3 hours. Go through UTC with the offset for the season and the zone, and WA's time is UTC + 8 all year.

??? check "Check yourself: it is January. What is the time difference between Perth and Adelaide, and why is it not the 1½ hours shown on a standard-time map?"
    2½ hours. Adelaide is on Central Daylight Time, UTC + 10½, in summer, while Perth stays on UTC + 8 all year because WA has no daylight saving.

## When daylight ends before the graph says

The darkness graph answers a narrow question: when does civil twilight end at sea level, with a clear sky and an unobstructed, flat horizon? Real evenings rarely match that. The light that matters to you is the light to see the ground, the horizon and the runway, and several things take it away early.

- **High terrain to the west.** The sun sets behind a ridge or range before it sets behind the true horizon, and shadow fills the valleys well before the published last light. Fly east of the Darling Scarp late in the day and the eastern side is in shadow while the coastal plain is still bright.
- **Cloud.** A heavy overcast, especially a thick layer to the west, cuts out the scattered light that makes twilight. Under it, dusk arrives early and fast.
- **Poor visibility.** Haze, smoke from autumn burns in the South West, dust in the wheatbelt, or rain all absorb light and blur the horizon. The light level falls sooner and the horizon you need for attitude goes first.
- **Low level or a valley.** The graph applies at the surface with an open horizon. Flying low, or in a valley, your horizon is higher and the useful light for map reading ends sooner than the astronomical twilight.
- **A destination lower or further east.** A destination at a lower elevation is in shadow sooner. One further east than the place you read the graph for has an earlier last light by the clock: a few minutes of longitude is a few minutes of light.

For these reasons CASA's advice is to plan to land with a margin on top of the 10-minute rule, and to treat last light as a hard deadline, not a target. A pilot without a night rating who arrives in deep twilight is landing in conditions the rating exists for.

??? check "Check yourself: the graph says last light at your destination is 1840 WST and the evening is clear, but a range of hills lies just west of the airstrip. Why should you plan to be on the ground well before 1830?"
    The graph assumes a flat horizon at sea level. The hills hide the sun and fill the strip's surroundings with shadow before the published time, so useful daylight there ends earlier than 1840, and the 10-minute rule is only the legal minimum.

## Bringing it together

It is a hazy autumn Friday and you want to fly Jandakot to Bunbury after work, about 40 minutes. The end-of-daylight graph for Bunbury gives 1820 LMT for the date. Arc to time for 115° 40' E is 7 h 43 min, so last light is $1820 - 0743 = 1037$ UTC, or 1837 WST, and the 10-minute rule puts you on the ground by 1827. Suppose a NOTAM lists a temporary restricted area on your track near Mandurah until 0930 UTC, which is 1730 WST: you will be clear of it if you leave after then. Now the factors. There is smoke from a burn-off south of Pinjarra, and the forecast mentions haze. Both steal light and horizon, so you add a margin and plan to land by 1815. Back from 1815, 40 minutes of flight and 15 minutes of start-up and taxi puts the latest sensible engine start at 1720, ten minutes before the restriction lifts. The answer is to plan a track around it or wait for another evening. Four kinds of time, one decision.

## Key points

- **2.2.1** UTC is Greenwich time, the hub of every conversion. LMT is time by the mean sun on your own meridian. Standard time is the zone's legal clock: WST = UTC + 8, CST + 9½, EST + 10. Summer time adds an hour (CDT + 10½, EDT + 11) in the south-eastern states from the first Sunday in October to the first Sunday in April; WA, Queensland and the NT do not use it.
- **2.2.2** The AIP daylight graphs give the beginning and end of civil twilight (sun 6 degrees below the horizon) in LMT, entered with the date and your latitude. Interpolate between the latitude curves and read to the nearest 5 minutes.
- **2.2.3** Arc to time: 15 degrees = 1 hour, 1 degree = 4 minutes, 1 minute of arc = 4 seconds; Perth is 7 h 44 min. East longitude: UTC = LMT − arc to time, then add the zone offset. Always write the UTC line and watch midnight.
- **2.2.4** Daylight ends early with high ground to the west, cloud, haze, smoke, dust or rain, at low level or in a valley, and at a destination that is lower or further east. Treat last light as a deadline with a margin.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Longitude ÷ 15 gives the hours; the remainder × 4 gives the minutes; minutes of arc × 4 give seconds. East longitude means LMT is ahead of UTC, so subtract. Convert graph times LMT to UTC first, then to WST (UTC + 8, no daylight saving), and check the answer with the shortcut: Perth LMT is about 16 minutes behind WST. The 10-minute rule applies to the end of daylight, not to sunset.
