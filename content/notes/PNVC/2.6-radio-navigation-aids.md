## What the exam expects (PNVC 2.6)

- **2.6.1** Find the details of an NDB or a VOR (identifier, frequency, position, hours, coverage, limitations) in ERSA or on the ERC, and state the rated coverage of a VOR at heights up to 10,000 ft.

## Why this matters

A VFR flight in WA is navigated by map and clock, but the radio aids are still there to cross-check you: a VOR/DME tells you which line you are on and how far out you are, and an ADF needle points at an NDB. Used well, they turn a doubtful fix over featureless wheatbelt into a certain one. Used carelessly (wrong station tuned, beyond its coverage, or an NDB needle swinging towards a thunderstorm) they put you confidently in the wrong place. The syllabus element is narrow, reading aid details from ERSA and the ERC and knowing VOR coverage, but you cannot read those entries sensibly without knowing what each aid measures. So this lesson teaches the aids from first principles, then where their details are published, then how far you can trust them.

## Two aids, two questions

Every radio aid answers one geometric question, and knowing which question is the whole key to using it.

- An **NDB** with an **ADF** in the aeroplane answers: *which way is the station from my nose?*
- A **VOR** answers: *which magnetic bearing from the station am I on?*
- A **DME**, usually co-located with a VOR, answers: *how far am I from the station?*

The first answer depends on which way the aeroplane is pointing. The second and third do not. That difference drives everything that follows.

## The NDB and the ADF needle

A **non-directional beacon (NDB)** is a ground transmitter that radiates the same medium-frequency signal in every direction (190 to 1,750 kHz). It carries no directional information at all. The cleverness is in the aeroplane: the **automatic direction finder (ADF)** uses its aerials to sense the direction from which the signal arrives, and drives a needle to point at the station.

On the basic instrument, the **fixed-card ADF**, the card has 0 at the top, which is always the nose. The needle therefore shows the **relative bearing (RB)**: the angle from the nose, measured clockwise, to the station. A needle on 000 means the station is dead ahead, 090 means off the right wingtip, 180 behind, 270 off the left wingtip.

You already use this idea in the circuit. Traffic called "two o'clock" is about 60 degrees right of the nose, wherever the nose happens to point. A relative bearing is the same thing in degrees.

![If the nose points 090, where is something at two o'clock?](diagram:clock-code-traffic "Traffic at two o'clock is 60 degrees right of the nose; on a heading of 090 that puts it on a bearing of 150. The ADF needle works the same way.")

Since the needle is tied to the nose, turn the aeroplane and the needle moves even though the station has not. That is why the ADF alone cannot tell you where you are. You need the heading as well.

## From relative bearing to magnetic bearing

The direction of the station that you can draw on a chart is its **magnetic bearing (MB)**: the angle from magnetic north, clockwise, to the station. You get it by adding the angle from north to your nose (the heading) and the angle from your nose to the station (the relative bearing):

$$\text{MB to the station} = \text{MH} + \text{RB}$$

If the sum is more than 360, subtract 360. The bearing *from* the station, the line you actually draw outward from the NDB on the chart, is the reciprocal: add or subtract 180. In Q-code terms the bearing to the station is the **QDM** and the bearing from it is the **QDR**. (The same addition appears in [PNVC 2.1](/notes/PNVC/2.1).)

![Which way is 160 degrees, and which way is its reciprocal?](diagram:compass-rose-headings "Directions run clockwise from north as three figures; any bearing and its reciprocal differ by 180.")

!!! example "Worked example: magnetic bearing from the ADF"
    Given: magnetic heading 120° M, ADF needle on 040 relative.

    1. Bearing to the station: $\text{MB} = 120^\circ + 040^\circ = \mathbf{160^\circ\ M}$.
    2. Bearing from the station (QDR): $160^\circ + 180^\circ = \mathbf{340^\circ\ M}$.

    You are somewhere on the 340° M line drawn outward from the NDB, north-north-west of it. One NDB gives one position line; cross it with a map feature or a second aid for a fix. If you turned onto 160° M, with no wind the needle would sit on 000, on the nose.

Now try one where the sum passes 360. Heading **300° M**, needle on **270** relative (off the left wingtip). What is the bearing to the station, and which position line from the station are you on?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    $\text{MB} = 300^\circ + 270^\circ = 570^\circ$, and $570 - 360 = $ **210° M** to the station. The reciprocal is $210 - 180 =$ **030° M**, so you are on the 030 line from the NDB, north-north-east of it. A check by picture: heading north-west with the station off the left wing puts the station to your south-west, which 210 is.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Reading the relative bearing as if it were a magnetic bearing. A needle on 040 says "40 degrees right of the nose", nothing more. Without the heading it says nothing about where you are, and the exam's distractors include the bare needle reading and the wrong reciprocal.

??? check "Check yourself: you turn the aeroplane 30 degrees right. What happens to the relative bearing and to the magnetic bearing of the NDB?"
    The relative bearing decreases by 30 degrees, because the nose has swung 30 degrees towards the station. The magnetic bearing is unchanged: the station and your position have not moved, and MH went up by exactly what RB went down.

## Why an NDB can mislead you

Medium-frequency signals do not travel only in straight lines. They follow the ground, reflect off the ionosphere and bend at boundaries, and each of these produces a known error that the exam lists.

- **Night effect.** After dark the ionosphere reflects more of the signal back to earth. The ADF receives the direct ground wave and a sky wave from a different direction at once, and the needle wanders. It is worst around dusk and dawn, when the ionosphere is changing.
- **Coastal refraction.** A ground wave crossing a coastline bends, because it travels slightly differently over sea and land. A signal crossing the coast at a shallow angle is bent most, so the needle points slightly away from the true direction of the station. Flying along the WA coast, treat bearings from an NDB whose signal crosses the coastline at a shallow angle with suspicion.
- **Thunderstorm (static) effect.** Lightning is a powerful medium-frequency radio transmitter. The needle can swing towards a thunderstorm and point at it rather than at the station.
- **Mountain effect.** Reflections from high ground make the needle fluctuate or point wrongly near mountains.

Because the signal travels along the ground and its strength depends on the transmitter, an NDB's useful range is set by its power, not by your altitude. Each NDB therefore has its own **rated coverage**, published in ERSA, typically 30 to 100 nm depending on power (verify each station in the current ERSA). Many Australian NDBs have been decommissioned under the navigation aid rationalisation program, so an NDB on an old chart may simply not be there: check the ERSA entry and NOTAMs before you plan to use one.

??? check "Check yourself: late on a summer afternoon a towering cumulus is building 20 nm to your left and the ADF needle has swung towards it. Which error is this, and what do you do?"
    Thunderstorm (static) effect: lightning is a strong medium-frequency source and the ADF points at it. Disregard the ADF until you are well clear and navigate by the map and the clock.

## The VOR: a bearing that does not care about your heading

A **VHF omnidirectional range (VOR)** works the other way round from an NDB: the information is built into the transmission. The station sends two signals. One is the same in every direction (the **reference**); the other rotates, so that the timing of it relative to the reference differs with direction from the station (the **variable** signal). The receiver measures that difference and from it works out the magnetic bearing *from* the station on which the aeroplane lies.

Each of those magnetic bearings from the station is a **radial**. Think of 360 spokes radiating from the VOR, numbered clockwise from magnetic north as three-figure groups exactly like the compass rose. An aeroplane on the **090 radial** is due magnetic east of the station; on the 180 radial, due south. The aeroplane's heading plays no part: point the nose anywhere and you are still on the same radial.

VORs operate between 108 and 118 MHz. Being VHF, their signal travels in straight lines, so it does not suffer the NDB's night, coastal and thunderstorm effects (a VOR bearing still carries an error of a degree or two, which matters for area navigation in [PNVC 2.7](/notes/PNVC/2.7)). The price is that the signal stops at the horizon, which is where the coverage rules below come from.

## Reading the VOR display

The VOR indicator has three parts.

- The **omni bearing selector (OBS)**: a knob that sets a course on a rotating card.
- The **course deviation indicator (CDI)**: a vertical needle that shows whether the selected course lies left or right of you. Fly towards the needle to centre it. The deflection shows angular distance off the selected course, not miles, so a given deflection means fewer miles off track as you get closer to the station.
- The **TO/FROM flag**: whether flying the selected course would take you *towards* the station or *away* from it. A red **OFF** (or **NAV**) flag means the signal is unusable.

The TO/FROM flag depends on where you are, not on your heading. Imagine a line through the station at right angles to the selected course. If flying the selected course from where you are would carry you across that line, you have not yet passed the station and the flag reads TO; if you are already beyond it, FROM. With the needle centred, a FROM flag means you are on the radial you have selected; a TO flag means you are on its reciprocal.

!!! example "Worked example: radial, position and the course to the station"
    Given: the receiver shows the aeroplane on the **090 radial** of a VOR.

    1. Position: a radial is the magnetic bearing *from* the station, so you are **due magnetic east** of the VOR.
    2. Course to the station: the reciprocal of the radial, $090^\circ + 180^\circ = \mathbf{270^\circ\ M}$.
    3. Set **270** on the OBS. The needle centres and the flag reads **TO**: flying 270 takes you to the station.
    4. Had you set **090**, the needle would also centre, but with **FROM**: flying 090 takes you away along the radial.

    To track to the station, set the inbound course with TO showing and keep the needle centred, adjusting heading for wind.

![Which OBS setting gives TO, and which gives FROM?](diagram:vor-radials-to-from "What to notice: on the 090 radial both 270 and 090 centre the needle; only 270 shows TO, the course that takes you to the station.")

Try the reverse. With **030** set on the OBS, the needle is centred and the flag reads **FROM**. Where are you, and what do you set to track to the station?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    Needle centred with FROM means you are on the radial you selected: the **030 radial**, north-north-east of the station. To track inbound, set the reciprocal, $030 + 180 =$ **210**, which centres the needle with **TO**. Fly about 210° M, corrected for drift.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Flying inbound with the outbound radial set (TO and FROM the wrong way round). The needle then works backwards, **reverse sensing**: you fly towards it and it moves further away. If you want to go to the station, the flag must read TO.

??? check "Check yourself: two aeroplanes are on the 180 radial of the same VOR, one heading north and one heading east. Do their VOR indications agree?"
    Yes. With the same course set, both show the same needle position and flag, because a VOR shows position relative to the station, not heading. The ADF needles of the same two aeroplanes tuned to an NDB at that site would point in different directions.

## DME: distance, measured on a slant

**Distance measuring equipment (DME)** is usually paired with a VOR (962 to 1,213 MHz). The aeroplane transmits a pulse, the ground station replies, and the receiver times the round trip to give distance in nautical miles. The distance is the straight line from the aeroplane to the station, the **slant range**, not the distance over the ground.

!!! example "Worked example: slant range close to the station"
    Given: you cross directly over a DME at 6,000 ft. One nautical mile is about 6,076 ft.

    1. Ground distance overhead: **0 nm**.
    2. Slant range: $\dfrac{6{,}000\ \text{ft}}{6{,}076\ \text{ft per nm}} \approx \mathbf{1.0\ nm}$.
    3. Twenty nautical miles out at the same height: $\sqrt{20^2 + 1.0^2} \approx \mathbf{20.02\ nm}$.

    Close in and high, the DME reads more than the ground distance; a few miles out the difference is negligible.

A VOR radial is a position line and a DME distance is a circle around the station. Together they give a **fix**: "the 090 radial at 20 DME". That pairing is the basis of VOR/DME area navigation in [PNVC 2.7](/notes/PNVC/2.7).

## How far can you trust a VOR

Because VHF travels in straight lines, a VOR can only be received while the aeroplane is above the station's radio horizon. Climb and the horizon moves further out; descend behind a hill and the signal is gone. So a VOR's usable range depends on your altitude, and the AIP publishes a **rated coverage**: the range, by altitude band, within which the signal is reliable. AIP GEN 1.5 gives it for VOR and DME (verify against the current AIP):

| Altitude | Rated coverage |
|---|---|
| Below 5,000 ft | 60 nm |
| 5,000 ft to below 10,000 ft | 90 nm |
| 10,000 ft to below 15,000 ft | 120 nm |
| 15,000 ft to below 20,000 ft | 150 nm |

So **up to 10,000 ft the rated coverage of a VOR is 90 nm**, and 60 nm below 5,000 ft.

![How far out can you trust a VOR at your altitude?](diagram:vor-rated-coverage "What to notice: coverage steps out with height, 60 nm below 5,000 ft and 90 nm up to 10,000 ft, so 75 nm is outside at 4,500 ft but inside at 6,500 ft.") Two things reduce it. Terrain between you and the station blocks the signal whatever the table says. And the ERSA entry overrides the table where a particular VOR has a restricted sector or reduced range. NDB coverage, as above, is per station in ERSA.

!!! example "Worked example: is the VOR usable on this leg?"
    Given: you plan to cruise at 4,500 ft and want to cross-check position against a VOR 75 nm away. No ERSA restriction applies to the sector.

    1. At 4,500 ft you are in the "below 5,000 ft" band: rated coverage **60 nm**.
    2. 75 nm is beyond 60 nm, so the station is **outside rated coverage**: you may receive it, but it is not reliable.
    3. At 6,500 ft (a VFR level for a westbound track) you are in the 5,000 to 10,000 ft band: coverage **90 nm**, so 75 nm is **within** it, terrain permitting.

    Plan the cross-check for the part of the leg within 60 nm, or at a level that brings the station within coverage, and check ERSA for any sector limitation.

??? check "Check yourself: why does an NDB have its rated coverage listed station by station in ERSA, while VOR coverage comes from a single altitude table?"
    An NDB's ground wave follows the curve of the earth, so its range depends on transmitter power, which differs between stations. A VOR's line-of-sight signal is limited mainly by your height above the radio horizon, so one table by altitude covers them all, with ERSA noting individual restrictions.

## Finding an aid in ERSA and on the ERC

The element asks you to extract an aid's details, and there are two places to find them.

**ERSA NAV/COMM** lists every aid. For each one it gives:

- the **identifier**, two or three letters transmitted in Morse code so that you can confirm you have tuned the right station;
- the **frequency**: kHz for an NDB, MHz for a VOR, and a channel for a DME;
- the **position**, as latitude and longitude, and the **elevation**;
- the **hours of operation**;
- the **rated coverage** or range;
- **limitations**: sectors unusable, scheduled maintenance.

The **ERSA FAC entry** for an aerodrome also lists the aids at or near that aerodrome, in its communications and navigation aids block.

![Where in an ERSA aerodrome entry are the navigation aids?](diagram:ersa-entry-anatomy "The communications and navigation aids block sits below the runway table; the full details of each aid are in the NAV/COMM section.")

The **ERC-L** (en route chart, low) shows each aid by its symbol (a compass rose for a VOR, a circle with a dot pattern for an NDB, a box for a DME), with its identifier, frequency and Morse code in a frequency box beside it, and the air routes the aids define. The VNC and VTC also show aids with their frequencies.

![Which chart shows the navigation aids without the topography?](diagram:chart-features-panel "The ERC-L carries airspace, routes and navigation aids but no terrain; the WAC carries terrain but no airspace.")

!!! example "Worked example: checking an aid before you rely on it"
    Given: your planned track to a wheatbelt aerodrome passes 40 nm from a VOR/DME shown on the ERC-L, and you will cruise at 5,500 ft.

    1. ERC-L: read the identifier, frequency and Morse code from the frequency box; note the radial your track crosses.
    2. ERSA NAV/COMM: confirm the frequency and identifier, the hours, and any **unusable sector** that contains your track.
    3. Coverage: 5,500 ft is in the 5,000 to 10,000 ft band, rated **90 nm**; 40 nm is **within** it.
    4. NOTAM: check the aid is not withdrawn or under maintenance.
    5. In flight: tune, **identify** by the Morse ident, check the OFF flag has cleared, then use it.

    Only after all five steps is the indication worth comparing with your map fix.

## Using the aids as a VFR pilot

For a PPL holder flying VFR, the aids are a cross-check on visual navigation, not a replacement for it. A VOR radial is a position line, a DME distance is an arc, an NDB bearing is a position line, and any two crossing at a good angle give a fix, exactly as two ground features do in [PNVC 2.5](/notes/PNVC/2.5). Tune, **identify** (listen for the Morse ident), and check the flags before trusting any indication. A needle that looks right on the wrong station is worse than no needle.

## Bringing it together

You are cruising at 3,500 ft north-east from Jandakot towards a country strip, haze has thickened and the WAC features have gone grey. On the ERC-L there is a VOR/DME behind you, 35 nm away; ERSA lists no restriction in your sector and 35 nm is within the 60 nm rated for your level. You tune it, hear the correct Morse ident, and with the needle centred the flag reads FROM on the 047 radial: a position line running away from the station on 047° M. The DME reads 35 nm. Radial and distance give a fix, and it puts you 2 nm left of your planned track, which the 1 in 60 rule from [PNVC 2.5](/notes/PNVC/2.5) turns into a heading correction. The ADF, tuned to an old NDB at your destination, is swinging towards a storm to the south: thunderstorm effect, so you ignore it.

??? check "Check yourself: in that scenario, why is the VOR/DME fix trustworthy while the ADF bearing is not?"
    The VOR/DME was identified, within rated coverage and free of ERSA restrictions, and VHF is not bent towards storms. The ADF was showing a known medium-frequency error, thunderstorm effect, so its bearing was meaningless until the storm was well clear.

## Key points

- **2.6.1** The ADF needle shows relative bearing; MB to the NDB = MH + RB, and the position line is its reciprocal. NDBs suffer night, coastal, thunderstorm and mountain effects, and each has its own coverage in ERSA. A VOR radial is the magnetic bearing *from* the station, independent of heading; centre the needle with TO to go to the station. DME gives slant range. Aid details (ident, frequency, position, hours, coverage, limitations) are in ERSA NAV/COMM and on the ERC-L. **VOR rated coverage: 60 nm below 5,000 ft, 90 nm from 5,000 to 10,000 ft.** Always identify the aid before using it.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Have the VOR rated coverage by heart: 60 nm below 5,000 ft, 90 nm from 5,000 to 10,000 ft (AIP GEN 1.5; verify). NDB coverage is per station in ERSA. Expect questions that ask where an aid's frequency, hours or identifier are published (ERSA NAV/COMM, and the ERC for the frequency box), that a radial is a bearing *from* the station, and MB = MH + RB arithmetic that passes 360. Identify every aid before using it.
