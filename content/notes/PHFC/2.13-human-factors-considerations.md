## What the exam expects (PHFC 2.13)

- **2.13.1** Explain how the brain processes information and makes decisions: how what you sense becomes a mental picture, and how personality, pride and peer pressure, the urge to complete the flight, anxiety, over-confidence, boredom and complacency, the two kinds of memory and their limits, aides-memoire and rules of thumb, workload, and skill, experience and currency each shape a decision.
- **2.13.2** Describe how good decisions are made and the ways a pilot can make better ones.
- **2.13.3** Explain stress: how it relates to arousal, its short- and long-term effects on performance and health, the environmental stressors of heat, cold, vibration and noise, what stress does to performance, and how to manage it.
- **2.13.4** Explain fatigue: its causes and its effects on a pilot, the coping strategies of sleep, relaxation, fitness and diet, and how acute fatigue differs from chronic.

## Why this matters

Most accidents in light aeroplanes are not caused by an engine or a wing; they are caused by a decision. The pilot who pressed on into lowering cloud over the Darling Scarp, the one who launched from Jandakot after a broken night's sleep, the one who missed a radio call while juggling a clearance, a frequency and a QNH: each had the skill to fly the aeroplane and each made a choice, or missed a cue, that set up the accident. This lesson looks inside the pilot. It explains how your brain builds its picture of the world, what bends your decisions, how to make better ones, and how stress and fatigue degrade all of it. The exam tests it with recall (the capacity of short-term memory, the shape of the arousal curve, acute versus chronic fatigue) and with short scenarios that ask you to name the trap a pilot fell into.

## From senses to a mental picture (2.13.1 a)

You are joining the circuit at Murrayfield. Your eyes take in the runway, the windsock and a speck that might be traffic; your ears take in a radio call; your inner ear and the seat of your pants report a gentle bank. None of that is yet *understanding*. Turning it into a picture you can act on takes several stages, and each one has limits.

**Sensing.** The eyes, ears, balance organs and touch gather raw information. Each sense has a short-lived **sensory store** that holds the raw impression for a fraction of a second, long enough for the brain to decide whether to pay attention to it. Most of it is never noticed and simply fades.

**Perception.** The brain interprets what it has noticed, and it does so by comparing it with past experience and with what it **expects**. That is why perception is fast: you recognise a runway without analysing every edge. It is also why perception can be wrong. If you expect "cleared to land", you may hear it in a call that said "continue approach". If you expect the airfield to be ahead, a similar-looking strip will do. The brain fills gaps with what it thinks should be there.

**The mental model.** From these perceptions you build and keep updating a **mental model** of where you are, what the aeroplane is doing and what is about to happen. Keeping that model accurate is **situational awareness**. When the model and reality drift apart, for example you believe you are downwind of the field when you are actually abeam a different one, every decision you make on top of it is wrong, however good your reasoning.

**Attention.** Your capacity to process information consciously is a limited, essentially **single channel**. You can switch quickly between tasks, and well-practised skills such as holding straight and level can run in the background, but you cannot truly attend to two demanding tasks at once. Reading a clearance back while searching for traffic means one of them gets less than it needs.

**Decision and action.** With the picture built, you choose what to do, act, and then **monitor the result** through your senses again. That feedback loop is what keeps the model current.

The basic model is therefore **senses, perception, decision, action**, with feedback. Errors can enter at every stage: a cue not sensed, a cue misperceived, attention elsewhere, a poor decision on a good picture, or a good decision badly executed.

![How does your brain turn what you sense into action?](diagram:information-processing-model "What to notice: everything passes through a single-channel attention and a small working memory, the mental model sits in the middle, and an error can enter at any stage.")

??? check "Check yourself: why can expectation make you hear a clearance that was never given?"
    Perception interprets incoming information against experience and expectation, and fills gaps with what it predicts should be there. If you are expecting a particular clearance, an ambiguous or partly heard call is readily perceived as that clearance. Reading back and listening for the readback's acknowledgement is the defence.

## Memory, and the tools that support it (2.13.1 v to vii)

Your mental model is built and stored in memory, and memory comes in two quite different kinds.

**Short-term (working) memory** is the scratch pad you hold a clearance, a frequency or a QNH on while you use it. It is small and volatile: it holds about **7 ± 2 items** for roughly **15 to 30 seconds** unless you rehearse them, and it is easily wiped by an interruption. Try holding "climb to 2,500, squawk 3412, QNH 1018, contact 118.1" while a passenger asks a question, and you will see the limit for yourself. Grouping items into meaningful chunks ("three-four-one-two" as "thirty-four, twelve") stretches it a little; writing them down removes the problem.

**Long-term memory** is large and durable. It holds your knowledge of the rules, the procedures you have learned, and the motor skills of flying. It is not perfectly reliable, though. Recall depends on cues, it can be reconstructed rather than replayed, and it suffers under stress: the emergency procedure you knew perfectly in the briefing room can be hard to retrieve with the engine coughing.

Because both kinds of memory are fallible, pilots use **aides-memoire**: written checklists, mnemonics such as BUMFH for pre-landing checks and HASELL before stalling, the habit of writing clearances on a kneeboard, and **rules of thumb** such as "1 hPa per 30 ft". They take load off working memory and give long-term memory a cue to retrieve the right procedure. They have a weakness of their own: a rule of thumb applied outside the conditions it was made for, or a mnemonic recited without actually looking at the switches, gives false reassurance. The tool supports thinking; it does not replace it.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Short-term memory is not "unlimited for a few seconds", nor does it keep things for hours. It holds about seven items for well under a minute without rehearsal, and an interruption can wipe it. That is the reason to write down every clearance.

## What bends a decision (2.13.1 b)

Two pilots with the same training can look at the same weather and make opposite decisions. The difference is usually not knowledge but the human factors acting on the decision. The syllabus names them; each one has a mechanism.

**Personality traits.** An **extrovert** tends to act quickly, enjoy a challenge and, on average, accept more risk. An **introvert** tends to be more careful but may be reluctant to ask for help, to query ATC or to admit uncertainty on the radio. Neither is wrong; each needs to know its own bias.

**Pride and peer pressure.** Nobody wants to look incompetent in front of passengers, other club members or the instructor who sent them solo. Turning back, declaring that you are unsure of your position, or cancelling while others are flying all feel like failures. That feeling pushes the decision away from the safe option.

**The desire to get the flight flown.** Often called **get-there-itis** or **plan continuation bias**: once a plan is under way, people tend to continue it even as the evidence against it builds. The aeroplane has to be back at the club for the next booking, the hire is paid for, the destination is only twenty minutes away. It is the most common trap behind VFR-into-cloud accidents, because each small step of continuing seems reasonable on its own.

**Anxiety, over-confidence, boredom and complacency.** **Anxiety** narrows attention onto the thing you are worried about, so other cues are missed. **Over-confidence** reduces checking: you "know" the fuel is fine. **Boredom** on a long, quiet leg to Kalgoorlie reduces vigilance. **Complacency**, the comfortable sense that nothing will go wrong because nothing has before, lets small errors through unchecked.

**Workload.** Both ends of the scale hurt. Too little gives boredom and complacency; too much gives **overload**, where the brain starts **shedding tasks**, often the wrong ones, such as the lookout or the fuel check. Workload peaks are predictable (departure, arrival, the circuit), so you prepare for them by doing what you can in the quiet phases.

**Skill, experience and currency.** **Skill** is what you can do; **experience** is what you have done; **currency** is how recently you did it. Skills fade without practice, especially fine ones like crosswind landings. Experience helps only if it is **relevant and recent**: a thousand hours over the Wheatbelt says little about your ability to handle a gusty coastal crosswind, and a pilot who has not flown for three months is less sharp than their logbook suggests.

??? check "Check yourself: why is plan continuation bias so hard to resist in the air?"
    Because the decision to continue is made in small steps, each of which seems reasonable on its own, while the commitment already made (time, money, people waiting) weighs on the decision. By the time the evidence is overwhelming, the safe options may already be gone.

## Making better decisions (2.13.2)

Good aeronautical decision-making is a **process**, not a flash of inspiration. The point of a process is to slow you down enough to see the options when stress, workload and the biases above are all pushing you to act on the first idea.

### The DECIDE loop

A widely taught model is **DECIDE**:

1. **Detect** that something has changed: a rough engine, a lowering cloud base, a radio call.
2. **Estimate** its significance: does it matter, and how much time do I have?
3. **Choose** a safe outcome: what end state do I want (on the ground at an alternate, back at the departure field)?
4. **Identify** the actions that will get me there.
5. **Do** them.
6. **Evaluate** the result: did it work, and what has changed now?

![How does the DECIDE model work in flight?](diagram:decision-making-decide "DECIDE is a loop: after you act, evaluate the result and start again by detecting what has changed.")

The last step is the one that makes it a loop: the result of your action is itself a change to detect.

!!! example "Worked example: DECIDE on a rough-running engine"
    You are at 4,500 ft between York and Northam when the engine develops a slight roughness and the RPM drops by about 100.

    1. **Detect**: roughness and a small RPM drop; oil pressure and temperature normal.
    2. **Estimate**: the engine is still producing power, so you have time, but it may get worse; carburettor ice is likely in today's humid air.
    3. **Choose**: the outcome you want is a safe landing at the nearest suitable aerodrome, Northam, with the engine problem diagnosed or fixed.
    4. **Identify**: carburettor heat on, mixture check, turn towards Northam, a radio call on the area frequency.
    5. **Do**: apply carburettor heat, turn, make the call.
    6. **Evaluate**: after the initial worsening the roughness clears and RPM recovers, consistent with carburettor ice. You re-enter the loop: continue to Northam and land, or resume the flight with regular carb heat checks.

    The model did not tell you anything you did not know. It stopped you skipping from "rough engine" straight to an action without choosing where you wanted to end up.

### Other tools

**PAVE** is a pre-flight risk check: the **P**ilot (am I fit?), the **A**ircraft (is it serviceable, and right for this trip?), the en**V**ironment (weather, terrain, airspace, daylight) and **E**xternal pressures (passengers, schedules, the desire to get there). The E is where most get-there-itis lives, and naming it on the ground makes it easier to resist in the air. **IMSAFE**, the personal fitness check, is covered with fatigue below.

### Methods of improving decision-making

- Set **personal minimums** (weather, wind, fuel) on the ground and stick to them. A decision made calmly at home is better than one made under pressure in the air.
- Plan alternates and a **decision point** before departure: "if the cloud base at Pinjarra is below 2,000 ft, I turn back".
- **Brief yourself**, and your passengers, before each phase of flight, so the threats and the plan are fresh.
- Recognise the five **hazardous attitudes** and use the **antidote** thought for each: **anti-authority** ("the rules are usually right"), **impulsivity** ("not so fast, think first"), **invulnerability** ("it could happen to me"), **macho** ("taking chances is foolish"), **resignation** ("I'm not helpless, I can make a difference").
- Train scenarios, **debrief every flight** honestly, and learn from others' incidents in ATSB reports and CASA's Flight Safety Australia.

??? check "Check yourself: why does setting personal minimums before the flight improve decisions in the air?"
    Because the decision is made in advance, calmly and without external pressures. In flight you only have to compare the conditions with the limit, which is far harder for stress, pride or get-there-itis to bend than a fresh judgement made on the spot.

## Stress and arousal (2.13.3)

Think about the last time you did a practice forced landing with the examiner watching. A little nervousness sharpened you: you scanned faster and your flying was crisp. Had you been terrified you would have fumbled the checks; had you been half asleep you would have missed the best paddock. That is the relationship this section explains.

**Stress** is the body's response to a demand placed on it. The demand is a **stressor**: an engine failure, a difficult crosswind, a mortgage, a hot cockpit. **Arousal** is your level of alertness and activation, from drowsy at one end to panic at the other. Stressors raise arousal.

![How does stress affect performance?](diagram:stress-performance-curve "Some arousal sharpens you, too little breeds boredom and complacency, and too much narrows attention towards panic.")

Performance follows an **inverted U**, often called the **Yerkes-Dodson curve**. With too little arousal you are bored and inattentive and miss things. A **moderate** level gives the best performance. Too much gives anxiety, narrowed attention, poor memory and finally panic. The peak is lower for complex tasks than for simple ones, so a demanding situation in the air needs you calmer, not more wound up.

### Short-term and long-term stress

**Short-term (acute) stress** is the rapid response to a demanding situation: the engine runs rough, you realise you are lost, a gust drops a wing in the flare. Adrenaline raises your heart rate and sharpens focus, which helps at first. If it is excessive or prolonged, performance degrades down the right-hand side of the curve.

**Long-term (chronic) stress** comes from ongoing problems in life: work, money, relationships, illness in the family. It does not switch off when you strap in. Over time it causes fatigue, sleep problems, illness and depression, and in the cockpit it shows as **preoccupation**: part of your limited attention is still on the problem at home.

### Environmental stress

The cockpit itself can be a stressor, and the syllabus names four:

- **Heat.** A light aeroplane on the apron at Kalgoorlie in January becomes an oven. Heat causes dehydration, fatigue and poor concentration, and you lose water through sweat faster than you notice.
- **Cold.** Shivering, numb fingers and poor dexterity; cold also narrows the blood vessels, a cause of stagnant hypoxia ([PHFC 2.12](/notes/PHFC/2.12)).
- **Vibration.** Continuous low-frequency vibration from the engine and propeller causes fatigue and, at some frequencies, blurred vision.
- **Noise.** Fatiguing in itself, it makes radio calls harder to hear and damages hearing over time ([PHFC 2.6](/notes/PHFC/2.6)).

They add up: a long summer flight in a hot, noisy, vibrating cabin leaves you more tired and less sharp on arrival than the same flight in a quiet, cool one.

### What stress does to your performance

Overload of stress shows in predictable ways:

- **Narrowed attention** (tunnelling): you fixate on one problem and stop scanning.
- **Missed information**: radio calls, warning lights, the fuel gauge.
- **Poor memory**: a checklist step forgotten, a clearance lost.
- **Rushed or poor decisions**, or no decision at all.
- **Reverting to old habits**: under pressure you do what you learned first, even on a type where it is wrong.
- Irritability, and an increase in errors of all kinds.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    "More stress makes you perform better" is only true on the left of the curve. High stress narrows attention and degrades memory; it never widens your monitoring. The answer the exam wants for "best performance" is a **moderate** level of arousal.

### Managing stress

Stress management follows the same logic as any threat: recognise it, remove it if you can, and prepare for what remains.

- **Identify** your stressors honestly, including the ones at home.
- **Remove or reduce** them where you can, and do not fly when you are preoccupied.
- **Plan and prepare** thoroughly, so the flight itself is less demanding: a good plan lowers in-flight stress more than anything else.
- Use **relaxation** and slow, controlled breathing when you notice arousal climbing.
- Keep fit, sleep well and eat properly, so you have reserves.
- **Talk** problems through with an instructor, a friend or a professional.

??? check "Check yourself: why does a long-term problem at home affect your flying even though you feel calm in the cockpit?"
    Chronic stress uses up part of your limited attention through preoccupation, disturbs sleep and wears down your health. Your arousal may look normal, but you have less capacity in reserve, so you miss cues and make errors more readily when workload rises.

## Fatigue (2.13.4)

Fatigue is the state of reduced mental and physical capability that comes from too little sleep, too long awake, or too much effort. Its danger is the same as hypoxia's: a fatigued pilot is a poor judge of their own fatigue.

**Causes.** The main ones are lack of sleep and long duty days. Close behind is a disturbed **circadian rhythm**, the body clock that runs your alertness on a roughly daily cycle, with its low point at about **0200 to 0600**. An early-morning departure after a short night puts you in that low on a sleep deficit. Other causes add to these: high workload, stress, illness, heat, noise, vibration, dehydration, hypoxia and poor diet.

**Effects.** Fatigue slows reactions, impairs judgement, reduces attention, produces lapses of memory, and leads you to accept lower standards: a sloppy approach "will do". It makes you irritable. In its extreme it brings **micro-sleeps**, brief lapses of a few seconds that you may not notice.

### Acute and chronic fatigue

**Acute fatigue** is short-term, the result of a single long day, heavy workload or one poor night. It comes on over hours, and it is relieved by a good night's sleep or rest. **Chronic fatigue** accumulates over weeks from ongoing sleep loss, stress or illness. One night's sleep does not fix it; recovery needs extended rest and often dealing with the underlying cause, sometimes with medical help.

| | Acute fatigue | Chronic fatigue |
|---|---|---|
| Cause | Short-term: a long day, heavy workload, one poor night | Accumulated over weeks: ongoing sleep loss, stress, illness |
| Onset | Hours | Weeks |
| Recovery | A good night's sleep or rest | Extended rest, often addressing the underlying cause; may need medical help |

### Coping strategies

- **Sleep management**: regular sleep of 7 to 9 hours, a dark quiet room, no caffeine or alcohol near bedtime, and avoid flying in the circadian low after little sleep.
- **Relaxation**: breaks during the day, relaxation techniques, and time away from flying.
- **Fitness and diet**: regular exercise, regular balanced meals, and staying hydrated, especially in a WA summer.

Before every flight, the **IMSAFE** check asks the fatigue question alongside the others.

![Am I fit for this flight?](diagram:im-safe-checklist "Illness, Medication, Stress, Alcohol, Fatigue, Eating: a no to any of them means do not fly, and you are the one who decides.")

??? check "Check yourself: a pilot who has slept badly for three weeks sleeps ten hours before a flight. Is the fatigue gone?"
    Probably not. Three weeks of poor sleep is chronic fatigue, which accumulates and is not cured by one good night. It needs extended rest and attention to whatever has been disrupting the sleep.

## Bringing it together

You have booked the club aeroplane for a 0630 departure from Jandakot to Busselton, a birthday breakfast for your partner, who is coming with you. Work was stressful all week and you slept five hours. At the hold-short the ATIS cloud base is lower than forecast and close to your personal minimum. Every element of this lesson is present. You are near the circadian low on a sleep deficit (fatigue), carrying a week of chronic stress, with a passenger and a booking pushing you to go (external pressure and the desire to get the flight flown), and your perception of the cloud is coloured by wanting it to be fine. The tools are also present: the personal minimum you set at home, PAVE's E for external pressures, and IMSAFE's S and F. The good decision is to apply the minimum you set when you were calm, and if the base is below it, drive to breakfast instead.

## Key points

- **2.13.1** Information flows senses, perception, decision, action, with feedback; perception uses expectation and can mislead; attention is a single channel. Short-term memory holds about 7 ± 2 items for 15 to 30 seconds; long-term memory is large but fallible under stress; aides-memoire help but do not replace thinking. Personality, pride and peer pressure, get-there-itis, anxiety, over-confidence, boredom, complacency, workload and skill, experience and currency all bend decisions.
- **2.13.2** Decide by a process: DECIDE as a loop, PAVE before flight. Improve decisions with personal minimums, pre-planned alternates and decision points, briefings, antidotes to the hazardous attitudes, and honest debriefs.
- **2.13.3** Performance follows an inverted U against arousal: best at a moderate level. Acute stress sharpens then degrades; chronic stress erodes health and attention. Heat, cold, vibration and noise are environmental stressors. High stress narrows attention, loses information and memory, and brings back old habits. Manage it by identifying, removing, preparing and relaxing.
- **2.13.4** Fatigue comes from sleep loss, long days, the circadian low (about 0200 to 0600) and environmental stressors, and slows reactions and judgement. Acute fatigue is relieved by sleep; chronic fatigue needs extended rest. Cope with sleep management, relaxation, fitness and diet.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Chronic fatigue is **not** cured by one good night's sleep. And a fatigued pilot is the worst judge of their own fatigue, just like a hypoxic one. Have by heart: 7 ± 2 items for 15 to 30 seconds, best performance at moderate arousal, the circadian low about 0200 to 0600, and the six steps of DECIDE. Scenario questions describe a pilot's behaviour and ask you to name the factor: pressing on to complete the trip is the desire to get the flight flown; tunnel vision under pressure is high stress.
