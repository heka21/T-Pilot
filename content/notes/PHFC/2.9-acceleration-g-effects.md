## What the exam expects (PHFC 2.9)

- **2.9.1** Describe what positive and negative accelerations do to (a) the heart and circulation, (b) vision and (c) consciousness.

## Why this matters

You will feel g on your first lesson of steep turns: in a 60 degree turn over the Jandakot training area you are pressed into the seat at twice your weight, your arms feel heavy and your head wants to sag. Pull out of a spiral dive too hard and you can go well beyond that in a second; hit a sharp down-gust over the scarp, or push the nose over abruptly, and you float up against the harness in negative g. None of this is exotic. What makes it worth understanding is the order in which things fail: under positive g your vision goes before your consciousness, and that gives you a warning, provided you know what it means and ease off. The exam asks for that order, for what g does to the blood, for the difference between positive and negative g, and for what raises or lowers your tolerance.

## What g is

When an aeroplane accelerates, everything in it, you included, has to be pushed along with it. In straight and level flight the seat pushes up on you with a force equal to your weight: that is **1 g**. In a turn or a pull-up the wing makes more lift than the weight, the aeroplane accelerates towards the centre of the turn, and the seat has to push on you harder to take you with it. You feel heavier.

**g** is that force expressed as a multiple of normal gravity, and it is the same number as the aeroplane's **load factor**, $n$, the ratio of lift to weight that you met in [RBKA 3.5](/notes/RBKA/3.5) and [RBKA 3.6](/notes/RBKA/3.6). At 2 g everything weighs twice as much: your head, your arms, and the blood in your body.

For the body, direction matters. The convention uses the long axis of the body, from head to feet:

- **Positive g** (+Gz) pushes you down into the seat, head towards feet. It comes from pull-ups, steep turns, and pulling out of a dive or a spiral.
- **Negative g** (−Gz) lifts you out of the seat, feet towards head. It comes from pushing the nose over sharply, outside manoeuvres, and severe turbulence.

In a level, balanced turn the load factor depends only on the bank angle:

$$n = \frac{1}{\cos\theta}$$

![How does the load factor grow with bank angle? Try it](widget:bank-angle)

!!! example "Worked example: the g in a level 45 degree turn"
    Given: a level, balanced turn at 45 degrees of bank; pilot's normal weight equivalent to 80 kg.

    1. Load factor: $n = 1/\cos 45° = 1/0.707 = \mathbf{1.41\ g}$.
    2. Felt weight: $80 \times 1.41 = \mathbf{113\ kg}$ equivalent.

    You feel about 40 percent heavier. That is comfortable, but every part of you is heavier by the same proportion, including the blood the heart has to lift to your brain.

Try one before opening the solution: what g do you pull in a level turn at 70 degrees of bank?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    $\cos 70° = 0.342$, so $n = 1/0.342 = \mathbf{2.9\ g}$ (2.92). The step from 45 degrees (1.41 g) to 70 degrees doubles the g, because $1/\cos\theta$ climbs steeply as the bank passes 60 degrees. At 2.9 g a relaxed pilot is in the range where heaviness and the first greying of vision can begin.

## Why g is a problem of blood

The body copes well with being heavy for a short time: muscles and bones can take a lot. The real problem is **blood**, because blood is a fluid and fluids flow downhill under load.

Your brain sits roughly **30 cm** above your heart. At 1 g the heart pumps blood up that column to the head all day without effort. The pressure needed to lift a column of fluid is proportional to its density, its height and the acceleration acting on it:

$$\Delta p = \rho \, (n\,g) \, h$$

The height $h$ and the density $\rho$ of blood do not change, but at load factor $n$ the effective gravity is $n$ times larger, so the pressure the heart must find just to get blood up to head level rises in direct proportion to the g. At 3 g the column "weighs" three times as much. Above a certain g the heart cannot supply that extra pressure, and the blood reaching the head dwindles.

There is a second, slower effect lower down. Below the heart, the same extra weight of blood stretches the veins of the legs and abdomen, and blood **pools** there. Less blood returns to the heart, so the heart has less to pump out, and the pressure at head level falls further.

![Why does g starve the brain of blood?](diagram:g-blood-column "What to notice: at 3 g the 30 cm column of blood from heart to eyes weighs three times as much, while blood pools in the legs; the pressure needed grows in step with the g.")

The body fights back. Pressure sensors in the arteries of the neck detect the fall, and reflexes speed up the heart and tighten the blood vessels to raise the pressure. But those reflexes take **several seconds** to act. A slow build-up of g gives them time; a sudden one hits before they can respond. That is why the same g is far worse when it arrives quickly.

![What happens to your vision as g increases?](diagram:g-effects-on-vision "Grey-out, tunnel vision and black-out come before G-LOC: vision goes first, so ease off at the first greying. Negative g brings red-out.")

??? check "Check yourself: why is a sudden pull to 3 g more likely to affect your vision than the same 3 g built up over several seconds?"
    The reflexes that raise heart rate and tighten blood vessels take several seconds to act. With a slow onset they keep up and the pressure at head level holds; with a sudden one the pressure falls before they respond.

## Positive g: circulation, vision, consciousness

### What happens to the circulation

Under positive g blood is driven towards the feet. It **pools in the legs and lower body**, less returns to the heart, the heart pumps out less, and the blood pressure at head level falls. Blood flow to the eyes and the brain drops. The heart rate climbs as the body tries to compensate. Starving the tissues of oxygen because the blood is not circulating where it is needed is a form of **stagnant hypoxia** (see [PHFC 2.12](/notes/PHFC/2.12)).

![Which kind of hypoxia does positive g cause?](diagram:hypoxia-types-and-causes "Positive g causes stagnant hypoxia: the blood is oxygenated, but it is not circulating to the brain and eyes.")

### What happens to vision

The eyes are affected first, before the brain, for a mechanical reason. The eyeball is a pressurised ball, and the fluid inside it pushes back on the small blood vessels feeding the retina. Blood must arrive at more than that internal pressure to get in. As g rises and the pressure at head level falls, the retina's supply fails while the brain, which has no such back-pressure, is still getting enough.

The effects arrive in a fixed order. These figures are approximate, for a relaxed, unprotected person, as given in the references; individuals vary widely:

| Approximate g | Effect |
|---|---|
| +2 to +3 g | Heaviness, hard to raise the arms and head; **grey-out** may begin |
| +3 to +4 g | **Grey-out**: loss of colour, dimming; **tunnel vision**: loss of peripheral vision |
| +4 to +5 g | **Black-out**: total loss of vision while still conscious |
| +5 to +6 g and above | **G-LOC**: g-induced loss of consciousness |

Take each stage as a picture. **Grey-out** is the world losing colour and brightness, as if someone turned down the contrast. **Tunnel vision** is the edges closing in, until you see the world through a narrowing tube. **Black-out** is total loss of sight. Throughout all three you are conscious: you can hear, think and, if you relax the back pressure, recover your vision within seconds as the blood returns.

### What happens to consciousness

Beyond black-out the brain's own supply becomes too small, and the pilot loses consciousness: **G-LOC** (g-induced loss of consciousness). Recovery is not instant. There is a period of confusion and disorientation afterwards, often with no memory of the event, and the total incapacitation can last **15 to 30 seconds or more**. Near the ground that is fatal. In a spiral dive that is already losing height fast, it is fatal at any height a light aeroplane normally flies.

The order is the useful part: **vision goes before consciousness**. Grey-out and tunnel vision are a warning that the blood supply to the head is failing. The only correct response is to ease the back pressure at once. With a very rapid onset of g the visual stages can pass too quickly to notice, which is one more reason to apply g smoothly.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Grey-out is not a "limit marker" to fly up to and hold. It is the first sign that the blood supply to your head is failing, and the next stages can follow in seconds. Ease off at the first greying. And the order is **grey-out, tunnel vision, black-out, then unconsciousness**: an exam option that puts unconsciousness before black-out is wrong.

??? check "Check yourself: why does the retina lose its blood supply before the brain does?"
    The fluid pressure inside the eyeball pushes back against the vessels feeding the retina, so blood must arrive at more than that pressure to get in. As g lowers the pressure at head level, the retina's supply fails first while the brain, with no such back-pressure, still gets enough.

### Raising your tolerance

Everything that raises your tolerance either gives the reflexes time or helps hold blood above the heart:

- **Apply g gradually**, so the reflexes can keep up.
- **Tense the leg and abdominal muscles** to squeeze blood out of the lower body and stop it pooling, and use a **straining manoeuvre**: strain against a closed throat while breathing in short bursts, which raises the pressure in the chest and the blood pressure with it.
- **Be fit, rested, well hydrated and fed.** Low blood sugar and dehydration both lower tolerance.

And everything that weakens the body's response lowers tolerance: **alcohol and hangover, hypoxia, heat, fatigue and illness**. A pilot who is dehydrated after a hot morning on the apron at Jandakot greys out sooner in the afternoon's steep turns.

The aeroplane can usually take more than you can. A normal category aeroplane is built for a limit load factor of +3.8 g, which sits right in the band where an unprotected pilot greys out or blacks out.

![How much g can the aeroplane take, compared with you?](diagram:load-factor-limits "A normal category aeroplane's +3.8 g limit is already in the grey-out to black-out band for a relaxed pilot: in a light aeroplane, your eyes are an early warning of the airframe's limit too.")

## Negative g: circulation, vision, consciousness

Push the nose over sharply and the g reverses. Now the force acts from feet to head, and the blood goes the other way.

In a training aeroplane you meet negative g less often than positive, and usually briefly: a sharp down-gust in rotor below the scarp on an easterly day, an abrupt push to avoid traffic, or a careless pushover at the top of a zoom climb. Loose items float up from the floor, your head heads for the roof, and a firmly tightened harness is what keeps you in the seat and in reach of the controls. Brief as these are, the body's response to them is worth knowing.

### What happens to the circulation

Blood is forced **towards the head**. The pressure in the vessels of the head and neck rises: you feel fullness and pressure in the head, a headache, and with enough negative g small blood vessels in the eyes and face can burst. The same pressure sensors in the neck now read a large rise, and the reflex is the reverse of before: they **slow the heart**. That causes a second problem. When the negative g suddenly stops, the heart is beating slowly and the blood drains back down, and the flow to the brain can drop sharply.

### What happens to vision

The eyes become congested with blood. Vision may be reddened or blurred, with watering and pain: **red-out**. The red colour is often attributed to the lower eyelid being pushed up across the eye, rather than to blood in the eye itself.

### What happens to consciousness

Tolerance to negative g is much lower than to positive g. Around **−2 to −3 g** is uncomfortable to painful, and prolonged negative g can lead to confusion and unconsciousness. Light aeroplanes are also built for less of it: the normal category negative limit is −1.52 g.

The most dangerous combination is the **push-pull effect**: going from negative g straight into positive g. During the negative g the heart slows and the blood vessels relax in response to the high head pressure. When positive g follows immediately, those defences are pointing the wrong way: the heart is slow, the vessels are open, and the blood drains from the head faster than usual. Positive g tolerance is sharply reduced, and G-LOC can come suddenly at a g you would normally handle. A pushover followed by a hard pull, or a recovery from an inverted attitude, sets it up.

??? check "Check yourself: why can a pull-up straight after a sharp pushover cause G-LOC at a g you would normally tolerate?"
    During the negative g the high head pressure slows the heart and relaxes the blood vessels. When positive g follows at once, those reflexes are working against you, so blood leaves the head faster than they can respond. This is the push-pull effect.

## Bringing it together

You are practising unusual attitude recoveries with your instructor in the training area south of Jandakot on a bumpy summer afternoon. The instructor hands you the aeroplane nose-low, banked 60 degrees, speed building: a spiral dive. Your instinct is to haul back. Trace what that does: with the wings banked, pulling only tightens the spiral; and pulling hard and fast at high speed sends the g past +4 in a second, too quickly for your reflexes, so your vision greys and narrows as the ground rushes up. The correct recovery from [RBKA 3.6](/notes/RBKA/3.6) is also the g-friendly one: throttle closed, roll the wings level, then **ease** out of the dive, keeping the pull smooth so the g stays moderate and builds slowly. Tense your legs and abdomen as you pull. If the edges of your view start to grey, relax the back pressure a little. Moments later a sharp down-gust lifts you against the harness; you note the negative g and you do not follow it with a hard pull, because you know the push-pull effect.

![Spin or spiral dive: why does the spiral recovery say "ease out"?](diagram:spin-vs-spiral-dive "In a spiral dive the speed is high and rising: roll level first, then ease out gently, which keeps the g within what you and the airframe can take.")

## Key points

- **2.9.1(a)** Positive g pools blood in the legs and lowers the pressure at head level; the heart has to lift the blood $n$ times harder; reflexes take several seconds, so rapid onset is worst. Negative g drives blood to the head, raises head pressure and slows the heart.
- **2.9.1(b)** Under positive g vision fails first because the eye's internal pressure resists its blood supply: grey-out, then tunnel vision, then black-out while still conscious (roughly +3 to +5 g for a relaxed person). Negative g brings red-out, congested and painful eyes.
- **2.9.1(c)** Beyond black-out comes G-LOC (roughly +5 to +6 g and above), with 15 to 30 seconds or more of incapacitation and confusion. Negative g tolerance is far lower (about −2 to −3 g is painful), and the push-pull effect cuts positive g tolerance sharply. Raise tolerance with gradual g, muscle tensing and straining, fitness, hydration and food; alcohol, hypoxia, heat, fatigue and illness lower it.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Know the order under rising **positive g**: **grey-out, tunnel vision, black-out, then unconsciousness**. Vision goes before consciousness, so grey-out is the signal to ease off. Positive g pools blood in the lower body and starves the head; **negative g** sends blood to the head, with red-out, headache and a slowing heart. Expect a load factor question ($1/\cos\theta$ for a level turn) and a "which increases tolerance" question: gradual onset, tensing and straining, fitness and hydration, never alcohol or hypoxia.
