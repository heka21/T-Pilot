## What the exam expects (PHFC 2.12)

- **2.12.1** Name the causes of hypoxia, and explain (a) what it does to night vision, (b) why its behavioural effects, such as losing self-criticism, over-confidence and a false sense of security, are so dangerous, (c) its symptoms and how they progress as you climb, (d) what makes a person more susceptible, and (e) how each form of hypoxia is prevented or treated.

## Why this matters

Hypoxia is the one physiological hazard that hides itself from the person who has it. In WA you meet it in quieter ways than a decompression at FL250: a long eastbound leg over the Darling Range at 11,500 ft to stay above the cloud, a night flight home from Geraldton, a cabin heater with a cracked exhaust muff on a cold July morning out of Northam, or a pilot who flies the day after giving blood. In each case the brain gets less oxygen than it needs, and the first thing to go is the judgement you would use to notice. The exam asks you to sort causes into the four types, to know that night vision suffers from about 5,000 ft, to recognise the behavioural danger, to quote an approximate time of useful consciousness, and to choose the right remedy for each type.

## What hypoxia is, and the four ways to get it (2.12.1)

In [PHFC 2.11](/notes/PHFC/2.11) you followed oxygen from the air to the cells: into the lungs, across the alveolar wall, onto haemoglobin, round the body with the heart, and into the cells that burn it. **Hypoxia** is what happens when that chain fails to deliver enough: a **lack of sufficient oxygen in the body's tissues** to keep them working normally. The brain and the retina of the eye use oxygen fastest and have no reserve, so thinking and vision suffer first.

The chain has four links, and each one can break. That gives the four types of hypoxia, and the exam expects you to place any cause in the right one.

![Where does each type of hypoxia break the oxygen supply?](diagram:hypoxia-types-and-causes "Hypoxic at the lungs (altitude), anaemic in the blood (CO), stagnant in the circulation (g, cold), histotoxic in the cells (alcohol); all are insidious.")

**Hypoxic (altitude) hypoxia: the air link.** The blood and the heart are fine, but the air arriving in the lungs has too low a partial pressure of oxygen to load the haemoglobin fully. This is the hypoxia of altitude: flying high without supplemental oxygen, or a pressurised cabin losing its pressure. For a private pilot it is the one most often meant by "hypoxia".

**Anaemic (hypaemic) hypoxia: the carrier link.** The air is fine, but the blood cannot carry enough oxygen. Either there is too little haemoglobin (anaemia, blood loss, a recent **blood donation**) or the haemoglobin is occupied by something else. That something is usually **carbon monoxide**, from a leaking exhaust in the cabin heater or from **smoking**. Carbon monoxide binds to haemoglobin far more tightly than oxygen, so even a small amount takes many carriers out of service.

**Stagnant hypoxia: the delivery link.** The blood is loaded but not getting where it is needed fast enough. **Positive g** in a steep turn pools blood in the legs and away from the head and eyes ([PHFC 2.9](/notes/PHFC/2.9)). **Cold** narrows the blood vessels in the limbs. **Shock**, heart problems and sitting cramped for hours do the same.

**Histotoxic hypoxia: the user link.** Oxygen reaches the cells, but they cannot use it because they have been poisoned. **Alcohol** is the common cause for pilots; some drugs and, at the extreme, cyanide also do it.

| Type | Cause | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Hypoxic** (altitude) hypoxia | Low partial pressure of oxygen in the lungs | Flying at altitude without oxygen; cabin decompression |
| **Anaemic** (hypaemic) hypoxia | Blood cannot carry enough oxygen | **Carbon monoxide**, smoking, blood donation, anaemia, blood loss |
| **Stagnant** hypoxia | Blood not circulating well | Positive g, cold, shock, heart problems, sitting cramped for long periods |
| **Histotoxic** hypoxia | Cells cannot use the oxygen delivered | **Alcohol**, some drugs, cyanide |

The types add together. A smoker with a mild hangover flying at 10,000 ft on a cold morning has a little of three of them at once, and the effects stack.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Carbon monoxide poisoning happens at any altitude, so it is tempting to file it with altitude hypoxia because the symptoms look alike. It is **anaemic** hypoxia: the problem is the blood's carrying capacity, not the air pressure. Alcohol is **histotoxic**, not anaemic: the blood carries oxygen normally; the cells cannot use it.

??? check "Check yourself: a pilot gave blood the day before flying. Which type of hypoxia are they at greater risk of, and why?"
    Anaemic hypoxia. Losing blood reduces the amount of haemoglobin available to carry oxygen, so even at a normal partial pressure the blood delivers less oxygen to the tissues until the body has replaced it.

## The real danger: you feel fine (2.12.1 b)

Picture a pilot an hour into a cruise at a height where they are slowly becoming hypoxic. They feel good. Better than good: relaxed, a little pleased with themselves, sure that the flight is going well. They have not updated the fuel log for a while, they let a radio call slide, and their heading has wandered ten degrees, but none of that bothers them. If you asked them they would tell you they are fine, and they would believe it.

This is what makes hypoxia so dangerous. It is **insidious**: it comes on gradually and without pain, and the part of the brain that suffers first is the part that does the checking. The typical early behavioural changes are:

- **euphoria**, a sense of well-being, sometimes the opposite (irritability or belligerence);
- **over-confidence**, a belief that you are performing well when you are not;
- **lack of self-criticism**, so errors go unnoticed or seem not to matter;
- a **false sense of security**, so a deteriorating situation does not alarm you.

Meanwhile judgement, memory, arithmetic and coordination are all getting worse. Recall from [PHFC 2.11](/notes/PHFC/2.11) that the urge to breathe comes from carbon dioxide, not oxygen, so there is no breathlessness either. The person who has hypoxia is very often the last to recognise it.

The conclusion for your flying is blunt. You **cannot rely on how you feel** to tell you whether you are hypoxic. You rely on the altitude rules, on supplemental oxygen when you are above them, on knowing your own early symptoms from training, and on objective checks: the fuel log that does not add up, the radio call you missed, a pulse oximeter if you carry one.

??? check "Check yourself: why can't a pilot rely on feeling well as evidence that they are not hypoxic?"
    Because hypoxia's early effects include euphoria, over-confidence and loss of self-criticism, and it produces no breathlessness. The faculties you would use to judge your own condition are the first to be impaired, so feeling fine is exactly what a hypoxic pilot reports.

## Night vision goes first (2.12.1 a)

Look at the back of your eye. The **retina** has two kinds of light sensors. **Cones**, packed into the centre, give colour and fine detail in daylight. **Rods**, spread around the edge, are far more sensitive and let you see in dim light, in black and white. You covered the anatomy in [PHFC 2.7](/notes/PHFC/2.7).

![Where are the rods, and why does hypoxia hit them first?](diagram:eye-anatomy-rods-and-cones "The rods around the edge of the retina do your night seeing; they are among the most oxygen-hungry cells in the body, so they suffer before you notice anything else.")

The retina is part of the brain in origin and uses oxygen at a very high rate, and the rods are especially sensitive to the oxygen supply. That makes night vision the earliest measurable casualty of altitude. It begins to deteriorate from about **5,000 ft** cabin altitude, well below the height at which you would notice any other effect, and by **10,000 ft** it may be reduced substantially: dim lights look dimmer, the horizon on a dark night is harder to find, and you need more light to read the chart.

For night flying the practical rule is to consider supplemental oxygen at lower altitudes than you would by day. Carbon monoxide and smoking make it worse at any height, because they reduce oxygen delivery before you have even left the ground. A smoker's night vision at sea level can be like a non-smoker's at several thousand feet.

??? check "Check yourself: from about what cabin altitude does hypoxia start to degrade night vision, and why so low?"
    From about 5,000 ft. The rods of the retina are among the most oxygen-sensitive cells in the body, so they are affected by the small fall in oxygen partial pressure long before thinking or coordination are noticeably impaired.

## Symptoms and how they develop with altitude (2.12.1 c)

Hypoxia does not switch on at a particular height. It develops by degrees as the oxygen partial pressure falls, and textbooks divide it into four stages by cabin altitude. The heights are approximate and vary with the person and the day.

| Cabin altitude (approx.) | Stage | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| Sea level to 10,000 ft | Indifferent | Night vision reduced from about 5,000 ft; little else noticeable |
| 10,000 to 15,000 ft | Compensatory | Breathing and heart rate increase; drowsiness, poor judgement, reduced coordination after some time |
| 15,000 to 20,000 ft | Disturbance | Clear impairment: poor judgement, memory and coordination, cyanosis, tunnel vision, euphoria |
| Above 20,000 ft | Critical | Rapid mental and physical incapacity, then unconsciousness |

Read the stage names as a story. In the **indifferent** stage the body is unbothered: haemoglobin stays nearly fully loaded and only night vision shows a change. In the **compensatory** stage the body notices and fights back, breathing a little faster and pushing the heart harder to keep the oxygen flowing, and for a while it succeeds, which is exactly why the effects of a long cruise there build "after some time". In the **disturbance** stage the compensation is no longer enough and the symptoms are obvious to an observer, if not to the pilot. In the **critical** stage the brain is failing.

![What happens to you in each stage of hypoxia as cabin altitude rises?](diagram:hypoxia-stages-by-altitude "What to notice: night vision fades from about 5,000 ft; the body compensates from 10,000 ft, impairment is clear from 15,000 ft, and above 20,000 ft incapacity comes fast.")

The symptoms themselves vary between individuals, which is why you are told to learn **your own**. The common ones:

- **Mental**: euphoria or belligerence, impaired judgement, slow thinking, poor memory, drowsiness.
- **Physical**: headache, dizziness, tingling, hot and cold flushes, increased breathing rate, poor coordination.
- **Visual**: blurred vision, **tunnel vision**, and the earlier loss of night vision.
- **What others see**: **cyanosis**, a blue tinge to the lips and fingernails as the blood carries less oxygen.
- Finally, **unconsciousness**.

Several of these, dizziness, tingling and visual disturbance, are also the symptoms of hyperventilation, and the two often go together: an anxious pilot who realises something is wrong at altitude can start over-breathing.

![Hypoxia or hyperventilation: how do you tell them apart?](diagram:hyperventilation-vs-hypoxia "Both bring dizziness, tingling and visual disturbance. Hypoxia brings euphoria and cyanosis and happens at altitude; hyperventilation brings muscle spasm and can happen anywhere. If unsure at altitude, treat for hypoxia first.")

### Time of useful consciousness

At high altitude the question is not whether you will be affected but how long you have to do something about it. **Time of useful consciousness (TUC)** is the time from losing your oxygen supply to losing the ability to take effective action: to put on a mask, to start a descent. It ends well **before** you become unconscious. Standard textbook values for a seated, resting person:

| Altitude | TUC (approx.) |
|---|---|
| 18,000 ft | 20 to 30 minutes |
| 22,000 ft | 5 to 10 minutes |
| 25,000 ft | 3 to 5 minutes |
| 30,000 ft | 1 to 2 minutes |
| 35,000 ft | 30 to 60 seconds |
| 40,000 ft | 15 to 20 seconds |

![How long do you have to act if the oxygen fails?](diagram:hypoxia-time-of-useful-consciousness "Time of useful consciousness falls from 20 to 30 minutes at 18,000 ft to seconds at 40,000 ft, and a rapid decompression roughly halves it.")

TUC is shortened by exercise, smoking, fatigue and a **rapid decompression**, which roughly halves it: the sudden drop in pressure pulls oxygen back out of the blood into the lungs, so you start from a lower reserve.

!!! example "Worked example: time of useful consciousness after a decompression at FL250"
    Given: a pressurised aircraft cruising at FL250 suffers a rapid decompression; TUC at 25,000 ft for a resting person is about 3 to 5 minutes; a rapid decompression roughly halves TUC.

    1. Table value at 25,000 ft: **3 to 5 minutes**.
    2. Halve for rapid decompression: $3 \div 2 = 1.5$ and $5 \div 2 = 2.5$, so **1.5 to 2.5 minutes**.

    Oxygen masks on immediately, then start the emergency descent. Every second spent diagnosing the problem before the mask is on comes out of a budget measured in tens of seconds.

Try one yourself before opening the solution: a pressurised piston twin cruising at 30,000 ft loses its cabin pressure suddenly. Roughly how long does the pilot have to get the mask on?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    The table gives 1 to 2 minutes at 30,000 ft for a resting person. A rapid decompression roughly halves it: $1 \div 2 = 0.5$ and $2 \div 2 = 1$, so **about 30 seconds to 1 minute**. Mask first, then descend.

??? check "Check yourself: what is the difference between time of useful consciousness and time to unconsciousness?"
    TUC ends when you can no longer take effective action to save yourself, such as donning a mask or starting a descent. You may remain conscious for some time after that, but uselessly, so TUC is always the shorter and the one that matters.

## Who gets it sooner (2.12.1 d)

Two pilots fly side by side at the same altitude. One is fine; the other is drowsy and making mistakes. The difference is susceptibility: anything that lowers the oxygen supply, raises the oxygen demand, or adds a second type of hypoxia on top of the first. Work through the list with that in mind.

- **Higher altitude and longer time there.** The lower the partial pressure, the faster the effects, and the compensatory stage only holds for so long.
- **Rapid rate of ascent.** The body has no time to adjust.
- **Physical activity.** Moving around, or even working hard at the controls in turbulence, raises the oxygen demand of the muscles.
- **Cold.** Shivering burns oxygen, and cold narrows the blood vessels, adding stagnant hypoxia.
- **Smoking and carbon monoxide.** Carbon monoxide already occupies some of the haemoglobin before you take off, adding anaemic hypoxia.
- **Alcohol and some medications.** These impair the cells' use of oxygen, adding histotoxic hypoxia, and alcohol's effect is stronger at altitude.
- **Fatigue, illness, anaemia, recent blood donation.** Less reserve, less carrying capacity.
- **Poor fitness, and individual differences.** Some people simply tolerate altitude less well, and the same person varies from day to day.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Fitness helps, but it does not make you immune. A fit athlete at 15,000 ft without oxygen is hypoxic; they may just compensate a little longer. No amount of fitness or experience changes the partial pressure of oxygen in the air.

??? check "Check yourself: why does smoking before flight increase your susceptibility to altitude hypoxia?"
    Smoke contains carbon monoxide, which occupies some of the haemoglobin before you leave the ground. You start the flight with anaemic hypoxia already, so the fall in oxygen partial pressure at altitude affects you sooner and harder.

## Combating hypoxia (2.12.1 e)

Each type breaks a different link in the chain, so each needs a different fix. Match the remedy to the link.

**Altitude (hypoxic) hypoxia** is a pressure problem, so the fixes are the two from [PHFC 2.11](/notes/PHFC/2.11): more total pressure or a higher oxygen fraction.

- **Descend** below 10,000 ft.
- **Use supplemental oxygen**. Requirements for unpressurised aircraft above **10,000 ft** are in the **Part 91 MOS** (there are limited allowances; check the current Part 91 MOS for the exact conditions and for passenger requirements).
- In a pressurised aircraft after decompression: oxygen mask first, then descend.
- Learn your symptoms (altitude chamber or hypoxia awareness training), so that you recognise your own early signs.

**Anaemic hypoxia** is a carrier problem. Remove the carbon monoxide source and give the haemoglobin back to oxygen: cabin heat off, fresh-air vents open, use 100 percent oxygen if carried, and land as soon as possible. Prevent it by not smoking before flight, not flying after blood donation, and having the exhaust and heater muff inspected.

![How does carbon monoxide get into the cabin?](diagram:carbon-monoxide-cabin-heater "A crack in the exhaust inside the heater muff puts colourless, odourless carbon monoxide into the cabin; heat off, vents open, land.")

**Stagnant hypoxia** is a delivery problem: limit g, keep warm, and move your limbs on long flights.

**Histotoxic hypoxia** cannot be treated in the air: oxygen does little to help when the cells cannot use it. The only remedy is **prevention**: no alcohol before flight, observing at least the legal minimum time between drinking and flying, and no unapproved drugs ([PHFC 2.10](/notes/PHFC/2.10) and [RFRC 2.3](/notes/RFRC/2.3) cover the rules).

!!! example "Worked example: choosing a cruising level over the Darling Range"
    Crossing the Darling Range eastbound in a non-turbocharged single, you plan 11,500 ft for an hour to get above turbulence and cloud.

    1. Is the plan above 10,000 ft? **Yes**, by 1,500 ft.
    2. What applies? Check the Part 91 MOS oxygen requirement for the planned time at that level.
    3. What else? Remember that judgement may be impaired even if you feel well, and night, smoking or fatigue would make it worse.

    9,500 ft may be the better plan.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Hypoxia and hyperventilation share symptoms (dizziness, tingling, visual disturbance), and the exam offers "breathe slowly into a bag" for a pilot at altitude. Hypoxia brings **euphoria and cyanosis** and happens at altitude; hyperventilation brings **muscle spasm** and can happen anywhere. At altitude with oxygen available, **treat for hypoxia first**.

??? check "Check yourself: why does supplemental oxygen fix altitude hypoxia but do little for histotoxic hypoxia?"
    Altitude hypoxia is a shortage of oxygen pressure in the lungs, which a higher oxygen fraction restores. In histotoxic hypoxia the oxygen is delivered but the cells are poisoned and cannot use it, so more oxygen does not help; the cure is not drinking or taking the drug in the first place.

## Bringing it together

A winter evening at Albany. You are planning to fly home to Jandakot after dinner in an aeroplane whose heater you need on a cold night, and the forecast has cloud tops near 9,000 ft along the coast. You had a cigarette after the meal. Run it through the lesson. It will be night, so your night vision starts to suffer from about 5,000 ft, and the smoke has already put carbon monoxide on your haemoglobin, so it will suffer more. A cruise above the cloud at 10,500 ft would put you above 10,000 ft, where the Part 91 MOS oxygen requirement needs checking, and into the compensatory stage, where you would feel fine. The cold and the heater add their own risks: shivering raises your oxygen demand, and a cracked heater muff would add more carbon monoxide. Each factor is small; together they stack across three of the four types. A lower route under the cloud, or a night's sleep and a morning departure, removes most of them.

## Key points

- **2.12.1** Hypoxia is too little oxygen reaching the tissues. Hypoxic (altitude, low partial pressure), anaemic (blood cannot carry it: carbon monoxide, smoking, blood loss or donation), stagnant (poor circulation: g, cold, shock), histotoxic (cells cannot use it: alcohol, drugs).
- **2.12.1 (a)** Night vision is the first casualty: it begins to deteriorate from about 5,000 ft cabin altitude and may be substantially reduced by 10,000 ft.
- **2.12.1 (b)** Hypoxia is insidious: euphoria, over-confidence, loss of self-criticism and a false sense of security mean the pilot feels fine while judgement fails. Rely on rules and oxygen, not on how you feel.
- **2.12.1 (c)** Stages: indifferent (to 10,000 ft), compensatory (10,000 to 15,000), disturbance (15,000 to 20,000), critical (above 20,000). TUC is about 20 to 30 minutes at 18,000 ft, 3 to 5 minutes at 25,000 ft, seconds above 35,000 ft, and a rapid decompression roughly halves it.
- **2.12.1 (d)** Susceptibility rises with altitude and time, fast ascent, activity, cold, smoking, alcohol and drugs, fatigue, illness, anaemia, blood donation and poor fitness.
- **2.12.1 (e)** Altitude hypoxia: descend or use oxygen (Part 91 MOS requirement above 10,000 ft). Anaemic: remove the carbon monoxide source and use oxygen. Stagnant: limit g, keep warm, move. Histotoxic: prevention only.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Expect one "which type of hypoxia is this cause" question (carbon monoxide is anaemic, alcohol is histotoxic, g and cold are stagnant), one on why hypoxia is dangerous (the pilot does not recognise it), the 5,000 ft night vision figure, a TUC value (3 to 5 minutes at 25,000 ft is the favourite) and the response to suspected hypoxia: oxygen if available and descend below 10,000 ft.
