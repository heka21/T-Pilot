## What the exam expects (PHFC 2.1)

- **2.1.1** Explain how everyday health affects the way you fly: diet and exercise, the coronary risk factors (smoking, cholesterol, obesity, family history), colds, hay fever and blocked sinuses, food poisoning and other stomach upsets, headache and migraine, pregnancy (when to stop flying and how it changes your fit in the cockpit), injuries, ageing, alcohol and smoking, giving blood, dehydration, and your emotional state (anxiety, depression, fears).
- **2.1.2** Recall that you must get medical clearance from a DAME (Designated Aviation Medical Examiner) before flying on any medication.
- **2.1.3** List what you, the pilot, are responsible for in keeping yourself medically fit to fly.

## Why this matters

A training aeroplane has one pilot and nobody to take over. On a solo navigation exercise from Jandakot to Northam and back, everything that happens depends on one brain, two eyes, two ears and four limbs working properly for two hours. A head cold, a skipped breakfast on a 38 degree February morning, a night out, or a worry you brought from home can each take away a slice of that, and the slices add up. Your medical certificate says you met the standard on the day of the examination; it says nothing about how you are this morning. The exam tests this subtopic as judgement: which factor causes which problem, when ear pain strikes, who decides you are fit, and what you must do before taking a new medicine.

## The pilot as a system

Think of your body the way you think of the aeroplane. The aeroplane needs fuel, oil, a sound structure and working instruments; you need fuel (food and water), oxygen delivered to the brain, a sound structure (no injury that stops you reaching the controls), working sensors (eyes, ears, balance) and a clear processor (a brain free of drugs, fatigue and distraction). Every factor in this lesson attacks one of those. When you meet a new one, ask which part of the system it degrades and whether it could fail suddenly. That turns a long list into a few ideas.

Two kinds of failure matter. **Subtle impairment** slowly degrades your performance without you noticing: dehydration, a hangover, low blood sugar, anxiety. **Sudden incapacitation** takes you out of the loop in seconds: a heart attack, a faint, a violent attack of gastro, a migraine that blanks part of your vision. A solo pilot can sometimes fly through subtle impairment, though worse than usual. Sudden incapacitation in a single-pilot aeroplane is usually fatal. Most of the medical standard exists to screen out the second kind.

## Fuel, fitness and water

**Diet.** Your brain runs almost entirely on glucose delivered by the blood, and it has very little store of its own. Skip breakfast before an early lesson and by mid-morning your blood sugar is falling: concentration slips, you become irritable and shaky, and your judgement degrades in exactly the way that makes you least likely to notice. A large, heavy meal has the opposite problem: blood is diverted to digestion and you feel drowsy. The fix is a balanced meal before you fly and a snack and water in your flight bag, so the fuel supply is steady through the flight.

**Exercise.** Regular moderate exercise makes the heart and lungs more efficient, so a fit pilot copes better with fatigue, stress, heat, g and the mild oxygen shortage of higher altitudes. It also improves sleep. The one caution is timing: an exhausting session immediately before flying leaves you tired and dehydrated, which defeats the purpose.

**Dehydration.** Water is lost through sweat and breathing all the time, and much faster in a cockpit sitting on a hot apron with the sun through the canopy. Even modest dehydration brings headache, fatigue, poor concentration and slower thinking, and it lowers your tolerance to g because there is less blood volume to keep the brain supplied. Thirst arrives late: by the time you feel thirsty you are already short. In a WA summer, drink water before you go to the aeroplane and carry it with you. The detail of symptoms and the best fluids for rehydration is in [PHFC 2.2](/notes/PHFC/2.2).

??? check "Check yourself: why does skipping breakfast before an early lesson degrade your flying even though you feel only mildly hungry?"
    The brain runs on glucose from the blood and stores very little. As blood sugar falls, concentration, mood and judgement decline, and the impairment includes your ability to notice that you are impaired.

## The heart: coronary risk factors

The arteries that feed the heart muscle itself are the **coronary arteries**. Over years, fatty deposits can narrow them. When one narrows or blocks suddenly, part of the heart muscle is starved of oxygen: chest pain, a heart attack, or a disturbed heart rhythm that can cause collapse within seconds. For a pilot this is the textbook sudden incapacitation, and it is why the medical examination checks blood pressure and, at set ages, an ECG.

The **coronary risk factors** are the things that make that narrowing more likely:

- **Smoking**, which damages the lining of the arteries and makes the blood more likely to clot.
- **High cholesterol**, which supplies the fatty deposits.
- **Obesity**, which raises blood pressure and cholesterol and makes diabetes more likely.
- **Hereditary factors**: a family history of heart disease at a young age.

You cannot change your parents, but the other three are under your control, and they add together. A pilot who smokes, carries extra weight and has high cholesterol has a much higher risk than one with any single factor. The exam asks you to recognise these as risk factors for incapacitation, not as causes of immediate impairment.

## Colds, hay fever and blocked sinuses

Your head contains air spaces: the **middle ear** behind each eardrum and the **sinuses** in the bones of the face and forehead. Each connects to the throat or nose through a narrow passage lined with the same soft tissue as the inside of your nose. The middle ear vents through the **eustachian tube**. In flight these passages let the air pressure inside equalise with the changing cabin pressure, as [PHFC 2.4](/notes/PHFC/2.4) and [PHFC 2.5](/notes/PHFC/2.5) explain in full.

A cold, hay fever or a sinus infection makes that lining swell and fill with mucus. On the **climb** the air inside expands and usually forces its way out through a narrowed passage. On the **descent** the outside pressure rises, and air has to get back *in* through a passage that is now swollen shut. It cannot, so the higher outside pressure pushes on the eardrum or the sinus lining: severe pain, muffled hearing, sometimes vertigo, and in the worst case a ruptured eardrum. That is an **ear block** or **sinus block**, and it happens at the end of the flight, when you are busiest.

![Why do ears block on descent and not on the climb?](diagram:ear-pressure-equalisation "Air escapes down the eustachian tube easily but must be pushed back up it: a cold-swollen tube blocks on descent. Climb back, equalise, descend slowly.")

A cold brings more than blocked passages. A fever and the general feeling of being unwell slow your thinking, and the medicines people take for colds bring their own problems. A decongestant may shrink the lining enough to get you airborne and then wear off during the flight, leaving you with a block on descent and no remedy. Many cold and hay fever tablets also contain antihistamines that cause drowsiness.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    "The climb is when trapped air causes trouble, so a head cold hurts on the way up." Not for the ears and sinuses. Expanding air vents out easily; the trouble comes on **descent**, when air must get back in through a swollen passage. Gut and teeth problems are the ones that come on the climb.

## Gut, head and injuries

**Food poisoning and other digestive problems.** Food poisoning and gastroenteritis cause cramps, vomiting and diarrhoea with very little warning, and they dehydrate you quickly. A sudden attack of severe cramps in the circuit is a real incapacitation: you cannot fly an approach well while doubled over. Even after the worst is over you remain weak and dehydrated. Do not fly until you have fully recovered and are eating and drinking normally.

**Headaches and migraines.** An ordinary headache is a distraction that competes for your attention, and it is often a symptom of something else worth knowing about: dehydration, hunger, fatigue, a hangover, or the early stage of an illness. A **migraine** is different in kind. Many migraine sufferers get an **aura**, a disturbance of vision such as flashing zigzag lines or a blank patch, followed by a severe headache, nausea and sensitivity to light and noise. Flying through that is not possible safely. Do not fly with a migraine, and check any migraine medication with a DAME, because several cause drowsiness.

**Injuries.** The question for any injury is whether you can still do everything the aeroplane might demand, including in an emergency. A sprained ankle may let you walk but not apply full rudder in a crosswind or stand on the brakes after an engine failure on take-off. A strapped wrist may not manage a firm pull on a go-around. Pain itself distracts, and painkillers strong enough to help may sedate you. If you could not handle an emergency with the injury you have today, you are not fit to fly today.

??? check "Check yourself: a fellow student with a sprained right ankle says he can drive, so he can fly. What question should he ask himself instead?"
    Whether he could apply full rudder and maximum braking in an emergency, such as a crosswind landing or an engine failure on the runway. Driving needs far less leg force than those, and any strong painkiller he is taking adds its own impairment.

## Pregnancy

Pregnancy changes the body steadily over nine months, and the syllabus asks about two things: when to stop flying and how it changes your fit in the cockpit.

Early in pregnancy, nausea, fatigue and a tendency to faint (blood pressure is lower than usual) can all affect a flight. Later, the growing abdomen changes the **cockpit ergonomics**: how far you can reach the controls, whether the control column has full travel without striking you, and how the lap belt and harness sit. A lap belt must lie low across the hips, never across the abdomen, and in a small cockpit there may come a point where it cannot be worn properly and full control movement is no longer possible.

The current note's guidance, which you must confirm: tell your DAME as soon as you know you are pregnant. Flying during an uncomplicated pregnancy may continue with the DAME's advice, and the DAME will tell you when to stop (check CASR Part 67 and current CASA guidance; the exact point is set case by case and the rules change). Any complication is a reason to stop and seek advice at once.

## Ageing

Ageing brings slow, predictable changes. Reaction times lengthen a little. The lens of the eye stiffens, so close focus goes, usually in your forties: **presbyopia**, which makes charts and instruments blurry without reading glasses. Night vision deteriorates because less light reaches the retina, and hearing loses its higher frequencies. The risk of heart disease rises with each decade. On the other side of the ledger, experience compensates for much of this: an older pilot often reads a situation earlier and makes fewer of the decisions that need fast reactions to rescue.

The medical system deals with ageing by examining older pilots more often and by attaching conditions such as "must wear corrective lenses". Your part is to keep those glasses with you and to be honest about the changes you notice.

## Alcohol and smoking

**Alcohol** is a depressant of the brain. Even small amounts degrade judgement, coordination and reaction time, and the first faculty to go is the one that would tell you that you are impaired. It also makes the body's cells less able to use the oxygen they receive, a form of oxygen starvation called **histotoxic hypoxia**, so a drinker reaches the effects of altitude sooner. The rule is at least **8 hours "bottle to throttle"** and a blood alcohol limit of **0.02 g per 100 mL** (CASR 91.520 and Part 99; verify current text), and 8 hours is a minimum, not a guarantee. How the body absorbs and clears alcohol, and why a hangover is impairment in its own right, are taught in [PHFC 2.2](/notes/PHFC/2.2).

**Smoking** fills the blood with **carbon monoxide** from the burning tobacco. Carbon monoxide binds to **haemoglobin**, the oxygen-carrying protein in the red blood cells, roughly two hundred times more strongly than oxygen does, and it does not let go quickly. The haemoglobin it occupies cannot carry oxygen. A heavy smoker can have several percent of their haemoglobin blocked this way before the engine starts, so their brain is as short of oxygen as a non-smoker's would be some thousands of feet higher. This is **anaemic hypoxia**: the air is fine, the blood cannot carry it. Night vision, which is very sensitive to oxygen, suffers first. In the long term smoking is also the leading coronary risk factor.

![Where in the oxygen chain do alcohol, smoking and blood loss do their damage?](diagram:hypoxia-types-and-causes "Smoking and blood donation break the carrying link (anaemic hypoxia); alcohol breaks the last link, the cells' use of oxygen (histotoxic hypoxia).")

??? check "Check yourself: a smoker and a non-smoker fly together at 8,000 ft. Why is the smoker more affected by the altitude?"
    Carbon monoxide from smoking occupies part of the smoker's haemoglobin, so less is free to carry oxygen. Add the lower oxygen pressure at altitude and the smoker's brain receives less oxygen than the non-smoker's, as if they were flying higher.

## Blood donation

Giving a unit of whole blood removes a sizeable share of your circulating blood. The body restores the fluid volume within a day or so, but the red cells take weeks to replace. In the meantime two things are reduced: the **volume** of blood available to keep the brain supplied when you pull g or stand up quickly, and its **oxygen-carrying capacity**, another form of anaemic hypoxia. The risk is fainting. The commonly recommended wait is **at least 24 hours** before flying (see [PHFC 2.2](/notes/PHFC/2.2) for how this varies between individuals and between types of donation).

## Your emotional state

Your attention is a limited resource. Flying an aeroplane uses a large part of it, and anything else that demands attention takes it away from the flight.

**Anxiety** is a state of heightened alertness to a threat. A little arousal improves performance; a lot narrows your attention to one thing, crowds out working memory and leads to fixation and poor decisions. The source might be the flight itself (a check flight, a first solo navigation exercise) or something outside it (an exam tomorrow, a sick relative, money worries). Anxiety is also the most common cause of hyperventilation, which is [PHFC 2.3](/notes/PHFC/2.3).

![Why does a little stress help and a lot hurt?](diagram:stress-performance-curve "Performance rises with arousal to a peak and then falls away: too much brings narrowed attention, poor memory and panic.")

**Depression** slows thinking and saps motivation, including the motivation to check things carefully and to make the conservative decision. It also affects sleep, so fatigue comes with it. Depression is a medical condition, not a weakness, and many pilots fly while it is treated, including on some antidepressants, but only with CASA approval through a DAME. Never self-manage it, and never stop or start such medication without the DAME knowing.

**Fears**, whether a specific fear of an aspect of flying (stalls, turbulence, a particular manoeuvre) or a fear of failure, distort judgement in a particular way: you may avoid the action you fear even when it is the correct one, such as a go-around after a bad approach. Recognising a fear and talking it through with your instructor usually removes much of its power.

The practical test for all three: if your mind is somewhere else at the holding point, you are not fully flying the aeroplane. Talk to someone, and do not fly while preoccupied.

## Medication and the DAME

Every medicine has an intended effect and side effects. A pilot cares about both, and about a third thing: the condition the medicine is treating may be a reason not to fly in itself. A headache tablet hides the headache but not the dehydration or illness behind it. A decongestant opens the nose but the infection is still there.

The **Designated Aviation Medical Examiner (DAME)** is a doctor approved by CASA to conduct aviation medicals and advise on aviation fitness. The rule for the exam, and for real life:

- **Check with a DAME (or CASA) before flying on any medication**, prescription or over-the-counter, including herbal products, unless you already have clearance for that medication.
- **Try any new medication on the ground first** to see how it affects you (commonly at least 24 to 48 hours).
- **Do not fly** on drowsy antihistamines, sleeping tablets, strong painkillers such as codeine, or anything labelled "may cause drowsiness" or "do not operate machinery".
- **A GP's or pharmacist's "that's fine" is not an aviation clearance.** They know medicine; the DAME knows medicine and the cockpit.

Why the ground trial? People respond differently, and the first dose is when an unexpected reaction, such as drowsiness, dizziness or an allergic response, is most likely. You want to discover it on the couch, not at 3,500 ft.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    "It's available without a prescription, so it must be safe to fly on." Over-the-counter cold and allergy tablets are among the commonest causes of drug impairment in pilots. Being sold off the shelf says nothing about how a medicine affects someone flying an aeroplane.

## Your responsibility to be fit

The medical certificate is a minimum, not a guarantee. CASA and the DAME set the standard and check you against it every few years; between examinations, the person who applies it is you. Under CASR Part 67 you must not exercise the privileges of your licence while you have a medically significant condition that could impair safe flight, and you must tell CASA (or a DAME) about medically significant conditions and changes (CASR 67.265 obligations; verify the current text).

In practice your responsibilities come down to four:

1. **Assess yourself before every flight.** A common checklist is **IMSAFE**: Illness, Medication, Stress, Alcohol, Fatigue, Eating (and hydration).
2. **Do not fly** if you are unwell, injured, on unapproved medication, affected by alcohol or drugs, or excessively tired.
3. **Report** illnesses, injuries, hospital admissions, new medications and pregnancy as Part 67 requires, and get clearance before resuming flying.
4. **Keep your medical certificate current** and carry any spectacles it requires.

![Am I fit to fly today?](diagram:im-safe-checklist "Six questions before every flight; one honest no is enough to cancel, and the decision is yours as pilot in command.")

IMSAFE works because it forces a pause and a separate answer to each question. The factors in this lesson do not usually arrive alone: a late night (fatigue), a couple of drinks (alcohol), no breakfast (eating) and a worry about the flight (stress) can each be "not too bad", and together be enough to make you unsafe. One honest "no" is enough to cancel.

!!! example "Worked example: applying IMSAFE to a blocked nose"
    Given: you wake with a blocked nose and a mild sore throat on the morning of a solo navigation exercise.

    1. **Illness**: a cold with a blocked nose. The ear and sinus risk on descent alone makes this a no.
    2. **Medication**: a decongestant to "get through it" adds a medication you have not cleared with a DAME, and it may wear off in flight.
    3. **Decision**: cancel and rebook.

    The flight will still be there next week; an ear block on descent into Jandakot with a ruptured eardrum would keep you on the ground far longer.

??? check "Check yourself: you hold a current Class 2 medical. A friend says that means you are fit to fly any day it is valid. What is wrong with that?"
    The certificate says you met the standard on the day you were examined. Fitness for a particular flight is assessed by you, every day, against how you are now: illness, medication, stress, alcohol, fatigue and food.

## Bringing it together

It is a Saturday in January. You have a solo navigation exercise booked from Jandakot to Northam and back, departing at 1100. You were at a barbecue last night and had two beers, finished by 2100. You slept badly because of a work problem, skipped breakfast, and you have a mild headache. The forecast for Northam is 39 degrees.

Run the lesson's ideas over it. The alcohol is legal: well over 8 hours have passed and two beers are long gone. But the headache is information. Two beers, a hot night, poor sleep and no breakfast point to dehydration and low blood sugar, and the work problem is an emotional load that will compete for your attention. None of these alone would cancel the flight. Together, on a hot day with a long solo leg, they make a poorer pilot than usual. The repairs are simple and take an hour: water, a proper meal, honest thought about whether the work worry will stay on the ground. If the headache is gone and you are clear-headed by the briefing, fly. If not, rebook, and that is a sound pilot-in-command decision, not a failure.

## Key points

- **2.1.1** Every health factor degrades part of the pilot system or threatens sudden incapacitation. Diet and water keep the brain fuelled; the coronary risk factors (smoking, cholesterol, obesity, family history) raise the risk of a heart attack; colds block the ears and sinuses on **descent**; gastro and migraine can incapacitate suddenly; injuries must not stop you handling an emergency; pregnancy changes reach and harness fit, and the DAME advises when to stop; ageing slows reactions and near vision; alcohol and smoking (carbon monoxide) starve the brain of oxygen; blood donation means at least 24 hours on the ground; dehydration brings headache and poor concentration; anxiety, depression and fears steal attention.
- **2.1.2** Any medication, prescription or over-the-counter, needs DAME clearance before you fly on it, and a ground trial first.
- **2.1.3** You are responsible for your fitness on the day: self-assess (IMSAFE), do not fly when unfit, report significant conditions under Part 67, and keep your medical current.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Expect a question on when a cold causes ear or sinus pain (on descent), one asking which item is a coronary risk factor, one on what to do before flying on a new medication (see a DAME), and one asking who decides whether you are fit for today's flight. That last answer is always **you, the pilot in command**: the DAME and CASA set the standard and issue the certificate; you apply it every day.
