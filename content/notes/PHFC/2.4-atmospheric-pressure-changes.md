## What the exam expects (PHFC 2.4)

- **2.4.1** Trapped gases: what a change of pressure does to gas trapped in the body's cavities, how that affects normal bodily function, and how to prevent and treat the problems.
- **2.4.2** What can happen if you fly soon after underwater diving, and the precautions to take before flying after a dive.

## Why this matters

Your body is full of small pockets of gas, and in an unpressurised aeroplane every one of them feels each change of height. Most of the time you notice nothing more than your ears popping on the climb out of Jandakot. With a head cold, the same descent back into the circuit can produce pain bad enough to make you abandon the approach. And WA has some of the best diving in the country, at Rottnest, Busselton and Ningaloo, which tempts pilots and passengers to dive in the morning and fly home in the afternoon, the one combination in this lesson that can cripple or kill. The physics behind both is simple, and once you have it the exam's questions answer themselves: which way the gas goes, whether it hurts on the climb or the descent, and how long to wait after diving. Expect one calculation of gas expansion and one question on the diving interval.

## Pressure falls as you climb

The air around you has weight. At sea level the whole column of air above you presses down with a pressure of about **1013 hPa**. As you climb, there is less air above you, so the pressure falls. It does not fall evenly: the air near the ground is compressed by the air above it, so the pressure drops quickly at first and more slowly higher up. In the International Standard Atmosphere the pressure is about 700 hPa at 10,000 ft and about **500 hPa, half the sea-level value, at 18,000 ft**.

![How do pressure and oxygen fall as you climb?](diagram:atmosphere-pressure-oxygen-vs-altitude "Total pressure halves by about 18,000 ft: 1013 hPa at sea level, about 700 at 10,000 ft, about 500 at 18,000 ft.")

In a light aeroplane the cabin is not pressurised. The air inside is the air outside, so the **cabin altitude is the aircraft altitude**, and your body feels the full pressure change of every climb and descent. An airliner keeps its cabin at a lower "cabin altitude" than it flies at, which softens the change but does not remove it: passengers' ears still pop.

## Boyle's law: why trapped gas expands

Take a balloon, partly inflated and tied off, up a mountain. It grows as you climb, and if you carry it back down it shrinks. Nothing has been added or removed; the gas inside is simply pushing outwards against the air outside, and when the outside pressure falls the gas expands until the pressure inside matches it again.

That is **Boyle's law**: at constant temperature, the volume of a fixed amount of gas is inversely proportional to its pressure. Halve the pressure and the volume doubles; double the pressure and the volume halves. As an equation, with the starting condition labelled 1 and the new condition labelled 2:

$$P_1 V_1 = P_2 V_2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad V_2 = V_1 \times \frac{P_1}{P_2}$$

The ratio $P_1/P_2$ is all you need. Climbing from sea level, $P_1$ is 1013 hPa and $P_2$ is smaller, so the ratio is bigger than one and the gas grows. Descending, the ratio is smaller than one and the gas shrinks.

| Altitude (ISA) | Approx. pressure | Trapped gas volume compared with sea level |
|---|---|---|
| Sea level | 1013 hPa | 1 |
| 10,000 ft | about 700 hPa | about 1.5 |
| 18,000 ft | about 500 hPa | about 2 |
| 25,000 ft | about 375 hPa | about 2.7 |

![How much does trapped gas expand as you climb?](diagram:boyles-law-gas-expansion "Half the pressure means double the volume: about ×1.5 at 10,000 ft and ×2 at 18,000 ft. Gut and teeth hurt on the climb, ears and sinuses on the descent.")

Notice the shape. The first 10,000 ft adds half again to the volume; it takes another 8,000 ft to add the next half. Because pressure falls fastest near the ground, a given change of height near the ground produces the largest change of volume. That matters on descent: the last few thousand feet into the circuit squeeze trapped gas hardest.

!!! example "Worked example: gas in the gut at a cruising level"
    Given: a 150 mL pocket of gas in the gut at sea level, 1013 hPa. You climb to cruise at 8,000 ft, where the pressure is about 753 hPa.

    1. Pressure ratio: $1013 \div 753 = \mathbf{1.35}$.
    2. New volume: $V_2 = 150\ \text{mL} \times 1.35 = \mathbf{202\ mL}$ (about 200).

    The pocket has grown by about a third at an ordinary PPL cruising level. A gut that was comfortable on the ground is now noticeably distended, which is why a fizzy drink before a long climb is a poor idea.

The same arithmetic at a higher level: a **120 mL** pocket of gas at sea level expands to about $\frac{1013}{500} \times 120 \approx 240$ mL at 18,000 ft, where the pressure is roughly half. In an unpressurised aeroplane the cabin altitude is the aircraft altitude, so at 10,000 ft the volume is about 1.5 times.

Try one yourself before opening the solution: the 150 mL pocket from the worked example is taken on up to 18,000 ft, where the pressure is about 500 hPa. What volume does it occupy?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    Ratio $1013 \div 500 = 2.03$. Volume $150 \times 2.03 = 304$, so **about 300 mL: double** the sea-level volume. Half the pressure means twice the volume, which is the one relationship to remember.

??? check "Check yourself: a pocket of gas trapped at 10,000 ft is carried down to sea level. Does it grow or shrink, and roughly by how much?"
    It shrinks, because the outside pressure rises from about 700 hPa to 1013 hPa. The volume falls to about 700/1013, roughly two-thirds of what it was. That shrinking is what pulls the eardrum inwards on descent if air cannot get back in.

## Where gas is trapped in the body

Gas in the body causes trouble only where it is **trapped**: enclosed in a space with rigid walls, or connected to the outside by a passage narrow enough to block. Where it can flow freely in and out, as in the lungs, the pressure equalises with every breath and nothing happens. There are four places that matter.

**The middle ear.** Behind the eardrum is a small air-filled cavity, the **middle ear**, connected to the back of the throat by the **eustachian tube**. On the climb, the air in the middle ear expands, the pressure inside rises above the outside pressure, and the excess pushes its way out down the tube easily: you feel the occasional "pop". On the **descent** the opposite happens. The outside pressure rises, the air in the middle ear contracts, and the eardrum is pushed inwards. To equalise, air must travel *up* the tube into the middle ear, and the lower end of the tube is a soft, slit-like opening that tends to stay shut until you open it by swallowing or yawning. If a cold or hay fever has swollen it, it may not open at all. The result is pain, reduced hearing, sometimes vertigo, and in severe cases a burst eardrum: **barotrauma**, injury caused by a pressure difference. The ear is taught fully in [PHFC 2.5](/notes/PHFC/2.5).

![Why do ears block on descent and not on the climb?](diagram:ear-pressure-equalisation "Air escapes down the eustachian tube easily but must be pushed back up it: a cold-swollen tube blocks on descent. Climb back, equalise, descend slowly.")

**The sinuses.** The **sinuses** are air spaces in the bones of the face and forehead, each draining into the nose through a small opening. With healthy linings the openings are clear and you never notice them. With a cold, hay fever or a sinus infection the openings swell shut and the sinus becomes a sealed box with rigid bony walls. On the descent its air contracts and the rising outside pressure cannot be relieved: severe pain in the forehead, behind the eyes or in the cheeks, usually on the **descent**.

**The gut.** The stomach and intestines always contain some gas, from swallowed air and from digestion. Their walls are soft, so the gas can expand, and as you **climb** it does: bloating, discomfort and cramping pain. The gas can usually escape eventually in either direction, but not always quickly. In extreme cases, at high altitude, a badly distended gut pushes up on the diaphragm and makes breathing difficult.

**The teeth.** A poor filling or a decaying tooth can hold a tiny bubble of gas against the nerve. As it expands on the **climb**, it presses on the nerve and causes toothache, called **barodontalgia**. The pain usually stops on descent.

![Where in the body does trapped gas hurt, and on the climb or the descent?](diagram:trapped-gas-body-map "What to notice: ears and sinuses hurt on the descent, when air must get back in; gut and teeth hurt on the climb, when gas expands where it cannot escape.")

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The exam likes to mix up the direction. **Ears and sinuses hurt on the descent**, because air has to get back in through a narrow passage that may be blocked. **Gut and teeth hurt on the climb**, because gas expands inside a space it cannot leave quickly. A question about a pilot with a cold who develops severe ear pain will usually be set on descent.

??? check "Check yourself: why does a cold cause ear pain on the descent rather than the climb?"
    On the climb, expanding air in the middle ear forces its way out down the eustachian tube even if it is narrowed. On the descent, air must get back into the middle ear through the tube, whose soft lower end stays shut unless opened, and a cold-swollen tube may not open at all, so the outside pressure pushes the eardrum in.

## What trapped gas does to a pilot

The syllabus asks for the effect on normal bodily function, and the answer is wider than "it hurts".

- **Pain** is the obvious one, and pain is a distraction. An ear or sinus block at the end of a flight arrives when you are busiest, on descent and in the circuit, and severe pain can make it very hard to fly an accurate approach.
- **Reduced hearing.** An eardrum pushed in cannot vibrate freely, so radio calls are muffled, just when you need to hear the CTAF or the tower.
- **Vertigo.** An unequal pressure in the two middle ears can disturb the balance organs next door and produce a sudden sense of spinning, called **alternobaric vertigo**, a disorientation risk with no visual horizon.
- **Injury.** A burst eardrum or damaged sinus lining may keep you out of the air for weeks.
- **Breathing.** A badly distended gut at altitude restricts the movement of the diaphragm.

Any of these can escalate from discomfort to **incapacitation**, and in a single-pilot aeroplane that is the real hazard.

## Prevention and treatment

Prevention follows from the mechanism. Keep the passages open, keep the gas down, and give the body time to equalise.

- **Do not fly with a cold, hay fever or sinus infection.** The single most effective measure, because a blocked passage is the cause of almost every ear and sinus problem.
- **Avoid gas-producing food and fizzy drinks** before a flight to higher levels.
- **Keep dental work in good condition**, and mention you fly to your dentist.
- **Descend slowly and equalise your ears often** on the way down: swallow, yawn, move the jaw, or use the **Valsalva manoeuvre** (pinch the nose, close the mouth and blow gently to push air up the eustachian tubes). Equalise before discomfort builds, not after.
- **Wake sleeping passengers before the descent.** People swallow far less when asleep, so their ears do not equalise.

Treatment in flight:

- **Ear or sinus block on descent: climb back** to the level where it was comfortable. That reduces the outside pressure, relieves the pressure difference, and lets the passage open. Equalise, then descend again more slowly. Continuing the descent through the pain is how eardrums burst.
- **Gut pain on the climb: stop climbing or descend**, loosen tight clothing, and let the gas escape.
- **Persistent pain after landing**: see a doctor or DAME before flying again.

??? check "Check yourself: on descent your passenger complains of sharp pain in one ear. What is the first thing to do with the aeroplane?"
    Stop the descent and climb back to the level where the ear was comfortable. Lowering the outside pressure reduces the pressure difference on the eardrum and lets the eustachian tube open; then have the passenger equalise and descend again more slowly.

## Flying after diving

Underwater, the pressure rises fast: every 10 m of seawater adds roughly the pressure of the whole atmosphere above it. A scuba diver breathes air from the tank at the same pressure as the surrounding water, so at depth they breathe air at two, three or more times the sea-level pressure.

That matters because of a second gas law. The amount of a gas that dissolves in a liquid is proportional to the pressure of that gas above it. Think of a bottle of soft drink: it is filled under pressure, so a large amount of carbon dioxide dissolves in the liquid and you see nothing. Open the cap and the pressure drops; the gas can no longer stay dissolved and comes out as bubbles. The analogy holds for the chemistry; where it breaks down is that the body does not fizz at once, because the gas has to move out of the tissues into the blood first.

A diver breathing high-pressure air dissolves extra **nitrogen**, which makes up most of the air, into the blood and tissues. On returning to the surface the excess nitrogen leaves slowly, through the blood and out of the lungs, over **many hours**. During those hours the diver's tissues hold more nitrogen than they would at sea-level pressure.

Now take that diver flying. The cabin pressure falls with height, which is exactly like opening the soft-drink bottle a second time. The nitrogen that was just staying dissolved at sea level can come out of solution as bubbles in the tissues and blood: **decompression sickness**, known as "the bends".

![Why does flying soon after scuba diving cause the bends?](diagram:nitrogen-bubbles-after-diving "What to notice: nitrogen dissolved at depth leaves only slowly; lowering the pressure again in flight, even at 5,000 to 8,000 ft, brings it out as bubbles. Wait 12 or 24 hours.")

The symptoms depend on where the bubbles form:

- **Joint pain**, usually in the shoulders, elbows or knees: the "bends".
- **Itching or a skin rash**: the "creeps".
- **Chest pain and coughing**: the "chokes", from bubbles in the lungs.
- **Tingling, numbness, paralysis and visual disturbance**, from bubbles in the nervous system.
- **Collapse.**

After diving, it can occur even at quite low cabin altitudes, **from about 5,000 to 8,000 ft**. That is ordinary cruising territory for a light aeroplane. Decompression sickness from altitude alone is rare in unpressurised light aircraft below about 18,000 ft; diving is what makes it a real risk.

**Precautions** (commonly taught; check current CASA guidance):

- Wait **at least 12 hours** after a dive that did not need decompression stops.
- Wait **at least 24 hours** after a dive that needed decompression stops, or after multiple dives over several days.
- **Snorkelling** (breath-hold diving) does not load nitrogen and needs no wait, because you are only breathing surface air.
- **If symptoms appear in flight**: descend, use oxygen if available, land and get medical help, and tell them you have been diving. Treatment is in a **recompression chamber**, which squeezes the bubbles back into solution and then lets the nitrogen out slowly.

!!! example "Worked example: flying home after a dive at Rottnest"
    Given: you finish a recreational scuba dive with no decompression stops off Rottnest at 1000, and plan to fly back to Jandakot at 1,500 ft that afternoon.

    1. Is this flying after diving? A low cabin altitude still lowers the pressure: **yes**.
    2. Minimum wait for a no-decompression dive: **12 hours**.
    3. Earliest departure: $1000 + 12\ \text{h} = \mathbf{2200}$.

    Wait until at least 2200; realistically, plan to fly the next day.

Try one yourself before opening the solution: you spend four days diving at Ningaloo, two dives a day, all without decompression stops. Your last dive ends at 1500 on Sunday. When is the earliest you should fly home from Exmouth?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    Multiple dives over several days fall under the longer interval, **at least 24 hours**, even though no single dive needed decompression stops, because repeated dives build up more nitrogen in the slower tissues. $1500\ \text{Sunday} + 24\ \text{h} = $ **1500 on Monday at the earliest**.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    "A low-level flight is safe straight after diving." No: decompression sickness after diving can start at cabin altitudes of about 5,000 to 8,000 ft, and any reduction in pressure moves the risk in the wrong direction. The wait applies whatever height you plan to fly.

??? check "Check yourself: why does snorkelling need no wait before flying, when scuba diving does?"
    A snorkeller breathes only surface air, at sea-level pressure, so no extra nitrogen dissolves in the tissues. A scuba diver breathes air at the higher pressure of the depth, which loads the tissues with nitrogen that later comes out as bubbles if the pressure falls.

## Bringing it together

You and a friend have flown up to Exmouth for a long weekend. Your friend dives on Saturday morning; you snorkel. On Saturday night your friend develops a head cold. You planned to fly home on Sunday at 8,500 ft.

Sort it element by element. You snorkelled, so you have no nitrogen load and no wait. Your friend dived once, without decompression stops, so the minimum is 12 hours: Sunday is legal on that count. But your friend now has a cold, so as a passenger they risk an ear or sinus block on the long descent into Jandakot. Plan a slow descent, have them equalise often, keep them awake for it, and be ready to level off or climb back if they report pain. If the cold is bad, a day's delay costs far less than a burst eardrum.

## Key points

- **2.4.1** By Boyle's law trapped gas expands as pressure falls and contracts as it rises: about 1.5 times at 10,000 ft and double at 18,000 ft, where the pressure is half. Ears and sinuses hurt on the **descent** (air cannot get back in through a blocked passage); gut and teeth hurt on the **climb** (expanding gas). The effects are pain, reduced hearing, vertigo, injury and in extreme cases impaired breathing. Prevent: do not fly with a cold, avoid gassy food and drinks, keep teeth sound, descend slowly and equalise. Treat an ear or sinus block by climbing back, equalising, then descending more slowly.
- **2.4.2** Scuba diving loads the tissues with nitrogen that leaves over many hours; flying lowers the pressure and the nitrogen forms bubbles: decompression sickness, possible from about 5,000 to 8,000 ft. Wait at least 12 hours after a no-decompression dive, at least 24 hours after a dive with decompression stops or multiple dives over several days; snorkelling needs no wait.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    For the calculation, multiply the volume by $1013 \div$ the new pressure; half the pressure means double the volume, at about 18,000 ft. For direction, remember **descent for ears and sinuses, climb for gut and teeth**. For diving, have **12 hours** (no decompression stops) and **24 hours** (decompression stops or multiple dives over several days) by heart, and remember that the danger is nitrogen coming out of solution, not the air in the ears.
