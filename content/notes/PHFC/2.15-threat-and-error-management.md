## What the exam expects (PHFC 2.15)

- **2.15.1** Describe the basic idea behind threat and error management (TEM).
- **2.15.2** Explain how TEM works and set out a process a pilot flying alone can use to find and deal with threats and errors.
- **2.15.3** Say what a threat is and give examples.
- **2.15.4** Give an example of an error that has been made and show how the pilot could act to keep the flight safe.
- **2.15.5** Explain how checklists and standard operating procedures stop errors.
- **2.15.6** Show, with examples, how an unmanaged threat or error turns into an undesired aircraft state.
- **2.15.7** Describe the resources a pilot can use to avoid or get out of an undesired aircraft state, such as being lost or flying into bad weather.
- **2.15.8** Explain why prioritising tasks matters when managing an undesired aircraft state.
- **2.15.9** Give examples of how good working relationships with other people make a flight safer.

## Why this matters

Every flight you make has things working against it. A sea breeze swinging the wind across the runway at Jandakot, a NOTAM about a crane near your departure path, a passenger who is quietly airsick, a fuel gauge that reads a little optimistic. And on every flight you will make mistakes: a frequency dialled wrong, a check rushed, a heading misread. None of these is unusual and none is, on its own, dangerous. Accidents happen when they are not noticed and dealt with, and one leads to another. **Threat and error management** is the framework the airlines use to turn that observation into a habit, and it scales down to a solo navigation exercise in a Cessna 172 without losing anything. The exam asks you to tell a threat from an error from an undesired aircraft state, to give the single-pilot process, to explain why checklists work, and to apply "aviate, navigate, communicate".

## The idea behind TEM (2.15.1)

Older approaches to safety tried to stop pilots making mistakes: more rules, more training, more discipline. They helped, but they never got the error rate to zero, because people make errors however well trained they are. Research in the 1990s, watching airline crews on normal flights, found that threats and errors turned up on almost every flight and that crews who flew safely were not the ones who made no errors. They were the ones who **caught and managed** them.

That is the premise of TEM: **threats are always present and humans will make errors**. Safety does not come from expecting perfection. It comes from anticipating threats, recognising threats and errors when they happen, and managing them before they lead to an accident. An error is not a moral failing to be hidden; it is an event to be trapped.

The model has three layers:

1. **Threats**: things outside the pilot's control that increase complexity and must be managed.
2. **Errors**: pilot actions or inactions that lead away from what was intended or expected.
3. **Undesired aircraft states (UAS)**: the aircraft in a position, speed, attitude or configuration that reduces safety margins, the last stage before an incident or accident.

Each layer can be **managed**, leaving the flight safe, or **mismanaged**, leading to the next layer. A mismanaged threat often produces an error; a mismanaged threat or error produces an undesired aircraft state; and a mismanaged undesired aircraft state produces the incident or accident.

![How do threats and errors turn into an accident, and where can you stop them?](diagram:tem-model "Each layer can be managed or mismanaged: trap errors with checklists, and recover from an undesired aircraft state before it becomes an incident.")

The model's value is that it gives you **three chances** to stop the chain, at three different layers, instead of one.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    "Well-trained pilots do not make errors" and "errors should be hidden" are both the opposite of TEM. The premise is that errors are inevitable, and the way to deal with them is to expect them, look for them and report them.

## Threats: what the world throws at you (2.15.3)

A **threat** is an event or error that occurs **outside the influence of the pilot**, increases operational complexity, and must be managed to maintain safety margins. The key words are *outside the influence of the pilot*: you did not cause it and you cannot make it go away, but you can plan for it and fly around it.

Threats come in three kinds by when you can see them. **Anticipated threats** are known before you go: a forecast crosswind, a busy Saturday circuit, an unfamiliar strip. **Unexpected threats** appear in flight without warning: a sudden traffic conflict, a bird strike, a rough-running engine. **Latent threats** are hidden in the system until they bite: an ambiguous chart, a poorly designed fuel selector, a club culture of "just going for a look".

| Environmental threats | Organisational and other threats |
|---|---|
| Weather: low cloud, thunderstorms, crosswind, turbulence, heat (density altitude) | Time pressure, commercial or social pressure |
| Terrain and obstacles, unfamiliar aerodromes | Aircraft defects (a rough engine, an unserviceable radio) |
| Other traffic, busy circuits (Jandakot!) | Passengers (distraction, illness, pressure to continue) |
| ATC clearances and changes, airspace | Poor or missing documentation, NOTAMs |
| Wildlife, kangaroos on outback strips | Fatigue imposed by schedules |

Notice that some of these come from other people's errors: an ATC clearance with a wrong level, a refueller who leaves a cap loose. From your seat those are threats, because they arose outside your influence. Your own mistakes are errors.

??? check "Check yourself: a passenger who keeps talking during the pre-landing checks: threat or error?"
    A threat. It comes from outside the pilot and increases the workload and the chance of a missed item. If the pilot then misses the flap selection because of it, that omission is an error, and managing the threat (a polite "quiet please until we're on the ground" in the briefing) prevents it.

## Errors: what you do wrong (2.15.4)

An **error** is a pilot action or inaction that leads away from what was intended or expected. TEM sorts them into three groups:

- **Handling errors**: flying the aeroplane: poor speed control on final, a ballooned flare, an unbalanced turn.
- **Procedural errors**: doing the wrong thing in the procedure, or skipping something: a missed check, a wrong frequency, a wrong altimeter setting.
- **Communication errors**: between you and anyone else: a misheard clearance, an incomplete readback, a passenger briefing that left out the door latch.

An error on its own often does nothing. What matters is what happens next. It may be **trapped** before it has any effect, it may be **inconsequential**, it may lead to **another error**, or it may produce an **undesired aircraft state**.

!!! example "Worked example: a committed error, trapped in the climb"
    Given: you are departing Jandakot eastbound for Northam over the Darling Scarp. The ATIS gives QNH 1012. On the ground you set 1022 on the subscale by mistake, a transposition error, and the pre-take-off check does not catch it.

    1. **What the error does**: the altimeter now reads as if the pressure level 1022 hPa were sea level. Using the rule of thumb of 30 ft per hPa, the error is $10 \times 30 = \mathbf{300\ ft}$, and with the subscale set too high the altimeter **over-reads**: at an indicated 3,500 ft you are about 3,200 ft.
    2. **Why it matters**: crossing the Scarp, 300 ft less clearance than you think from terrain and towers, and in cloud or at night that is the margin that matters.
    3. **How it is caught**: in the cruise check you cross-check the subscale against the area QNH you noted on the flight log, and they disagree.
    4. **The action**: reset 1012, confirm the aeroplane's actual height against terrain and your planned level, check that no airspace limit has been infringed, and tell ATC if you are in or near controlled airspace.

    The error was committed and escaped the first trap. Because the cruise check exists and you used it, it was caught before it produced an undesired aircraft state, and the flight stayed safe.

??? check "Check yourself: the error in the worked example slipped past the pre-take-off check. Does that mean TEM failed?"
    No. TEM expects some errors to escape the first defence, which is why it has several layers. The error was recognised at the next check and managed before it created an undesired aircraft state, which is TEM working as intended.

## Checklists and SOPs: built-in error traps (2.15.5)

The [memory lesson in PHFC 2.13](/notes/PHFC/2.13) showed how fragile short-term memory is under workload, stress and interruption. A checklist is the answer to that fragility, and a **standard operating procedure (SOP)**, a fixed way of doing a task every time, is its partner.

**Checklists and SOPs** prevent errors by:

- Taking the load off **memory**, which is unreliable under workload, stress and interruption.
- Making actions **standard**, so deviations stand out.
- Providing a structured **cross-check** that traps errors before they matter.
- Making sure critical items are done in the right order at the right time.

The second point is the subtle one. If you always set the flaps, then the trim, then the fuel pump in the same order, a missed item leaves a gap in a familiar pattern, and the gap is noticeable. If you do things in a different order each time, nothing looks out of place.

How you use them depends on the phase:

- **Read-and-do** for normal checks on the ground: read the item, do it, move on.
- **Flow then verify** in flight: do the items from memory in a fixed sweep of the cockpit, then read the checklist to confirm.
- **Memory items then the checklist** in emergencies: the first actions from memory because there is no time to read, then the checklist to confirm nothing was missed.

The trap with any checklist is reciting it without looking. "Fuel: on" said while your eyes are on the runway is not a check. A checklist traps errors only when each item is confirmed by looking at, or touching, the thing it names.

??? check "Check yourself: why does flying a standard flow make a missed item easier to spot?"
    Because a standard flow makes the cockpit look the same at the same point every time. A missed item leaves a switch or lever out of its usual position in a familiar pattern, and the difference stands out, whereas in a random sequence nothing looks unusual.

## The single-pilot TEM process (2.15.2)

In an airliner two pilots share the work of watching for threats and catching each other's errors. Flying alone, you have to do both jobs yourself. The single-pilot process is three words, applied to every phase of flight:

1. **Anticipate**: in planning and before each phase (departure, cruise, arrival), ask "what threats are ahead and what will I do about them?" Brief yourself out loud.
2. **Recognise**: monitor and cross-check so threats, errors and UAS are spotted early.
3. **Recover**: take action to restore safety margins: trap the error, manage the threat, get out of the UAS.

Countermeasures include planning, briefings, checklists and SOPs, monitoring and cross-checking, workload management, and personal minimums.

The briefing out loud is the heart of single-pilot TEM. Saying "the threats on this departure are the crosswind from the left, the traffic in the training area, and the trees at the end of the runway; if the engine fails below 500 ft I land ahead" turns vague awareness into a plan, and makes the plan easy to recall when the threat arrives. It is the same loop as the DECIDE model, run in advance.

![How does the decision loop fit into TEM?](diagram:decision-making-decide "Anticipate is DECIDE run on the ground; recognise is the Detect step in flight; recover is Choose, Identify and Do, with Evaluate to check it worked.")

!!! example "Worked example: an arrival brief at an unfamiliar strip"
    You are 15 nm from Wagin on a hot, gusty afternoon, arriving for the first time.

    1. **Anticipate (threats)**: an unfamiliar aerodrome; a crosswind from the north-west, gusting; density altitude high for the go-around; possible kangaroos near the strip late in the day; other traffic on the CTAF.
    2. **Anticipate (plan)**: overfly to inspect the strip and windsock; crosswind technique with a gust margin on the approach speed; a decision point at 300 ft: if not stable, go around early, knowing the climb will be sluggish.
    3. **Recognise**: on final, speed and alignment checks against the brief; watch the strip edges for movement.
    4. **Recover**: at the decision point the approach is unstable in the gusts; go around as briefed, and try again with more margin or divert.

    The go-around was not a surprise because it was part of the plan before you joined the circuit.

## From threat or error to undesired aircraft state (2.15.6)

An **undesired aircraft state** is the result of a threat or error that was not managed: the aeroplane is somewhere, at a speed, in an attitude or in a configuration that reduces the safety margin. It is the last layer before an incident or accident, so recognising it early is critical. Each example below follows the same chain.

- **A density altitude threat, not planned for.** A hot afternoon at a short country strip with trees at the end. The pilot uses the sea-level take-off distance from memory. The aeroplane accelerates slowly, lifts off late and climbs at a shallow angle towards the trees: low, slow and near obstacles.
- **A navigation error, not checked.** A wrong heading set on the heading indicator, unchecked against the compass. Twenty minutes later the pilot is lost, low on fuel, or infringing controlled airspace.
- **A gusty crosswind, not anticipated.** No gust margin on the approach speed, and a sudden lull drops the airspeed on short final. The aeroplane is low and slow, sinking below the approach path.
- **A distracting passenger, not briefed.** A missed checklist item and a landing with the flap not set.

**Recognise the UAS early and recover**: go around from an unstable approach, turn back from weather, follow the lost procedure. The recovery is almost always to **restore a margin** (height, speed, distance from cloud) before trying to diagnose what went wrong.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Do not confuse the layers. "Low and slow on short final" is not a threat or an error; it is the **undesired aircraft state** that a threat or error produced. The exam describes the aeroplane's condition and asks which layer it is.

## Resources: you have more than you think (2.15.7)

When things start to go wrong, pilots often act as if they are completely alone. You rarely are. A pilot has more resources than they often think:

- **ATC and Flight Information Service**: ask for help or a position fix; declare PAN PAN or MAYDAY early. ATC radar and ADS-B surveillance can identify your position.
- **121.5 MHz** emergency frequency; other aircraft on frequency (relay).
- **Navigation aids**: GNSS, charts, ERSA, the planned flight log, the visual features around you.
- **The aircraft**: POH procedures, autopilot (if fitted), fuel and endurance information.
- **Passengers**: can help look for landmarks, hold the chart, watch for traffic.
- **Before flight**: forecasts, NAIPS, your instructor, personal minimums, an alternate.

The reluctance to use them is a human factor in itself: pride, and the fear of looking foolish on the radio ([PHFC 2.13](/notes/PHFC/2.13)). A call made early, while you still have fuel, height and daylight, gives the people helping you options. The same call made late gives them very few.

**Lost**: hold or orbit near a recognisable feature or fly the planned heading, conserve fuel, check heading indicator against compass, use the radio early, consider a precautionary landing before the fuel or daylight runs out.

**Adverse weather**: turn back early (180 degrees on instruments if needed), descend only if terrain allows, tell ATC, land at the nearest suitable aerodrome.

??? check "Check yourself: why is an early call to ATC or Flight Information Service better than a late one when you are uncertain of position?"
    Because early you still have the fuel, height and daylight that give everyone options: surveillance can find you, a heading to a known feature can be given, and a diversion can be planned. Late, with fuel and light running out, the options shrink to an emergency.

## Prioritising: aviate, navigate, communicate (2.15.8)

You are climbing out of Rottnest at 300 ft when the door pops open with a bang and a roar of air. Your attention snaps to the door. This is the moment the prioritising rule is for.

When something goes wrong, workload jumps and attention narrows (the right-hand side of the arousal curve in [PHFC 2.13](/notes/PHFC/2.13)). Without priorities pilots fixate on the problem, a door, a radio, a GPS, and lose control of the aircraft. Many accidents have begun with a minor fault and ended with a perfectly serviceable aeroplane flown into the ground while the pilot dealt with it.

![Why does a sudden problem make it hard to keep flying the aeroplane?](diagram:stress-performance-curve "A sudden fault pushes arousal up the right-hand side of the curve: attention narrows onto the problem, and the aeroplane is the first thing to be forgotten.")

The order is:

**Aviate, navigate, communicate** (and then manage the systems).

Fly the aeroplane first, keep it clear of terrain and cloud, then work out where you are going, then talk. Shed lower-priority tasks.

Applied to the open door: **aviate**: hold the climb attitude and airspeed, keep it balanced, and ignore the door, which in most light aeroplanes is noisy but not dangerous (check your POH). **Navigate**: continue the circuit and plan to land back on the runway you left. **Communicate**: a short call to tell traffic you are returning. **Systems**: deal with the door on the ground. Every one of those decisions is easy once you refuse to let the problem set your priorities.

??? check "Check yourself: in an undesired aircraft state, why does 'aviate' always come before diagnosing the fault?"
    Because the aeroplane's flight path is what keeps you alive, and attention narrows under stress. If you diagnose first, the attitude, speed and height drift unmonitored, and a minor fault can become a loss of control. Flying the aeroplane first preserves the margin you need to deal with everything else.

## People: relationships that keep you safe (2.15.9)

TEM is not only about you and the aeroplane. The people around a flight are among your best defences, if the relationships allow them to help.

- A clear, friendly **passenger briefing** turns passengers into extra eyes for traffic and makes it easier for them to speak up, and easier for you to say "we're not going today".
- Respectful, clear radio use builds a good working relationship with **ATC**, who are then more able to help.
- An open relationship with your **instructor** or club means you will ask questions and report mistakes.
- Talking with **other pilots** shares local knowledge of strips, weather and hazards.
- Being assertive but courteous with **maintenance engineers** gets defects fixed properly.

The common thread is that safety information has to flow, and it only flows between people who trust each other. A passenger who has been made to feel part of the flight will tell you about the smell of fuel; one who feels a nuisance will sit in silence. A club where errors are discussed openly learns from each one; a club where they are mocked hides them until one becomes an accident.

## Bringing it together

You are planning a Saturday flight from Jandakot to Busselton with two friends. **Anticipate**: the threats are a forecast sea breeze at Busselton giving a crosswind, a busy Jandakot departure, two passengers who have not flown in a light aeroplane before, and a social plan for lunch that creates pressure to arrive. You brief the passengers on the doors and harnesses, on staying quiet during take-off and landing, and on helping to spot traffic, and you tell them that if the weather is wrong you will turn back and they will still get lunch. **Recognise**: abeam Mandurah the cloud ahead is lower than forecast, and you notice you have drifted 200 ft below your planned level while looking at it. **Recover**: you climb back to your level (error trapped), check the Busselton weather with Flight Information Service (a resource), and decide on a decision point at Lake Clifton. At the decision point the cloud is still lowering, so you aviate first, turn back, then tell your passengers and ATC. A threat was managed, an error was trapped, and the undesired aircraft state never happened.

??? check "Check yourself: in that flight, which items were threats, which was an error, and what would the undesired aircraft state have been?"
    Threats: the sea breeze crosswind, busy traffic, inexperienced passengers, social pressure and the lowering cloud. Error: drifting below the planned level. The undesired aircraft state, if both were mismanaged, would have been the aeroplane low, close to cloud and terrain in deteriorating weather.

## Key points

- **2.15.1** Threats and errors are inevitable; safety comes from anticipating, recognising and managing them. The model has three layers: threats, errors, undesired aircraft states.
- **2.15.2** Single-pilot TEM is **anticipate, recognise, recover** on every phase, with a self-brief out loud before each.
- **2.15.3** A threat arises outside the pilot's influence and adds complexity: weather, terrain, traffic, defects, passengers, pressure.
- **2.15.4** An error is the pilot's own action or inaction; one that escapes the first trap can still be caught at the next check and corrected before it produces a UAS.
- **2.15.5** Checklists and SOPs unload memory, standardise actions so deviations stand out, and provide a cross-check, but only if each item is actually looked at.
- **2.15.6** A UAS (wrong position, speed, attitude or configuration) is what a mismanaged threat or error produces; recover by restoring a margin first.
- **2.15.7** Resources: ATC and FIS, 121.5 MHz, other aircraft, GNSS and charts, the POH, passengers, and the planning done before flight. Use them early.
- **2.15.8** Aviate, navigate, communicate: fly the aeroplane first, then everything else.
- **2.15.9** Good relationships with passengers, ATC, instructors, other pilots and engineers let safety information flow.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    A **threat** comes from outside (weather, traffic, defects); an **error** is the pilot's own action or inaction. An undesired aircraft state is what results when either is mismanaged. And in any UAS, **fly the aircraft first**. Have by heart: anticipate, recognise, recover; aviate, navigate, communicate; and the four things checklists and SOPs do to trap errors.
