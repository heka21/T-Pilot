## What the exam expects (PHFC 2.8)

- **2.8.1** State the underlying cause of motion sickness.
- **2.8.2** List the factors that make motion sickness worse.
- **2.8.3** List the ways of combating motion sickness in flight.

## Why this matters

Most student pilots feel queasy at some point early in training: the third lesson of steep turns on a bumpy February afternoon over the Jandakot training area, or a first flight in the back seat while a friend does circuits. Almost everyone adapts, and knowing why it happens is a large part of adapting. It matters beyond comfort. Motion sickness eats away at concentration and motivation long before anyone reaches for a sick bag, and a pilot who is vomiting is incapacitated in a single-pilot aeroplane. As a pilot in command you will also carry passengers who have never flown in a light aeroplane, and how you plan and fly the trip decides whether they enjoy it. The exam asks for the cause (a sensory conflict, not a weak stomach), the things that make it worse, and the remedies, including why the pilot does not take the tablets the passengers might.

## Your brain's model of motion

Before you can see why motion sickness happens you need to see how your brain knows how you are moving. It does not get the answer from one sense. It builds a picture from three, all at once.

- The **eyes** report movement of the scene around you: the horizon tilting, the ground sliding past, the cabin staying still.
- The **vestibular apparatus** of the inner ear reports accelerations of your head. The **semicircular canals** sense rotation in roll, pitch and yaw; the **otolith organs** sense linear acceleration and the pull of gravity. You met both in [PHFC 2.5](/notes/PHFC/2.5) and saw their limits in [PHFC 2.7](/notes/PHFC/2.7).
- The **proprioceptive** senses in muscles, joints and skin report how you are pressed into the seat and how your body is positioned: the **seat of the pants**.

![Which parts of the inner ear sense motion?](diagram:ear-anatomy "The semicircular canals sense rotation and the otolith organs sense linear acceleration and gravity: the inner ear's half of every motion picture.")

On the ground, walking or driving, the three agree. When you turn your head, your eyes see the room swing, your canals feel the rotation and your neck muscles feel the turn, and the brain gets one consistent story. It also learns over a lifetime what each of your own movements will feel like, so it predicts the signals before they arrive. When the prediction and the signals all match, you are not even aware of the process.

## The cause: a sensory conflict

**Motion sickness** is what happens when the senses tell the brain **different stories about motion**, or when the story they tell does not match what the brain expected. The core conflict is between the **eyes** and the **vestibular apparatus**, with the seat of the pants joining in.

Picture the classic case. You are in the right seat over the Darling Range in afternoon thermals, head down, reading the frequency off a chart on your knee. The aeroplane is bouncing and rolling. Your inner ear reports every bump: up, down, roll left, roll right. But your eyes are fixed on a chart that is moving with you, so they report that you are perfectly still. Two senses, two incompatible pictures. The brain cannot reconcile them, and it responds with sickness.

![Why does looking at a chart in turbulence make you queasy?](diagram:motion-sickness-sensory-conflict "The eyes say still, the inner ear says moving: get the senses to agree with fresh air, eyes on the horizon, a still head and your hands on the controls.")

The conflict can run the other way too. In the back seat of a four-seater with no view of the horizon, the cabin walls fill your view and look stationary, while the vestibular system feels every turn and gust. A passenger watching the wingtip trace the horizon in a steep turn has eyes and inner ear disagreeing about which way is down, because the seat of the pants and the otoliths say "straight down into the seat" while the eyes show the earth tilted at 45 degrees.

The second half of the cause is **expectation**. The brain predicts the motion that is coming. When you fly the aeroplane yourself you know a turn is coming because you started it, so the brain's prediction matches what the senses then report. A passenger, or a student while the instructor demonstrates, gets the motion with no warning: the senses report movement the brain did not predict. That mismatch between expected and actual motion adds to the conflict between the senses, which is why the person at the controls is usually the last to feel sick.

Why the brain responds with nausea, of all things, is not fully settled. One widely quoted explanation is that the brain treats a sensory conflict as a sign of poisoning, since many toxins disturb the senses in just this way, and the response to suspected poison is to empty the stomach. Whatever the reason, the important point for you is the cause: **conflicting information from the balance senses**. It is a normal response of a normal nervous system, not a weakness and not a sign that you are unsuited to flying.

### How it progresses

Motion sickness builds through a recognisable sequence, and the early stages are easy to miss:

1. Loss of interest and a vague unease; you go quiet.
2. Yawning and increased saliva.
3. Pallor: the face goes pale.
4. Cold sweating, often with a feeling of warmth.
5. Nausea.
6. Vomiting.

Performance falls well before stage 6. Concentration and motivation drop in the first stages, the pilot stops flying accurately, stops looking out and stops caring about the radio. An instructor watching a student go pale and silent knows what is happening before the student admits it. That is why the early signs matter: they are the time to act.

![How does motion sickness build, and when should you act?](diagram:motion-sickness-progression "What to notice: your flying deteriorates in the first, quiet stages, long before vomiting; going pale and silent is the signal to act.")

??? check "Check yourself: why is the pilot at the controls usually less likely to be sick than the passenger beside them in the same turbulence?"
    The pilot makes the control inputs and is looking outside, so the brain knows what motion is coming and the eyes see the horizon move as the inner ear feels it. The passenger gets the motion unannounced, often while looking at the cabin, so the senses conflict with each other and with what the brain expected.

## What makes it worse

Every aggravating factor does one of three things: it **increases the conflict**, it **increases the amount of unfamiliar motion**, or it **lowers the threshold** at which the conflict makes you sick. Sort them that way and the list becomes easy to remember.

### Factors that increase the conflict

- **Looking inside the cockpit for long periods**: reading charts, programming a GPS, looking at a tablet or the instruments. The eyes see a still scene while the inner ear feels motion, which is the classic conflict.
- **No view of the horizon**, such as a rear seat, a small side window or a high instrument panel for a short passenger. Nothing outside gives the eyes the motion the inner ear is feeling.
- **Head movements, especially during turns.** Moving the head while the aeroplane rotates sends confusing signals from several canals at once, the same mechanism as the Coriolis illusion in [PHFC 2.7](/notes/PHFC/2.7). Turning to talk to a back-seat passenger in a turn is a good way to make both of you queasy.
- **Being a passenger rather than the pilot**: no control over the motion and no way to anticipate it.

### Factors that increase the motion

- **Turbulence**, particularly the low-level thermal turbulence of a hot WA summer afternoon, when the air below the inversion is a mass of bumps from the sun-baked paddocks and suburbs. Mechanical turbulence in the lee of the Darling Scarp in a strong easterly has the same effect.
- **Manoeuvres**: steep turns, stalls, spins and aerobatics, and repeated accelerations up and down. Slow, repeated vertical motion is especially provocative, which is why a long ride through thermals is often worse than a single sharp bump.
- **Unfamiliar motion**: the inexperienced pilot's vestibular system has no stored pattern for the motions of flight, so every one of them is a surprise.

### Factors that lower your threshold

- **Anxiety, fear and stress.** A nervous student, a passenger on a first flight, or anyone worried about the next exercise feels sick sooner. Anxiety and nausea feed each other.
- **Heat and stuffy air.** A cabin that has been sitting on the Jandakot apron in summer is hot before you start, and warmth and stale air are among the strongest aggravators.
- **Smells**: fuel, exhaust, hot oil, and the smell of someone else being sick.
- **An empty stomach, or a heavy or greasy meal** before flight.
- **Alcohol and hangover, fatigue and illness**, all of which lower your tolerance to many kinds of stress at once.
- **Hypoxia** at altitude and **carbon monoxide** from a leaking heater, both of which can bring on nausea themselves (see [PHFC 2.10](/notes/PHFC/2.10)).
- **Inexperience**: a lack of adaptation, the single biggest factor in early training.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Exam options sometimes offer "a weak stomach", "low blood sugar" or "a disorder of the digestive system" as the **cause** of motion sickness. The cause is a sensory conflict between the eyes and the inner ear. Hunger and a heavy meal are aggravating factors, not the cause.

??? check "Check yourself: a passenger in the back seat is reading a book during a bumpy flight over the hills. Which two aggravating factors are at work, and what do they do?"
    Looking inside (at the book) makes the eyes report "still" while the inner ear feels the bumps, the core sensory conflict. The turbulence supplies more of the motion. Being a passenger, with no control and no anticipation, adds the mismatch between expected and actual motion.

## Combating it in flight

Every remedy follows from the cause: make the senses agree, reduce the motion, raise the threshold, and give the brain a way to anticipate what is coming.

### Make the senses agree

- **Look outside at the horizon.** Now the eyes see the horizon tilt and bob in step with what the inner ear feels, and the conflict shrinks. Minimise time head-down; when you have to read a chart, bring it up near the windscreen so the horizon stays in your peripheral vision, and look up often.
- **Keep your head still.** Move your eyes rather than your head, and avoid turning to look into the back of the cabin while the aeroplane is turning.
- **Take the controls.** Flying the aeroplane lets the brain predict each motion before it happens, removing the mismatch between expected and actual motion. Instructors use this routinely: a queasy student who is given straight and level to fly often recovers within minutes. For a nervous passenger, a simple task such as holding a heading or looking for a landmark has some of the same effect, where it is safe and appropriate.

### Reduce the motion

- **Fly smoothly**, with gentle turns and gradual changes of attitude, and stop the manoeuvring exercise for a while: straight and level is the gentlest thing an aeroplane does.
- **Avoid turbulence where you can.** Fly early in the morning before the thermals build, or climb above the thermal layer into the smoother air on top, and plan routes around known rough areas such as the lee of the scarp in a strong easterly.

### Raise the threshold

- **Open the fresh-air vents** and keep the cabin cool. Cool air on the face is one of the most effective remedies there is.
- **Loosen tight clothing** and sit upright.
- **Reduce anxiety**: a good briefing for passengers, a clear plan for the lesson, and an instructor who knows you are uncomfortable.

### Speak up, and know when to stop

- **Tell your instructor early.** The first signs (going quiet, yawning, feeling warm) are the time to say something, while the exercise can still be changed. Pushing on in silence makes it worse and wastes the lesson anyway, because you are not learning while you feel sick.
- **If symptoms progress, return and land.** Do not push on through severe nausea: vomiting at the controls of a single-pilot aeroplane is an incapacitation.
- **Carry sick bags** for passengers, where they can reach them, and brief their use.

### Medication: passengers yes, pilots no

Anti-motion-sickness tablets work on the brain's response to the sensory conflict, and most of them also cause **drowsiness, blurred vision and slowed reactions**. Those side effects are exactly what a pilot cannot afford, and many such drugs are unacceptable for flying without DAME advice. So the rule is: **a pilot does not take anti-motion-sickness medication** before or during flight unless a DAME has approved it. Passengers may take it.

??? check "Check yourself: why does handing the controls to a queasy student often settle their stomach within minutes?"
    Flying the aeroplane makes the student look outside at the horizon, so the eyes see the motion the inner ear feels, and the brain now predicts each motion because the student commands it. Both kinds of mismatch shrink, so the trigger for the sickness weakens.

## Adaptation: why it gets better

The brain's motion model is learned, and it keeps learning. Each flight teaches the vestibular system what the motions of a light aeroplane feel like and how they relate to what the eyes see, and the conflict gradually stops being a surprise. This is **adaptation**, and it is why most students who feel sick in the first few lessons are fine by first solo.

You can help it along with **repeated, gradually increasing exposure**: short lessons at first, smooth air early in the day, then progressively more manoeuvring as tolerance grows. A sensible plan for the next lesson after a queasy one is an earlier start, a light meal beforehand, and a shorter manoeuvring session. If susceptibility persists, talk to your instructor and to a DAME rather than self-medicating. Adaptation fades with time away from flying, so a long break can bring the queasiness back for a lesson or two.

## Bringing it together

You take two friends for a Saturday flight from Jandakot up the coast to Lancelin and back. It is January and the forecast is 38 degrees. Using this lesson, you plan the flight before you leave home. Book an early slot, airborne by 0700 before the thermals build. Brief your friends: light breakfast, not a big greasy one, no drinks the night before. On the day, park in the shade if you can and air the cabin before start. Put the more nervous friend in the front seat with a view of the horizon, and give them a job: spot the Lancelin sand dunes and call out the beach towns on the way. Show the back-seat passenger the fresh-air vent and the sick bag, and ask them to say early if they feel unwell rather than reading on their phone. Fly gentle turns, no steep turns "for fun", and climb to the smoother air above the morning haze layer. On the way home the bumps arrive and the back-seat passenger goes quiet and pale: vents fully open, eyes on the horizon, head still, smooth flying, and you head directly for home rather than adding a scenic loop.

??? check "Check yourself: of all the remedies, why is 'eyes outside on the horizon' the one that works on the cause itself?"
    The cause is the eyes and inner ear disagreeing about motion. Looking at the horizon lets the eyes see the same tilting and bobbing the inner ear feels, so the two senses tell one story. Fresh air and smooth flying help, but they lower the threshold or the motion rather than removing the conflict.

## Key points

- **2.8.1** Motion sickness is caused by **conflicting information from the balance senses**: mainly the eyes against the inner ear's vestibular apparatus, with the seat of the pants joining in, made worse by motion the brain did not expect. It is a normal response, not a weakness, and it erodes performance well before vomiting.
- **2.8.2** Aggravating factors increase the conflict (head-down work, no view of the horizon, head movements in turns, being a passenger), increase the motion (turbulence, manoeuvres, unfamiliar motion) or lower the threshold (anxiety, heat, smells, hunger or a heavy meal, alcohol, fatigue, illness, hypoxia, CO, inexperience).
- **2.8.3** Combat it with fresh cool air, eyes outside on the horizon, a still head, taking the controls, smooth flying and avoiding turbulence; tell the instructor early, land if it progresses, carry sick bags; passengers may take medication, pilots do not without DAME advice; gradual exposure builds adaptation.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    The **cause** is a sensory conflict between the eyes and the inner ear, never "a weak stomach". The best in-flight remedies are **fresh air, eyes outside on the horizon, head still, and having the person fly**. Aggravating factors include turbulence, heat, anxiety, head-down work and head movements. Medication is for passengers, not pilots, because of drowsiness and slowed reactions.
