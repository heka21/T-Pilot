## What the exam expects (PHFC 2.5)

- **2.5.1** Outline how the ear works, for hearing and for balance.
- **2.5.2** Explain what the eustachian tube is for and what changes of atmospheric or cabin pressure do to the ear.
- **2.5.3** State the effects of noise exposure on hearing (short-term and long-term loss), on how well you understand speech, and on fatigue.

## Why this matters

Your ears do two jobs in the cockpit, and you rely on both on every flight. They hear: the tower at Perth giving you a clearance through the Class C step, another aircraft calling turning base at Serpentine, a change in the engine note that tells you something is wrong. And they balance: the organs that tell you which way is up and whether you are turning sit in the same small space as the hearing organ, and their limitations are the source of the illusions in [PHFC 2.7](/notes/PHFC/2.7). The ear is also the part of your body most affected by flying itself: by every change of pressure on the climb and descent, and by hours of engine noise at a level that slowly destroys hearing. The exam asks which part does what, why ears block on descent, and what noise does in the short and long term.

## How the ear hears

Sound is a pressure wave in the air: the air is squeezed and stretched in rapid alternation, hundreds or thousands of times a second. The number of squeezes per second is the **frequency**, measured in hertz (Hz), and it is what you hear as pitch. The size of the pressure change is the **intensity**, what you hear as loudness. The ear's job is to turn those tiny pressure waves in the air into nerve signals, and it does it in three stages, in three parts.

![Which part of the ear hears, and which part balances?](diagram:ear-anatomy "The cochlea hears and the semicircular canals and otoliths sense balance, all in the fluid-filled inner ear; the air-filled middle ear connects to the throat through the eustachian tube.")

**The outer ear** is the part you can see, the ear flap, and the **ear canal** that runs inwards to the **eardrum**, a thin, tight membrane across the end of the canal. The flap and canal collect sound and channel it to the eardrum, which vibrates in step with the pressure waves.

**The middle ear** is a small air-filled cavity behind the eardrum. Across it runs a chain of three tiny bones, the **ossicles**: the **hammer**, attached to the eardrum, the **anvil**, and the **stirrup**, whose footplate sits in a small window into the inner ear. The chain turns the eardrum's vibration into mechanical movement and passes it on. It also solves a problem. The inner ear is filled with fluid, and sound travelling from air into fluid is mostly reflected, in the same way that you hear very little of a conversation on the shore when your head is under water. The ossicles collect the force from the large eardrum and deliver it onto the much smaller window of the inner ear, concentrating it so that the vibration gets into the fluid. For that chain to work, the eardrum must be free to vibrate, which needs the same air pressure on both sides of it. Hold that thought: it is the whole reason for the eustachian tube.

**The inner ear** is a set of fluid-filled passages in the bone of the skull. The hearing part is the **cochlea**, a coiled tube shaped like a snail shell. When the stirrup pushes on the fluid, a wave runs along the cochlea. Lining it are rows of fine **hair cells**, each tuned by its position along the coil to a particular range of frequencies: high frequencies near the entrance, low frequencies further in. When the wave bends a hair cell it fires, and the signals travel along the **auditory nerve** to the brain, which hears them as sound.

Two facts about the hair cells matter for the rest of this lesson. They are delicate, and loud sound damages them. And they **do not grow back**: a hair cell that dies is gone for good, along with your ability to hear the frequencies it handled.

??? check "Check yourself: why does the middle ear need the same air pressure on both sides of the eardrum?"
    The eardrum has to vibrate freely to pass sound to the ossicles. If the pressure is higher on one side, the drum is pushed in or out and stretched tight, so it vibrates poorly: hearing is muffled and, if the difference is large, it hurts.

## How the ear balances

The inner ear holds a second set of organs, the **vestibular apparatus**, which senses movement of the head. It shares the fluid, the bone and the nerve supply with the cochlea, which is why some ear problems bring vertigo as well as deafness.

**The semicircular canals** are three fluid-filled loops, set at right angles to each other like the three axes of an aeroplane, so that between them they sense **angular acceleration** in roll, pitch and yaw. When your head starts to rotate, the bony loop turns with it, but the fluid inside lags behind because of its inertia, like the water in a glass that stays still when you start to turn the glass. The lagging fluid bends a small gelatinous flap with hair cells in it, and that tells the brain you are rotating. If the rotation continues steadily, friction brings the fluid up to speed, the flap straightens, and the signal stops: the canals sense only *changes* in rotation, not steady turning.

**The otolith organs** are two small patches of hair cells covered by a jelly layer with tiny crystals in it, which make it heavier than the surrounding fluid. When you tilt your head, gravity pulls the weighted layer sideways and bends the hairs. When you accelerate forwards, the layer lags behind and bends them the same way. The otoliths therefore sense **linear acceleration and gravity**, the direction of "down", and they cannot tell the difference between a tilt and an acceleration.

Those two limitations, canals that stop signalling in a steady turn and otoliths that confuse acceleration with tilt, are the source of the balance illusions taught in [PHFC 2.7](/notes/PHFC/2.7): the leans, the graveyard spiral and the false climb on a dark take-off.

![When do the balance organs lie?](diagram:vestibular-illusions "The canals adapt to a steady turn and the otoliths mistake acceleration for tilt: the leans, the graveyard spiral and the somatogravic illusion all come from those two limits.")

| Part | Contents | Job |
|---|---|---|
| **Outer ear** | Ear flap and ear canal, ending at the eardrum | Collects sound and channels it to the eardrum |
| **Middle ear** | Air-filled cavity with three tiny bones (ossicles: hammer, anvil, stirrup) | Turns eardrum vibration into mechanical movement and passes it to the inner ear |
| **Inner ear** | Fluid-filled **cochlea** (hearing) and **vestibular apparatus** (balance) | Converts vibration into nerve signals; senses head movement |

??? check "Check yourself: which part of the ear senses a roll into a turn, and why does the sensation fade if the turn continues?"
    The semicircular canals, which sense angular acceleration. In a steady turn the fluid catches up with the canal walls, the sensing flap straightens and the signal stops, so you feel level although you are still turning.

## The eustachian tube and pressure changes

The middle ear is an air-filled box sealed on the outside by the eardrum. If it were sealed completely, every change of outside pressure would push the eardrum in or out, and hearing would suffer with every change of height or weather. The **eustachian tube** is the vent. It runs from the middle ear down to the back of the throat, and its purpose is to **equalise the pressure in the middle ear with the outside air** so the eardrum can vibrate freely. It also lets the middle ear **drain** into the throat.

The tube does not behave the same way in both directions, and that one fact explains everything the exam asks about it. Its upper part, near the ear, is held open by bone. Its lower end, in the throat, is soft tissue that lies closed like a slit, and opens only when muscles pull it apart as you swallow or yawn. So it works like a **one-way valve**: easy to push air *out* through, hard to draw air *in* through.

**On the climb**, the outside pressure falls. The air in the middle ear is now at a higher pressure than outside, and it pushes outwards on the eardrum and down the tube. Air at higher pressure pushing on a slit-like valve from the inside forces it open: a small puff escapes, the pressure equalises, and you feel the familiar **pop**. This happens every so often all the way up, without you doing anything, and is rarely a problem.

**On the descent**, the outside pressure rises. The air in the middle ear is now at a *lower* pressure than outside. The eardrum is pushed inwards, and the higher pressure in the throat squeezes the soft end of the tube *shut*. Air will only go up the tube into the middle ear if you open it actively, by swallowing, yawning or moving the jaw. If you do that often, a little at a time, the ear equalises comfortably. If you leave it too long, the pressure difference holds the tube closed so firmly that swallowing no longer opens it.

![Why do ears block on descent and not on the climb?](diagram:ear-pressure-equalisation "Air escapes down the eustachian tube easily but must be pushed back up it: a cold-swollen tube blocks on descent. Climb back, equalise, descend slowly.")

If the lining of the tube is swollen from a **cold or hay fever**, it may not open at all. That is an **ear block**, and the injury it causes is **otic barotrauma**: pain that can be severe, muffled hearing, sometimes vertigo, and in severe cases a ruptured eardrum. The trapped-gas physics behind this, Boyle's law and the shrinking of the middle-ear air on descent, is in [PHFC 2.4](/notes/PHFC/2.4).

**How to equalise on descent:**

- **Swallow, yawn, or move the jaw** from side to side. Each opens the soft end of the tube for a moment.
- **The Valsalva manoeuvre**: pinch the nose, close the mouth and gently blow, to push air up the tubes into the middle ears. Gently: a hard blow against a blocked tube can damage the ear.
- **Descend at a moderate rate and equalise often**, before discomfort builds, not after.
- **Wake sleeping passengers before the descent**. People swallow far less when asleep, so a sleeping passenger's ears do not equalise and they wake in pain.

**If a block occurs: climb back** to the level where it was comfortable. That lowers the outside pressure, reduces the difference across the eardrum and lets the tube open. Equalise, then descend again more slowly. A decongestant may help in an emergency, but it is not a reason to fly with a cold: it can wear off during the flight and leave you blocked on the way down.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    "Ear trouble happens on the climb, when the air expands." The climb is the easy direction: air pushes out through the tube by itself. Ear block happens on **descent**, because air must be drawn back in through a valve that tends to stay shut. The fix is to climb again and then descend slowly while equalising, not to keep descending through the pain.

??? check "Check yourself: why does the eustachian tube let air out easily but not back in?"
    Its lower end in the throat is soft tissue that lies closed. Higher pressure in the middle ear (on the climb) pushes it open from inside; higher pressure in the throat (on the descent) squeezes it shut, so it opens only when you swallow, yawn or use the Valsalva manoeuvre.

## Noise and what it does

### Measuring noise

The ear handles an enormous range of sound intensity, from a whisper to a jet engine, and to make that range manageable sound level is measured in **decibels (dB)**, on a **logarithmic** scale: each step on the scale multiplies the energy rather than adding to it. The figure to know is that **every increase of 3 dB doubles the sound energy**. So 88 dB carries twice the energy of 85 dB, and 94 dB eight times as much. The "(A)" in **dB(A)** means the measurement is weighted to match the ear's sensitivity, which is greatest at the middle frequencies of speech.

The cabin of a typical piston single is roughly **85 to 100 dB**, and more near the engine. Most of that comes from the propeller, the engine exhaust and the airflow, and it is there for the whole flight.

### Hearing loss, short-term and long-term

**Short-term loss** is a **temporary threshold shift**: the quietest sound you can hear (your threshold) rises for a while after noise exposure. The hair cells have been overworked and need time to recover. After a noisy flight sounds seem muffled, and you may have ringing in the ears, **tinnitus**. Hearing usually recovers within hours to a day. A temporary shift is a warning that the exposure was enough to damage the ear if repeated.

**Long-term loss** is **noise-induced hearing loss**, and it is permanent. Repeated exposure kills hair cells in the cochlea, and they do not grow back. It usually appears first at the high frequencies, **around 4,000 Hz**, partly because the hair cells for those frequencies sit near the entrance of the cochlea where the energy of every sound arrives first. It is **painless and gradual**, so you often do not notice until it is advanced. **Ageing** adds its own high-frequency loss, called **presbycusis**, and the two together can leave an older pilot with a serious handicap on the radio.

A commonly used guide is that exposure above about **85 dB(A) averaged over 8 hours** risks permanent damage, and each 3 dB increase halves the safe exposure time. The halving follows directly from the decibel scale: 3 dB more is twice the energy, so you reach the same total dose in half the time.

![How long can you be exposed to cabin noise before it damages hearing?](diagram:noise-levels-and-hearing-damage "Every 3 dB halves the safe time: 8 hours at 85 dB but only about 15 minutes at 100 dB, the top of a typical piston cabin.")

!!! example "Worked example: safe exposure time in a noisy cabin"
    Given: the guide of 8 hours at 85 dB(A), halving for every 3 dB more. The level at your ear on a cross-country to Geraldton is 94 dB(A).

    1. Excess over 85 dB: $94 - 85 = \mathbf{9\ dB}$.
    2. Number of halvings: $9 \div 3 = \mathbf{3}$.
    3. Safe time: $8\ \text{h} \div 2^3 = 8 \div 8 = \mathbf{1\ h}$.

    A two-hour leg at that level is twice the daily dose by this guide. A headset that brings the level at your ear down to 85 dB(A) restores the full 8 hours, which is why hearing protection is part of the aeroplane, not an optional extra.

Try one yourself before opening the solution: a pilot flies with an old headset whose ear seals have hardened, and the level at his ear is 97 dB(A). By the guide, what is his safe exposure time?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    Excess $97 - 85 = 12$ dB; $12 \div 3 = 4$ halvings; $8 \div 2^4 = 8 \div 16 = 0.5$ h, so **about 30 minutes**. Twelve decibels sounds like a small change, but it is four doublings of the energy: sixteen times the dose rate.

How headsets cut the noise, and how to choose and look after hearing protection, is [PHFC 2.6](/notes/PHFC/2.6).

### Speech intelligibility

Understanding speech depends heavily on the **consonants** (s, t, f, th, k), which distinguish one word from another, and they are carried by the **higher frequencies**. Vowels are louder and lower. Background noise **masks** speech: the steady engine and airflow noise drowns the quiet, high-pitched consonants first, so you hear that someone spoke but not exactly what they said. "Five" and "nine", "climb" and "descend to", "two" and "to" start to blur.

![Why does cabin noise make radio calls hard to understand?](diagram:speech-masking-by-cabin-noise "What to notice: the loud, low vowels stand above the noise but the quiet, high consonants that tell words apart are buried in it, in the same band where hearing loss begins.")

In the cockpit that means radio calls are misheard, read-backs are wrong, and passenger briefings are missed. The brain fills the gaps with what it expects to hear, which is a well-known route to taking a clearance meant for someone else. High-frequency hearing loss makes it much worse, because it removes exactly the frequencies the consonants live in. Turning the volume up helps less than you would expect, because it amplifies the noise as well, whereas reducing the noise at the ear with a good headset improves understanding directly.

### Fatigue

Continuous noise, and the vibration that comes with it, is an environmental **stressor**. The brain keeps working to filter it out and to pick speech out of it, and that effort is not free. Over a long flight noise increases **fatigue**, irritability and workload, and reduces concentration and the ability to monitor. You arrive at the end of a three-hour cross-country more tired than the flying alone explains, at the moment you most need to be sharp: joining the circuit at an unfamiliar aerodrome.

??? check "Check yourself: why does high-frequency hearing loss make radio calls harder to understand even when they are loud enough to hear?"
    The consonants that tell words apart are carried by the higher frequencies. Lose those and you hear that someone is speaking but cannot tell similar words apart, and cockpit noise masks the same frequencies further.

## Bringing it together

You are flying a PPL cross-country from Jandakot to Albany, about two and a half hours, in a training aeroplane whose passenger headset has a cracked ear seal. Your passenger has a mild cold and falls asleep over the wheatbelt. The flight touches all three elements. The cracked seal lets cabin noise in at near full level, so your passenger is getting far more than a safe daily dose and will step out with muffled hearing and ringing ears, a temporary threshold shift and a warning; on your own side, hours of noise will leave you more tired than you expect on arrival. Before the descent into Albany you wake the passenger, because a sleeping, cold-affected passenger will not swallow and the descent is when the eustachian tubes must let air back in. You descend at a moderate rate, ask them to swallow and yawn often, and are ready to level off or climb back if they report ear pain. And when Albany's CTAF gets busy, you will understand the calls better if the noise at your own ear is low, because it is the consonants that the noise takes first.

## Key points

- **2.5.1** The outer ear collects sound onto the eardrum; the air-filled middle ear's three ossicles carry the vibration into the fluid-filled inner ear; the cochlea's hair cells turn it into nerve signals, and they do not grow back. The vestibular apparatus balances: semicircular canals sense angular acceleration, otoliths sense linear acceleration and gravity.
- **2.5.2** The eustachian tube connects the middle ear to the throat to equalise pressure across the eardrum and to drain the middle ear. It vents easily on the climb but must be opened actively on the descent; a cold-swollen tube causes ear block and barotrauma on descent. Swallow, yawn or Valsalva often; if it blocks, climb back, equalise and descend more slowly.
- **2.5.3** Noise causes a temporary threshold shift and tinnitus in the short term, and permanent, painless noise-induced hearing loss, first around 4,000 Hz, in the long term; the guide is 85 dB(A) over 8 hours, halving the time for every 3 dB. Noise masks the high-frequency consonants of speech, so calls are misheard, and it adds to fatigue on long flights.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Know which part does what: **cochlea** hears, **semicircular canals** sense angular acceleration (roll, pitch, yaw), **otoliths** sense linear acceleration and gravity. The eustachian tube **equalises middle-ear pressure**, and ear block happens on **descent** because the tube lets air out easily but not back in. For noise, long-term exposure gives **permanent high-frequency loss**, gradual and painless; the figures to have are 85 dB(A) over 8 hours and 3 dB halving the safe time.
