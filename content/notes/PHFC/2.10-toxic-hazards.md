## What the exam expects (PHFC 2.10)

- **2.10.1** State where carbon monoxide comes from in an aeroplane, its symptoms, what it does to the body, and how to treat it.
- **2.10.2** Recall what breathing air contaminated by fuel and other noxious or toxic aviation products does to you.

## Why this matters

On a July morning at Northam the paddocks are white with frost, the cabin is 3 degrees, and the first thing you do after start is pull the cabin heat knob. In most light singles that heat comes from a metal jacket wrapped around the exhaust, and if the exhaust inside it has cracked, you are now breathing engine exhaust. The poison in it, carbon monoxide, cannot be seen, smelt or tasted, and its first effect is to dull the judgement you would need to notice it. Fuel vapour, smoke from hot wiring and the chemicals that ag aircraft carry across the wheatbelt are the other toxic hazards of light aviation, and each has a simple first response that you need to know before you meet it. The exam asks for the sources, the symptoms, the treatment and the classic trap: you will not smell carbon monoxide.

## Carbon monoxide: what it is and where it comes from

Burning fuel combines carbon from the fuel with oxygen from the air. With plenty of oxygen each carbon atom takes two oxygen atoms and becomes **carbon dioxide** (CO₂). When the oxygen supply is short, some carbon atoms get only one and become **carbon monoxide** (CO): a product of **incomplete combustion**. A piston engine never burns its fuel completely, and it runs rich of the chemically perfect mixture for much of the time to keep cylinder temperatures down, so its exhaust always carries carbon monoxide.

CO is **colourless, odourless and tasteless**. None of your senses can detect it. Exhaust gas does have a smell, from the other things in it, so an exhaust smell in the cabin is a clue that exhaust, and therefore CO, is getting in. But you can be poisoned by CO with no smell at all, because a small leak, or a leak whose smelly components have been absorbed or diluted, can still deliver enough CO.

### How it gets into the cabin

The classic source is the **cabin heater**. Most piston singles heat the cabin by passing fresh air through a metal shroud, the **heater muff**, wrapped around part of the exhaust system. The air picks up heat from the hot exhaust pipe and flows into the cabin at your feet. It is cheap, simple and effective, and it puts the cabin's heating air millimetres away from the exhaust gas, separated only by the wall of the pipe. Exhaust pipes live a hard life of heating and cooling, vibration and corrosion. A crack in the section inside the muff puts exhaust gas straight into the heating air and straight onto the floor of the cabin.

![How does carbon monoxide get into the cabin?](diagram:carbon-monoxide-cabin-heater "A crack in the exhaust inside the heater muff feeds odourless CO straight to your feet: heater off and fresh air on at the first headache.")

That is why **winter and heater use** are the danger times. In a WA summer the heater stays off and the vents are open; on a frosty wheatbelt morning or a long cold climb, the heater is on and the vents are shut.

The other sources:

- **Exhaust leaks forward of the firewall**: cracked exhaust stacks, failed gaskets, and gaps in the firewall, cowling and floor seals that let exhaust from the engine bay into the cabin.
- **Exhaust re-entering the cabin** on the ground: running the engine with the doors or windows open with a tailwind, or taxiing and holding behind another aircraft whose exhaust blows into your vents.
- **Smoking**. Tobacco smoke is loaded with CO. A heavy smoker carries several percent of their haemoglobin already bound to CO before they even get into the aeroplane, and so does everyone breathing their smoke in a closed cabin.

??? check "Check yourself: why is the risk of CO poisoning in a light single much higher in winter than in summer?"
    Because the usual source is the cabin heater, which warms air in a muff around the exhaust. In winter the heater is on and the fresh-air vents are closed, so any exhaust leaking into the muff goes straight into a sealed cabin. In summer the heater is off and fresh air dilutes any leak.

## What carbon monoxide does to the body

To see why such an invisible gas is so dangerous, follow the oxygen. In the lungs oxygen passes into the blood and attaches to **haemoglobin**, the protein in red blood cells that carries it around the body to the tissues. Each haemoglobin molecule has a limited number of sites for oxygen.

Carbon monoxide attaches to exactly the same sites, and it binds about **200 to 250 times more readily than oxygen**. Haemoglobin with CO attached is **carboxyhaemoglobin**, and every site taken by CO is a site that cannot carry oxygen. So even a small concentration of CO in the air steadily takes over a large share of your haemoglobin. You are breathing normally, the air in your lungs has its full share of oxygen, but your blood can no longer carry it.

![How does a little carbon monoxide stop the blood carrying oxygen?](diagram:carboxyhaemoglobin-binding "What to notice: CO binds about 200 to 250 times more readily than oxygen, so a trace of it in the cabin takes over most of the seats on haemoglobin.")

That is **anaemic hypoxia** (also called hypaemic hypoxia): oxygen starvation caused by blood that cannot carry enough oxygen, rather than by a lack of oxygen in the air. You meet the other kinds of hypoxia in [PHFC 2.12](/notes/PHFC/2.12).

![Where in the oxygen chain does carbon monoxide break it?](diagram:hypoxia-types-and-causes "CO causes anaemic hypoxia: the air and the lungs are fine, but the blood cannot carry the oxygen to the cells.")

Three features make it worse than it sounds.

**It is insidious.** Your urge to breathe is driven mainly by the carbon dioxide level in your blood, not by its oxygen content. With CO poisoning your carbon dioxide is normal, so nothing makes you feel short of breath in the early stages. Meanwhile the brain, the organ most sensitive to oxygen shortage, is the first to suffer, and the first thing to go is judgement: the very faculty you would need to recognise the problem.

**It clears slowly.** CO lets go of haemoglobin only slowly. Breathing ordinary air, it can take **many hours** to clear. Walking away from the source does not make you well, and a pilot poisoned on the morning leg is still affected on the afternoon one.

**It adds to altitude.** At altitude there is less oxygen pressure in the lungs to begin with ([PHFC 2.11](/notes/PHFC/2.11)). CO takes away part of the blood's carrying capacity on top of that, so the two effects stack. A smoker, whose blood already carries some CO, is effectively at a higher **physiological altitude** than the altimeter shows, and feels the effects of altitude sooner. The same goes for night vision, which is the first casualty of oxygen shortage ([PHFC 2.7](/notes/PHFC/2.7)).

![Why does CO make altitude worse?](diagram:atmosphere-pressure-oxygen-vs-altitude "Oxygen pressure in the air falls with height; CO then takes away part of the blood's ability to carry what is left, so the two add together.")

??? check "Check yourself: why does CO poisoning not make you feel breathless in the early stages, even though your tissues are short of oxygen?"
    Your breathing drive responds mainly to the carbon dioxide in your blood, and CO poisoning leaves that normal. The lungs also have plenty of oxygen; it is the blood that cannot carry it. So nothing triggers the feeling of breathlessness while the brain is being starved.

## Recognising carbon monoxide poisoning

The symptoms build roughly in this order as more of your haemoglobin is taken over:

- **Headache**, often throbbing, or a tight band across the forehead.
- **Dizziness, tiredness and sluggishness.**
- **Nausea and vomiting.**
- **Impaired judgement, confusion and poor vision.**
- **Shortness of breath and a rapid pulse.**
- **Cherry-red lips and skin**: a late sign, and not a reliable one.
- **Convulsions, unconsciousness and death.**

Look at the first few and you will see the difficulty: headache, tiredness and nausea are exactly what you would expect from a long flight, a late night, a bumpy ride or ordinary hypoxia. Nothing about them says "carbon monoxide". The clue is **timing and context**: symptoms that start soon after the heater goes on, symptoms shared by more than one person on board, symptoms that get worse the longer the heater runs.

That is why a **CO detector** is worth its weight. There are two kinds. A **spot-card detector** is a small card with a chemical patch that darkens in the presence of CO. It is cheap, but it must be within its date, it must be looked at to be any use, and it responds slowly. An **electronic detector** measures CO continuously and sounds an alarm, which works even when you are not looking at it and even when your judgement is already slipping. If you fly in winter, an electronic detector is the better choice.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    "You would smell carbon monoxide" is false: CO is **colourless, odourless and tasteless**. An exhaust smell in the cabin means CO is probably present too, but the absence of a smell proves nothing. Another trap: cherry-red skin is a **late** sign, not an early warning.

## Treating carbon monoxide poisoning

The treatment follows from the mechanism: stop the CO coming in, dilute what is already in the cabin, give the blood every chance to swap CO for oxygen, and get on the ground.

In flight, at the first suspicion:

1. **Cabin heat off.** This closes the most likely source.
2. **Open all the fresh-air vents**, and the windows if your type allows it in flight, to flush the cabin.
3. **Use supplemental oxygen** if it is carried. A high concentration of oxygen in the lungs helps push CO off the haemoglobin faster.
4. **No smoking.**
5. **Land as soon as possible.** Do not press on to the planned destination with a poisoned pilot.
6. **Get medical attention** and tell the doctor you suspect CO, so that they test for it.
7. **Report the defect** and have the exhaust and heater system inspected before the aeroplane flies again.

Do the first two straight away, before you are sure. Turning the heater off and opening the vents costs you nothing but some warmth, and waiting for certainty means waiting while your judgement degrades.

**Prevention** comes back to the same mechanism. Have the heater muff and exhaust inspected at the intervals the maintenance schedule requires, as CASA's airworthiness bulletins on carbon monoxide advise. On the preflight, look for **soot stains or exhaust streaks** around the cowling joints and exhaust stacks, and listen after start for a changed or dull exhaust note. Do not smoke, and do not let passengers smoke, in or near the aeroplane. Carry a detector.

??? check "Check yourself: you suspect CO but you have no symptoms yet and no smell. Is it worth turning the heater off and opening the vents before you are sure?"
    Yes. The cost is a cold cabin; the cost of waiting is a growing dose of a gas that clears slowly and first attacks the judgement you are using to decide. With CO you act on suspicion, not certainty.

## Fuel vapour and other toxic aviation products

Carbon monoxide is the hazard the exam asks most about, but it is not the only thing in an aeroplane you should not breathe.

### Fuel: AVGAS and Jet A-1

Fuel vapour gets into the cabin from a leaking fuel line or fitting, a leaking primer, a fuel selector valve weeping into the cabin, an overfilled tank venting, or a spill during refuelling or fuel drains that soaks into clothing or carpet. Fuel vapours are absorbed through the lungs and act on the brain much like an anaesthetic.

- **Headache, dizziness and nausea**, with **irritated eyes and throat**.
- An **intoxicated, "drunk" feeling** with impaired judgement and coordination.
- **Drowsiness**, and in high concentrations **unconsciousness**.
- And a serious **fire risk**: the vapour that is making you dizzy is also an explosive mixture looking for a spark.

AVGAS also contains **tetraethyl lead**, which is toxic with repeated skin contact or inhalation. That is a reason for good habits on the ground: avoid skin contact during fuel drains and refuelling, wash your hands afterwards, keep fuel-soaked rags out of the cabin, and never siphon fuel by mouth.

### Smoke and the products of fire

Burning plastics, wiring insulation and cabin materials give off far more than smoke. The fumes include **carbon monoxide** and **cyanide** among other toxic gases, which is why even a small electrical fire behind the panel is so dangerous in a closed cabin. Smoke in the cockpit is an emergency. Follow the POH procedure for your type: in broad terms, identify and cut the source (the master switch or the affected electrical circuit), ventilate the cabin unless the fresh air would feed the fire, use oxygen if carried, and land.

### Fluids, extinguishers and cargo

- **Hydraulic fluid, engine oil and de-icing fluid**: fumes and mists from a leak onto a hot exhaust irritate the eyes and lungs and may cause nausea. Oil smoke from the vents after a leak is also a sign of an engine problem.
- **Fire extinguisher agents**, such as halon, in a confined cockpit can cause dizziness and reduce the oxygen available. Ventilate the cabin after using one.
- **Agricultural chemicals**: pesticides and fertilisers in ag operations, and their residue on and in aircraft that have done ag work.
- **Cargo, batteries and dangerous goods** that passengers bring on board: solvents, gas bottles, a leaking battery. Your passenger briefing and loading check are the defence.

### What to do when fumes enter the cockpit

The response is the same shape whatever the fumes:

1. **Identify and stop the source** if you can.
2. **Ventilate** with fresh air.
3. **Use oxygen** if it is available.
4. **Avoid anything that could ignite** fuel vapour: minimise electrical switching if fuel is the suspected source, and follow the POH.
5. **Land as soon as practicable.**
6. **Report the defect** and have it fixed before further flight.

??? check "Check yourself: why is fuel vapour in the cabin more than just a breathing hazard?"
    The same vapour that causes headache, dizziness and an intoxicated feeling is flammable, so the cabin holds a fire risk as well as a toxic one. That is why the response includes avoiding ignition sources as well as ventilating and landing.

## Bringing it together

A cold June departure from Narrogin for Jandakot with a passenger. You preflight in the dark and, under the torch, you notice a grey sooty streak along the lower cowling joint near the exhaust. You almost dismiss it, then remember what it means: exhaust is escaping where it should not, and in winter the heater will be carrying it into the cabin. You report it and the flight waits for an engineer, who finds a cracked exhaust inside the heater muff. A week later, in a different aeroplane, the electronic CO detector on the coaming chirps fifteen minutes after you selected cabin heat over Brookton. You feel fine, and there is no smell. You do not wait to feel unwell: heat off, all vents open, tell your passenger, and the readings fall away. You land at the nearest suitable aerodrome rather than pressing on to Jandakot, mention the exposure to a doctor, and write up the defect. The fuel smell that greeted you when you opened the door that morning gets the same treatment: find the source before you fly.

## Key points

- **2.10.1** CO is a colourless, odourless, tasteless product of incomplete combustion, mostly from a cracked exhaust in the cabin heater muff (also exhaust leaks, exhaust re-entering the cabin, smoking). It binds to haemoglobin 200 to 250 times more readily than oxygen, causing anaemic hypoxia that clears slowly and adds to altitude. Symptoms: headache, dizziness, tiredness, nausea, impaired judgement, then breathlessness, late cherry-red skin, unconsciousness. Treatment: heater off, vents open, oxygen if carried, no smoking, land as soon as possible, medical attention, inspect the system.
- **2.10.2** Fuel vapour causes headache, dizziness, nausea, eye and throat irritation, an intoxicated feeling and drowsiness, and is a fire risk; AVGAS lead is toxic on repeated contact. Burning materials release CO and cyanide; hydraulic fluid, oil, de-icing fluid, extinguisher agents, ag chemicals and dangerous goods also contaminate cabin air. Stop the source, ventilate, oxygen, no ignition, land, report.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    The favourite trap is "you would smell carbon monoxide": you will not, because it is **colourless and odourless**. Know the classic source (**the cabin heater muff around the exhaust**), the mechanism (**binds to haemoglobin 200 to 250 times more readily than oxygen: anaemic hypoxia**) and the first actions on suspicion (**heater off, fresh air on**, oxygen if carried, land). For fuel vapour, the answer combines intoxication-like effects with a fire risk; for burning wiring, toxic gases such as CO and cyanide.
