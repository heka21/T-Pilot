## What the exam expects (PHFC 2.11)

- **2.11.1** Give the make-up of the air by its main gases, and describe how temperature and pressure change as you climb.
- **2.11.2** Outline how the lungs and the blood circulation deliver oxygen to the body and carry carbon dioxide away.
- **2.11.3** Explain what the partial pressure of oxygen is and why it, not the oxygen percentage, is what matters.

## Why this matters

Every physiological problem in the rest of this unit, from hypoxia to blocked ears to the bends after diving, starts with one fact: the air thins as you climb. On a trip from Jandakot to Kalgoorlie at 7,500 ft you are breathing air whose pressure is about three-quarters of what it was on the apron, and your body has quietly adjusted. Climb another few thousand feet over the Darling Range to get above the bumps and the adjustment starts to run out. This lesson builds the foundation: what the air is made of, how its temperature and pressure change with height, how your lungs and blood move oxygen around, and why the **partial pressure** of oxygen is the number that counts. The exam asks for the composition of air, an ISA temperature or two, the mechanism of oxygen transport, and almost always a question designed to catch anyone who thinks the air at altitude "contains less oxygen".

## What the air is made of (2.11.1)

Take a lungful of air on the beach at Cottesloe. Most of it does nothing for you. About four-fifths is **nitrogen**, an inert gas your body breathes in and out unchanged. About a fifth is **oxygen**, the gas every cell in your body needs to release energy from food. The last one percent is mostly argon, with a trace of **carbon dioxide** (about 0.04 percent) and other gases.

| Gas | Proportion of dry air |
|---|---|
| Nitrogen | about 78 percent |
| **Oxygen** | about **21 percent** |
| Argon, carbon dioxide (about 0.04 percent) and other gases | about 1 percent |

On top of these the air holds a variable amount of **water vapour**, from almost none on a dry easterly day in the Wheatbelt to several percent in a humid tropical air mass over the Kimberley. That is why the table is quoted for **dry air**.

Now the key point of the whole lesson. The atmosphere is constantly stirred by weather, so the gases stay thoroughly mixed. The **proportions stay the same** from sea level to well above any height a light aeroplane can reach. Air at 18,000 ft is still 21 percent oxygen. What changes as you climb is not the recipe but the **amount** of air in each lungful, and the measure of that amount is the pressure.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    "At altitude the air contains less oxygen" sounds right and is the exam's favourite distractor. The **percentage** of oxygen is unchanged at every height you will fly. What falls is the total pressure, and with it the pressure of the oxygen. An answer that says the oxygen percentage has fallen is wrong.

## Why pressure falls as you climb (2.11.1)

Picture the atmosphere as a tall stack of blankets. The blanket at the bottom carries the weight of all the others, so it is squashed flat. A blanket near the top carries almost nothing and stays fluffy. Air behaves the same way. **Atmospheric pressure** at any height is simply the weight of the column of air above you, pressing on each unit of area. At sea level that column weighs enough to give, in the ISA, **1013 hPa**.

Climb and there is less air above you, so the pressure falls. Air is compressible, so the lower layers are squeezed denser by the weight above them. That has a consequence the exam likes: pressure falls **fastest near the ground**, where the air is densest, and more slowly higher up, where each thousand feet contains less air. Near sea level you lose roughly **1 hPa for every 30 ft**, the rule of thumb you also use when setting the altimeter subscale ([RFRC 2.3](/notes/RFRC/2.3) covers altimetry).

Because each layer is thinner than the one below, the pressure does not fall in a straight line; it halves over a fixed-ish height interval. The ISA figures to know:

| Altitude (ISA) | Pressure | Fraction of sea level | Temperature (ISA) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sea level | 1013 hPa | 1 | +15°C |
| 10,000 ft | about 700 hPa | about 0.7 | -5°C |
| 18,000 ft | about 500 hPa | about **0.5** | -21°C |
| 25,000 ft | about 375 hPa | about 0.37 | -35°C |
| 34,000 ft | about 250 hPa | about 0.25 | -53°C |

Read the fraction column. By 18,000 ft you have climbed above half of all the air in the atmosphere: the pressure is about **half** the sea level value. It halves again, to a quarter, by about 34,000 ft. It took 18,000 ft to lose the first half but 16,000 ft to lose half of what was left, because the air you climb through gets thinner all the way up.

![What falls with altitude: the oxygen percentage or its pressure?](diagram:atmosphere-pressure-oxygen-vs-altitude "Oxygen stays 21% of the air at every height; the total pressure and with it the oxygen partial pressure halve by 18,000 ft.")

The heights a VFR pilot in a non-turbocharged single flies, up to about 10,000 ft, sit in the steep lower part of the curve. Between the ground and 10,000 ft you have already lost about 30 percent of the pressure, which is why the body starts to notice above that height (the subject of [PHFC 2.12](/notes/PHFC/2.12)), and why trapped gas in your ears, sinuses and gut expands most on the first part of the climb ([PHFC 2.4](/notes/PHFC/2.4)).

## Why temperature falls as you climb (2.11.1)

You know from driving up into the hills that it gets cooler with height. The reason is that the atmosphere is heated mainly from **below**. Sunlight passes through the air largely without warming it, heats the ground, and the ground warms the air in contact with it. The further you are from that heated surface, the cooler the air. Air that rises also expands as the pressure on it falls, and expanding air cools.

The real atmosphere varies from day to day, so aviation uses an agreed average, the **International Standard Atmosphere (ISA)**. In the ISA:

- the temperature at sea level is **+15 degrees**;
- it falls at about **2 degrees per 1,000 ft** (the **lapse rate**);
- it keeps falling until the **tropopause**, at about **36,000 ft**, where it reaches about **-56.5 degrees**;
- above the tropopause it stays constant.

The 2 degrees per 1,000 ft figure is a rounded rule (the exact ISA value is 1.98). It gives the formula you need:

$$T_{\text{ISA}} = 15 - 2 \times \frac{\text{altitude in ft}}{1{,}000}$$

The tropopause matters to airline pilots and to weather (it caps thunderstorm tops), but not to your cruising level. What matters to you is that the ISA gives a reference against which the real day is compared: a hot afternoon at Northam is "ISA plus 20", and that difference drives density height, which you meet in performance planning ([BAKC 6.2](/notes/BAKC/6.2)).

![How does ISA temperature change with height?](diagram:isa-temperature-profile "What to notice: +15 degrees at sea level, down about 2 degrees per 1,000 ft, to about -56.5 degrees at the tropopause near 36,000 ft, then constant.")

!!! example "Worked example: ISA temperature at 8,000 ft"
    Given: ISA sea level temperature +15 degrees, lapse rate 2 degrees per 1,000 ft, altitude 8,000 ft.

    1. Thousands of feet: $8{,}000 / 1{,}000 = \mathbf{8}$.
    2. Temperature drop: $2 \times 8 = \mathbf{16\,°C}$.
    3. ISA temperature: $15 - 16 = \mathbf{-1\,°C}$.

    On a standard day you would expect the air at 8,000 ft to be just below freezing. Any visible moisture there is a potential icing hazard, and the cabin will need heat.

Try one yourself before opening the solution: what is the ISA temperature at 6,500 ft, a common westbound VFR cruising level on the way home to Perth?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    Thousands of feet: $6{,}500 / 1{,}000 = 6.5$. Drop: $2 \times 6.5 = 13\,°C$. ISA temperature: $15 - 13 = \mathbf{2\,°C}$. Half thousands work the same way: multiply by 6.5, not by 6 or 7.

??? check "Check yourself: why does the pressure fall faster in the first 5,000 ft of a climb than between 15,000 and 20,000 ft?"
    Because air is compressible. The lower layers carry the weight of everything above them and are squeezed denser, so each thousand feet near the ground contains more air, and climbing through it removes more pressure. Higher up the air is thinner, so each thousand feet removes less.

## How you get oxygen from the air to your cells (2.11.2)

Oxygen is only useful once it reaches the cells that burn it, above all the cells of your brain and the retina of your eye, which use oxygen fastest and have no store of it. Getting it there takes two systems working together: the **respiratory system**, which brings air into contact with blood, and the **circulatory system**, which carries the blood around the body. Follow one breath on its way.

### Breathing in

You do not suck air in. Your **diaphragm**, the dome of muscle under the lungs, contracts and flattens, and the muscles between your ribs lift the ribcage. The chest gets bigger, the pressure inside the lungs drops slightly below the outside air pressure, and air flows in to fill the space. Breathing out is mostly passive: the muscles relax, the chest springs back and pushes air out.

The air travels through the nose or mouth, down the windpipe (**trachea**), into two **bronchi**, one to each lung, and then through smaller and smaller branches, like the twigs of a tree, until it reaches millions of tiny air sacs called **alveoli**. The alveoli are where the work is done. Each one is wrapped in a net of the finest blood vessels, the **capillaries**, and the wall between the air and the blood is only a cell or two thick.

### Gas exchange by diffusion

Oxygen does not get pumped across the alveolar wall. It **diffuses**: gas molecules move from where their pressure is higher to where it is lower, the way a drop of ink spreads through a glass of water. Air in the alveoli has a higher oxygen pressure than the blood arriving from the body, which has given up much of its oxygen, so oxygen flows into the blood. The blood arriving from the body carries a higher carbon dioxide pressure than the alveolar air, so carbon dioxide flows the other way, into the lungs, to be breathed out.

Hold on to that idea: the **driving force is a difference in pressure**. Everything in the partial pressure section below depends on it.

### Carrying the oxygen

Very little oxygen dissolves in blood plasma. Almost all of it is carried by **haemoglobin**, an iron-containing protein packed inside the **red blood cells**. Haemoglobin picks oxygen up where the oxygen pressure is high, in the lungs, and releases it where the pressure is low, in the working tissues. That is what makes it such a good carrier, and it is also its weak point: carbon monoxide binds to haemoglobin far more strongly than oxygen does and takes its place, which is why a leaking cabin heater is so dangerous ([PHFC 2.10](/notes/PHFC/2.10)).

### The two loops of the circulation

The heart is two pumps side by side, driving blood round two loops:

1. The **right side** pumps oxygen-poor blood, returning from the body, through the **lungs**, where it picks up oxygen and gives up carbon dioxide.
2. The **left side** pumps the now oxygen-rich blood out through the **arteries** to the **body's tissues**. In the capillaries of the tissues oxygen leaves the blood, the cells use it to release energy from food, and they produce **carbon dioxide** as waste. The blood returns to the heart through the **veins**.

Carbon dioxide makes the return journey mostly dissolved in the plasma in the form of **bicarbonate**, and is released again in the lungs.

![How does oxygen get from the air to your cells?](diagram:respiration-and-circulation "What to notice: the right side of the heart sends oxygen-poor blood through the lungs, the left side sends oxygen-rich blood to the body; diffusion moves the gases at each end.")

The whole chain looks like this: air into the lungs, oxygen across the alveolar wall, onto haemoglobin, round the body by the heart, off the haemoglobin into the cells, used by the cells. Every link can fail, and each failure has a name.

![Where does each type of hypoxia break the oxygen supply?](diagram:hypoxia-types-and-causes "Follow the chain from air to cells: hypoxic hypoxia is a failure at the lungs, anaemic in the blood, stagnant in the circulation, histotoxic in the cells.")

That diagram is the bridge to the next lesson. Low oxygen pressure in the lungs (altitude), less usable haemoglobin (carbon monoxide, blood donation, anaemia), poor circulation (g, cold, shock), or cells unable to use the oxygen they are given (alcohol, some drugs): these are the four types of hypoxia in [PHFC 2.12](/notes/PHFC/2.12).

??? check "Check yourself: what carries almost all of the oxygen in your blood, and where?"
    Haemoglobin, inside the red blood cells. It loads oxygen in the lungs, where the oxygen pressure is high, and unloads it in the tissues, where the pressure is low. Only a small amount travels dissolved in the plasma.

### What makes you breathe

You might expect your body to breathe harder when oxygen runs low. Mostly it does not. The brain's breathing centre controls the rate and depth of breathing mainly by sensing the **level of carbon dioxide** in the blood, and the acidity that carbon dioxide causes. When you exercise you produce more carbon dioxide, the level rises, and you breathe harder. When you sit still in the cockpit, carbon dioxide production is steady and so is your breathing.

That design has two consequences you will meet again:

- **Hypoxia creeps up unfelt.** At altitude your oxygen supply falls but your carbon dioxide does not rise, so nothing triggers the feeling of breathlessness. You can be short of oxygen with no sensation of being short of air.
- **Over-breathing upsets the balance.** If anxiety makes you breathe faster and deeper than you need, you blow off too much carbon dioxide. The level falls below normal and you get dizziness, tingling and eventually muscle spasm: **hyperventilation** ([PHFC 2.3](/notes/PHFC/2.3)).

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The exam offers "the level of oxygen in the blood" as the main control of breathing. It is the level of **carbon dioxide**. That is precisely why hypoxia gives no warning of breathlessness.

??? check "Check yourself: why does a pilot becoming hypoxic at altitude not feel short of breath?"
    Because the urge to breathe comes mainly from rising carbon dioxide, not falling oxygen. At altitude carbon dioxide production is unchanged, so the breathing centre sees nothing wrong while the oxygen supply to the brain falls.

## Partial pressure of oxygen (2.11.3)

Go back to the stack of blankets, but now imagine each blanket woven from three colours of thread: 78 percent blue for nitrogen, 21 percent red for oxygen, one percent green for the rest. The total pressure at the bottom of the stack is shared between the colours in exactly those proportions. The share of the pressure due to one gas in a mixture is that gas's **partial pressure**, and the rule that the partial pressures add up to the total is **Dalton's law**.

For oxygen, the **partial pressure of oxygen**, written $P_{O_2}$, is therefore:

$$P_{O_2} = 0.21 \times P_{\text{total}}$$

Why does this matter more than the percentage? Go back to diffusion. Oxygen crosses the alveolar wall because its pressure in the alveoli is higher than in the blood. The rate at which it crosses, and how fully the haemoglobin loads up, depends on that **pressure**, not on what fraction of the air oxygen happens to be. A gas at 21 percent of 1013 hPa pushes far harder than the same gas at 21 percent of 500 hPa. When $P_{O_2}$ falls, less oxygen diffuses into the blood and the haemoglobin leaves the lungs less than fully loaded.

The partial pressures at the levels in the table:

- Sea level: $0.21 \times 1013 \approx 213$ hPa (about 160 mmHg).
- 10,000 ft: $0.21 \times 700 \approx 147$ hPa, roughly 70 percent of sea level.
- 18,000 ft: $0.21 \times 500 \approx 105$ hPa, half the sea level value.

The pattern is simple because the 21 percent never changes: the oxygen partial pressure is always the same fraction of sea level as the total pressure is. Total pressure halves by about 18,000 ft, so the partial pressure of oxygen halves too, with the oxygen fraction still 21 percent.

!!! example "Worked example: oxygen partial pressure at 5,000 ft"
    Given: total pressure at 5,000 ft about 843 hPa; oxygen 21 percent of the air; sea level $P_{O_2}$ about 213 hPa.

    1. Partial pressure: $0.21 \times 843 = \mathbf{177\ hPa}$ (177.0).
    2. Compare with sea level: $177 / 213 = \mathbf{0.83}$.

    At 5,000 ft each breath pushes oxygen into your blood with about 83 percent of the sea level pressure. A healthy pilot sitting still notices nothing, but this is already the height at which night vision begins to suffer (see [PHFC 2.12](/notes/PHFC/2.12)).

### What the lungs actually see

The air deep in your lungs is not quite outside air. By the time it reaches the alveoli it has been warmed and saturated with **water vapour**, and it is mixed with the **carbon dioxide** coming out of the blood. Both take up part of the total pressure, so the oxygen partial pressure in the alveoli is noticeably lower than the figures above. The water vapour and carbon dioxide shares do not shrink as you climb, so they take a bigger bite out of a smaller total the higher you go.

Haemoglobin is forgiving at first. Over a range of oxygen pressures near sea level it stays almost fully loaded, so the drop to 5,000 or 8,000 ft makes little difference to how much oxygen the blood carries. Below a certain alveolar pressure its grip weakens and saturation falls away steeply. In practice that point is reached somewhere above about **10,000 ft**, which is why blood oxygen saturation drops noticeably there, why the symptoms of hypoxia begin to appear, and why the rules on supplemental oxygen start at about that height (see [PHFC 2.12](/notes/PHFC/2.12) for the requirement and its source).

![Why does 8,000 ft barely change what your blood carries, when higher still it does?](diagram:oxygen-saturation-curve "What to notice: haemoglobin stays almost fully loaded on the flat part of the curve; somewhere above about 10,000 ft it falls away steeply, which is where hypoxia symptoms begin.")

### Putting the pressure back

If the problem is a low oxygen partial pressure, there are only two ways to fix it, and both follow from the formula:

- **Raise the total pressure**: descend, or pressurise the cabin so the air you breathe is at a lower "cabin altitude".
- **Raise the oxygen fraction**: breathe **supplemental oxygen**, which lifts the fraction above 21 percent so that the same low total pressure gives a higher oxygen partial pressure.

To see the second one in round numbers, ignoring water vapour: to get back the sea level 213 hPa of oxygen at 18,000 ft, where the total is about 500 hPa, the gas you breathe would need to be about $213 / 500 \approx 43$ percent oxygen. That is the principle behind every oxygen system, from a cannula in a Cessna 210 to an airliner's drop-down mask.

??? check "Check yourself: at 18,000 ft, which has halved compared with sea level: the oxygen percentage, the total pressure, or the oxygen partial pressure?"
    The total pressure and the oxygen partial pressure have both halved. The oxygen percentage is still 21 percent, because the gases stay mixed at all the heights you fly.

## Bringing it together

You plan a summer trip from Jandakot to Esperance, and the forecast has the smoothest air at 9,500 ft. Work through what the atmosphere is doing to you there. The ISA temperature is $15 - 2 \times 9.5 = -4$ degrees, though on a hot summer day the real air will be much warmer than ISA. The pressure is a little above 700 hPa, so every breath holds roughly 70 percent of the oxygen molecules it held on the ground, still 21 percent of the air. The partial pressure of oxygen is about 150 hPa, well down from 213. In the alveoli it is lower still once water vapour and carbon dioxide take their share, and you are close to the point where haemoglobin's saturation starts to fall away. Your breathing centre is watching carbon dioxide, which has not changed, so it will not warn you. If you are tired, a smoker, or flying at night, you will be affected sooner (the factors in [PHFC 2.12](/notes/PHFC/2.12)). None of this means 9,500 ft is unsafe; it means you understand why the next few thousand feet are a different matter.

??? check "Check yourself: name the two ways to raise the oxygen partial pressure you breathe, and how each works."
    Increase the total pressure, by descending or by pressurising the cabin, or increase the oxygen fraction by breathing supplemental oxygen. Either raises $0.21 \times P_{\text{total}}$ (or its equivalent with a higher fraction), so more oxygen diffuses into the blood.

## Key points

- **2.11.1** Dry air is about 78 percent nitrogen, 21 percent oxygen and 1 percent other gases, and the proportions do not change with height. Pressure falls with height, fastest near the ground (about 1 hPa per 30 ft), to about half at 18,000 ft. ISA temperature is +15 degrees at sea level and falls about 2 degrees per 1,000 ft to about -56.5 degrees at the tropopause (about 36,000 ft).
- **2.11.2** The diaphragm draws air into the alveoli, oxygen diffuses into the blood and carbon dioxide diffuses out; haemoglobin in the red cells carries the oxygen; the right heart pumps blood through the lungs and the left heart pumps it to the body. Breathing is driven mainly by the carbon dioxide level, which is why hypoxia gives no breathlessness.
- **2.11.3** The partial pressure of oxygen is its share of the total pressure, $0.21 \times P_{\text{total}}$. It is the pressure, not the percentage, that drives oxygen into the blood, and it falls in step with the total pressure: about 213 hPa at sea level, 147 at 10,000 ft, 105 at 18,000 ft.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    The **percentage** of oxygen at 18,000 ft is still about 21 percent. What has halved is the total pressure, and so the oxygen **partial pressure**. Have by heart: 78/21/1 for the air, +15 degrees and 2 degrees per 1,000 ft for the ISA, 1013 hPa at sea level and about half at 18,000 ft, haemoglobin as the oxygen carrier, carbon dioxide as the breathing trigger, and $P_{O_2} = 0.21 \times P_{\text{total}}$ for any partial pressure question.
