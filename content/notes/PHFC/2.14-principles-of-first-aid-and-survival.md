## What the exam expects (PHFC 2.14)

- **2.14.1** Know what first aid and survival guidance the ERSA carries, and be able to recall and apply it: the first aid action plan and the treatment of common injuries, the survival priorities after a forced landing, and the signals that help searchers find you.

## Why this matters

Western Australia is a big, empty place to have a forced landing. Between Kalgoorlie and the South Australian border, or north of Meekatharra, you can fly for an hour over station country and salt lakes with no town, no road traffic and no mobile coverage, on a summer day when the ground is over 40 degrees. A forced landing there that injures nobody can still kill you if you walk off into the heat, and a minor injury left untreated can become a serious one before help arrives. This lesson teaches the first aid and survival principles in the ERSA's emergency section, and the reasoning behind each, so that you would know what to do and in what order with no one to ask. The exam asks where the information lives, the order of the first aid action plan, the CPR ratio, the right survival action after a remote forced landing, and the meaning of the ground-to-air signals.

## Where the information lives (2.14.1)

Every flight in Australia carries, on paper or on a tablet, the **ERSA** (En Route Supplement Australia), the book you use for aerodrome details and frequencies. It has an **EMERG** section, short for emergency procedures. That section contains emergency procedures and signals, search and rescue information, and first aid and survival guidance. It is the reference the exam means whenever it asks where a pilot finds first aid, survival or ground-to-air signal information in flight.

Know where it is in your copy before you need it. On an outback flight, after a forced landing, it may be the only reference you have, and a tablet with a flat battery is no reference at all. Content and layout change between editions, so check the current ERSA, and read the section through on the ground at least once: the time to learn what an arrow on the ground means is not when you are building one.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The exam lists other documents as distractors: the maintenance release, the weight and balance schedule, the NOTAMs. None of them carries first aid or survival guidance. The answer is always the **EMERG section of the ERSA**.

## First aid: the action plan (2.14.1)

Picture the scene. You have made a forced landing in a paddock east of Merredin. The aeroplane has run through a fence and stopped on its nose. You are shaken but unhurt; your passenger is slumped and not answering. The instinct is to rush straight to them. The first aid action plan exists to stop that instinct from creating a second casualty, and to put the life-saving steps in the order that saves the most lives.

That plan is **DRSABCD**:

| Step | Action |
|---|---|
| **D**anger | Check for danger to yourself, bystanders and the casualty (fuel, fire, a running engine, power lines) |
| **R**esponse | Is the person conscious? Talk and gently squeeze |
| **S**end for help | Call 000 (or 112 on mobile), or activate the ELT/PLB in a remote area |
| **A**irway | Open and clear the airway |
| **B**reathing | Look, listen and feel for normal breathing |
| **C**PR | If not breathing normally: **30 compressions to 2 breaths**, about 100 to 120 compressions per minute |
| **D**efibrillation | Apply an AED if available |

![What is the order of the first aid action plan?](diagram:first-aid-drsabcd "Danger first, always: fuel, fire and a running engine come before the casualty. CPR is 30 compressions to 2 breaths.")

Each step has a reason for its place.

**Danger first.** A crashed aeroplane is a hazardous place: fuel may be leaking onto a hot exhaust, the propeller may still be turning if the engine has not stopped, and a wire strike may have left live power lines on the ground. If you become a casualty too, nobody helps anyone. Turn the fuel and the magnetos off if it is safe to reach them, and if there is a fire risk, getting the casualty away from the aeroplane comes before anything else.

**Response.** Talk to the casualty and gently squeeze their shoulders. A person who answers is conscious and breathing, and you can move on to their injuries. A person who does not respond needs the rest of the plan, and quickly.

**Send for help.** Help takes time to arrive, so start it early, before you are busy with the casualty. In a town that is a phone call to **000** (or **112** on a mobile). In the outback it means activating the **ELT** (the aircraft's emergency locator transmitter) or your **PLB** (personal locator beacon), which alert the search and rescue system by satellite.

**Airway.** An unconscious person's tongue can fall back and block the throat, and vomit or blood can do the same. Open the airway by tilting the head back and lifting the chin, and clear anything visible from the mouth. Nothing else works if air cannot get in.

**Breathing.** Look at the chest, listen at the mouth and feel for breath on your cheek. Occasional gasps are **not** normal breathing.

**CPR.** If the person is unresponsive and not breathing normally, their heart is probably not pumping effectively and the brain is starting to die for lack of oxygen ([PHFC 2.11](/notes/PHFC/2.11) explains why the brain is so vulnerable). Chest compressions push blood round the body by hand; the breaths put oxygen into it. The ratio is **30 compressions to 2 breaths**, at about **100 to 120 compressions per minute**: push hard and fast in the centre of the chest, and keep going until help arrives, the person recovers, or you physically cannot continue.

**Defibrillation.** An **AED** (automated external defibrillator) can restart a heart in some kinds of cardiac arrest, and it tells you by voice what to do. You will not find one in a paddock, but you will at many airports and clubs.

??? check "Check yourself: why does checking for danger come before checking the casualty?"
    Because a rescuer who is injured or killed by fire, a turning propeller or live power lines cannot help anyone and becomes another casualty. Making the scene safe, or moving the casualty out of immediate danger, protects both of you and lets the rest of the plan be carried out.

## Treating common injuries (2.14.1)

Once the action plan has dealt with airway, breathing and circulation, look for the injuries a forced landing typically causes. The ERSA's first aid guidance covers the principles; the reasoning is what makes them stick.

**Bleeding.** Blood loss reduces the haemoglobin available to carry oxygen, the anaemic hypoxia of [PHFC 2.12](/notes/PHFC/2.12), and enough of it causes shock. Apply firm **direct pressure** with a pad on the wound, **raise the limb** if you can, and **keep the pressure on**. Lifting the pad to look lets the clot break and the bleeding restart.

**Burns.** Heat keeps damaging tissue after the source is removed. Cool the burn with clean running water for **20 minutes**, which stops the damage and eases the pain. Do not use ice, butter or creams: ice damages tissue further and grease traps the heat. Cover the burn loosely with something clean, such as cling film or a clean cloth, to keep out infection.

**Fractures.** A broken bone that moves damages the nerves, blood vessels and muscle around it. **Immobilise it in the position you found it**, with padding and a splint if you have one. Do not try to straighten it.

**Shock.** Shock here is not fright; it is a circulation failure, where too little blood reaches the organs, from blood loss, burns or severe injury. The casualty is pale, cold and clammy, with a fast weak pulse. Lie them down, keep them warm, reassure them, and give nothing by mouth in case they need surgery.

**Spinal injury.** If the casualty may have hurt their neck or back (a heavy impact, neck pain, numbness or tingling in the limbs), moving them could cause permanent paralysis. **Avoid moving them** unless there is immediate danger such as fire, which outweighs the spinal risk.

**The recovery position.** An unconscious casualty who **is** breathing should be rolled onto their side in the **recovery position** with the airway clear, so that vomit drains out of the mouth rather than into the lungs. Keep checking their breathing.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    With a burn, the urge is to apply something cold and quick: ice, or a cream from the first aid kit. The ERSA principle is cool **running water** for 20 minutes, and nothing greasy or frozen. Likewise with a fracture, "straighten it to make the splint fit" is wrong: splint it as found.

??? check "Check yourself: an unconscious passenger is breathing normally after a forced landing, with no sign of spinal injury and no fire. What position do you put them in, and why?"
    The recovery position, on their side with the airway open. An unconscious person cannot protect their own airway, and on their side the tongue falls forward and any vomit drains out instead of being inhaled.

## Survival: what comes first (2.14.1)

Now change the scenario. You have made a successful forced landing on a claypan two hours' flying from Kalgoorlie. Nobody is hurt. The aeroplane is intact but cannot fly. It is 2 pm in February. What do you do, in what order?

The survival priorities, in order, are **protection**, **location**, **water**, then **food**.

![What comes first after a forced landing in the outback?](diagram:survival-priorities "Protection, location, water, food, in that order, and stay with the aircraft: it is far easier to find than a person walking.")

**Protection** comes first because the immediate threats are injury and exposure. Treat anyone who is hurt, using the action plan above. Then get out of the sun or the cold: in the WA outback in summer the heat can kill in a day, while at night in winter it can be close to freezing. The aircraft and its wing give you ready-made shade and shelter.

**Location** comes next: help rescuers find you. A search will start, but it can only end when they see you, so everything that makes you easier to see shortens your wait. Location is treated in detail below.

**Water** is third because a person can survive weeks without food but only **days without water**, and in heat far less. In the WA outback heat, **dehydration** is the main killer.

**Food** is last. You will be hungry, but hunger will not harm you in the time a search is likely to take. Eating also uses water to digest, so if water is short, eat little.

### Stay with the aircraft

The single most important survival decision is to **stay with the aircraft**. There are three reasons, and each one is strong on its own:

1. **It is far easier to see.** From the air a person on foot in scrub is almost invisible; a light aeroplane, especially with its doors open and bright material spread around it, is a large, unnatural shape that searchers are trained to spot.
2. **It gives shelter.** Shade under the wing, protection from wind, and materials (seat cushions, cowlings, oil, fuel) for signals and warmth.
3. **The search starts where you said you would be.** Your **flight notification**, the flight plan or **SARTIME** you lodged before departure, tells the search and rescue system your planned route. If you do not cancel your SARTIME, the search is triggered and it begins along that route. Walk away from the aircraft and you leave the place they will look.

Leave the aircraft only if you are **certain** help is close and reachable: a homestead in sight, a road with traffic visible. A shimmer on the horizon that might be a building is not certainty.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    "Walk to the nearest town for help" and "split up to cover more ground" are the exam's wrong answers. Both take people away from the aircraft, use up water and make them harder to find. The right answer is to stay together, with the aircraft.

## Being found (2.14.1)

**The ELT or PLB.** Activate the **ELT** or **PLB** (406 MHz) and **keep it activated**. The 406 MHz signal is picked up by satellites and gives rescuers your position. Turning it off to save the battery stops the very thing that will bring help.

**The radio.** If the aircraft's radio works, try the area frequency and **121.5 MHz**, the emergency frequency. An aircraft passing high overhead may hear you even when nobody on the ground can.

**Visual signals.** Searchers look for anything that does not belong in the landscape: smoke, flashes, straight lines, bright colours. Prepare them in advance so they are ready the moment you hear an aircraft:

- a **fire**, with **smoke by day** (add green leaves, oil or a tyre) and **flame by night**, kept well away from the aircraft and any spilt fuel;
- a **signal mirror**, or any shiny surface, to flash at an aircraft;
- **bright material** spread on the ground: clothing, cowlings, life jackets;
- the **ground-to-air signal codes** shown in the ERSA.

### The ground-to-air codes

The ERSA shows a set of standard symbols that survivors lay out on the ground to tell searchers what they need. For example:

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **V** | Require assistance |
| **X** | Require medical assistance |
| **N** | No |
| **Y** | Yes |
| **Arrow** | Proceeding in this direction |

![What do the ground-to-air codes look like laid out?](diagram:ground-to-air-survival-codes "What to notice: V for assistance and X for medical assistance are the two to know; make every symbol large and contrasting, and lay an arrow only if you leave.")

Make them **large and contrasting**: rocks on red sand, dark branches on a pale claypan, or trenches dug so that their shadows show. A symbol a few metres across is invisible from a search aircraft at height; make each line as long and as wide as the materials allow. The arrow is the one to use only if you really do leave, and it points the way you have gone.

??? check "Check yourself: survivors who decide to walk to a homestead they can see should lay out which symbol, and why?"
    An arrow pointing the direction they are going, meaning "proceeding in this direction". Searchers who find the aircraft then know where to look, rather than starting a fresh search around the wreck.

## Water and heat: the WA survival problem (2.14.1)

In a WA summer the survival problem is mostly a water problem, and the way to solve it is to cut the losses rather than to ration the intake.

You lose water mainly through **sweat**, and the harder you work and the hotter you are, the more you lose. So **rest in the shade by day** and do any work, such as building signals or collecting wood, in the cool of the evening and early morning. Stay clothed and covered: skin exposed to the sun and the hot wind loses more water, not less. Sit off the hot ground if you can.

Drink what you need. Rationing water to a few mouthfuls a day while you work in the sun risks dehydration with water still in the bottle; the water does you more good inside you than in the container. Conserve **sweat**, not water.

The don'ts follow from the same physiology:

- **Do not walk away** from the aircraft unless you are certain help is close and reachable.
- **Do not ration water** to the point of dehydration; drink what you need and reduce sweat loss instead.
- **Do not drink alcohol, urine or seawater.** Each makes the body lose more water than it gains.
- **Do not smoke or light fires near spilt fuel.**

Keep the group **together** and give everyone a task: one on the signal fire, one watching and listening for aircraft, one looking after an injured passenger. Activity with a purpose keeps morale up, and morale keeps people doing the right things.

## Bringing it together

!!! example "Worked example: the first hour after an outback forced landing"
    A pilot makes a forced landing on a station track 120 nm east of Kalgoorlie on a 40°C day, uninjured, with 4 litres of water.

    1. **Protection**: no injuries to treat; move into the wing's shade, out of the sun.
    2. **Location**: activate the PLB and leave it on; make a radio call on the area frequency and 121.5 MHz.
    3. **Signals**: set out the "V" (require assistance) signal near the aircraft, large and contrasting.
    4. **Water**: stay in the wing's shade during the day to cut sweat loss, and drink as needed.
    5. **Ready for searchers**: prepare a smoky fire, away from the aircraft and fuel, to light when an aircraft is heard.

    The pilot stays with the aircraft throughout: the search will start along the notified route, and the aeroplane is what the searchers will see.

??? check "Check yourself: why is reducing sweat loss a better strategy than strictly rationing water?"
    Because the body loses water mainly through sweat, and dehydration harms you whether the water is lost or never drunk. Resting in shade and working only in the cool reduces the loss at its source, while drinking what you need keeps the water where it does good.

## Key points

- **2.14.1 Where**: first aid, survival and ground-to-air signal information is in the **EMERG section of the ERSA**. Read it before you need it, and check the current edition.
- **2.14.1 First aid**: DRSABCD, Danger first. CPR is 30 compressions to 2 breaths at about 100 to 120 a minute. Direct pressure for bleeding, 20 minutes of cool running water for burns, immobilise fractures as found, keep a shocked casualty lying down and warm, do not move a suspected spinal injury unless in danger, and put an unconscious breathing casualty in the recovery position.
- **2.14.1 Survival**: protection, location, water, food. Stay with the aircraft and stay together: it is easier to see, gives shelter, and the search follows your notified route.
- **2.14.1 Being found**: ELT or PLB (406 MHz) on and left on, radio on area frequency and 121.5 MHz, smoke by day and flame by night, a mirror, bright material, and the ground-to-air codes: V assistance, X medical assistance, N no, Y yes, arrow proceeding this way.
- **2.14.1 Water**: dehydration is the main killer in the WA outback. Conserve sweat, not water.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    The standard survival answer is **stay with the aircraft**, activate the ELT, and conserve water by reducing sweat loss. Searchers look for the aircraft along the notified route. Have by heart: ERSA EMERG for where, DRSABCD for the order, 30:2 for CPR, protection-location-water-food for survival, and V and X for assistance and medical assistance.
