## What the exam expects (PHFC 2.3)

- **2.3.1** Recognise hyperventilation from its symptoms, and state what to do to stop it.
- **2.3.2** Define hyperventilation and recall what causes it.

## Why this matters

Hyperventilation is the one physiological problem in this unit that you can give yourself on the ground, in the circuit at Jandakot at 1,000 ft, with nothing wrong with the aeroplane or the air. All it needs is anxiety: a first solo, a check flight, a moment of being unsure of your position over the wheatbelt, a passenger who is frightened of the bumps on a hot afternoon. Its symptoms are frightening, they make the anxiety worse, and they overlap with those of hypoxia, which is the far more dangerous condition at altitude. This lesson explains what over-breathing actually does to the body, how to recognise it, how to tell it from hypoxia, and the simple things that stop it within a few minutes. The exam asks for the definition, the causes, the symptoms that distinguish it, and the correct action, including the case where you cannot tell which of the two you have.

## What breathing is for

Every cell in your body burns fuel with oxygen and produces **carbon dioxide** (CO2) as waste, in the same way that the engine burns fuel with the oxygen in the air and produces exhaust. The blood carries oxygen from the lungs to the cells and carries carbon dioxide back to the lungs. Each breath does two jobs at once: it brings in fresh oxygen and it blows off carbon dioxide.

Here is the part most people do not expect. Your brain does not set your breathing rate mainly by measuring oxygen. It sets it mainly by measuring **carbon dioxide**. Sensors in the brainstem watch the level of CO2 in the blood, or more precisely the acidity it creates, because carbon dioxide dissolved in the blood forms a weak acid. When CO2 rises, as it does when you exercise, the blood becomes slightly more acidic, the sensors detect it and you breathe faster and deeper. When CO2 falls, the urge to breathe falls with it. It is a thermostat, and the setting it holds is a steady CO2 level.

At rest that thermostat gives you about **12 to 16 breaths a minute**, which is exactly enough to remove the carbon dioxide your body is producing and keep the blood's acidity in its narrow normal range. A modest level of carbon dioxide in the blood is not a waste product to be got rid of at all costs: it is one of the body's control signals, and several systems depend on it being where it should be.

??? check "Check yourself: what does the brain mainly measure to decide how fast you breathe?"
    The level of carbon dioxide in the blood, through the acidity it creates. More CO2 makes you breathe faster and deeper; less CO2 reduces the drive to breathe. Oxygen plays a much smaller part at normal altitudes.

## What hyperventilation is

Now breathe faster or deeper than your body needs for a minute. You are still producing carbon dioxide at the resting rate, but you are blowing it off much faster. The CO2 level in the blood falls below normal, a state called **hypocapnia**, and with less carbonic acid the blood becomes more alkaline.

That is the definition the exam wants. **Hyperventilation is breathing at a rate or depth greater than the body needs, so that too much carbon dioxide is blown off and the level of carbon dioxide in the blood falls.** The problem is not too much oxygen. The problem is too little carbon dioxide.

Three things follow from the low CO2, and between them they explain every symptom.

**The blood vessels of the brain constrict.** Carbon dioxide is the main signal that keeps the brain's arteries open. When it falls, they narrow, and blood flow to the brain drops. Less blood means less oxygen delivered, however rich the blood is.

**Haemoglobin holds on to its oxygen.** In the lungs, haemoglobin in the red cells picks oxygen up; in the tissues it is supposed to let it go. Carbon dioxide and acidity are what tell haemoglobin to release it: they are highest in hard-working tissue, which is exactly where oxygen is needed. With low CO2 and alkaline blood, haemoglobin grips its oxygen more tightly and releases less of it to the cells.

**Nerves and muscles become irritable.** Alkaline blood reduces the amount of free calcium available to the nerves, and nerves short of calcium fire on their own. That produces the tingling, pins and needles, and in more severe cases the muscle spasms that cramp the hands.

Put the first two together and you have the central paradox of hyperventilation: **the brain is short of oxygen even though you are breathing in more oxygen than ever.** The blood leaving the lungs is fully loaded; it is just not getting to the brain in enough quantity, and when it gets there it will not let go.

??? check "Check yourself: a hyperventilating pilot is breathing in more air than normal. Why is the brain short of oxygen?"
    Over-breathing lowers the blood's carbon dioxide. Low CO2 narrows the brain's blood vessels, so less blood reaches it, and makes haemoglobin hold on to its oxygen, so less is released to the brain cells.

## What causes it

The causes have one thing in common: something makes you breathe more than your CO2 thermostat would ask for.

- **Anxiety, fear, stress or excitement.** This is the most common cause in light aircraft by a long way. The body's alarm response prepares you for physical action, and one of the things it does is speed up your breathing. In a cockpit there is no physical action, so the extra breathing is not needed and the CO2 falls. Typical triggers are a first solo, a check flight or flight test, getting lost, deteriorating weather, an unusual noise from the engine, or a passenger's fear of turbulence.
- **Pain or shock**, which drive the breathing rate up in the same way.
- **Motion sickness**, which often comes with rapid, shallow breathing.
- **Heat and vibration**, which act as background stressors and raise the breathing rate.
- **Hypoxia.** At altitude the body detects a shortage of oxygen and responds by breathing harder. That extra breathing blows off CO2, so a hypoxic pilot can also be hyperventilating. This is the link that makes the two conditions so easy to confuse.
- **Deliberate deep breathing**, for example taking "a few deep breaths to calm down", or over-breathing under heavy workload without realising it.

![Why does a little stress help and a lot hurt?](diagram:stress-performance-curve "Past the peak of the curve, more arousal brings anxiety, narrowed attention and panic: the state in which over-breathing starts.")

The anxiety cause explains why hyperventilation clusters around particular moments in training. The stress-performance curve rises to a peak and falls away: a moderate level of arousal sharpens you, but past the peak the same alarm response that narrows your attention also speeds your breathing. Hyperventilation is one of the physical signs that you have gone over the top of the curve.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    "Hyperventilation is caused by altitude." Altitude can contribute, through hypoxia, but the commonest cause is anxiety, and hyperventilation happens at **any altitude, including on the ground**. If an exam option says it occurs only above 10,000 ft, it is wrong.

## Recognising it

Hyperventilation usually develops over a minute or so, and it feeds on itself. The symptoms are alarming; the alarm makes you more anxious; the anxiety makes you breathe harder; the harder breathing deepens the symptoms. Knowing the symptoms in advance is what breaks that circle, because a symptom you recognise is far less frightening than one you do not.

![Why does hyperventilation keep getting worse once it starts?](diagram:hyperventilation-vicious-circle "What to notice: the symptoms feed the anxiety that drives the over-breathing; recognising it, slowing the breathing and talking out loud break the loop, deep breaths feed it.")

Each symptom traces back to the three effects of low CO2:

- **Dizziness and light-headedness**, from reduced blood flow to the brain.
- **Tingling or pins and needles** in the fingers, toes and **around the lips**, from irritable nerves.
- **Feeling hot or cold, and sweating.**
- **Visual disturbance**: blurred vision or tunnel vision, because the eye and the visual parts of the brain are very sensitive to oxygen supply.
- **A rapid heart rate and a sense of anxiety or panic**, part cause and part effect.
- **Muscle spasms or stiffness**, especially in the hands, which may cramp into a claw: the most distinctive sign, and the one hypoxia does not usually produce.
- **Eventually, unconsciousness.** Once you are unconscious, breathing returns to normal under the brainstem's control, CO2 rises and you recover. On the ground that is a harmless faint. In an aeroplane the unconsciousness may already have been fatal.

The classic picture is **tingling plus dizziness in someone who is anxious and breathing quickly**. Notice that rapid breathing is not always obvious from the inside. Over-breathing can be deep rather than fast, and many people do not realise they are doing it until someone points it out.

??? check "Check yourself: which symptom points most strongly to hyperventilation rather than hypoxia?"
    Muscle spasm or stiffness, especially in the hands, together with tingling around the lips and fingers in an anxious, fast-breathing person. The low CO2 and alkaline blood make the nerves irritable; hypoxia does not usually do that.

## Hyperventilation or hypoxia?

**Hypoxia** is a shortage of oxygen reaching the body's tissues. At altitude it is caused by the falling pressure of the air: the air is still about 21 percent oxygen, but there is less of everything in each breath, so less oxygen is pushed into the blood. It is taught fully in [PHFC 2.12](/notes/PHFC/2.12). It matters here because its symptoms overlap with hyperventilation's, and because one can cause the other.

![Why does the oxygen in each breath fall as you climb?](diagram:atmosphere-pressure-oxygen-vs-altitude "Oxygen stays about 21 percent of the air, but its partial pressure falls with total pressure: about 147 hPa at 10,000 ft against 213 at sea level.")

| | Hyperventilation | Hypoxia |
|---|---|---|
| Cause | Too little CO2 from over-breathing | Too little oxygen reaching the tissues |
| Altitude | Can happen at **any altitude**, including on the ground | Mainly at altitude (or from CO, alcohol, blood loss) |
| Onset | Fairly rapid, often with anxiety | Gradual and insidious; often unnoticed |
| Typical signs | Tingling, muscle spasm, dizziness, rapid breathing | Euphoria, poor judgement, cyanosis (blue lips and nail beds) |
| Breathing | Fast or deep | May be normal until late |

![Is it hyperventilation or hypoxia?](diagram:hyperventilation-vs-hypoxia "They share dizziness, tingling and visual disturbance; muscle spasm and anxiety point to hyperventilation, euphoria and cyanosis at altitude to hypoxia. Unsure up high: oxygen first.")

Read the table row by row and the difference in *character* stands out. Hyperventilation feels bad: you know something is wrong and you are frightened by it. Hypoxia often feels good: it brings a false sense of well-being (**euphoria**) and erodes the judgement you would need to notice it. That is why hypoxia is the more dangerous of the two, and why the rule for the uncertain case is built the way it is.

**The rule when you cannot tell.** If you are **above about 10,000 ft with oxygen available**, treat it as **hypoxia first**: go on to oxygen, or descend, and then slow your breathing. The reasoning is an asymmetry of consequences. If it is hypoxia and you treat it as hyperventilation, you spend your few minutes of useful consciousness breathing slowly in thin air, and you may not get another chance. If it is hyperventilation and you give oxygen, you have done no harm, and the act of controlling your breathing on the mask treats the hyperventilation too. Without oxygen on board, descent is the treatment for hypoxia and does no harm to a hyperventilating pilot either.

![How long do you have once hypoxia takes hold?](diagram:hypoxia-time-of-useful-consciousness "The time of useful consciousness shrinks fast with altitude, so a wrong guess costs more the higher you are.")

At the levels where most RPL and PPL flying happens, well below 10,000 ft, hypoxia in a healthy pilot is unlikely and anxiety is the likely cause of tingling and dizziness. But keep the other causes of hypoxia in mind at any height: carbon monoxide from a leaking cabin heater ([PHFC 2.10](/notes/PHFC/2.10)) produces dizziness and headache at 2,000 ft as readily as at 9,000.

??? check "Check yourself: why is giving oxygen the right first action when you cannot tell hypoxia from hyperventilation at altitude?"
    Oxygen cures hypoxia and does no harm in hyperventilation. Treating real hypoxia as hyperventilation wastes the short time of useful consciousness, so the asymmetry favours oxygen (or descent) first, then slowing your breathing.

## Stopping it

Because the cause is breathing too much, the cure is to breathe less until the carbon dioxide builds back up. Each of these does that in a different way.

1. **Recognise it.** Tingling plus dizziness while anxious is the classic picture. Naming it to yourself ("this is over-breathing, it is not dangerous if I control it") breaks the panic that drives the cycle.
2. **Deliberately slow your breathing rate and depth** to normal, about **one breath every 4 to 5 seconds**. That is 12 to 15 breaths a minute, back in the normal resting range. Breathe in gently through the nose, pause, breathe out slowly. With CO2 production continuing at the normal rate and less being blown off, the level recovers within a few minutes and the symptoms fade.
3. **Talk out loud.** Call out your checks, make a radio call, talk to your passenger. You cannot over-breathe while speaking, because speech forces slow, controlled breathing out. This is the most practical method in a cockpit, and it also gives your attention something useful to do.
4. **At altitude, use oxygen or descend**, in case the cause is hypoxia.
5. **Breathing into a paper bag**, so that you re-breathe your own carbon dioxide, works on the ground but is rarely practical in a cockpit, and it must never be used when hypoxia is possible, because it lowers the oxygen in each breath too.

And two things not to do:

- **Do not take deep breaths to calm down.** It is the instinct, and it is exactly wrong: deep breaths blow off more CO2 and make the symptoms worse.
- **Do not ignore it.** It can progress to unconsciousness, and a pilot who faints in the circuit does not wake up in time.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    "Take several deep breaths" appears as an answer option because it is what people say to someone who is anxious. For hyperventilation it is the wrong action. The correct answer slows the rate and depth of breathing.

Once you are breathing normally, deal with whatever caused the anxiety, so it does not restart. If you are lost, run the lost procedure step by step, aloud. If it is a passenger, give them something to do: holding the map, watching for traffic or describing the view, which keeps them talking.

## Bringing it together

You are flying a PPL cross-country from Jandakot to Kalgoorlie at 9,500 ft on a hot afternoon, with a friend in the right seat who has never been in a light aeroplane. Over Southern Cross the thermals make it bumpy. Your friend goes quiet, grips the seat, and then says her fingers are tingling and she feels light-headed. You notice she is breathing fast.

Run the lesson over it. The most likely cause is anxiety-driven hyperventilation: a nervous first-time passenger, bumpy air, rapid breathing, tingling and dizziness, all on the hyperventilation side of the table. But you are at 9,500 ft without oxygen, so mild hypoxia is possible and could be adding to the picture. The actions that treat both are the ones to take. Descend to a lower level where the air is smoother and denser. Talk to her calmly and get her talking back: ask her to read out the next town on the map. Coach her breathing, slow and gentle, about one breath every 4 or 5 seconds. Within a few minutes the tingling should fade. If it does not, or if *you* start to feel the same symptoms, consider whether something else, such as carbon monoxide from the cabin heater, is at work.

## Key points

- **2.3.1** Recognise hyperventilation by dizziness, tingling in the fingers and around the lips, visual disturbance, a racing heart, anxiety and, distinctively, muscle spasm in the hands; untreated it ends in unconsciousness. Combat it by slowing the rate and depth of breathing (one breath every 4 to 5 seconds) and talking aloud; never take deep breaths. If in doubt above about 10,000 ft with oxygen available, treat as hypoxia first: oxygen or descent, then slow breathing.
- **2.3.2** Hyperventilation is breathing more than the body needs, so too much CO2 is blown off. Low CO2 narrows the brain's blood vessels and makes haemoglobin hold its oxygen, so the brain goes short of oxygen despite the extra breathing. Causes: anxiety and stress above all, pain, motion sickness, heat and vibration, hypoxia, and deliberate deep breathing. It can happen at any altitude.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    The definition is about **carbon dioxide**, not oxygen: too much CO2 blown off. The commonest cause is **anxiety**, and it can happen at any height, including on the ground. The correct action is to **slow the breathing** and talk aloud. If the question puts you above about 10,000 ft with oxygen and unsure whether it is hypoxia or hyperventilation, the answer is **oxygen first**.
