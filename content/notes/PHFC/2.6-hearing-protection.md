## What the exam expects (PHFC 2.6)

- **2.6.1** State the recommended ways of protecting your hearing in and around aircraft: what to wear, how to wear it, how to manage radio volume and exposure, and how to keep track of your hearing over a flying career. (The MOS gives this subtopic a single element; it builds directly on the noise effects in [PHFC 2.5](/notes/PHFC/2.5).)

## Why this matters

Climb out of Jandakot in a Cessna 172 or a Piper Warrior and the noise in the cabin sits somewhere around 85 to 100 dB for the whole flight. That is loud enough to damage hearing, and it does so silently: there is no pain, nothing bleeds, and the loss accumulates one flight at a time over hundreds of hours until high-pitched sounds and the consonants in radio calls start to vanish. The same noise also tires you, masks readbacks and hides the stall warning. Good protection is therefore a safety measure on today's flight as well as a health measure for your career. The exam asks about this subtopic by recall: which method works best, which kind of headset suits which noise, what breaks a headset's protection, and what to do on the ground. This lesson gives each recommendation its reason so that the right answer is obvious when you see it.

## How noise damages hearing: a quick recap

You met the mechanism in [PHFC 2.5](/notes/PHFC/2.5); here is the part that protection depends on. Sound is a pressure wave in the air. Your eardrum vibrates with it, the three small bones of the middle ear pass the vibration to the fluid-filled **cochlea**, and inside the cochlea rows of fine **hair cells** bend with the fluid and fire the auditory nerve. Each hair cell is tuned to a band of frequencies.

Loud sound bends the hair cells harder than they are built for. After a noisy flight some are fatigued: sounds seem muffled and your ears may ring. That is a **temporary threshold shift**, and it usually recovers within hours to a day. Repeat the insult often enough and hair cells die. They do not grow back. That is **noise-induced hearing loss**: permanent, painless and cumulative, usually starting around 4,000 Hz, which is exactly where the consonants that distinguish "five" from "nine" live.

![How long can you be exposed to cabin noise before it damages hearing?](diagram:noise-levels-and-hearing-damage "Every 3 dB halves the safe time: 8 hours at 85 dB but only about 15 minutes at 100 dB, the top of a typical piston cabin.")

The damage depends on the **dose**: how loud, multiplied by how long. A commonly used guide (from [PHFC 2.5](/notes/PHFC/2.5)) is that about **85 dB(A) averaged over 8 hours** risks permanent damage, and that each **3 dB** increase halves the safe exposure time, because the decibel scale is logarithmic and 3 dB more is twice the sound energy. So the guide gives about 4 hours at 88 dB, 2 hours at 91, 1 hour at 94, 30 minutes at 97 and 15 minutes at 100 dB.

That one rule tells you how every method of protection works. There are only two levers: **lower the level at your eardrum**, or **shorten the time** you spend at that level. Every recommendation below pulls one of the two, and the level lever is by far the stronger, because each 3 dB you take off doubles the time you can safely spend.

??? check "Check yourself: why does taking 6 dB off the level at your ear make such a large difference to your safe exposure time?"
    Each 3 dB halves (or doubles) the sound energy, and the guide halves the safe time for every 3 dB. Taking off 6 dB doubles the time twice, so you can spend four times as long at the new level for the same dose.

## Headsets: the first line of protection

The single most effective protection in a light aeroplane is a good aviation headset, worn properly, on every flight. It does two jobs at once: it blocks the cabin noise from your ears and it delivers the radio and intercom directly into them, so you can hear speech without having to shout over the engine.

### How a passive headset blocks noise

A **passive** headset has no electronics for noise. It works by putting a barrier between the cabin and your ear: a hard, heavy shell (the ear cup) lined with absorbent foam, pressed against your head by the headband through a soft **ear seal** or cushion. Sound reaching your ear has to get through the shell, which reflects and absorbs most of it, or leak around the edge of the seal.

Two consequences follow from that design, and both matter in the exam.

First, a passive barrier works much better on **high-frequency** sound than on **low-frequency** sound. Low-frequency sound has long wavelengths and carries a lot of energy in slow, heavy pressure swings, which make the whole shell vibrate and pass straight through. The deep drone of a piston engine and the beat of the propeller are low-frequency, so a passive headset takes the hiss and whine out of the cabin noise much more effectively than it takes out the drone.

Second, the protection is only as good as the **seal**. Sound behaves like water finding a gap: a small leak around the cushion lets a large share of the noise straight in, and the heavy shell is then doing nothing. Anything that holds the cushion off the skin opens a leak. The common ones are the arms of glasses, hair or a cap under the cushion, and cushions that have cracked or hardened with age and sun. A headset left on the coaming of an aeroplane parked on the apron at Jandakot through a Perth summer has cushions that go hard and crack far sooner than you would expect.

The practical recommendations follow directly:

- **Check the attenuation rating** when you choose a headset. Manufacturers publish how much the headset reduces noise, usually as a single number and by frequency band.
- **Replace ear seals** when they crack, harden or flatten. They are cheap; the hearing they protect is not.
- **Keep the seal clear**: hair back, no cap under the cushion, and thin-armed glasses that let the cushion close around them.
- **Adjust the headband** so the cups press firmly and evenly. A loose headset is a leaking headset.

### How active noise reduction works

An **active noise reduction (ANR)** headset is a passive headset with electronics added. A small microphone inside each ear cup listens to the noise that has got through. A circuit inverts it, producing a sound wave that is the mirror image of the noise, a pressure peak wherever the noise has a trough and a trough wherever it has a peak, and plays it through the speaker. Where the two waves meet they cancel: **destructive interference**. What reaches the eardrum is much quieter.

![How does an ANR headset cancel engine noise?](diagram:anr-noise-cancelling "ANR plays an opposite wave that cancels the low-frequency engine drone; every headset also relies on an unbroken ear seal.")

The trick works best on exactly the noise that a passive headset handles worst. A low-frequency wave changes slowly, so the electronics have time to measure it and produce the opposite wave in step with it. A high-frequency wave changes so fast that any small delay in the circuit puts the cancelling wave out of step, and then it adds to the noise instead of cancelling it. So ANR is designed to work on the low-frequency band, and the passive shell and seal look after the high frequencies. Together they cover the whole range, and ANR is especially effective against the low drone of a piston engine and propeller.

![Which noise does a passive headset block, and which does ANR add?](diagram:passive-vs-anr-attenuation-by-frequency "What to notice: the passive shell and seal handle the high-frequency hiss; ANR fills in the low-frequency engine drone; together they cover the range, and both need the seal.")

Two cautions. The ANR electronics sit inside a passive headset and still depend on the passive seal: a broken seal lets in more noise than the circuit can cancel. And ANR needs power, from batteries or the aircraft. If it fails, a good ANR headset should still give you its passive protection, so you are no worse off than with a passive headset, but you will notice the drone return. Carry spare batteries on a long flight.

The other benefit of ANR is fatigue. The low-frequency drone is the part of cabin noise you feel in your chest and that wears you down over a long leg. Taking it out leaves you fresher at the far end, which is when you need to be sharp for the arrival.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    ANR does not make a headset "better at everything". It is effective against **low-frequency** noise (the engine and propeller drone). High-frequency noise is handled by the passive shell and the seal, which is why the seal still matters on an ANR headset and why a broken seal ruins either kind.

??? check "Check yourself: a passive headset and an ANR headset are both worn with thick-framed glasses that lift the cushions. Which one loses protection?"
    Both. An ANR headset is a passive headset with electronics added, and both depend on the cushion sealing against the head. A gap lets noise in around the cushion, and the ANR circuit cannot cancel more noise than it was designed for.

## Earplugs: protection that goes inside the ear

An **earplug** blocks the ear canal itself, so noise has to pass through the plug to reach the eardrum. There are three common kinds: soft **foam** plugs you roll and insert, pre-moulded flanged plugs, and plugs custom-moulded to your own ear canal. Good plugs, properly fitted, give good attenuation.

Like a headset, a plug works only if it seals. Foam plugs must be inserted properly: roll the plug into a thin cylinder, pull the top of the ear up and back to straighten the canal, push the plug well in, and hold it there while it expands to fill the canal. A plug that is just resting in the outer part of the canal, sticking half out, protects very little. A plug that keeps working its way out needs refitting.

![How do you fit a foam earplug so it actually protects?](diagram:earplug-insertion "What to notice: roll it thin, pull the ear up and back, push it well in and hold it while it expands; a plug resting half out protects very little.")

Earplugs have two uses in flying. In a very noisy aircraft (an open-cockpit or high-powered type, or an old aeroplane with a poor cabin) they can be worn **under a headset** for extra protection. Combining two barriers gives more protection than either alone, though less than the two figures added together, because some sound reaches the inner ear through the bones of the skull and no plug or cup stops that. And on the ground, a pair of foam plugs in your pocket is protection you can use anywhere.

With plugs under a headset, check before take-off that you can still hear the radio clearly at a moderate volume and that you can hear the **aural warnings**: the stall warning horn, and the gear warning in a retractable. Protection that hides the stall warning has traded one hazard for a worse one.

Cotton wool and tissue are not hearing protection. They do not seal the canal and the fibres pass sound easily. Fingers in the ears on the apron are not a plan either.

??? check "Check yourself: you wear foam plugs under your headset for the first time. What do you check before you taxi, and why?"
    That you can still hear the radio at a reasonable volume and that the stall warning and any other aural warnings are audible. Plugs lower everything you hear, wanted sounds included, and a missed warning is a safety risk on its own.

## Radio volume: the protection you can undo yourself

Here is the trap that catches pilots with good headsets. The engine is loud, the radio is hard to understand, so you turn the volume up. Every bit of extra volume is extra sound energy delivered straight to your eardrum, on top of the cabin noise that leaks in. Turning the radio up to override the engine adds to the dose; it does not protect anything.

The better answer is to **lower the noise the radio has to compete with**. With a well-sealed headset, and especially with ANR, the background level at your ear falls, and the radio becomes intelligible at a lower volume. The recommendation is to keep the radio and intercom **as low as gives clear speech**, and to fix poor intelligibility with a better seal or ANR rather than more volume. If you find yourself turning the volume up through a flight, ask why: often it is a seal that has slipped, or fatigued ears from the noise dose so far.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    "Turn the volume up so you can hear the radio over the engine" appears as a distractor. It increases the sound at the eardrum and is the opposite of protection. The right answer always reduces the noise reaching the ear.

## On the ground: run-ups, refuelling and passengers

Hearing damage does not care whether you are airborne. Standing beside an aeroplane during an engine run, walking past a run-up bay, refuelling while another aircraft starts up on the next bay, or helping passengers in and out near a running engine all expose you to noise at least as loud as the cabin, often louder, and without the shell of the cabin around you.

The recommendation is simple: **wear protection whenever you are near running engines**, on the apron, at the run-up bay and around the refuelling point. Earmuffs or plugs for anyone working outside the aircraft, and the headset on before start, not after the engine is running. Your passengers need the same: give each passenger a headset (or at least plugs) for the flight, and show them how to fit it properly in the passenger briefing. A passenger in the back seat without protection on a two-hour flight is taking the full cabin dose.

## Managing exposure: the time lever

The dose is level multiplied by time, so the second lever is time. You cannot shorten a cross-country, but you can avoid adding to the day's total.

- **Short breaks** in quiet between flights let fatigued hair cells recover. Two training flights back to back with a headset off in the run-up area between them is a bigger dose than the same flights with a quiet break.
- **Avoid other loud noise the same day**: a concert, power tools, a shooting range, a motorbike ride home. Your ears add up the whole day's noise, not just the aviation part.
- **Notice the warning signs**. Ringing in the ears (tinnitus) or muffled hearing after a flight is a temporary threshold shift: your ears are telling you the dose was too high. Treat it as a signal to improve your protection, not as normal.

!!! example "Worked example: noise dose on a cross-country to Kalbarri"
    Given: a 3-hour flight in a piston single with a cabin level of about 94 dB. Use the guide from PHFC 2.5: 85 dB for 8 hours, safe time halving for each 3 dB more.

    1. Unprotected safe time at 94 dB: $94 - 85 = 9$ dB, which is three halvings: $8 \div 2^3 = \mathbf{1\ hour}$.
    2. Dose for a 3-hour flight unprotected: $3\ \text{h} \div 1\ \text{h} = \mathbf{3}$, three times the daily limit.
    3. Suppose a well-sealed headset brings the level at your ear down to 82 dB. Safe time: $85 - 82 = 3$ dB below the guide, one doubling: $8 \times 2 = \mathbf{16\ hours}$.
    4. Dose for the same flight protected: $3\ \text{h} \div 16\ \text{h} = \mathbf{0.19}$, about a fifth of the daily limit.

    The same flight goes from three times the safe dose to a fifth of it, purely by lowering the level at the ear. The 82 dB here is an illustration, not a headset rating: check the rating of your own headset.

Try one before opening the solution. Your headset keeps the level at your ear to 88 dB on a 2-hour flight, and then you spend another hour in the run-up area without protection at about 91 dB. What fraction of the daily guide have you used?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    At 88 dB the guide gives 4 hours, so 2 hours is $2/4 = 0.5$ of the limit. At 91 dB it gives 2 hours, so 1 hour is $1/2 = 0.5$. Total $0.5 + 0.5 = 1.0$: **the whole daily guide**. The unprotected hour on the ground cost as much as the two hours in the air.

## Hearing checks: knowing where you stand

Noise-induced loss creeps up so gradually that you rarely notice it yourself until it is well advanced. The only way to see it coming is to measure it.

Your hearing is assessed at your aviation medical examinations, with **audiometry** (a hearing test across a range of frequencies) at intervals set under Part 67; check the current requirements with your DAME, as they change. Beyond the minimum, get an **audiogram early in your flying career** as a baseline. Later tests can then be compared with it, and a dip appearing around 4,000 Hz is the early signature of noise damage, visible long before you would hear the difference.

See a doctor if you notice ringing in the ears or muffled hearing that does not clear within a day, or if you find yourself regularly asking ATC to "say again". Persistent tinnitus or muffled hearing is not something to fly on and hope it goes away.

??? check "Check yourself: why get a baseline hearing test early in your training rather than waiting for the medical to find a problem?"
    Noise damage is gradual and painless, so you will not notice it yourself until it is advanced. A baseline lets later tests show the first change, usually a dip around 4,000 Hz, while there is still time to improve your protection.

## Bringing it together

You are planning a Saturday cross-country from Jandakot to Kalbarri, about three hours each way in a 172 with a cabin level in the 90s. Your old passive headset has hard, cracked cushions, and you wear glasses with chunky frames. On the last long flight you landed tired, with ringing ears, after asking for two repeats from Perth Centre. Every part of that has a fix from this lesson. New ear seals and thin-armed glasses close the leak. An ANR headset takes out the low-frequency drone that was wearing you down. With the background lower, the radio goes down to a comfortable volume and the repeats stop. Foam plugs in your flight bag cover the run-up and the refuel at Geraldton, and the passenger gets a headset and a thirty-second briefing on fitting it. Sunday is quiet: no concert, no power tools. And the ringing ears are now on your list for the next medical.

## Key points

- **2.6.1** The best protection is **reducing the noise reaching the ear**: a well-sealed aviation headset on every flight, with **ANR** for the low-frequency engine and propeller drone; keep ear seals in good condition and clear of glasses arms, hair and caps; use properly inserted earplugs where needed (under a headset in very noisy aircraft, and on the ground) while still hearing the radio and warnings; keep radio volume as low as gives clear speech; protect yourself and passengers around running engines; limit total daily exposure and take quiet breaks; and have regular hearing tests from a baseline early in your career.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Expect questions on the most effective method (reduce the noise at the ear with a well-sealed headset or plugs), on what ANR is good at (**low-frequency** noise), on what spoils a headset (a broken seal: glasses arms, hair, worn cushions) and on ground protection (wear it near running engines and give passengers headsets). Wrong answers include **turning the volume up**, cotton wool, and "protection is only needed on long flights". Have the 2.5 figures to hand: about 85 dB over 8 hours, safe time halving with each 3 dB.
