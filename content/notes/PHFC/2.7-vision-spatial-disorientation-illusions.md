## Why this matters

You will look for a white Jabiru against a white cumulus over the Swan Valley, judge your height over the glare of a salt lake near Kalgoorlie, and come back into Jandakot at last light with the ocean black behind you. In each case your senses give a confident answer that can be wrong. Mid-air collisions and loss of control after disorientation are two classic ways competent pilots die, and both start with trusting a sense where it cannot work. The exam asks mostly by recall: which receptor does what, how long dark adaptation takes, which way each illusion pushes you. This lesson gives every fact its mechanism, so you can work out the direction of any illusion for yourself.

## How the eye works by day and by night

The eye works like a camera. Light enters through the **cornea**, the clear curved window at the front, which does most of the focusing. It passes through the **pupil**, the opening whose size the coloured **iris** sets: wide in the dark, small in bright light. The **lens** behind it fine-tunes the focus by changing shape, and the image lands on the **retina**, the light-sensitive lining at the back, which sends it to the brain along the **optic nerve**.

The retina has two kinds of receptor, and most of the first half of this lesson follows from how differently they behave.

**Cones** need plenty of light. They see **colour and fine detail**, and they are packed into a small pit at the centre of the retina, the **fovea**. Whatever you look straight at lands on the fovea, which is why only a small patch of your view is ever truly sharp.

**Rods** work in **low light**. They are far more sensitive than cones but see only shades of grey, give poor detail and are very good at detecting **movement**. They lie around the periphery, and there are **none in the fovea**.

![Where are the rods and cones, and why look off-centre at night?](diagram:eye-anatomy-rods-and-cones "The fovea is all cones (sharp, colour, daylight) and has no rods, so at night an object stared at disappears: look 10 to 15° to one side.")

By day the cones do the work. At night there is too little light for them, the rods take over, and vision becomes grey, blurred and peripheral.

Where the optic nerve leaves the eye there are no receptors: the **blind spot**. With both eyes open each covers the other's and you never notice. But if a window post blocks one eye's view, an aircraft can sit unseen in the other eye's blind spot.

## Night vision and dark adaptation

Walk from a lit hangar onto a dark apron and at first you see almost nothing; half an hour later you can see the taxiway edges. That is **dark adaptation**: the receptors rebuilding the light-sensitive pigment that bright light bleaches. The cones adapt in about **7 to 10 minutes** and then stop improving. The rods take about **30 minutes** to reach full sensitivity. One glance at a bright light bleaches the pigment again.

So the methods of dark adapting all keep bright light off the rods:

- **Avoid bright light for about 30 minutes** before a night flight, and wear sunglasses in strong sun earlier that day.
- **Keep cockpit lighting dim.** Red light preserves adaptation because rods barely respond to it, but red markings on charts vanish under it; dim white is the common compromise.
- **Close one eye** when a bright light is unavoidable, to keep that eye adapted.

Several things degrade night vision, mostly by starving the oxygen-hungry retina. **Hypoxia** affects night vision first and from low altitude, noticeably from about **5,000 ft**. **Carbon monoxide** from smoking does the same at sea level (see [PHFC 2.10](/notes/PHFC/2.10)). **Alcohol**, **fatigue**, **vitamin A deficiency** (the rod pigment is built from it), **age** and **glare** all reduce it.

The limitation the exam tests most: because the fovea has no rods, there is a **night blind spot** in the centre of your vision. Stare at a faint light at night and it fades, because its image sits on the one patch of retina that cannot see in the dark. The fix is **off-centre viewing**: look about **10 to 15 degrees to one side** so the image falls on the rods. Keep the eyes moving too, because an image held still on the rods also fades within seconds.

??? check "Check yourself: why does a faint light disappear when you look straight at it at night?"
    Looking straight at it puts its image on the fovea, which has only cones and no rods. In the dark the cones do not work, so the centre of your vision is blind. Look 10 to 15 degrees to one side so the image falls on the rods.

## What the eye cannot do in flight

**Spotting aircraft and wires.** Only the fovea sees detail, and it covers a tiny part of the sky. Peripheral vision notices movement, but a small aircraft that is not moving across your view goes undetected. Each fixation needs about **1 to 2 seconds** to focus and register, so a sweep of the eyes that never stops sees almost nothing. A light aeroplane head-on is a thin wing edge and a propeller disc. **Powerlines and wires** are thinner still and usually seen only by their towers, which is why low flying along a wheatbelt valley is so dangerous.

**Empty-field myopia.** With nothing to focus on, the lens does not stay focused at infinity: it relaxes to a resting focus about **1 to 2 m** ahead. Over a featureless blue sky, above a hazy cloud layer, or in haze that hides the horizon, you believe you are looking into the distance while your eyes are focused just beyond the windscreen. The cure is to focus on something distant every few seconds (a wingtip, a cloud edge, a ground feature) and then search the sky at that focus.

**Glare** is light much brighter than its surroundings: a low sun, sunlight off cloud tops or a salt lake, light scattered by a dirty or scratched windscreen. It dazzles and cuts contrast, so aircraft and terrain blend into the background. Flying west into Jandakot late on a summer afternoon, into the sun and the sea glare, is the classic case.

**Colour vision.** Aviation uses colour to carry meaning: navigation lights, tower light signals, PAPI lights, runway lighting and chart symbols. A colour defect may make you misread any of them, so colour vision is assessed at the medical.

**Common defects** are mismatches between the eye's focusing power and its length.

- **Myopia** (short sight): the image of a distant object forms in front of the retina, so **distant objects blur**. Bad for spotting traffic.
- **Hyperopia** (long sight): the image of a near object would form behind the retina, so **near objects blur**.
- **Astigmatism**: the cornea is curved more in one direction than the other, so the image is **distorted**.
- **Presbyopia**: the lens stiffens with age and cannot round up for near focus. From about **40**, charts and labels get harder to read while distance vision may be fine.

**Flicker vertigo.** Light flickering at about **4 to 20 times a second** can cause dizziness, nausea and disorientation, and in rare cases seizures. In an aeroplane it comes from the sun through a turning propeller (low rpm, taxiing or descending towards a low sun) and from a strobe reflecting off cloud. Break the flicker: change rpm, look away or shade your eyes, turn off the strobes in cloud.

## Glasses and sunglasses

Vision changes so slowly that you adapt and do not notice, and an out-of-date prescription means you see traffic later than you think. If the medical requires correction, keep the **prescription current** with regular eye checks and carry the spare pair it may require.

**Sunglasses** are flight equipment:

- **Neutral grey**, so all colours darken evenly and light signals and PAPIs keep their meaning.
- Good **optical quality**, free of distortion.
- Roughly **10 to 15 percent** light transmission: enough to cut glare without hiding things in shadow.
- **Not polarised.** Polarising lenses can black out LCD displays and remove the sun glint off another aircraft, often the first thing you see of it.
- Not **photochromic**: the windscreen blocks the ultraviolet that darkens them.

??? check "Check yourself: why are polarised sunglasses a poor choice for flying when they cut road glare so well?"
    They cut glare by blocking light polarised in one plane, which also removes the glint of sun off another aircraft and can black out LCD screens. The glint is often your first sight of traffic.

## See-and-avoid and scanning

Mid-air collisions mostly happen in **good weather, by day, near aerodromes**: in the circuit, on joining, and on converging tracks near training areas. That is where traffic concentrates and where pilots are busiest with other things.

The geometry is the key. Two aircraft on converging tracks that will collide keep a **constant relative bearing**: from your seat the other aircraft stays fixed on the windscreen, neither drifting sideways nor rising nor falling. It does not move, so your movement-hunting peripheral vision ignores it. It grows slowly while distant and then very fast in the last few seconds; pilots describe it "blossoming" out of nowhere. Small target size, windscreen posts and wings blocking the view, head-down workload, empty-field myopia and glare complete the trap. The ATSB's study of the limits of see-and-avoid shows how unreliable unaided looking is.

![Why is an aircraft on a collision course so hard to see?](diagram:constant-bearing-collision "What to notice: on a collision course the line of sight keeps the same direction, so the other aircraft sits still on the windscreen and only grows.")

**Scanning technique** works with the eye's limits. Divide the sky into **sectors of about 10 to 15 degrees** and fix on each for **about 1 second** before moving on, systematically across the windscreen. Focus on something distant first. Scan most where traffic is likely, clear blind spots by lifting a wing before turning or weaving gently in the climb, and use the radio and the clock code to build a picture of who is where.

![How do you tell someone where traffic is?](diagram:clock-code-traffic "Twelve o'clock is the nose: traffic at two o'clock is 60 degrees right of the nose, wherever the aircraft is heading.")

??? check "Check yourself: an aeroplane sits motionless at your ten o'clock, slowly growing. Why is that more dangerous than one sliding across the windscreen?"
    No movement against the windscreen means a constant relative bearing, the geometry of a collision course, and gives your peripheral vision no movement to detect. One moving across the windscreen will pass ahead or behind.

## Disorientation and your three balance senses

**Spatial disorientation** is an incorrect perception of your attitude, motion or position relative to the earth: believing you are level when banked, or climbing when level.

Your sense of balance blends three systems:

- the **eyes**, by far the strongest: give them a horizon and you know your attitude instantly;
- the **vestibular apparatus** of the **inner ear**, beside the cochlea from [PHFC 2.5](/notes/PHFC/2.5);
- the **proprioceptive** senses, pressure and stretch receptors in skin, muscles and joints that feel how you are pressed into the seat: the **seat of the pants**.

![Which parts of the ear sense balance?](diagram:ear-anatomy "The semicircular canals sense angular acceleration and the otolith organs sense linear acceleration and gravity, all in the fluid-filled inner ear.")

The vestibular apparatus has two parts, and their design explains every illusion that follows.

The **semicircular canals** are three fluid-filled loops at right angles, one per axis. In each is a small flap of hair cells, the **cupula**. When your head starts to rotate the canal turns with it but the fluid lags, by inertia, and bends the cupula: you feel rotation. Two flaws follow. A rotation that starts very gently bends the cupula too little to register, so a **slow roll goes unnoticed**. And in a **steady rotation** friction soon brings the fluid up to speed, the cupula straightens, and the sensation dies away in about **10 to 20 seconds**. The canals sense the start and stop of rotation, not a steady turn.

![Why do the canals feel the start and stop of a turn but not the turn itself?](diagram:semicircular-canal-fluid-lag "What to notice: the fluid lags at the start and runs on at the stop, bending the cupula; in a steady turn it catches up within 10 to 20 seconds and the sensation dies.")

The **otolith organs** are pads of hair cells topped with tiny crystals. Gravity pulls the crystals and bends the hairs, which tells you which way is down. A linear acceleration pulls them in exactly the same way, so the otoliths **cannot tell gravity from acceleration**; they report only the direction of the total force. The seat of the pants shares the flaw: in a balanced turn the total force presses you straight into the seat at any bank angle.

So, element 2.7.9: **the inner ear and the seat of the pants are not reliable in flight**. They miss slow changes, stop sensing steady turns and confuse acceleration with tilt. With a horizon, the eyes overrule them. Without one, in cloud, on a dark night, in haze or over featureless ground, they are all you have, and they mislead you with total conviction.

??? check "Check yourself: in a steady, balanced turn in cloud, why do neither your inner ear nor your seat of the pants tell you that you are turning?"
    The canals sense only changes in rotation, and in a steady turn the fluid catches up and the signal fades within 10 to 20 seconds. The total force in a balanced turn presses you straight into the seat, so the otoliths and the seat of the pants report "level".

## The vestibular illusions

![Which balance illusions catch pilots, and what do they feel like?](diagram:vestibular-illusions "The leans, somatogravic pitch-up, the graveyard spiral and the wandering light all feel completely real: believe the attitude indicator.")

**The leans.** Without a horizon, the aeroplane rolls slowly into a bank, too gently for your canals: you still feel level. You notice the attitude indicator and roll briskly back to level. That roll does register, so the canals report a roll from level into a bank the other way, and you now feel banked opposite although you are level. The urge is to lean, or roll, back towards the original bank. Hold wings level on the attitude indicator and let the sensation fade.

**Linear acceleration: the somatogravic illusion.** Accelerate forward and the otolith crystals lag backwards, exactly as they would if your head tilted back. The otoliths combine gravity and the acceleration into one force pointing down and back, and without a horizon you perceive it as a **nose-up pitch**. The perceived tilt follows that resultant, $\tan\theta = a/g$: a forward acceleration of a quarter of $g$ feels like about 14 degrees nose-up ($\tan^{-1} 0.25 \approx 14°$). The danger is any strong acceleration near the ground without a horizon, such as a take-off over unlit country or a **go-around** at night with full power and flap coming up. The pilot feels a steep climb, pushes forward, and flies a serviceable aeroplane into the ground. **Deceleration** does the reverse: it feels like nose-down, and the pilot pulls up towards the stall.

![Why does accelerating forward feel like pitching nose-up?](diagram:somatogravic-resultant-vector "What to notice: the otoliths feel only the sum of gravity and the acceleration, tilted back; a quarter of g feels like about 14 degrees nose-up, and pushing forward flies into the ground.")

**Unperceived pitch, roll and yaw.** Any change below the canals' threshold goes unnoticed. A gentle wing drop while you refold a chart, a slow nose-down drift while you retune the radio, and the aeroplane eases into a descending turn you do not feel. Without a horizon this is how a **spiral dive** begins (see [RBKA 3.6](/notes/RBKA/3.6)).

**Angular acceleration: the Coriolis illusion.** In a prolonged turn the yaw canal has adapted. Now move your head sharply, down to the fuel selector or round to the back seat, and you swing canals into and out of the plane of the turn, so fluid starts and stops in several at once. The result is a violent sensation of tumbling about an axis you are not rotating on. Avoid sudden head movements in turns and in cloud.

**The graveyard spin.** In a spin of several turns the canals adapt and the rotation seems to slow. On recovery the rotation stops, the fluid runs on and bends the cupula the other way, and you feel a **spin in the opposite direction**. Without visual references, a pilot who applies rudder against this false spin re-enters the original one.

**The graveyard spiral.** The same adaptation in a prolonged turn makes the wings feel level. The aeroplane, still banked, loses height; the pilot sees the altimeter unwinding and pulls back. With the wings banked, back pressure tightens the turn: the descent steepens, speed builds and the spiral winds tighter. If the pilot does roll level, the canals report a turn the other way and the pilot rolls back in.

**Autokinesis.** Stare at a single light against darkness, a star or a lone farmhouse light out in the wheatbelt, and after several seconds it seems to move. The eye's small involuntary movements are normally judged against the surrounding scene; with nothing else in view, the brain reads them as movement of the light. Pilots have chased stars thinking they were aircraft. Do not fixate: keep the eyes moving and check the light against a fixed reference such as the window frame.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    In the leans you feel banked **opposite** to the bank you just corrected. In the somatogravic illusion forward acceleration feels **nose-up**, and the fatal response is pushing forward. Work each from the mechanism and the direction is never in doubt.

??? check "Check yourself: why is a go-around at night more dangerous for the somatogravic illusion than the approach was?"
    On the go-around the aeroplane accelerates strongly close to the ground. The otoliths cannot separate that acceleration from gravity, so it feels like a steep nose-up pitch, and pushing forward to lower it flies you towards the ground.

## When illusions strike and how to beat them

Illusions occur when **outside visual cues are poor or absent** (cloud, a dark night, haze, smoke, dust, water or featureless terrain), when cues are **misleading** (a sloping cloud bank), and during **accelerations, prolonged turns and head movements**, the moments the inner ear is at its worst.

The defining experience is a **conflict between the instruments and your senses**. The attitude indicator says wings level; your whole body says banked left. The instruments rest on gyroscopes that do not adapt, lag or confuse acceleration with tilt; your senses are doing what they were built for, in an environment they were not built for. Disorientation does not feel like confusion. It feels like certainty, which is what makes it dangerous.

You are more susceptible when **fatigued**, after **alcohol** or with a **hangover**, when **hypoxic**, on **medication**, **stressed** or **overloaded**, **unwell**, or out of **instrument practice**. Each reduces your ability to keep cross-checking against an insistent feeling.

To overcome illusions:

- **Believe the instruments.** Fly the attitude indicator, cross-check the others, and let the feeling fade.
- **Keep instrument skills current** so the scan is your default under stress.
- **Avoid the situations**: no cloud or dark nights without the training and rating; turn back early from deteriorating visibility.
- **Minimise head movements** and **do not stare** at single lights.

??? check "Check yourself: in thickening bushfire haze the attitude indicator shows 20 degrees of right bank while you feel level. What do you trust, and why?"
    The attitude indicator. Your canals do not register a slow roll and the seat of the pants reads a balanced bank as level, so feeling level proves nothing. Roll level on the instrument, accept the false sensation that follows, and turn towards clearer conditions.

## Visual illusions

The eyes can be fooled too: not by failing but by being shown a scene that looks like something it is not.

**False horizons.** The brain accepts the first line that looks like a horizon. A **sloping cloud bank**, a sloping ridge, or at night a line of coastal or road lights can be taken as level, and you bank or pitch to match it. Offshore from Perth at night the dark sea and sky can merge, so that boat lights look like stars. Cross-check the attitude indicator whenever the horizon is uncertain.

**Depth perception.** You judge height and distance from texture and familiar objects: fences, trees, paddocks. Over **water**, **snow**, **desert** and **salt lakes** there is no texture, and you usually believe you are **higher than you are**; Lake Lefroy near Kambalda or a glassy sea off Rottnest gives no cue at all. **Haze, smoke and dust** cut contrast so objects look further away, and you tend to fly lower to reach a runway that seems distant. Entering **fog** on an approach can feel like the nose pitching up, so you steepen the approach. Use the altimeter, a planned descent and, over featureless surfaces, a shoreline or road as reference.

**Windscreens.** **Rain on the windscreen** bends the light and can make you feel higher than you are, so you fly a lower approach. A **scratched, dirty or crazed** screen scatters light into glare and hides small targets; a curved screen distorts at its edges.

### Landing illusions

On approach you judge your angle by the shape the runway makes in the windscreen. Your brain has learned the picture of a correct approach to your usual runway; change the runway and it misreads the new picture as a change of angle.

- **Narrow or long runway**: looks like a normal runway seen from higher up, so you feel **high** and fly **too low**.
- **Wide runway**: you feel **low** and fly **too high**.
- **Upsloping runway**: the far end is higher than the near end, so the runway looks steeper and you feel **high** and fly **too low**.
- **Downsloping runway**: it looks flattened, so you feel **low** and fly **too high**.
- **Sloping approach terrain** works the same way: ground rising towards the threshold makes you feel high and fly low.

![Which runway pictures make you fly too low or too high?](diagram:visual-illusions-panels "Anything that makes you feel high (black hole, narrow or upsloping runway) leads to a low approach that lands short; use the PAPI or a planned descent profile.")

The deadliest is the **black hole approach**. At night over dark terrain or water, with nothing lit between you and the runway, the runway lights are all you can see. With no ground texture to give height, your brain judges the approach by the runway's shape and tends to fly a path that keeps that shape constant. That path is not a straight slope but a curve that sags below the glidepath, low and shallow, meeting the ground short of the threshold, and you feel high all the way down. In WA it is a night approach to a lit country strip surrounded by dark bush or wheatbelt, or a coastal runway approached from over the sea. Approaches over **water** by day give a milder version.

![What path does a pilot fly on a black hole approach?](diagram:black-hole-approach-profile "What to notice: holding the runway picture constant gives a curve that sags below the glidepath and meets the ground short; you feel high all the way down, so fly the PAPI and a planned profile.")

The countermeasure for all of them is to **fly the numbers, not the picture**: use the PAPI where there is one, plan a descent profile of height against distance and check it on the altimeter, and look up the runway's width and slope in ERSA beforehand.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Every illusion that makes you **feel high** (black hole, narrow or long runway, upslope) leads to an approach that is **too low** and lands short. The distractors swap the direction. Ask "do I feel high or low?", and the error is the opposite.

**Relative motion.** Sit in a stationary aeroplane while the one beside you rolls forward and you feel yourself rolling back. The brain takes movement of a large part of the scene as your own. Check a fixed reference before you act.

## Bringing it together

A winter return to Jandakot from Northam, running late. Westbound over the scarp into a low sun and coastal haze, you fight glare and empty-field myopia; a Warrior on a converging track sits fixed at your one o'clock, and you find it only because you are scanning in 10 degree steps through a clean screen and grey, non-polarised glasses. By the circuit the sun is down, the sea beyond the airport is black and the city lights make a tilted false horizon. On final the dark ground short of the runway is a small black hole, so you fly the PAPI and the planned heights, not the picture. A kangaroo forces a go-around: full power pushes you into the seat and it feels like a steep climb, but you hold the climb attitude on the instrument, because you know why it feels that way.

## Key points

- **2.7.1** Cones (in the fovea) give colour and detail by day; rods (periphery, none in the fovea) see in dim light and detect movement.
- **2.7.2** Cones adapt in 7 to 10 minutes, rods in about 30; one bright light undoes it. Hypoxia (from about 5,000 ft), CO, alcohol, fatigue, age and glare degrade night vision.
- **2.7.3** The fovea is a night blind spot: look 10 to 15 degrees off-centre and keep the eyes moving.
- **2.7.4** Fixations take 1 to 2 seconds; wires and head-on aircraft are hard to see; empty fields relax focus to 1 to 2 m; glare cuts contrast; colour carries meaning; myopia blurs distance, hyperopia near, astigmatism distorts, presbyopia from about 40; flicker at 4 to 20 per second causes flicker vertigo.
- **2.7.5** Keep prescriptions current; sunglasses neutral grey, 10 to 15 percent transmission, not polarised.
- **2.7.6** Mid-airs: good weather, daytime, near aerodromes; constant bearing means collision. Scan 10 to 15 degree sectors, about 1 second each.
- **2.7.7** Disorientation: an incorrect perception of attitude, motion or position relative to the earth.
- **2.7.8** Eyes (dominant), inner ear (canals for rotation, otoliths for gravity and acceleration), seat of the pants.
- **2.7.9** Canals miss slow changes and adapt in 10 to 20 seconds; otoliths and seat of the pants cannot tell acceleration from gravity.
- **2.7.10** Leans: banked the opposite way. Somatogravic: acceleration feels nose-up. Unperceived changes lead to a spiral. Graveyard spin and spiral: adaptation, then a false opposite sensation. Coriolis: head movement in a turn. Autokinesis: a stared-at light moves.
- **2.7.11** No horizon plus accelerations, turns or head movements produce illusions that feel certain; fatigue, alcohol, hypoxia, drugs and stress make it worse; believe the instruments.
- **2.7.12** Sloping cloud makes false horizons; water, salt lakes and haze defeat depth judgement; rain on the screen makes you feel high; narrow, long or upsloping runways and the black hole make you fly low; wide or downsloping runways make you fly high.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Have by heart: rods 30 minutes, cones 7 to 10; off-centre 10 to 15 degrees; scan sectors 10 to 15 degrees for about 1 second; canals adapt in 10 to 20 seconds; flicker 4 to 20 per second; sunglasses grey and not polarised. For an illusion, reason from the mechanism: the leans feel opposite, acceleration feels nose-up, feeling high makes you fly low, and the black hole lands you short. When your senses disagree with the attitude indicator, **believe the instruments**.
