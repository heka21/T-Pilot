## What the exam expects (PHFC 2.2)

- **2.2.1** Medical standards: why you are examined and how often, how to find a DAME, what the examination involves, and CASA's role, including the principle that only conditions which are a hazard to flight safety disqualify you.
- **2.2.2** Alcohol: how the body absorbs and gets rid of it, what a hangover is and how it affects flying, how a hangover relates to blood alcohol and how long recovery takes, what changes the rate of elimination, how alcohol and illicit drugs affect judgement, comprehension, attention, the senses, coordination and reaction time, and the symptoms of dehydration and the right fluids to correct it.
- **2.2.3** Drugs: why drug abuse is a behavioural problem whether or not the user is addicted or a frequent user, what a psychoactive substance is, the effects of cannabis, amphetamines (including ecstasy) and opiates (codeine, heroin) and how long they last, and the side effects of common over-the-counter and prescription medicines.
- **2.2.4** Blood donation: its effect on flying, and the recommended wait before your next flight and why it varies.

## Why this matters

Most student pilots in Perth are of an age when Friday nights are social, and most flying schools run lessons early on Saturday mornings. That collision is where this lesson lives: how long a night out actually stays in your body, why the legal 8 hours is only a floor, and why the morning-after headache is a reason not to fly even when a breath test would read zero. The same physiology explains why the hay fever tablet from the chemist at Jandakot's shopping centre can be a bigger hazard than the hay fever. The exam tests this subtopic with a short calculation (when is the alcohol gone?), with facts about elimination and hangovers that have a tempting wrong answer, and with recall of drug side effects and the blood donation wait.

## Medical standards: why, how often and who

An aeroplane is unforgiving of a pilot who suddenly stops functioning, and a medical condition can do that with no warning: a heart rhythm disturbance, an epileptic seizure, a diabetic low, a stroke. Other conditions do their damage quietly: failing eyesight, hearing loss, a mental illness that distorts judgement. The **aviation medical examination** exists to find both kinds before they show up in the air, and to manage them so that a pilot who has one can keep flying safely where possible.

CASR Part 67 sets the classes of medical certificate:

- **Class 1** for commercial and air transport pilots.
- **Class 2** for private pilots (PPL).
- **Basic Class 2** and other recreational-level standards may be usable for some RPL operations, with restrictions.

![Which medical can an RPL holder use, and what does each one limit?](diagram:rpl-medical-certificates "The easier the medical is to get, the tighter the limits: a Class 2 from a DAME carries no extra operational limits, a Basic Class 2 or a RAMPC from a GP does.")

**Frequency.** A certificate is valid for a set period that depends on the class and on your age, and it gets **shorter as you get older**, because the risk of the sudden-incapacitation conditions, above all heart disease, rises with each decade. Check CASR Part 67 for the current periods rather than relying on memory; [RFRC 2.2](/notes/RFRC/2.2) sets out the certificates an RPL holder can use.

**Finding a DAME.** A **Designated Aviation Medical Examiner (DAME)** is a doctor CASA has approved to conduct aviation medicals. You find one with the medical examiner search on the CASA website; Perth has several, and some regional centres have one.

**The process**, in order:

1. Apply through CASA's online medical records system and fill in your medical history honestly.
2. Book with a DAME and bring your history and any specialist reports for conditions you have declared.
3. Attend the examination: eyesight (distance, near and colour), hearing, blood pressure, heart and lungs, a urine test, an ECG at set ages, and a general check.
4. The DAME either issues the certificate or, for anything outside straightforward limits, refers your case to CASA.

**CASA's role** is to set the standards and to decide the difficult cases. Its guiding principle, and the one the exam asks about, is that **only conditions that present a hazard to flight safety are disqualifying**. Having a condition is not the question; the question is whether it could make you incapacitated or impaired in flight. Many conditions are acceptable with treatment, monitoring, or a condition on the certificate, such as "must wear distance correction" or a requirement for regular specialist reports. Being honest on the form is how you get to that outcome; hiding a condition is how you lose the certificate altogether.

??? check "Check yourself: a pilot has well-controlled high blood pressure. Does that automatically disqualify her from holding a medical?"
    No. CASA disqualifies only conditions that present a hazard to flight safety. A controlled condition is often acceptable, perhaps with monitoring or a condition on the certificate; the DAME and CASA assess it case by case.

## How the body handles alcohol

### Absorption

When you drink, alcohol passes from the stomach into the **small intestine**, which has a huge surface area and absorbs it rapidly into the bloodstream; a little is absorbed through the stomach wall. Most of a drink reaches the blood within about **30 to 90 minutes**. Food in the stomach holds the drink there longer, so absorption is slower and the peak lower and later, but food does **not** reduce the total: all of it gets into the blood eventually. Once in the blood, alcohol spreads into all the body's water, including the brain and, importantly for a pilot, the fluid of the inner ear.

### Elimination

About **90 percent or more** of the alcohol is broken down by the **liver**; the rest leaves in breath, urine and sweat (which is why a breath test works). The liver's enzymes work at a fixed maximum pace, so the liver removes a **fixed amount per hour** however much is waiting: roughly **one standard drink (10 g of alcohol) per hour**. It is like a single checkout operator in a supermarket: a long queue does not make her scan faster, and nothing you do to the queue changes her pace.

That is why **nothing speeds it up**. Coffee makes you more awake but does not remove alcohol. A cold shower, exercise, fresh air and sleep do not touch the liver's rate. Only time works.

**Factors that affect elimination** and the blood alcohol you reach: body size and composition (a smaller person, or one with more body fat and less body water, reaches a higher concentration from the same drink), sex (on average women reach a higher level from the same amount), liver health, regular drinking habits, food, and some medications. These change the starting blood level more than the hourly rate. So two pilots who drink the same thing do not have the same morning: the smaller one starts higher and takes longer to reach zero.

![When is the alcohol gone after a late night?](diagram:alcohol-and-blood-levels-timeline "The liver clears about one drink an hour whatever you do, so six drinks finished at 2300 last until about 0500; 0700 is only the legal minimum.")

### The rules

CASR 91.520 and Part 99 (verify current text): do not consume alcohol within **8 hours** before acting as a crew member, do not fly while affected by it, and the prescribed blood alcohol limit for safety-sensitive aviation activities is **0.02 g per 100 mL** of blood. The rules themselves are covered in [RFRC 2.3](/notes/RFRC/2.3). Put the rule beside the one-drink-an-hour rate and you can see at once that **8 hours is a minimum, not a guarantee**: anyone who drank more than about eight drinks is still carrying alcohol when the 8 hours expire.

!!! example "Worked example: when can you fly after six drinks?"
    Given: you finish your 6th standard drink at 2300. The liver removes about one standard drink per hour.

    1. Time for the liver to clear six drinks: $6 \text{ drinks} \times 1\ \text{h per drink} = \mathbf{6\ h}$.
    2. Blood alcohol near zero at about: $2300 + 6\ \text{h} = \mathbf{0500}$.
    3. Earliest legal time under the 8-hour rule: $2300 + 8\ \text{h} = \mathbf{0700}$.
    4. Take the later of the two: **0700 at the earliest**.

    The hangover may last well beyond 0700, so 0700 is the earliest you could go, not the time you will be fit to go.

Try one yourself before opening the solution: at a wedding at a Swan Valley winery you have ten standard drinks, the last at midnight. Your lesson is booked for 0900. Which limit decides when you can fly, and can you make the lesson?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    Liver clearance: $10 \times 1\ \text{h} = 10\ \text{h}$, so blood alcohol is near zero at about **1000**. The 8-hour rule gives **0800**. The later time governs, so **1000 at the very earliest**: you cannot make a 0900 lesson legally or safely, even though the 8 hours have passed. After that many drinks a hangover will follow, so rebook for the next day.

??? check "Check yourself: a friend says a strong coffee and a run will get him clear for his morning flight. Why is he wrong?"
    The liver removes alcohol at a fixed rate of about one standard drink an hour, and neither caffeine nor exercise changes that rate. Coffee only makes him feel more alert while the alcohol is still there.

## What alcohol and drugs do to a pilot

Alcohol is a **depressant**: it slows the brain's processing. Many illicit drugs act on the same faculties through different chemistry. The syllabus names six things they degrade, and each matters in a particular part of a flight:

- **Judgement** and risk perception. You accept weather, fuel margins or a tight gap you would refuse sober, and the first faculty to fail is the one that would warn you.
- **Comprehension**. Radio calls, clearances and instructions take longer to understand and are misunderstood more often.
- **Attention to detail**. Checklist items are skipped, a fuel cap left off, a wrong frequency dialled.
- **The senses**. Vision suffers, especially night vision and the eyes' ability to track; the balance system in the inner ear is disturbed.
- **Coordination**. Smooth, accurate control inputs, such as a flare or a crosswind correction, become coarse.
- **Reaction time** lengthens, so the response to an engine failure or a gust in the flare comes late.

Alcohol also makes the body's cells less able to use oxygen (**histotoxic hypoxia**), so a pilot with alcohol in the blood suffers the effects of altitude sooner. See [PHFC 2.12](/notes/PHFC/2.12) for the types of hypoxia.

## The hangover

A **hangover** is the collection of after-effects of heavy drinking that appear *as blood alcohol falls* and persist after it has reached zero: headache, nausea, fatigue, thirst, poor concentration, sensitivity to light and noise, and disturbed balance. It is not simply "still being drunk". Several things cause it at once:

- **Dehydration.** Alcohol makes the kidneys pass more water than you drink, so you wake dried out, with the headache and poor concentration that dehydration brings on its own.
- **Toxic breakdown products.** As the liver processes alcohol it makes intermediate chemicals that cause nausea and the general poisoned feeling.
- **Low blood sugar and poor sleep.** Alcohol disturbs the body's control of blood sugar and destroys the quality of sleep, so you are fatigued even after a full night in bed.
- **The inner ear.** Alcohol diffuses into the fluid and structures of the balance organs and changes how they respond to gravity. It goes in slowly and comes out slowly, so it can leave **positional vertigo**, a spinning sensation when you tilt your head, for many hours after blood alcohol has returned to zero.

**The effect on flying** follows from those causes: slower and poorer thinking, reduced attention, fatigue, and a balance system that is giving false signals, which in an aeroplane means a much higher risk of disorientation in turbulence or poor visibility (see [PHFC 2.7](/notes/PHFC/2.7)).

**Hangover and blood alcohol.** The two are related but separate. Blood alcohol measures what is still in your blood; the hangover measures the damage the alcohol has done. A pilot with a hangover can have **zero blood alcohol and still be seriously impaired**. A breath test that reads zero proves only that you are legal, not that you are fit.

![How do blood alcohol and the hangover play out after a heavy night?](diagram:blood-alcohol-curve-and-hangover "What to notice: blood alcohol keeps rising for 30 to 90 minutes after the last drink, falls about one drink an hour, and is still there when the 8 hours expire; the hangover starts on the way down and outlasts it.")

**Recovery period.** The more you drank, the higher your peak blood alcohol, and the longer both the elimination and the hangover last. The recovery is not finished when the alcohol is gone; after heavy drinking allow **24 hours or more** before flying.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    "The morning after, her blood alcohol is zero, so she is fit to fly." Zero blood alcohol is not fitness. The hangover, dehydration and inner-ear effects continue after the alcohol has gone, and they impair her just as surely. The exam also likes "coffee speeds elimination"; it does not.

??? check "Check yourself: why can a pilot feel the room spin when he turns his head the morning after a heavy night, even with no alcohol left in his blood?"
    Alcohol soaked slowly into the inner ear's balance organs and leaves them slowly too, so they still respond wrongly to gravity after the blood is clear. The result is positional vertigo, which in flight is a disorientation risk.

## Dehydration and rehydration

Dehydration matters here because both a hangover and a hot day cause it, and because its symptoms look like other problems. The **symptoms** of dehydration are thirst, headache, a dry mouth, dark urine, fatigue, dizziness (especially on standing) and poor concentration. Thirst is a late signal: by the time you are thirsty you are already short of water. Dark urine is the most reliable early sign you can check yourself.

The best fluids for **rehydration** are the ones the body absorbs fastest and keeps:

- **Water**, which the gut absorbs quickly and which has nothing in it that makes the kidneys pass more.
- **Diluted electrolyte drinks** (oral rehydration solutions), which also replace the salts lost in sweat. A little salt and sugar in the right proportion actually helps the gut take up water, which is why they are used to treat gastro and heat exhaustion.

Avoid **alcohol** and large amounts of **caffeine**, which make the kidneys pass more water, so you lose more than you drink. Avoid **very sugary drinks**, such as undiluted soft drink and cordial: concentrated sugar slows the emptying of the stomach and draws water *into* the gut rather than out of it, so they rehydrate slowly. On a February day at Northam, a litre of water before you walk out to the aeroplane does more for your landing than any amount of cola.

## Drugs

### Why drug abuse is a behavioural problem

**Drug abuse** is using a substance in a way that harms safety or health. The key word for the exam is that it is a **behavioural** problem: it describes what you do, not what has happened to your body. It is therefore independent of:

- **Dependence (addiction)**, a state in which the body or mind needs the drug. A pilot who has never been addicted to anything abuses a drug the first time he smokes cannabis the night before a lesson.
- **Frequent use.** One use before a flight is abuse; the frequency does not matter.

The point is that you cannot excuse a dangerous choice by saying "I'm not an addict" or "I hardly ever do it". The hazard is the drug in your system when you fly.

### Psychoactive substances

A **psychoactive substance** is any substance that changes how the brain works: mood, perception, thinking or behaviour. They come in two groups. **Illicit** substances are illegal: cannabis, ecstasy, heroin. **Non-illicit** substances are legal, and many are in every home: alcohol, caffeine, nicotine, codeine, sedatives and some cold and allergy medicines. Legal does not mean harmless in a cockpit.

Their **adverse effects** on a pilot fall into a short list: impaired perception, slower information processing, longer reaction time, poor judgement, and after-effects (a "come-down", drowsiness or a hangover-like state) that outlast the obvious effects.

| Substance | Effect on perception, processing and reaction | Duration of impairment |
|---|---|---|
| Cannabis (marijuana, ganja) | Distorted perception of time and distance, poor short-term memory, cannot divide attention between tasks | 24 hours or more; residues persist for weeks |
| Amphetamine-type (including ecstasy) | False confidence and risk-taking while active, then exhaustion and depression on the "come-down" | Hours, followed by a day or more of fatigue |
| Opiates (codeine, heroin) | Drowsiness, slowed reactions, poor concentration | Many hours |

Each row has a reason. **Cannabis** acts on the parts of the brain that handle memory and the sense of time, and it is stored in body fat and released slowly, which is why its effects on complex tasks such as flying last a day or more after the user feels normal. **Amphetamines** are stimulants: they make the user feel alert and capable while degrading judgement, and when they wear off the body pays back the borrowed energy. **Opiates** are depressants of the brain and of breathing; codeine is an opiate, which is why the over-the-counter and prescription painkillers that contain it are on the do-not-fly list.

??? check "Check yourself: a student says he smoked cannabis on Friday night but feels fine for his Saturday lunchtime lesson. Why is that not good enough?"
    Cannabis impairs the perception of time and distance, short-term memory and divided attention for 24 hours or more, well after the user feels normal. Feeling fine is not evidence of being unimpaired, and using it before flying is drug abuse whatever the frequency.

### Over-the-counter and prescription medicines

The medicines that cause most trouble for pilots are not illicit at all. The syllabus names these and their side effects:

- **Aspirin**: stomach irritation and a tendency to bleed more easily. Also, the pain or fever it masks may be a reason not to fly.
- **Antihistamines** (for hay fever and allergies): drowsiness and slowed reactions; many older types are strongly sedating, and the effect can last into the next day.
- **Nasal decongestants**: a rapid heart rate and anxiety; and they can wear off in flight, with **rebound congestion** leaving the ears or sinuses blocked just as you descend.
- **Amphetamines** (prescribed for some conditions): overconfidence, then fatigue.
- **Tranquillisers and sedatives**: drowsiness lasting into the next day.
- **Antibiotics**: the infection being treated matters most; some antibiotics also cause nausea or sensitivity to light.

The rule from [PHFC 2.1](/notes/PHFC/2.1) applies to all of them: check with a DAME before flying on any medication, and try it on the ground first.

## Blood donation

Giving a unit of whole blood removes about **470 mL**. That has two effects on a pilot. First, there is less blood volume to keep the brain supplied, so standing up quickly, pulling g or a hot cockpit can make you faint. Second, you have fewer red cells and so less **oxygen-carrying capacity**: a mild **anaemic hypoxia**, which lowers your tolerance to altitude. The body replaces the fluid quickly but the red cells much more slowly.

![Which link in the oxygen chain does a blood donation weaken?](diagram:hypoxia-types-and-causes "Blood donation breaks the carrying link: anaemic hypoxia, the blood cannot carry enough oxygen even though the air and lungs are fine.")

The commonly recommended wait is **at least 24 hours** before flying. Recovery varies between individuals with fitness, body size and hydration: a small person who gives the same 470 mL as a large one has lost a bigger share of their blood. Plasma or platelet donation differs from whole blood, because most of the red cells are returned to you. Ask your DAME about your own case, and tell the donor centre that you are a pilot.

## Bringing it together

It is Friday evening. You have a 1000 dual navigation lesson tomorrow from Jandakot, and a friend's birthday dinner tonight. You strained your back moving furniture on Thursday, so before dinner you take two of the codeine-containing painkillers the pharmacist recommended. At dinner you have four standard drinks, the last at 2230.

Work it through. Four drinks clear in about four hours, so the alcohol is near zero by about 0230; the 8-hour rule gives 0630. On alcohol alone you are legal for a 1000 departure. Four drinks over an evening, with food, should not leave a serious hangover, but you will be a little dry: water at breakfast. The painkiller is the bigger question. Codeine is an opiate: it causes drowsiness and slows reactions for many hours, alcohol adds to that sedation, and you have not cleared it with a DAME. And the back strain itself raises the question from [PHFC 2.1](/notes/PHFC/2.1): could you apply full rudder and brake in an emergency? The honest answer is to call your instructor in the morning. The alcohol is not what stops this lesson; the medicine and the injury might.

## Key points

- **2.2.1** Medicals catch conditions that could incapacitate or impair you; validity shortens with age; find a DAME through the CASA website; the DAME examines and issues or refers to CASA; CASA disqualifies only conditions that are a hazard to flight safety.
- **2.2.2** Alcohol is absorbed in 30 to 90 minutes and removed by the liver at a fixed rate of about one standard drink an hour; nothing speeds it up. The 8-hour rule and the 0.02 limit are minimums. A hangover is impairment that outlasts the alcohol, from dehydration, toxic by-products, poor sleep and alcohol in the inner ear; the heavier the drinking the longer the recovery, 24 hours or more. Rehydrate with water or diluted electrolyte drinks, not alcohol, caffeine or sugary drinks.
- **2.2.3** Drug abuse is a behaviour, independent of addiction or frequency. Psychoactive substances, legal or not, slow perception, processing and reaction; cannabis impairs for 24 hours or more, amphetamines bring a come-down, opiates sedate. Watch the side effects of antihistamines, decongestants, aspirin, sedatives and antibiotics.
- **2.2.4** A blood donation reduces blood volume and oxygen-carrying capacity; wait at least 24 hours, longer for some people, and ask your DAME.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Have these by heart: about **one standard drink per hour**, **8 hours** bottle to throttle as a minimum, **0.02 g per 100 mL**, **24 hours or more** after heavy drinking, **24 hours** after giving blood. In an alcohol calculation, work out both the liver time and the 8-hour time and take the later. The distractors are predictable: coffee or exercise speeds elimination (false), zero blood alcohol means fit (false), drug abuse requires addiction or frequent use (false).
