## What the exam expects (RMTC 2.2)

- **2.2.1** Understand the forecasts, reports and broadcasts that cover the area you fly in: what each product is for, how to decode it, and how to use it to decide whether a VFR flight can go.

## Why this matters

Before every flight beyond the circuit you will open NAIPS or the Bureau's aviation site and be handed a page of coded text: a GAF for the area, a TAF for Jandakot, a METAR from Northam, perhaps a SIGMET. Then, taxiing out, you will listen to the ATIS. Each of these answers a different question, in a different time frame, sometimes with a different datum for the same cloud base, and the coded ones are written in a compact language designed for teleprinters. CASA lists "interpretation of meteorological forecasts and requirements" as a weak area in the RPLA exam, and in the air the same weakness shows up as a pilot who read the main body of a TAF and missed the TEMPO group that put showers over the destination at arrival time. This lesson teaches the products first by purpose, then the code group by group, then how to put them together into a go or no-go.

## Three kinds of weather information

Every product you will meet is one of three things.

A **forecast** tells you what the Bureau expects to happen. It is the only thing that can tell you about your arrival time two hours from now, but it is an expectation, and it carries the forecaster's uncertainty in its change and probability groups.

An **observation**, or **report**, tells you what was actually measured at a place and time. It is fact, but it is already old when you read it and says nothing about the future.

A **broadcast** delivers an observation by radio, so that you get it in the cockpit at the moment you need it.

A good briefing uses all three: the forecast to plan, the latest observation to check that the forecast is coming true, and the broadcast to confirm on the day, at the aerodrome.

The products also differ in **scale**. Some describe a whole area, so that you know what to expect en route. Others describe a single aerodrome and the few kilometres around it. A third group are **warnings**, issued only when something hazardous is happening.

## The area products: GAF, GPWT and area QNH

The **Graphical Area Forecast** (**GAF**) is the en route forecast. It is a chart of a forecast area, Western Australia being covered by the WA-N and WA-S areas, divided into subdivisions, with a table for each subdivision giving the cloud, visibility, weather, icing and turbulence expected, and the freezing level. The Bureau of Meteorology issues it four times a day, and each one is valid for 6 hours (AIP GEN 3.5; verify the current issue times and validity). For a flight from Jandakot to Northam you find the subdivisions your track crosses and read what each one says for the time you are in it.

One fact about the GAF catches more students than any other: its cloud heights are **above mean sea level** (AMSL). That makes sense for an area product, because a GAF subdivision may cover a coastal plain at sea level and a plateau at 1,000 ft, and the only datum common to the whole area is the sea. An aerodrome forecast, by contrast, measures cloud from the aerodrome itself, because the question it answers is how high the cloud is above the runway.

![Why is the same cloud base a different number in the GAF and the TAF?](diagram:cloud-height-datum "GAF cloud heights are above mean sea level, TAF and METAR heights are above the aerodrome: at an elevated aerodrome the TAF figure is smaller by the aerodrome elevation.")

!!! example "Worked example: comparing a GAF cloud base with the aerodrome"
    Given: the GAF for the subdivision covering an inland aerodrome with an elevation of 650 ft gives a cloud base of 3,500 ft.

    1. The GAF base is AMSL: **3,500 ft above sea level**.
    2. Height above the aerodrome: $3{,}500 - 650 = \mathbf{2{,}850\ ft}$.

    A TAF for that aerodrome describing the same cloud would show it at about 2,850 ft, not 3,500. Over hills higher than the aerodrome the gap between the ground and the cloud is smaller still: always subtract the height of the ground you will actually fly over.

The **Grid Point Wind and Temperature** chart (**GPWT**) gives forecast wind and temperature at set heights, for example 2,000, 5,000, 7,000 and 10,000 ft, on a grid of points. It is the product you use for planning headings, groundspeeds and TAS, and its temperatures feed your performance and density height calculations. It is also a turbulence clue: a big jump in wind between 2,000 and 5,000 ft is shear (see [RMTC 2.3](/notes/RMTC/2.3)).

The **area QNH** is a forecast QNH for each area, valid for **3-hour periods**, given in the GAF and the NAIPS briefing. You set it on the altimeter when you have no local QNH from a station within 100 nm (see [RFRC 2.3](/notes/RFRC/2.3)).

??? check "Check yourself: why does an area forecast give cloud heights above sea level when an aerodrome forecast gives them above the aerodrome?"
    An area forecast covers ground at many different heights, so the only datum common to all of it is mean sea level. An aerodrome forecast describes one place, and what the pilot needs there is the height of the cloud above the runway.

## The aerodrome forecast: the TAF

The **TAF** (aerodrome forecast) is a forecast of wind, visibility, weather and cloud at one aerodrome, with change groups that say when and how the conditions will change. A TAF is valid for 12, 18 or 24 hours depending on the aerodrome; TAF3 variants are updated every 3 hours (AIP GEN 3.5; verify).

### Time is UTC

All aviation weather products use **Coordinated Universal Time** (UTC), marked with a **Z** ("Zulu"). Western Australia has no daylight saving and **WST = UTC + 8**: add 8 hours to the UTC time to get Perth local time, and watch for the date ticking over when the sum passes 2400. The date and time groups are packed as day, hours and minutes: `030400Z` is the 3rd at 0400 UTC.

!!! example "Worked example: converting a TAF validity to local time"
    Given: a TAF validity group `0306/0318`.

    1. Start: the 3rd at 0600 UTC; $0600 + 8 = \mathbf{1400}$ WST on the 3rd.
    2. End: the 3rd at 1800 UTC; $1800 + 8 = 2600$, which is **0200 WST on the 4th**.

    The forecast covers your afternoon and evening on the 3rd and runs past midnight local time. A flight planned for 1100 WST on the 3rd (0300 UTC) is not covered by it at all.

Try one before opening the solution: a change group reads `FM031000`. At what local time does the change happen?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    `FM031000` is from the 3rd at 1000 UTC. Add 8 hours: **1800 WST on the 3rd**, early evening in Perth.

### Wind, visibility and cloud: the language

Each group of the code says one thing, always in the same order.

**Wind** is five digits and a unit: three for the direction the wind blows **from**, in degrees **true**, and two for the speed. `12012KT` is 120 degrees true at 12 kt. A `G` adds gusts, and `VRB` means variable. Forecasts and reports give true wind because they are meteorological products, tied to the map; the tower and the ATIS give it **magnetic**, because it is meant to be compared with runway headings, which are magnetic. Near Perth the difference is small, but the exam asks about it.

**Visibility** is four digits in metres: `4000` is 4,000 m, and `9999` means 10 km or more.

**Weather** is a set of letter codes. The common ones: RA rain, DZ drizzle, TS thunderstorm, TSRA thunderstorm with rain, SH showers, FG fog, BR mist, HZ haze, FU smoke, DU dust, DS dust storm, SQ squall, GR hail, FC funnel cloud. A `-` in front means light, a `+` heavy, and `VC` means in the vicinity. So `SHRA` is showers of rain and `+TSRA` a heavy thunderstorm with rain.

**Cloud** is an amount and a height. The amount is measured in **oktas**, eighths of the sky: **FEW** is 1 to 2 oktas, **SCT** (scattered) 3 to 4, **BKN** (broken) 5 to 7 and **OVC** (overcast) 8. The height is three digits in hundreds of feet above the aerodrome: `045` is 4,500 ft. A **BKN** or **OVC** layer is a **ceiling**, because at that amount the sky is more cloud than gap and you cannot count on climbing or descending through it visually.

![How much sky does each cloud code mean?](diagram:cloud-amounts-in-oktas "FEW 1 to 2, SCT 3 to 4, BKN 5 to 7, OVC 8 oktas: only BKN and OVC make a ceiling.")

**CAVOK** ("cav-oh-kay", ceiling and visibility OK) replaces the visibility, weather and cloud groups when all of these are true: visibility 10 km or more, no cloud below 5,000 ft or the highest minimum sector altitude, no cumulonimbus, and no significant weather.

### Change groups: when the conditions apply

A TAF's opening conditions are only the starting point. The **change groups** say how they change, and they are where most decoding errors happen.

- **FM** (from) marks a **permanent** change at a set time. `FM031000` means from the 3rd at 1000 UTC everything that follows replaces what came before.
- **BECMG** (becoming) is a **gradual** change over the period given; by the end of it the new conditions apply.
- **TEMPO** means **temporary** fluctuations, each lasting **30 to 60 minutes** at a time, within the period given (AIP GEN 3.5; verify current text).
- **INTER** means **intermittent** fluctuations, each lasting **less than 30 minutes**, within the period given.
- **PROB30** or **PROB40** gives the **probability** (30 or 40 percent) of the condition that follows.

![When does each part of the TAF apply?](diagram:taf-change-groups "FM is a permanent change from a set time; TEMPO spells last 30 to 60 minutes each, INTER less than 30 minutes, and BECMG changes gradually over its period.")

The key to TEMPO and INTER is that the main conditions still apply most of the time, but at any moment within the period the worse conditions may be there. For planning you must allow for them, because you cannot choose to arrive during a gap.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Reading only the first line of a TAF. The opening line may be CAVOK while a TEMPO or INTER group puts showers and a 1,500 ft ceiling over the aerodrome for the very hours you plan to arrive. Read every change group against your arrival time.

??? check "Check yourself: what is the practical difference for a pilot between FM and TEMPO?"
    FM is a permanent change: after the time given, the old conditions no longer apply. TEMPO describes spells of 30 to 60 minutes each within its period, with the main conditions applying in between; you must still plan for the worse conditions, because you cannot be sure of arriving between spells.

### Decoding a TAF from start to finish

Now put it together.

```
TAF YPJT 030400Z 0306/0318 12012KT 9999 FEW030 SCT045
  FM031000 09008KT CAVOK
  TEMPO 0306/0309 4000 SHRA BKN020
  T 24 22 19 17 Q 1016 1015 1013 1012
```

![What does each group of a TAF mean?](diagram:taf-anatomy "A TAF reads left to right: where, when issued, when valid, then wind, visibility and cloud, then the change groups and the 3-hourly temperature and QNH.")

!!! example "Worked example: decoding the Jandakot TAF"
    Read it group by group, left to right.

    1. `TAF YPJT`: an aerodrome forecast for **Jandakot**.
    2. `030400Z`: issued on the **3rd at 0400 UTC** (1200 WST).
    3. `0306/0318`: valid from **0600 to 1800 UTC on the 3rd** (1400 WST on the 3rd to 0200 WST on the 4th).
    4. `12012KT`: wind from **120 degrees true at 12 kt**.
    5. `9999`: visibility **10 km or more**.
    6. `FEW030 SCT045`: **few** cloud (1 to 2 oktas) at **3,000 ft** and **scattered** (3 to 4 oktas) at **4,500 ft above the aerodrome**. No ceiling.
    7. `FM031000 09008KT CAVOK`: from **1000 UTC** (1800 WST) a permanent change to wind **090 at 8 kt** and **CAVOK**.
    8. `TEMPO 0306/0309 4000 SHRA BKN020`: between **0600 and 0900 UTC** (1400 to 1700 WST), temporary spells of 30 to 60 minutes each of visibility **4,000 m** in **showers of rain**, with **broken** cloud, a ceiling, at **2,000 ft**.
    9. `T 24 22 19 17`: forecast temperatures at 3-hourly intervals from the start of the validity, **24, 22, 19 and 17 degrees**.
    10. `Q 1016 1015 1013 1012`: forecast QNH for the same intervals, **1016 falling to 1012 hPa**.

    For a VFR arrival at 1530 WST: you must plan for the TEMPO conditions, 4,000 m in showers with a ceiling at 2,000 ft, which is below the 5,000 m VMC visibility. Arrive after 1700 WST and the main conditions apply; after 1800 WST, CAVOK.

Now a second one to try yourself. It is a different day, but every group is of a type you have just decoded.

```
TAF YPJT 040400Z 0406/0418 23015G25KT 9999 SCT025
  FM041200 18008KT 9999 FEW030
  INTER 0406/0410 3000 SHRA BKN015
  T 21 20 18 16 Q 1012 1013 1014 1015
```

Decode it, and decide what it means for a circuit session from 1500 to 1700 WST.

??? solution "Show the solution"
    Jandakot, issued the 4th at 0400 UTC, valid 0600 to 1800 UTC on the 4th (1400 WST on the 4th to 0200 WST on the 5th). Wind 230 degrees true at 15 kt gusting 25 kt; visibility 10 km or more; scattered cloud at 2,500 ft. From 1200 UTC (2000 WST) a permanent change to 180 at 8 kt, 10 km or more, few at 3,000 ft. Between 0600 and 1000 UTC (1400 to 1800 WST), intermittent spells of less than 30 minutes each of 3,000 m in showers of rain with a broken ceiling at 1,500 ft. Temperatures 21 falling to 16 degrees; QNH 1012 rising to 1015 hPa. For 1500 to 1700 WST the whole session sits inside the INTER period: expect gusty south-westerlies and showers that will close the circuit for spells, below VMC visibility while they last. A rising QNH, a falling temperature and a gusty south-westerly with showers are the signature of the cold air behind a front.

## The observations: METAR and SPECI

A **METAR** is a routine observation of the actual conditions at an aerodrome, issued every half hour or hour. It uses the same groups as a TAF for the observed values, and adds two of its own: the **temperature and dew point** (`24/18`) and the **QNH** (`Q1016`). It ends with any recent weather and a remarks section.

A **SPECI** is the same message, but a special one: it is issued when conditions change significantly or are below set criteria, so the word SPECI in place of METAR is itself a warning that something has changed. **AUTO** means the report came from an automatic station with no human observer, so the cloud and weather groups may be incomplete: an automatic sensor sees cloud only directly above it and can miss a storm two kilometres away.

![What does each group of a METAR tell you?](diagram:metar-anatomy "A METAR is an observation, not a forecast: the same groups as a TAF plus the temperature/dew point and QNH.")

!!! example "Worked example: decoding a Jandakot METAR"
    The message: `METAR YPJT 030430Z 12012KT 9999 FEW030 24/18 Q1016`

    1. `METAR YPJT`: a routine observation at **Jandakot**.
    2. `030430Z`: observed on the **3rd at 0430 UTC** (1230 WST).
    3. `12012KT`: wind **120 degrees true at 12 kt**.
    4. `9999`: visibility **10 km or more**.
    5. `FEW030`: **few** cloud at **3,000 ft above the aerodrome**.
    6. `24/18`: temperature **24 degrees**, dew point **18 degrees**, a spread of 6 degrees.
    7. `Q1016`: QNH **1016 hPa**.

    Set beside the TAF decoded earlier, issued half an hour before this observation, it shows the conditions the TAF opens with already in place: same wind, same visibility, the lower cloud layer. Checking the forecast against what is actually happening is what an observation is for.

Try a second one before opening the solution: `SPECI YPJT 030715Z 12014KT 4000 SHRA BKN020 18/16 Q1015`. What has happened, and how does it compare with the TAF?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    A special observation at Jandakot on the 3rd at 0715 UTC (1515 WST): wind 120 degrees true at 14 kt, visibility 4,000 m in showers of rain, broken cloud at 2,000 ft above the aerodrome, temperature 18 and dew point 16, QNH 1015 hPa. The TAF's TEMPO conditions have arrived, exactly the spell it warned of, and the word SPECI tells you it was the change that triggered the report. The temperature has dropped in the rain-cooled air and the spread has narrowed to 2 degrees.

??? check "Check yourself: a METAR for a country aerodrome is marked AUTO and shows no cloud. Can you take that to mean the sky is clear?"
    Not with confidence. AUTO means an automatic station with no observer, whose sensor sees only the cloud directly above it and may not report all weather. Check the TAF, the GAF and any other nearby reports before relying on it.

## The broadcasts: ATIS and AWIS

The **ATIS** (automatic terminal information service) is a recorded broadcast at a controlled aerodrome such as Jandakot or Perth, on a published frequency. It gives the runway in use, the wind, the QNH, the cloud, the visibility and the temperature, and each version is identified by a letter, "information Bravo", updated when conditions change. You listen to it before you call for taxi or inbound and tell the tower which letter you have, so that the controller knows you have the current information. The wind on the ATIS is magnetic, ready to compare with the runway.

The **AWIS** (automatic weather information service) is found at some non-towered aerodromes and gives the current wind, QNH, temperature, cloud and visibility by radio or telephone. It is an observation, so it tells you what is happening now, which is exactly what you want before descending into a country aerodrome or before leaving a farm strip for it.

## The warnings: SIGMET and AIRMET

A **SIGMET** is a warning of significant en route weather: thunderstorms, severe turbulence, severe icing, volcanic ash and tropical cyclones. It is issued as needed and is valid for up to 4 hours. An **AIRMET** is a warning for low-level flights of weather hazardous to light aircraft below 10,000 ft that the GAF does not already show. Neither appears unless there is a hazard, so their absence from your briefing is good news, and their presence on your route is usually a no-go for a light aeroplane.

The products side by side, as a recap:

| Product | What it is | Who issues it and how long it is valid |
|---|---|---|
| **GAF** (Graphical Area Forecast) | A chart of a forecast area (Western Australia is covered by the WA-N and WA-S areas) divided into subdivisions, with a table of cloud, visibility, weather, icing and turbulence for each subdivision and the freezing level | Bureau of Meteorology, valid for 6 hours, issued four times a day |
| **GPWT** (Grid Point Wind and Temperature) | A chart of forecast wind and temperature at set heights (for example 2,000, 5,000, 7,000, 10,000 ft) on a grid, used for planning headings, groundspeed and TAS | Bureau of Meteorology, per forecast period |
| **TAF** (Aerodrome Forecast) | A forecast of wind, visibility, weather and cloud at an aerodrome, with change groups (FM, BECMG, TEMPO, INTER) and a PROB group | Valid 12, 18 or 24 hours depending on the aerodrome; TAF3 variants are updated every 3 hours |
| **METAR** | A routine observation of actual conditions at an aerodrome, issued every half hour or hour | Observed conditions, not a forecast |
| **SPECI** | A special observation issued when conditions change significantly or are below set criteria | Observed |
| **ATIS** | The automatic terminal information service broadcast at a controlled aerodrome: runway in use, wind, QNH, cloud, visibility, temperature, identified by a letter | Broadcast on a published frequency, updated when conditions change |
| **AWIS** | Automatic weather information service at some non-towered aerodromes, by radio or phone, giving the current wind, QNH, temperature, cloud and visibility | Observed |
| **Area QNH** | Forecast QNH for each area, valid for 3-hour periods, used for the altimeter when a local QNH is not available | In the GAF and the NAIPS briefing |
| **SIGMET** | A warning of significant en route weather: thunderstorms, severe turbulence, severe icing, volcanic ash, tropical cyclones | Issued as needed, valid up to 4 hours |
| **AIRMET** | A warning for low-level flights of weather hazardous to light aircraft below 10,000 ft, where the GAF does not already show it | As needed |

The validity periods and issue times are from AIP GEN 3.5 and the Bureau's services guide as they stood when this lesson was written; verify them against the current AIP.

## Using the forecasts for a VFR flight

The products fit together in the order you use them:

1. **GAF** for every subdivision you fly through: cloud base and amount, visibility, weather, and whether thunderstorms or turbulence are forecast. Remember that its cloud is **AMSL**.
2. **GPWT**, or the GAF winds, for headings and groundspeeds, and the forecast temperature for performance.
3. **TAF** for departure, destination and any alternate, read against the VMC minima for the time of your flight, **including** the TEMPO and INTER groups. An alternate or extra fuel is required when the forecast conditions at the destination are below the alternate minima for your arrival period; the detail is in AIP ENR 1.1 and the Part 91 MOS.
4. The latest **METAR or SPECI**, to see whether the forecast is coming true.
5. **SIGMETs and AIRMETs** on the route.
6. The **area QNH** for the altimeter.
7. On the day, the **ATIS or AWIS** to confirm what is actually happening.

## Bringing it together

You plan a solo flight from Jandakot to Northam, departing 1300 WST (0500 UTC) and arriving about 1340 WST. The GAF for the subdivision east of the Scarp shows scattered cumulus based at 4,000 ft with isolated showers. The Northam TAF has a main body of 9999 with scattered cloud at 3,500 ft, but a TEMPO group covering 0500 to 0800 UTC gives 3,000 m in showers with broken cloud at 1,500 ft. The Scarp lies between the two aerodromes, rising to roughly 1,000 ft above the plain. Put the numbers together: a GAF base of 4,000 ft AMSL over hills of about 1,000 ft leaves roughly 3,000 ft of clearance en route, which is workable, but the TEMPO at Northam covers your arrival time and puts the visibility below 5,000 m and a ceiling at 1,500 ft above the aerodrome during the spells. You cannot arrive between spells by choice, so the flight as planned is not a good one. The options are to leave later, after 0800 UTC (1600 WST), if the TAF for that period is better, or to choose another destination. On the day you would still check the latest Northam METAR before starting, because a SPECI showing showers already over the town would settle the question.

??? check "Check yourself: in the scenario above, why is the GAF the right product for the Scarp crossing but the TAF the right one for the arrival?"
    The GAF covers the subdivision you fly across and gives cloud above sea level, so you compare it with the terrain height. The TAF covers only the aerodrome and its immediate surroundings, with cloud above the aerodrome, which is what you need for the circuit and landing.

## Key points

- **2.2.1** Forecasts (GAF, GPWT, area QNH, TAF) tell you what to expect; observations (METAR, SPECI) tell you what is happening; broadcasts (ATIS, AWIS) deliver it in the cockpit; warnings (SIGMET, AIRMET) appear only when there is a hazard.
- Times are UTC (Z); WST = UTC + 8. Forecast and report winds are true; ATIS and tower winds are magnetic.
- Cloud: FEW 1 to 2, SCT 3 to 4, BKN 5 to 7, OVC 8 oktas; BKN and OVC are a ceiling. GAF heights are AMSL; TAF and METAR heights are above the aerodrome.
- Change groups: FM permanent, BECMG gradual, TEMPO spells of 30 to 60 minutes, INTER spells under 30 minutes, PROB30 and PROB40 probabilities. Plan for the worst group that covers your time.
- A METAR adds temperature/dew point and QNH; SPECI means a significant change; AUTO means no observer and possibly incomplete cloud and weather.
- GAF valid 6 hours, issued four times a day; area QNH valid for 3-hour periods; SIGMET valid up to 4 hours.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    CASA lists "interpretation of meteorological forecasts and requirements" as a weak area in the RPLA exam. Practise decoding TAFs and METARs until the cloud, visibility, change and probability groups are automatic, and remember: GAF cloud heights are AMSL, TAF and METAR cloud heights are above the aerodrome, and forecast winds are true. Expect a question that gives you a TEMPO or INTER group and asks what it means, and one that turns on the difference between a forecast and an observation.
