## What the exam expects (RMTC 2.1)

- **2.1.1** Show a working knowledge of the weather where you fly: when and where thunderstorms, low cloud, poor visibility and turbulence are likely, and how each one threatens the safety of a flight.

## Why this matters

The RPL keeps you close to home, so the weather that matters is the weather over the Swan coastal plain, the Darling Scarp and the wheatbelt, and it follows a pattern you can learn. A summer morning at Jandakot starts with a gusty easterly tumbling off the hills, the afternoon brings the Fremantle Doctor and thermals strong enough to throw your head against the canopy, and a February evening at Northam can end under a dry thunderstorm with a dust-laden gust front. A winter morning starts with fog in the Avon valley and ends with a cold front, low cloud on the Scarp and showers sweeping in from the west. The exam asks about these phenomena by recall (when are they likely, what do they do to the aeroplane), and the right answer is nearly always the one that avoids, delays or turns back early. This lesson builds each phenomenon from its cause, so that you can predict it rather than memorise it.

## The weather machine over the south-west

Before the individual hazards, you need the big picture, because every one of them follows from where the high pressure systems sit.

Air flows from high pressure towards low pressure, but the Earth turns underneath it, and the effect of that rotation, the **Coriolis effect**, deflects moving air to the **left in the Southern Hemisphere**. Air spilling out of a high is turned left and ends up circling it **anticlockwise**; air flowing into a low is also turned left and circles it **clockwise**. Northern Hemisphere textbooks show the reverse. The detail is in [PMTC 2.5](/notes/PMTC/2.5).

![Which way does the wind go round a high and a low here?](diagram:southern-hemisphere-highs-and-lows "In the Southern Hemisphere air spirals anticlockwise out of a high and clockwise into a low, so the northern side of a high has an easterly.")

Picture a high pressure system, an **anticyclone**, over the Great Australian Bight. On its northern side, over southern Western Australia, its anticlockwise circulation blows from east to west. That is the summer pattern: from about November to March the highs track across the far south and the wind over Perth is a dry, hot **easterly** off the interior. A trough of low pressure, the **west coast trough**, forms along the coast as the land bakes, and its position each day decides whether Perth gets a scorching easterly or an early sea breeze.

In winter the belt of highs moves north over the continent, the south-west lies in the **westerlies** beneath them, and **cold fronts** sweep in from the Indian Ocean every few days. Ahead of each front the wind is a freshening northerly and the cloud lowers; with it comes a change to the west or south-west, rain and sometimes thunderstorms; behind it come cold, showery south-westerlies. Hold those two pictures, summer easterlies and winter fronts, and the timing of every hazard in this lesson falls into place (fronts in full: [PMTC 2.6](/notes/PMTC/2.6)).

![Why does Perth get easterlies in summer and fronts in winter?](diagram:wa-summer-winter-pressure-pattern "In summer the high sits in the Bight and Perth is on its easterly northern side; in winter the highs move north over the continent and cold fronts reach the south-west.")

??? check "Check yourself: a high is centred south of Esperance. What wind direction would you expect over Perth, and why?"
    Easterly. In the Southern Hemisphere air circulates anticlockwise around a high, so on the high's northern side, where Perth lies, the air flows from east to west.

## Thunderstorms

### How a storm builds itself

A thunderstorm needs three things: **moisture**, so that rising air makes cloud; **instability**, meaning air that once lifted stays warmer than its surroundings and keeps rising on its own; and a **trigger** that gives it the first lift. The trigger can be surface heating, a front, a trough, hills that force air upwards, or the line where a sea breeze meets the inland air.

Instability is about buoyancy. Rising air expands and cools; if it stays warmer than the air around it, it is lighter and keeps going, like a hot-air balloon. When it reaches its dew point the vapour condenses into cloud and releases heat, which drives it higher still. In unstable air that feedback can carry the cloud to 40,000 ft.

A single storm cell lives through three stages, and the hazards change with each:

1. **Cumulus stage.** Updraughts throughout a growing tower; no rain at the ground yet, but updraughts strong enough to carry a light aeroplane up thousands of feet.
2. **Mature stage.** Rain and hail grow too heavy to be held up and fall, dragging air with them. Rain evaporating into drier air chills it, so the falling air is heavier than its surroundings and accelerates: a **downdraught** beside the updraught. Rain at the surface and lightning mark this stage, the most dangerous one.
3. **Dissipating stage.** The downdraught takes over the cell and chokes off its warm inflow; the rain eases and the top spreads into an **anvil** of ice cloud.

A single cell lasts about an hour, but new cells grow on the flanks, so a storm area can last all afternoon.

![How does a storm cell grow and die?](diagram:thunderstorm-life-cycle "Updraughts build the cumulus; the mature stage begins when rain reaches the ground and is the most dangerous; the downdraught then chokes the cell and leaves the anvil.")

### What a storm does to an aeroplane

When the downdraught hits the ground it spreads out in every direction like water poured on a floor. The leading edge of that cold outflow is the **gust front**, and it can run many kilometres ahead of the storm, arriving as a sudden wind shift, a squall and often a wall of dust. An intense, concentrated downdraught is a **microburst**, and it produces wind shear that can push an aeroplane on approach into the ground (the encounter itself is in [RMTC 2.3](/notes/RMTC/2.3)).

![What makes a thunderstorm dangerous, and how far away should you stay?](diagram:thunderstorm-hazards "The danger reaches well outside the cloud: the microburst and gust front spread out ahead of the storm, so keep at least 10 nm away and never go under the anvil.")

The full list of hazards: **severe turbulence** inside and near the cell, enough to break an aeroplane; **hail**, which can fall from the anvil well outside the visible rain; **lightning**; **heavy rain** that removes the visibility in seconds; **icing** in the cloud above the freezing level; and the **microburst and gust front** with their wind shear on take-off and landing.

For a VFR pilot the only safe response is to stay well clear: at least **10 nm** from a mature cell, more from a severe one, never under the anvil, and never attempting to fly through a line of storms. If storms are forecast at your destination for your arrival time, plan to be on the ground early or go somewhere else.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The danger is not confined to the cloud you can see. The gust front can reach the aerodrome before the rain, hail falls from the anvil into clear air, and the clear air under the anvil is where the downdraughts are. "Stay clear of the cloud" is not enough; stay 10 nm from the cell.

### When and where in Western Australia

Each storm type has its own trigger, and the trigger tells you the time of day and year.

**Summer afternoon storms inland and over the Scarp.** The trigger is surface heating. Moist air drawn down from the tropics in a trough meets the afternoon heat over the wheatbelt and the goldfields, and storms build from about midday, peak in the late afternoon and die away after dark when the heating stops. Because the inland air is dry near the ground, the dew point is far below the temperature and the cloud base is high, often 8,000 to 10,000 ft. Rain falling from that high base evaporates before it lands, which is why these storms are often **dry**: lightning, gust fronts and dust with little rain. That evaporation also chills the downdraught, so dry storms produce some of the strongest microbursts. Their lightning starts many of WA's bushfires.

**Frontal storms in the south-west.** The trigger is the lifting along a cold front or the trough ahead of it. These come mostly in late spring and autumn, and with the more vigorous winter cold fronts along the coast. They do not care about the time of day; a front can bring a line of storms through Perth at dawn.

**Tropical storms in the north.** In the wet season, October to April, the Kimberley and Pilbara get heavy afternoon and evening storms, often severe, and tropical cyclones in the same months.

??? check "Check yourself: why are summer storms over the wheatbelt often 'dry', and why does that make them more dangerous at the surface rather than less?"
    The air beneath the cloud is dry, so the cloud base is high and the rain evaporates on its way down. Evaporation cools the falling air, making it colder and heavier, so the downdraught accelerates and strikes the ground as a strong microburst and gust front, often carrying dust, with little rain to warn you.

## Low cloud

### Cooling air to its dew point

Air always holds some water vapour. The **dew point** is the temperature to which the air would have to be cooled, with no change in its moisture, for that vapour to start condensing. The gap between the air temperature and the dew point, the **dew point spread**, tells you how close the air is to making cloud: a spread of 15 degrees is dry air, a spread of 1 degree is air on the edge of cloud or fog. Cloud forms when something cools the air to its dew point, and low cloud forms when that cooling happens close to the ground. There are four ways it happens near Perth.

**Lifting over hills.** Moist air pushed up a slope expands and cools. Behind a winter front the moist south-westerly rides up the Darling Scarp and can reach its dew point a few hundred feet above the hills, capping them in **orographic** cloud while the plain stays clear, exactly where a flight from Jandakot to Northam must cross.

![Why does cloud cap the Scarp while the plain stays clear?](diagram:orographic-cloud-on-the-scarp "Forced up the Scarp, the moist air expands and cools to its dew point a few hundred feet above the hills: cloud on the ridges, clear air over the plain.")

**Ahead of a front.** Warm moist air is lifted over the colder air and the base lowers through the day, from high cloud to low cloud and rain.

**Sea air moving inland.** The sea breeze carries moist ocean air over the land, where it can cool further in the evening and form low stratus along the coast that spreads inland overnight.

**Overnight cooling of the ground** under clear skies, which gives fog or very low stratus: the next section.

Low stratus is common on the south-west coast on winter mornings and in the humid north during the wet season.

!!! example "Worked example: estimating the cumulus base from the temperature and dew point"
    Given: a mid-morning observation at Northam showing temperature 18 degrees and dew point 10 degrees.

    1. Dew point spread: $18 - 10 = \mathbf{8}$ degrees.
    2. Rule of thumb, rising air loses about 1 degree of spread for every 400 ft: $8 \times 400 = \mathbf{3{,}200\ ft}$ above the ground.

    When the heating sets off thermals, expect the cumulus base at roughly 3,200 ft above Northam. The rule only works for cloud formed by air rising from the surface (cumulus), not for stratus lowered by a front, and it is an estimate.

### Why low cloud kills VFR pilots

For a VFR pilot, low cloud attacks two rules at once. You must stay a set distance from cloud, or at least clear of it, to remain in VMC (see [RFRC 2.3](/notes/RFRC/2.3)), and you must stay above the minimum height over the ground. As the base lowers, the space between those two limits shrinks.

Over rising ground that squeeze is lethal, because the terrain comes up to meet the cloud. Picture the winter flight home from Northam after a front: ahead the Scarp is capped, the base a few hundred feet above the ridges. Each mile you press on, the ground rises and the cloud does not, and by the time you admit there is no gap there may be no room to turn either.

![Why turn back early when the cloud base is lowering?](diagram:low-cloud-and-rising-terrain "The gap between a lowering base and rising ground closes ahead of you: turn while there is still room to turn.")

The response is to **turn back early**, while there is still room, not to press on. "Scud running" under low cloud into rising ground is one of the main causes of fatal VFR accidents, and many of those pilots were within a few minutes of clear air behind them.

??? check "Check yourself: a moist south-westerly is blowing behind a winter front. Where are you most likely to find low cloud between Perth and York, and why there?"
    On the Darling Scarp and the hills just east of it. The wind forces the moist air up the slope, it expands and cools as it rises, and it reaches its dew point a few hundred feet above the hills, while the coastal plain can stay clear.

## Poor visibility

### How radiation fog forms

On a still, clear winter night the ground radiates its heat into space. With no cloud to send that heat back, the ground cools quickly, and it cools the thin layer of air resting on it. If that air is moist enough, a small dew point spread at sunset, it reaches its dew point before dawn and the vapour condenses into droplets so small they stay suspended. That is **radiation fog**, and it needs four conditions together:

- **Clear skies**, so the ground loses heat freely.
- **Moist air**, so only a little cooling brings it to the dew point. A day of rain the day before helps.
- **Light wind**, about 2 to 5 kt. A light breeze stirs the cooled air through a layer a few hundred feet deep. Dead calm gives dew or frost on the grass but only a shallow ground fog; a wind much over 10 kt mixes the chilled air with warmer air above and stops the fog from forming at all, sometimes giving low stratus instead.
- **Long nights**, so there is time to cool. Winter, May to September in the south-west, is fog season.

![What turns a clear, humid night into fog by dawn?](diagram:temperature-dew-point-overnight "The temperature falls all night towards an almost steady dew point and meets it near dawn; a light wind of about 2 to 5 kt makes a fog layer, calm gives dew, over 10 kt gives none.")

Cold air is heavier than warm air, so on a sloping landscape the chilled air drains downhill overnight and pools in the lowest ground. That is why fog lies in the river valleys: the **Avon valley** around Northam and York, the Swan valley, and low-lying airfields along the coast. A hilltop farm strip can be clear while the valley aerodrome ten miles away is in fog.

![Why does fog form on still, clear, humid winter mornings?](diagram:radiation-fog-overnight "Clear skies let the ground lose heat overnight; calm, humid air touching it cools to its dew point, and the fog lies in the valleys until the sun burns it off by mid-morning.")

After sunrise the sun heats the ground through the fog and the droplets evaporate from below. The fog usually **clears by mid-morning**, often lifting first into low stratus. Because the coldest time is just after sunrise, it often thickens briefly after first light before it improves.

??? check "Check yourself: the evening forecast for Northam is clear skies and a 20 kt south-westerly overnight, with high humidity. Why is radiation fog unlikely despite the humidity?"
    The wind is too strong. It mixes the air cooled at the ground with warmer air above, so no layer stays in contact with the cold ground long enough to reach its dew point. Radiation fog needs a light wind of a few knots, not 20 kt.

### The other ways visibility goes

**Smoke** from bushfires and controlled burns in late summer and autumn spreads over hundreds of kilometres and is worst looking into the sun. **Dust** is raised by strong winds over the dry interior and the wheatbelt, ahead of fronts and in thunderstorm gust fronts, and can take the visibility to a few hundred metres in minutes. **Rain and showers** with fronts can reduce visibility below VMC quickly, and post-frontal showers come in bands that can close behind you. **Haze** in the warm, humid north takes the edge off the horizon over long distances.

### Why poor visibility is dangerous

Poor visibility hides terrain, obstacles and other aircraft until late. Worse, it can take away the **horizon**, and the inner ear cannot tell a gentle descending turn from level flight (see [PHFC 2.7](/notes/PHFC/2.7)): that is how a pilot in haze over featureless wheatbelt enters a spiral without knowing it.

The VMC minimum of 5,000 m flight visibility is a legal floor, not a comfortable working figure for a new pilot. A horizon that is merging into haze, or a visibility that is falling as you fly, is a sign to stay on the ground or turn back.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    A forecast of clear skies is not a forecast of good visibility. The clear night is the one that produces fog, and a cloudless February sky can be full of smoke. Read the visibility and weather groups, not just the cloud.

## Turbulence

Turbulence is air moving irregularly, up, down and sideways, so that the aeroplane is jolted about. Each kind has a different cause, and the cause tells you where and when to expect it.

### Thermal turbulence

Bare paddocks, bitumen and dark soil heat faster than crops, bush or water. The air over a hot patch warms, becomes lighter and breaks away as a rising bubble, a **thermal**, while the air between thermals sinks to replace it. Fly through that pattern and you are lifted, dropped, lifted again: **thermal** or **convective turbulence**.

It follows the sun: starting mid-morning, worst in the early to mid afternoon, dying away in the evening, and worst at low level over bare ground. The circuit at Northam or Cunderdin on a January afternoon is hard work. If the air is moist enough each thermal is capped by a flat-based cumulus, and the turbulence reaches up to that base.

### Mechanical turbulence and rotor off the Scarp

Wind blowing over an obstruction goes over and around it and tumbles into eddies on the far side: **mechanical turbulence**, stronger the stronger the wind and the bigger the obstacle. A 20 kt crosswind across a row of hangars or a tree line sends eddies over the runway exactly where you flare.

On the larger scale the obstacle is the Darling Scarp. On a summer morning, with the high in the Bight, a strong easterly crosses the plateau, pours down the face of the Scarp, roughly 1,000 ft, and on the lee side, the Perth side, rolls into a **rotor**: a horizontal eddy of tumbling air with gusts and sudden sink close to the ground. The easterly is often strongest overnight and early in the morning, so a dawn departure from Serpentine or Jandakot towards the hills can be rough below 2,000 ft.

![Where is the turbulence when a strong wind blows over a hill or a building?](diagram:rotor-in-the-lee-of-the-scarp "The rough air is on the downwind (lee) side: in a strong easterly that is the Perth side of the Darling Scarp, and the same happens behind hangars and trees.")

### Frontal and wind shear turbulence

**Frontal turbulence** comes at the wind change as a front passes: two air masses moving in different directions meet, and the boundary between them is rough.

**Wind shear turbulence** occurs wherever the wind changes sharply over a short distance: at the top of a **nocturnal inversion**, where calm, chilled air at the surface lies under wind that keeps blowing a few hundred feet up, and at the **sea breeze front**.

**Clear air turbulence** at high levels near jet streams is not usually an RPL concern. Wake turbulence from other aircraft is a separate subject in [BAKC 4.4](/notes/BAKC/4.4).

### The Fremantle Doctor

On a sunny morning land and sea get the same sunshine, but the land heats far faster: the sea spreads its heat through a deep, mixing layer and spends some of it on evaporation, while the land concentrates it in the top few centimetres. By late morning the air over the coastal plain is several degrees warmer than over the ocean. The warm column expands upwards, so aloft the pressure over the land is higher than over the sea and air flows out to sea up there; that leaves less air above the land, the surface pressure there falls below the pressure over the sea, and the surface air flows from sea to land: the **sea breeze**. The returning air sinks over the sea, completing a circulation a few thousand feet deep.

In Perth it is the **Fremantle Doctor**, a cool south-westerly that typically reaches the coast around noon to early afternoon and pushes inland through the afternoon. A strong gradient easterly holds it offshore, and the Doctor arrives late or not at all: those are the hottest days.

![What drives the sea breeze, and where is its front?](diagram:sea-breeze-front "Cool sea air pushes inland under the warm land air as a shallow wedge: the wind shift, shear and any cumulus line are at its leading edge.")

The leading edge of the cool air is the **sea breeze front**. As it passes the wind swings from easterly to south-westerly within minutes, the temperature drops and the humidity rises; at the front there is shear and turbulence, and in moist air a line of cumulus. For you that means a midday runway change at Jandakot, a crosswind or tailwind if you are caught on the old runway, and a rough patch as you fly through the front.

### What turbulence does, and what you do about it

Turbulence makes the aeroplane hard to control at low level, upsets the instruments, causes airsickness and, if severe, can overstress the airframe; a sharp gust can also stall a wing flying near the critical angle (see [RBKA 3.6](/notes/RBKA/3.6)). In rough air slow to the manoeuvring speed or the POH turbulence speed, so that a gust stalls the wing before it can overstress it, hold the attitude rather than chasing height and airspeed, keep the seat belts tight and avoid the lee of hills in strong winds. Better still, fly before the thermals start or after they die.

??? check "Check yourself: on a summer morning the wind at Jandakot is a strong easterly. Where would you expect the roughest air on a departure towards the hills, and why?"
    Low down on the coastal plain close to the foot of the Scarp. The easterly pours down the Scarp and rolls into rotor and gusts on its lee (western) side, the side Jandakot and Serpentine are on, so the worst turbulence is below about 2,000 ft near the hills.

## Bringing it together

A Saturday in late January, Jandakot to Northam and back. The forecast shows a high in the Bight, a trough inland and storms possible east of the Scarp in the afternoon. At 0730 the easterly at Jandakot is 18 kt and the climb-out towards the hills is bumpy below 2,000 ft: rotor in the lee of the Scarp. By 1100 the Northam paddocks are hot, small cumulus are popping up and the circuit is rough with thermals. At 1230 high-based towers are building to the north-east with rain shafts that do not reach the ground: the dry storms. You leave now rather than at 1500, because a gust front can arrive before the storm does. Approaching the Scarp westbound, a line of small cumulus marks the sea breeze front, the air is rough as you descend through it, and Jandakot has changed to runway 24 in a fresh south-westerly. Every phenomenon arrived on the schedule its cause predicted.

??? check "Check yourself: why do you leave Northam early rather than waiting for the storms to pass?"
    Because the afternoon storms are driven by heating, they will build further, not pass, until evening. Their gust fronts and microbursts can reach well ahead of the cells, so the safe choice is to be on the ground at home before they mature, not to try to time a gap.

## Key points

- **2.1.1 (a) Thunderstorms** need moisture, instability and a trigger. In WA: summer afternoon storms inland and on the Scarp (often dry, high based, with strong microbursts), frontal storms in the south-west, tropical storms in the north from October to April. Hazards: turbulence, hail, lightning, heavy rain, icing, microburst and gust front. Stay at least 10 nm from a mature cell and never under the anvil.
- **2.1.1 (b) Low cloud** forms when moist air is cooled to its dew point near the ground: lifted over the Scarp, ahead of fronts, sea air moving inland, overnight cooling. Between a lowering base and rising ground, turn back early.
- **2.1.1 (c) Poor visibility**: radiation fog on clear, light-wind, humid winter nights, pooling in valleys like the Avon and clearing by mid-morning; smoke, dust, rain and haze. Loss of the horizon brings disorientation; 5,000 m is a floor, not a target.
- **2.1.1 (d) Turbulence**: thermal on hot afternoons over bare ground, mechanical and rotor downwind of obstacles (the Perth side of the Scarp in strong easterlies), frontal at the wind change, shear at inversions and the sea breeze front. Slow to the turbulence speed, hold attitude, avoid the lee of hills.
- Southern Hemisphere: anticlockwise round highs, clockwise round lows. Summer brings easterlies and the Fremantle Doctor; winter brings westerlies and cold fronts.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Think "when and where" for each phenomenon: thunderstorms with heating and fronts, fog on clear calm humid mornings, low cloud when moist air is cooled or lifted, thermal turbulence on hot afternoons and mechanical turbulence downwind of hills. The safety response for all of them is to avoid, delay or turn back early. Have 10 nm from a mature thunderstorm by heart, and remember that in the Southern Hemisphere the wind goes anticlockwise round a high.
