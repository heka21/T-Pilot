## What the exam expects (RMTC 2.3)

- **2.3.1** Recognise the signs, in the forecast and in what you and other pilots see, of (a) turbulence, thermals and dust devils, and (b) wind gradient and wind shear; and describe what each does to the way the aeroplane flies.

## Why this matters

The forecast tells you what the air is likely to do; the sky, the ground and the aeroplane tell you what it is doing. A line of cumulus over the Scarp, a column of dust spinning across a paddock at Cunderdin, smoke from a burn-off lying flat and then torn apart, the whitecaps on the Swan as you turn final at Jandakot: each one is a measurement, if you know how to read it. Reading them matters most in the last few hundred feet of an approach and the first few hundred feet after take-off, where the aeroplane is slow, close to the stall and close to the ground, and where a sudden loss of airspeed has nowhere to go. The exam tests the signs by recall and the effects by reasoning, and CASA names "the effect of wind shear on aircraft performance" as one of the weak areas in the RPLA exam. This lesson builds both from the physics, so that you can work out the effect of any shear rather than memorise a list.

## Two kinds of evidence

Every sign in this lesson comes from one of two places.

**The forecast and the reports** give you warning before you leave the ground. You learnt to decode them in [RMTC 2.2](/notes/RMTC/2.2); here you learn which groups point to rough air and shear.

**Your own observations**, and those of other pilots, tell you what is happening now. Clouds, dust, smoke, birds, water and the wind sock are all instruments that show you the motion of air you cannot otherwise see. A report from a preceding aircraft ("wind shear on final, plus or minus 10 kt") is the most direct evidence of all.

A good pilot uses the forecast to know what to look for, and the view out of the window to confirm it.

## Signs of turbulence

### In the forecast

Several products point to turbulence before you have seen a cloud.

- **Turbulence groups in the GAF**, giving moderate or severe turbulence and the heights it extends between.
- **SIGMETs for severe turbulence.**
- **Strong surface winds in the TAF**, and especially a **gust group** `G` of **10 kt or more above the mean wind**. Gusts are the turbulence of the surface wind itself: a wind of 15 kt gusting 27 kt means the air near the ground is tumbling over obstacles hard enough to change speed by 12 kt in seconds.
- **Forecasts of thunderstorms or a front.**
- A **large difference between the surface wind and the wind at 2,000 ft or 5,000 ft** in the GPWT. A big change in wind over a short height is shear, and shear makes turbulence.

### Thermals and the clouds they build

You met the cause of thermals in [RMTC 2.1](/notes/RMTC/2.1): uneven heating of the ground sends bubbles of warm air upwards, with sinking air between them. You cannot see a dry thermal, but you can see what it does.

The clearest sign is **cumulus cloud**. A thermal rises, cools as it expands, and at the height where it reaches its dew point the vapour condenses. Because all the thermals in an area start with roughly the same temperature and moisture, they reach their dew point at roughly the same height, which is why a field of cumulus has **flat bases** at one level. Each cloud is the visible top of a column of rising air, and the blue gaps between them are where the air is sinking. The **growing tops** show how vigorously the air is still rising: small, flat cumulus with tops close to the base mean weak thermals; tall, bubbling cumulus with **ragged, rapidly growing tops** mean strong thermals and a rough ride under and inside them. Cumulus is the picture of an unstable atmosphere, and towering cumulus is a thunderstorm in its first stage.

![What do thermals look like from the cockpit?](diagram:visible-signs-of-thermals "Flat-based cumulus, dust devils, soaring birds and smoke that rises then breaks up all mark rising air, with sinking air in between.")

The other visible signs:

- **Smoke** from a chimney or a burn-off that rises steadily, then breaks up and spreads at a certain height. The rise shows the air is unstable; the break-up shows the turbulence at the top of the rising layer.
- **Birds soaring in circles** without flapping, eagles and kites over the wheatbelt in particular. They are riding a thermal, and they mark it for you.
- **Gliders** doing the same thing. On a summer day near the gliding fields at Narrogin or Cunderdin, circling gliders tell you exactly where the lift is, and that there is sink around it.

### Dust devils: thermals you can see

On a hot afternoon with little wind, the ground over a bare paddock or a dry runway strip can heat the air touching it to far above the temperature of the air just above. That layer is extremely unstable: it is lighter than the air on top of it and is waiting for a reason to rise. When it finally breaks away at one point, air rushes in along the ground from all sides to replace it. Any slight rotation in that inflow, from a gust or the shape of the ground, is concentrated as the air converges, in the same way that a figure skater spins faster when she pulls her arms in. The result is a narrow, fast-spinning column of rising air that picks up dust: a **dust devil**.

![How does a hot paddock make a dust devil?](diagram:dust-devil-formation "A very hot layer breaks away, air rushes in along the ground, and the converging inflow spins up into a narrow column of strong rising air.")

A dust devil is therefore a **visible thermal**, and an unusually strong and narrow one. It marks a **strong updraught** in a column only a few metres to tens of metres across, with **sharp changes in wind speed and direction** around it, because the air is spinning. For a light aeroplane at low level, flying through one means a sudden gust from one side and then from the other, enough to roll the aeroplane or swing it on the ground. Dust devils are common inland and on dry aerodromes in summer, and they need hot ground and light wind; a fresh wind mixes the hot surface layer away before it can build up. Avoid them on take-off and landing, and treat one as a sign that the air over that area is very unstable.

??? check "Check yourself: why do dust devils appear on hot afternoons with light wind, but rarely when the wind is fresh?"
    They need a very hot, very unstable layer of air sitting on the ground until it breaks away. A light wind lets that layer build up; a fresh wind mixes it with the cooler air above before it can become unstable enough to form a spinning column.

### Clouds and surfaces that show wind turbulence

Some signs mark mechanical turbulence rather than thermals.

**Lenticular** (lens-shaped) **clouds** sit smooth and stationary downwind of hills and ranges, even in a strong wind. They form in the crests of **mountain waves**, the standing waves that strong wind makes as it flows over a ridge and oscillates up and down on the lee side. Beneath them, close to the ground, ragged **rotor clouds** mark rolling eddies of very rough air. Even without cloud, a strong wind across a ridge produces turbulence in its lee: in WA that means the Perth side of the Darling Scarp in a strong easterly.

![What do lenticular and rotor clouds tell you?](diagram:mountain-wave-lenticular-rotor "Lenticulars sit still in the wave crests and mark smooth lift and sink; the ragged rotor cloud at about ridge height marks the severe turbulence beneath them.")

![Where is the turbulence when a strong wind blows over a hill or a building?](diagram:rotor-in-the-lee-of-the-scarp "The rough air is on the downwind (lee) side of the Scarp, and the same happens on a smaller scale behind hangars, buildings and trees.")

**Ragged, fast-moving low cloud and whitecaps** on water show a strong, gusty surface wind, and with it mechanical turbulence close to the ground. A wind sock standing straight out and swinging back and forth is the aerodrome's own gust indicator.

**Virga**, rain falling from a cloud and evaporating before it reaches the ground, and the **dark base of a cumulonimbus**, show downdraughts and gust fronts. Evaporating rain cools the air, the cooled air sinks, and it hits the ground and spreads out; virga under a high-based inland storm is the warning of a dry microburst.

### What turbulence does to the aeroplane

Turbulence causes sudden changes in altitude, attitude and airspeed, and makes it hard to hold a heading and height. Each up-gust changes the angle of attack and therefore the load factor, so turbulence can **stall the wing** if you are slow, or **overstress the airframe** if you are fast; that trade-off is why the POH gives a turbulence penetration speed (see [RBKA 3.6](/notes/RBKA/3.6)). On approach it means a rough ride and a risk of a hard or long landing.

Thermals have a particular effect on the approach: they produce a gain of height over the updraught and a loss in the sinking air around it. Final approach over a hot bare paddock can be sinking air followed by lift over the runway, or the reverse. The aeroplane balloons over the threshold when you thought you were on the path, or sinks short of it. Expect it, correct early with power and attitude, and be ready to go around.

??? check "Check yourself: two fields of cumulus have the same base. In one the tops are flat and a few hundred feet deep; in the other they are tall with ragged, rapidly growing tops. Which promises the rougher ride, and why?"
    The tall, ragged cumulus. The depth and growth of the tops show how strongly the air is still rising, so they mark stronger thermals and more vigorous updraughts and downdraughts beneath and inside them.

## Wind gradient

### Why the wind slows near the ground

Air moving over the ground is dragged back by friction against the surface, the trees and the buildings. The air closest to the ground is slowed the most, the air a little higher less, and above a few hundred to a couple of thousand feet the wind blows at its full gradient speed. That steady change of wind speed with height, caused by friction, is the **wind gradient**. Friction also lets the surface wind turn across the isobars, so the wind a few hundred feet up is stronger and often from a slightly different direction than the wind at the surface. Over rough ground and in strong winds the gradient is steeper; over smooth water it is gentler.

### Why a change of wind changes the airspeed

To see what the gradient does to an aeroplane, you need one idea that applies to all wind shear: **the aeroplane's inertia keeps its speed over the ground for a moment, and the airspeed changes instead.** An aeroplane flying at 70 kt into a 25 kt headwind is moving over the ground at 45 kt. If the headwind suddenly fell to 10 kt, the aeroplane, a tonne of metal with momentum, could not instantly change its groundspeed. For a moment it is still doing 45 kt over the ground, but now only 10 kt of wind is coming the other way, so its airspeed is 55 kt. The wing feels 55 kt, not 70 kt, and makes lift accordingly.

![Why does a loss of headwind cost airspeed?](diagram:wind-shear-inertia "Inertia holds the 45 kt groundspeed, so when the headwind drops from 25 to 10 kt the airspeed falls from 70 to 55 kt: 15 kt gone in a moment.")

So:

- A **decreasing headwind** (or an increasing tailwind) makes the airspeed **fall**. The aeroplane loses lift, sinks and drops below the path. This is **performance-decreasing** shear.
- An **increasing headwind** (or a decreasing tailwind) makes the airspeed **rise**. The aeroplane gains lift, floats and rises above the path. This is **performance-increasing** shear.

Given time the aeroplane settles back to its trimmed airspeed, but that takes seconds, and close to the ground you may not have them.

### The gradient on approach and after take-off

On approach into a strong headwind you descend through the gradient, so the headwind **decreases** as you go down. That is a steady performance-decreasing shear: the airspeed tends to **decay**, the rate of descent increases, and the aeroplane undershoots unless you add power.

![What happens to the approach as you descend through a wind gradient?](diagram:wind-gradient-on-approach "The headwind dies away near the ground, so the airspeed decays and the aeroplane sinks below the path: be ready to add power.")

!!! example "Worked example: airspeed lost in a wind gradient on final"
    Given: on a windy winter afternoon you are on final at 70 kt into a headwind of 25 kt at 500 ft. The surface wind is 10 kt straight down the runway. Flap-down stall speed 48 kt.

    1. Groundspeed at 500 ft: $70 - 25 = \mathbf{45\ kt}$.
    2. Loss of headwind from 500 ft to the surface: $25 - 10 = \mathbf{15\ kt}$.
    3. If inertia held the 45 kt groundspeed through the descent, airspeed near the ground: $45 + 10 = \mathbf{55\ kt}$.
    4. Margin above the stall: $70 - 48 = 22$ kt at 500 ft, $55 - 48 = \mathbf{7\ kt}$ near the ground.

    In practice the gradient is gradual and the aeroplane partly recovers its speed as it goes, but the trend is real: in a strong wind the airspeed bleeds away in the last few hundred feet. That is why you carry extra speed and expect to need power in the flare.

After take-off into a headwind you climb through the gradient the other way: the headwind **increases** as you climb, which gives a brief airspeed gain and a better climb angle. A **tailwind** gradient does the opposite: the tailwind increases with height, the airspeed falls as you climb, and the climb gradient is reduced, which is one more reason never to take off downwind towards obstacles.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    "Loss of headwind means loss of airspeed" is true only because of inertia, and only for a moment. Do not reason from groundspeed: the trap answer says that a decreasing headwind on approach increases the groundspeed and so the aeroplane "floats". The wing flies on airspeed, the airspeed falls, and the aeroplane sinks.

## Wind shear

### Where to expect it

**Wind shear** is any sudden change in wind speed or direction over a short distance, horizontally or vertically. The wind gradient is a gentle, steady version of it; the dangerous versions are sudden.

**Low-level inversions and nocturnal jets.** On a clear night the ground cools the air touching it, and a layer of cold, dense air forms near the surface under warmer air: an **inversion**. The cold layer is cut off from the wind above, so at the surface it is calm, while a few hundred feet up the wind may be 20 or 30 kt, sometimes stronger than the gradient wind, as a **nocturnal low-level jet**. Climbing or descending through the top of the inversion gives a sharp change. Summer dawns on the coastal plain, with a strong easterly above a calm surface, are the classic case.

![Why the sudden wind change climbing out at dawn?](diagram:nocturnal-inversion-low-level-jet "Calm, cold air lies under the inversion and the jet blows just above it: crossing the top can change the wind by 20 or 30 kt at once, and climbing into a headwind the airspeed jumps.")

**Sea breeze fronts**, where the cool sea air meets the inland air. Flying through the front, or approaching an aerodrome as it passes, you can go from an easterly to a south-westerly in a few hundred feet or a few hundred metres, with turbulence at the boundary.

![Where is the shear in a sea breeze?](diagram:sea-breeze-front "Cool sea air pushes inland under the warm land air as a shallow wedge: the shear and turbulence are at its leading edge, the sea breeze front.")

**Fronts**, at the wind change as the front passes. A winter cold front can swing the wind from a north-westerly to a strong south-westerly within minutes.

**Terrain and buildings.** The wind curls and shears around hills, hangars and tree lines beside the runway, so the last 50 ft of an approach in a strong crosswind can bring sudden changes of both speed and direction.

**Thunderstorms.** The gust front and microburst beneath a storm are the most dangerous shear of all, because they combine a large change in horizontal wind with a strong downdraught, close to the ground.

### The microburst

A **microburst** is an intense downdraught, typically less than a few kilometres across, that hits the ground and spreads out in all directions. Follow an aeroplane on approach through one. Entering the outflow, it meets a **headwind**: performance-increasing shear, so the airspeed and lift rise and it floats above the path. A pilot who reacts by reducing power has made the next part worse. In the centre it meets the **downdraught**, which pushes it down. On the far side the outflow is now a **tailwind**: performance-decreasing shear, so the airspeed and lift fall, just as the downdraught has pushed the aeroplane low and the ground is close. The total change in wind across the microburst can exceed what a light aeroplane can out-climb.

![What happens to an aeroplane that flies through a microburst on approach?](diagram:microburst-encounter "First a headwind makes it float, then the downdraught pushes it down, then the tailwind takes away airspeed and lift just as the ground gets close.")

The only defence is avoidance. Do not take off or land through a thunderstorm gust front, or beneath a storm, or under virga from a high-based inland storm.

### What shear does to the aeroplane

The effect on the aeroplane is an **abrupt change in indicated airspeed, lift and rate of descent**, and a **change in drift** if the wind direction changes. A sudden loss of headwind on final can drop the airspeed towards the stall and the aeroplane below the glidepath. A sudden tailwind on take-off lengthens the ground run and reduces the climb. Because the aeroplane's inertia keeps its groundspeed roughly constant for a moment, the airspeed change is immediate, and you must respond with power and attitude at once.

??? check "Check yourself: climbing out at dawn, you pass through the top of an inversion into a 25 kt wind from directly ahead. What happens to the airspeed and the climb, and why?"
    The airspeed rises and the aeroplane climbs more steeply for a moment. The headwind has increased suddenly, but inertia keeps the groundspeed, so the airspeed jumps by up to the change in wind. It is performance-increasing shear; the same layer met on descent, or with the wind from behind, would do the opposite.

## Responding to the signs

When shear or turbulence is forecast or suspected, the response is the same set of tools whatever the cause:

- **Add a margin to the approach speed.** Half the gust factor is the usual rule: add half the difference between the gust and the mean wind to your normal approach speed. Your POH or your instructor may set a limit on how much to add.
- **Use less flap** if the POH allows, so that the aeroplane is less affected by gusts and easier to accelerate.
- **Be ready to go around early**, and go around at the first sign that the speed or the path is getting away from you.
- **Do not take off or land through a thunderstorm gust front** at all.
- **Listen to other pilots.** On a windy day a report of "wind shear on final" from a preceding aircraft is a reason to add speed and be ready for a go-around, and passing on your own report helps the next pilot.

!!! example "Worked example: approach speed with a gust margin"
    Given: the ATIS gives the wind as 250 degrees at 14 kt gusting 24 kt, close to straight down the runway. Your normal approach speed is 65 kt.

    1. Gust factor: $24 - 14 = \mathbf{10\ kt}$.
    2. Margin, half the gust factor: $10 \div 2 = \mathbf{5\ kt}$.
    3. Approach speed: $65 + 5 = \mathbf{70\ kt}$.

    The extra 5 kt is there to be lost in a lull without bringing you near the stall. Expect a longer float if the gust is still blowing when you flare.

Try one before opening the solution: the TAF for your arrival gives `22012G30KT` and your normal approach speed is 62 kt. What approach speed does the rule give, and what else does that wind tell you?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    Gust factor $30 - 12 = 18$ kt; half is 9 kt; $62 + 9 = 71$ kt. A gust of 18 kt above the mean is well past the 10 kt that signals turbulence in a forecast, so expect a rough approach and strong mechanical turbulence near the ground, check the crosswind component against your limit, and decide before you leave whether this is a day for a student pilot to be landing there at all.

## Bringing it together

A February afternoon. You are returning to Northam from a training area to the north at 1500 WST. The TAF gave a gusty north-easterly, the GPWT showed the 2,000 ft wind at 30 kt, and the GAF carried moderate turbulence below 5,000 ft. On the way back you see cumulus with ragged, growing tops building to the east, one dark-based cloud trailing virga, and two dust devils wandering across the bare paddocks south of the aerodrome. Read them in turn: strong thermals, so a rough ride at circuit height; a downdraught and possible gust front from the virga cloud, so watch it and keep your distance; very unstable air near the surface around the aerodrome, so expect sharp gusts on short final. The wind sock is swinging between 15 and 25 kt, so you add half the gust factor, 5 kt, to the approach speed. On late final you descend through the gradient, the airspeed sags and you add power; over the hot runway the aeroplane balloons in a thermal, and you hold the attitude and let it settle. Had the virga cloud moved over the aerodrome, the answer would have been to hold off well clear, or divert, not to try to land underneath it.

??? check "Check yourself: in the scenario, which single observation would most change your plan, and why?"
    The dark-based cloud trailing virga moving towards the aerodrome. Virga under a high-based storm marks a cooling downdraught that can reach the ground as a microburst and gust front, the most dangerous shear there is; the thermals, dust devils and gusts are managed with speed and attention, but a microburst is only managed by staying away.

## Key points

- **2.3.1 (a)** Turbulence is forecast by GAF turbulence groups, severe-turbulence SIGMETs, TAF gusts of 10 kt or more above the mean wind, thunderstorms and fronts, and big wind changes with height in the GPWT. Thermals show as flat-based cumulus (taller and more ragged means stronger), smoke that rises and breaks up, and soaring birds and gliders. Dust devils are visible, strong, narrow thermals on hot, light-wind days: avoid them near the ground. Lenticular and rotor clouds mark mountain wave and lee turbulence; whitecaps and ragged low cloud mark gusty wind; virga and dark cumulonimbus bases mark downdraughts. Effect: sudden changes in height, attitude and airspeed, a risk of stall or overstress, sink and lift on final.
- **2.3.1 (b)** Wind gradient is friction slowing the wind near the ground. Because inertia holds the groundspeed, a loss of headwind or gain of tailwind costs airspeed and lift (the aeroplane sinks), and a gain of headwind adds them (it floats). Descending into a strong headwind the airspeed decays: add power. Expect shear at inversions, sea breeze fronts, fronts, around buildings and, worst of all, in thunderstorm microbursts (headwind, downdraught, tailwind). Respond with half the gust factor on the approach speed, less flap if the POH allows, an early go-around, and no take-off or landing through a gust front.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Loss of headwind means loss of airspeed: that is the whole point of wind shear questions. Descending through a wind gradient into a strong headwind, the airspeed decays and the aeroplane sinks, so expect to add power. Dust devils are visible thermals and should be avoided on take-off and landing. Lenticular and rotor clouds point to lee turbulence, and a microburst gives headwind, then downdraught, then tailwind. For gusts, add half the gust factor to the approach speed.
