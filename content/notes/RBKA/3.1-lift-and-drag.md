## What the exam expects (RBKA 3.1)

- **3.1.1** Say whether lift and drag go up or down when the flap setting is changed.
- **3.1.2** Recall the typical angles of attack at which a basic low-speed wing makes its maximum lift (about 16 degrees) and is most efficient, with its best lift/drag ratio (about 4 degrees).
- **3.1.3** Describe how those two angles of attack become two speeds: the stall speed and the best glide speed.
- **3.1.4** State how attitude, angle of attack and airspeed are tied together in level flight.

## Why this matters

Two moments in your training depend on this lesson. The first is the engine failure after take-off from Murrayfield or Serpentine: the only thing you can do with the energy you have left is fly the speed that gives the most distance for the height, and that speed exists because of one particular angle of attack. The second is the landing that floats halfway down Jandakot's runway because you arrived 10 kt fast and the wing, a few feet off the ground, suddenly had less drag than it should. In between sits flap, which you use on every circuit and which changes lift, drag and the attitude the aeroplane flies at. This lesson builds on the lift formula and the drag curve from [BAKC 4.2](/notes/BAKC/4.2) and turns them into the handful of angles and speeds the exam asks about.

## One wing, one angle, one speed

Start with what you already know from [BAKC 4.2](/notes/BAKC/4.2). Lift depends on the density of the air, the square of the speed, the wing area and the **lift coefficient**, $C_L$, a number that says how hard the wing is working at its present angle of attack:

$$L = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho V^2 S\, C_L$$

The **angle of attack** is the angle between the wing's chord line and the relative airflow, the air coming at the wing from the direction it is travelling. Tilt the wing to a larger angle and $C_L$ rises in almost a straight line, until the air can no longer follow the upper surface and lift collapses.

![What happens to lift as the angle of attack increases?](diagram:cl-vs-angle-of-attack "The line is straight until about 16 degrees; past the critical angle the airflow separates and lift falls even though the nose is higher.")

Now put the aeroplane in level flight at a fixed weight. Lift must equal weight, so the left-hand side of the formula is fixed. On a given day, at a given height, the density is fixed too, and so is the wing area. That leaves only two things that can trade against each other: speed and $C_L$. Fly fast and $V^2$ is large, so $C_L$ must be small: a small angle of attack. Fly slowly and $C_L$ must be large: a large angle of attack.

The conclusion is the backbone of this whole subtopic: **in level flight at a given weight, each airspeed goes with exactly one angle of attack**, and each angle of attack goes with exactly one airspeed. When the syllabus asks how an *angle* relates to a *speed*, this is the link.

## The two angles that matter

Of all the angles a wing can fly at, two have names, and the exam expects you to know both for a basic low-speed aerofoil, the kind on a training aeroplane.

### About 16 degrees: maximum lift

At about **16 degrees** the lift coefficient reaches its peak, **$C_{L\max}$**. This is the **critical angle of attack**. The wing is making the most lift it can for a given speed, but it is right at the edge: increase the angle any further and the airflow over the top separates, lift falls and drag rises steeply. That is the stall, which [RBKA 3.6](/notes/RBKA/3.6) covers in full. For a typical light aeroplane the critical angle lies somewhere around 15 to 18 degrees; the exam's figure is 16.

### About 4 degrees: the most lift for the least drag

The second angle is about efficiency, and it needs one new idea: the **lift/drag ratio**, written L/D. It is simply the lift the wing makes divided by the drag it costs. A wing at an L/D of 10 is producing ten units of lift for every unit of drag. The higher the number, the more efficient the wing.

L/D changes with angle of attack. Picture what happens as you move through the range.

At a very small angle the wing makes little lift, so to hold the aeroplane up you must be flying fast. At high speed **parasite drag**, the drag of pushing the shape of the aeroplane through the air, is large, because it rises with the square of the speed. Lots of drag for the lift: a poor ratio.

At a large angle, near the stall, the wing makes plenty of lift at a low speed, but the price of making lift, **induced drag**, is large. Induced drag comes from the air spilling round the wing tips and the downwash behind the wing, and it is greatest at high angles of attack and low speeds. Again lots of drag for the lift: a poor ratio.

In between there is an angle where the two kinds of drag are equal and their total is smallest. In level flight lift is fixed at the weight, so smallest drag means largest L/D. For a basic low-speed aerofoil that angle is about **4 degrees**: the **best L/D** angle. Either side of it the ratio falls away.

![How does total drag change with airspeed, and where is it least?](diagram:drag-curves-vs-airspeed "Parasite drag rises and induced drag falls with speed; total drag is least where they are equal, the speed of the best lift/drag ratio.")

Notice the two angles are a long way apart. At 4 degrees the wing is working efficiently with a large margin to the stall; at 16 degrees it is making all the lift it can and has no margin left. You fly most of your flying between them. The table is the recap, now that each row has a reason:

| Angle of attack | What happens | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| About **4 degrees** | **Best lift/drag ratio**: the most lift for the least drag, minimum total drag | Most efficient: best glide, best range, least thrust needed for level flight |
| About **16 degrees** | **Maximum lift coefficient ($C_{L\max}$)**; the critical angle | Any increase beyond this causes the stall |

![At what angle is the wing most efficient, and how far is that from the stall?](diagram:ld-ratio-vs-angle-of-attack "L/D peaks at about 4 degrees and CL at about 16: the efficient angle sits about 12 degrees below the stall.")

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The exam lists pairs of angles and asks which is best L/D and which is maximum lift. Maximum lift is the *larger* angle, 16 degrees; the most *efficient* angle is the smaller one, 4 degrees. A wing at maximum lift is not efficient: it is paying a huge induced drag bill for that lift.

??? check "Check yourself: why is the lift/drag ratio poor both at a very small angle of attack and near the critical angle?"
    At a very small angle the wing must fly fast to make enough lift, so parasite drag is large. Near the critical angle it makes the lift slowly, but induced drag is large. Only around 4 degrees are the two drags balanced so that total drag is least for the lift made.

## From angles to speeds: the stall and the best glide

Put the two ideas together. Each angle of attack corresponds, in level flight at a given weight, to one airspeed. So each of the two key angles has a speed attached to it.

**The stall speed** is the speed at which the angle of attack needed to hold the aeroplane up reaches the **critical angle**, about 16 degrees. At that speed the wing is producing $C_{L\max}$ and only just making enough lift. Fly any slower and no angle of attack will give enough lift: the angle needed would be beyond the critical angle, and the wing stalls. So the stall speed is the speed that corresponds to $C_{L\max}$.

**The best glide speed** is the speed that puts the wing at the **best L/D angle**, about 4 degrees. To see why that speed gives the best glide, look at the forces when the engine is not helping. In a steady glide the aeroplane has no thrust; lift and drag together balance the weight, and the steepness of the glide path is set by the ratio of drag to lift. Less drag for the lift you are making means a flatter path. The flattest path of all, and so the greatest distance in still air for the height you have, comes when D/L is smallest, which is when L/D is greatest: at about 4 degrees.

![What keeps a gliding aeroplane moving with no thrust, and what sets the glide angle?](diagram:forces-in-a-glide "Lift and drag together balance the weight; the glide angle is set by drag over lift, so the flattest glide is at the best L/D.")

A wing at an L/D of 9 glides 9 ft forward for every foot it descends. The details of glide distance, and what happens if you glide at the wrong speed, are in [RBKA 3.4](/notes/RBKA/3.4). What matters here is the link: **best glide speed is the speed for the best L/D angle**, and the minimum-drag speed on the drag curve is the same speed.

### What weight does to both speeds

The angles belong to the wing; they do not change with weight. The speeds do. A heavier aeroplane needs more lift, and at the same angle of attack the only way to make more lift is to fly faster. So at a higher weight, the wing reaches 4 degrees at a higher speed and 16 degrees at a higher speed: **both the best glide speed and the stall speed rise with weight**, and both fall when you are lighter.

Because lift goes with the square of the speed, the speed for any given angle goes with the **square root** of the weight:

$$V_\text{new} = V_\text{old} \sqrt{\frac{W_\text{new}}{W_\text{old}}}$$

![How do the two key angles of attack become the stall speed and the best glide speed?](diagram:best-ld-and-critical-angle-speeds "Each speed needs one angle in level flight. About 4 degrees gives best glide (70 kt heavy, about 63 kt light) and about 16 degrees gives the stall; lighter, both speeds drop but the angles stay.")

!!! example "Worked example: best glide speed at a light weight"
    Given: the POH best glide speed is 70 kt at the maximum weight of 1,000 kg. You are solo with low fuel at 810 kg.

    1. Weight ratio: $810 / 1000 = \mathbf{0.81}$.
    2. Square root: $\sqrt{0.81} = \mathbf{0.90}$.
    3. Speed for the best L/D angle today: $70 \times 0.90 = \mathbf{63\ kt}$.

    At the lighter weight the best L/D still occurs at about 4 degrees, but less lift is needed, so the speed that gives 4 degrees is lower. The POH figure is for maximum weight. Gliding at 70 kt when light costs a little distance, though the glide angle at the correct speed is the same at both weights.

Try one before opening the solution: an aeroplane's best glide speed is 65 kt at 750 kg. What is the speed for the same angle of attack at the maximum weight of 900 kg?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    Weight ratio $900/750 = 1.20$; $\sqrt{1.20} = 1.095$; $65 \times 1.095 = 71.2$, so **about 71 kt**. Heavier means a higher best glide speed, by the square root of the weight ratio. The best L/D angle, and so the best glide angle and the still-air distance from a given height, are unchanged.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    "A heavier aeroplane glides less far." It does not, if flown at its correct best glide speed. Weight changes the *speed* at which the best L/D angle is reached, not the L/D itself, so the glide angle and the still-air distance are the same. The heavy aeroplane simply gets there faster and with a higher rate of descent.

??? check "Check yourself: what is the link between the critical angle of attack and the stall speed?"
    In level flight at a given weight each speed needs one angle of attack. The stall speed is the speed at which that angle reaches the critical angle, about 16 degrees, where $C_L$ is at its maximum. Any slower and the angle needed would be past the critical angle.

## What flap does to lift and drag

You lower flap on every circuit. It is worth understanding what it does to the wing rather than just learning the answers.

A trailing-edge **flap** is a hinged section of the rear of the wing that moves down. Lowering it increases the wing's **camber**, the curvature of the aerofoil, and on some types (such as Fowler flaps, which slide back as well as down) it increases the wing area too. A more cambered wing turns the airflow more sharply, so at any given angle of attack it makes more lift. It also makes more drag: the deflected flap presents more of itself to the airflow, and the extra lift brings extra induced drag.

So the answer to element 3.1.1 is short. At the same angle of attack and airspeed:

| Flap change | Lift | Drag |
|---|---|---|
| Flap lowered | **Increases** (higher $C_L$ at a given angle of attack, higher $C_{L\max}$) | **Increases** |
| Flap raised | **Decreases** | **Decreases** |

![What does lowering flap do to the lift curve?](diagram:flap-effect-on-lift-curve "The whole curve moves up: more lift at every angle, a higher CL max and so a lower stall speed, the same lift at a smaller angle (nose lower), and more drag.")

Look at the diagram and read off three more consequences.

**The stall speed is lower with flap down.** $C_{L\max}$ is higher, so the wing can make the lift it needs at a lower speed before reaching its maximum. That is the point of flap for landing: you can approach and touch down slower.

**The same lift comes at a smaller angle of attack.** At a given speed in level flight the lift needed is the same as before, but the cambered wing makes it at a lower angle. The aeroplane flies with its nose lower, which is why the view over the nose on a flapped approach is so much better. The critical angle with flap down is also slightly lower, though the stall *speed* is lower too.

**The lift/drag ratio is worse.** Flap adds drag in a bigger proportion than it adds useful lift, so the best L/D with flap down is lower than with flap up. That is why a flapped glide is steeper, which you use deliberately on approach ([RBKA 3.4](/notes/RBKA/3.4)).

How much of each you get depends on how far you lower it. The **first stage** of flap, often about 10 degrees, gives a large increase in lift for a modest increase in drag; some types use it for take-off, to lift off at a lower speed. **Full flap** adds relatively little extra lift but a **lot of drag**: it steepens the approach and slows the aeroplane down, and is used for landing. Your POH says which settings your type uses for what.

??? check "Check yourself: you lower flap while holding the same airspeed in level flight. Will the nose need to be higher or lower, and why?"
    Lower. The more cambered wing makes the lift you need at a smaller angle of attack, and in level flight a smaller angle of attack means a lower nose attitude. Drag has gone up too, so you also need a little more power to hold the speed.

## Attitude, angle of attack and airspeed in level flight

You cannot see the angle of attack from the cockpit. You can see the **attitude**, the angle of the nose relative to the horizon, and you can read the airspeed. In level flight those three are locked together, and the reason is the direction of the airflow.

In **level flight** the flight path is horizontal, so the relative airflow comes straight at the aeroplane horizontally. The angle of attack is the angle between the chord line and that horizontal airflow. The chord line is fixed to the fuselage at a small angle, the **rigging angle** (angle of incidence). So in level flight, the angle of attack is simply the pitch attitude plus that fixed rigging angle. Raise the nose two degrees and the angle of attack goes up two degrees.

Put that together with "each speed has one angle of attack" and you get the relationship the syllabus wants:

| Airspeed | Angle of attack needed | Pitch attitude |
|---|---|---|
| High | Low | Nose low (flatter) |
| Medium (best L/D) | About 4 degrees | Slightly nose-up |
| Low (approaching the stall) | High, approaching 16 degrees | Nose high |

For every airspeed there is one angle of attack and one attitude that holds level flight at a given weight and configuration. Slow down and you must raise the nose (more angle of attack) to stay level, and add power to overcome the extra induced drag. Speed up and you must lower the nose, or you climb. That is why your instructor teaches "attitude for airspeed": in level flight, a chosen attitude *is* a chosen airspeed, once the power is set to match.

![Where does the angle of attack come from? Move attitude and flight path separately](widget:attitude-and-angle-of-attack "The wing stalls at an angle of attack, not an attitude: the same nose position can be flying or stalled depending on where the aeroplane is going.")

The link only holds in level flight. In a climb or descent the relative airflow is inclined to the horizon, because it comes from wherever the aeroplane is going. A climbing aeroplane with its nose 10 degrees up, on a path climbing at 6 degrees, has an angle of attack of only about 4 degrees. A nose-low aeroplane being pulled hard out of a dive can be at the critical angle. Try both in the widget above.

![Why is the angle of attack only 4 degrees when the nose is 10 degrees up?](diagram:angle-of-attack-vs-attitude "Measure the angle of attack from the flight path, not the horizon: nose 10 degrees up on a 6 degree climb is about 4 degrees.")

!!! warning "Common trap"
    "A nose-high attitude means a high angle of attack." Only in level flight. In a climb the nose is high but the angle of attack can be small, and in a steep descent or a pull-out the nose can be below the horizon with the wing at the critical angle. The exam uses "attitude" as the distractor in stall and angle-of-attack questions.

??? check "Check yourself: you hold level flight at 90 kt, then at 110 kt, at the same weight. Which needs the lower nose attitude, and why?"
    110 kt. At the higher speed the wing makes the lift equal to the weight at a smaller angle of attack, and in level flight the angle of attack is the attitude plus the fixed rigging angle, so a smaller angle means a lower nose.

## Ground effect: the wing near the runway

There is one place where a wing's drag changes without any change in speed or angle of attack: within about a wingspan of the ground. You meet it in every flare.

Recall where induced drag comes from. A wing making lift pushes air down behind it, the **downwash**, and the high-pressure air underneath spills round the tips into **wing-tip vortices**. The downwash tilts the airflow over the wing, which tilts the lift vector slightly rearwards, and that rearward part is induced drag.

Now bring the wing close to the ground. The ground is in the way: the air cannot be pushed down as far, and the tip vortices cannot fully form. The downwash is reduced, the airflow over the wing is less tilted, and the lift vector stands more upright. The result is **less induced drag**, and a little **more lift** at the same angle of attack. This is **ground effect**. It is noticeable from about one wingspan up and becomes significant within about half a wingspan, which for a training aeroplane is the last 15 ft or so of the approach. It is strongest on a low-wing aeroplane, whose wing sits closer to the ground.

![Why does the aeroplane float in the flare?](diagram:ground-effect "Within about half a span the ground blocks the downwash and squashes the tip vortices: less induced drag, so it floats on landing and can lift off before it can climb.")

Two consequences follow, one for each end of the runway.

**On landing**, induced drag falls away just as you close the throttle and flare. With less drag the aeroplane does not slow down the way it did on the approach, and it **floats**. Arrive with excess speed and it floats a long way, using runway you may not have. That is why the approach speed matters so much in the last few hundred feet: extra knots on final become extra hundreds of metres in the flare.

**On take-off**, the reverse catches people out. Ground effect lets the aeroplane lift off at a speed at which it could not climb out of ground effect. It flies a few feet up, then as it climbs out of ground effect induced drag returns, and the aeroplane may sink back or refuse to accelerate. On a hot day at a high density height, with a heavy aeroplane, this is how strips are run off the end. The cure is to lift off at the POH speed, and if you do get airborne early, to stay low in ground effect and accelerate before climbing.

??? check "Check yourself: why does an aeroplane float further in the flare if it arrives at the runway fast?"
    In ground effect induced drag falls, so the aeroplane loses speed more slowly than on the approach. Any excess speed has to be bled off with less drag to do it, so it floats a long way before it is slow enough to touch down.

## Bringing it together

You depart Murrayfield solo on a warm afternoon, 820 kg, with the first stage of flap set as your POH allows for take-off. The flap gives more lift at every angle, so you lift off at a lower speed, and you resist the urge to haul the aeroplane off early because ground effect would let it fly before it can climb. Flap up at a safe height: lift falls at the same angle, so the nose comes up a little to keep climbing. At 1,500 ft the engine stops. You lower the nose to the best glide attitude, because the most distance for your height comes from flying the wing at its best L/D angle, about 4 degrees. The POH says 70 kt at maximum weight; at your weight the same angle comes at about 63 kt, and you fly close to that rather than the book figure. You do not raise the nose to stretch the glide: a higher angle of attack takes you off the best L/D and steepens the path. On short final with full flap the nose is noticeably lower, the glide steeper, the stall speed lower. Arriving at the right speed, the float in ground effect is short.

## Key points

- **3.1.1** Lowering flap increases both lift and drag at the same angle of attack and speed; raising it decreases both. Flap raises $C_{L\max}$ (lower stall speed), gives the same lift at a smaller angle (nose lower) and lowers the best L/D (steeper glide).
- **3.1.2** A basic low-speed wing makes maximum lift at about 16 degrees, the critical angle, and is most efficient at its best L/D, about 4 degrees, where total drag is least.
- **3.1.3** In level flight each speed needs one angle of attack: the stall speed is the speed for the critical angle; the best glide speed is the speed for the best L/D angle. Both speeds rise with weight (by the square root of the weight ratio); the angles do not.
- **3.1.4** In level flight the angle of attack is the attitude plus a fixed rigging angle: high speed, low angle, nose low; low speed, high angle, nose high. The link breaks in a climb or descent, because the relative airflow comes from the flight path.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Have **4 degrees** (best L/D, best glide) and **16 degrees** (maximum lift, the stall) by heart, and do not swap them. Flap questions want "both increase" for lift and drag when flap is lowered, and "nose lower" for the attitude at the same speed. A question about a heavier aeroplane wants the best glide speed and the stall speed **higher**, the angles **unchanged**. And any question that equates attitude with angle of attack is true only in level flight.
