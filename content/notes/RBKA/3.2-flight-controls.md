## What the exam expects (RBKA 3.2)

- **3.2.1** Describe what the elevator, rudder and ailerons each do first (the primary effect) and what follows from it (the further effect), and about which axis: longitudinal, lateral or normal.
- **3.2.2** Describe how changes of power and airspeed alter the pitch trim and how well the elevator, rudder and ailerons work.
- **3.2.3** Describe what trim controls are for.
- **3.2.4** State what lowering or raising flap does to lift, drag and attitude.

## Why this matters

The first time you open the throttle for a go-around at Jandakot, with the aeroplane trimmed nose-up for a 65 kt approach and full flap hanging out, the nose rears up and swings left, and you need a firm push and a bootful of right rudder to keep it where you want it. Nothing has gone wrong: the controls are doing exactly what this lesson describes, and the aeroplane is reacting to power, airspeed and flap the way every single-engine trainer does. Understanding why turns that moment from a surprise into a sequence you expect and fly calmly. The exam asks which axis each control works about, what each control does beyond its main job, which controls the slipstream helps, why you trim, and what flap does to lift, drag and attitude.

## Three axes through one point

An aeroplane in the air is free to rotate in any direction, and every rotation happens about its **centre of gravity**, the point where its whole weight can be taken to act. To describe those rotations we use three imaginary lines through the centre of gravity, each at right angles to the other two.

- The **longitudinal axis** runs from the nose to the tail. Rotation about it is **roll**: one wing goes up, the other down.
- The **lateral axis** runs from wing tip to wing tip. Rotation about it is **pitch**: the nose goes up or down.
- The **normal axis** (or vertical axis) runs from top to bottom, at right angles to the other two. Rotation about it is **yaw**: the nose swings left or right.

![Move the controls and watch which axis the aeroplane rotates about](widget:axes-and-controls "Every axis passes through the centre of gravity. Ailerons roll, elevator pitches, rudder yaws; adverse yaw swings the nose the wrong way first.")

Each primary control works one axis:

| Control | Primary effect | Movement | About the axis |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Elevator** | Pitch | Nose up or down | **Lateral** axis (wing tip to wing tip) |
| **Ailerons** | Roll | One wing up, the other down | **Longitudinal** axis (nose to tail) |
| **Rudder** | Yaw | Nose left or right | **Normal (vertical)** axis |

All three work the same way. A control surface is a hinged rear section of an aerofoil: the tailplane, the fin, or the outer wing. Deflecting it changes the camber of that aerofoil, so the aerofoil makes more lift, or less, or lift in the opposite direction. That force acts some distance from the centre of gravity, and force times distance is a turning moment. The tail surfaces are a long way behind the centre of gravity and the ailerons are out near the wing tips, so a small force produces a large rotation.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The names of the axes do not match the names of the movements in the way you might guess. The **lateral** axis is the one that runs sideways, from tip to tip, and rotation about it is *pitch*. Rotation about the **longitudinal** axis is *roll*. If in doubt, picture a skewer through the aeroplane along the named axis and ask how it could spin about it.

## The elevator: pitch, then speed

Move the control column back. The **elevator**, the hinged rear part of the tailplane, moves **up**. That increases the downward force the tailplane makes, the tail goes down, and the aeroplane pitches **nose up** about the lateral axis. Forward on the column does the reverse: elevator down, tail up, nose down.

The **further effect** of the elevator is on angle of attack and airspeed. Raising the nose increases the wing's angle of attack, which makes more lift and more drag. Without a change of power, the extra drag slows the aeroplane: raise the nose and the **airspeed decreases**; lower it and the **airspeed increases**. That is the foundation of how you fly: with the power set, the attitude you hold with the elevator sets the airspeed.

## The ailerons: roll, then yaw

Move the control column to the right. The **right aileron goes up** and the **left aileron goes down**. The down-going aileron increases the camber of the left wing's outer section, so it makes more lift; the up-going aileron reduces the right wing's lift. The left wing rises, the right wing drops, and the aeroplane rolls **right** about the longitudinal axis.

Ailerons have two further effects on yaw, and they happen in sequence.

### Adverse yaw: the nose swings the wrong way first

The wing that is making more lift, the rising left wing in our example, also makes more **induced drag**, the drag that comes with producing lift. So while you are rolling right, the left wing is being held back harder than the right, and the nose yaws **left**, away from the direction of the roll. This is **adverse aileron yaw**. It is strongest at low speed and with large aileron deflections, when the down-going aileron is working the wing hardest.

![Why does the nose swing the wrong way when you roll, and how does rudder fix it?](diagram:adverse-yaw "The down-going aileron makes more induced drag on the rising wing, yawing the nose away from the turn; rudder the same way as the aileron cancels it.")

The cure is the **rudder**, applied in the same direction as the aileron: right aileron, right rudder. The rudder pushes the tail left and the nose right, cancelling the adverse yaw so the nose follows the roll. That is what **coordination** means. Designers help too. **Differential ailerons** move the up-going aileron further than the down-going one, adding a little drag on the descending wing to balance the rising wing, and **Frise ailerons** are shaped so the leading edge of the up-going aileron dips into the airflow and adds drag on that side. Both reduce adverse yaw; neither removes the need for rudder.

### Then the yaw reverses: the aeroplane slips into the turn

Once the aeroplane is banked, the lift is tilted and the aeroplane begins to **sideslip** towards the lower wing. The air now comes slightly from the side, from the low-wing side, and strikes the large side area of the fin and rear fuselage. Like a weathervane, the aeroplane swings its nose into that airflow: the nose yaws **towards the lower wing**. Left alone, the nose drops, the bank steepens, the speed builds and the aeroplane develops into a descending **spiral**.

So the full further effect of aileron is: adverse yaw away from the roll, then yaw towards the lower wing as the aeroplane slips.

??? check "Check yourself: you roll left with aileron alone at low speed. Which way does the nose swing first, and why?"
    To the right, away from the roll. The right aileron goes down to raise the right wing, so the right wing makes more lift and more induced drag, which holds it back and yaws the nose right. Left rudder with the left aileron cancels it.

## The rudder: yaw, then roll

Press the **right rudder pedal**. The rudder, the hinged rear of the fin, moves right. The fin now makes a sideways force to the left, the tail swings left, and the nose yaws **right** about the normal axis.

The **further effect** of rudder is **roll in the same direction**. As the nose yaws right, the left wing, now on the outside, moves forward faster through the air than the right wing, which moves back. The faster wing makes more lift, so the left wing rises and the aeroplane rolls **right**. On an aeroplane with **dihedral** (wings angled up from root to tip) the effect is stronger: the yaw makes the aeroplane skid, the air comes from the side, and the wing on the leading side meets it at a larger angle of attack and rises. Uncorrected, the roll steepens, the nose drops and this too ends in a spiral descent.

Yaw and roll are linked both ways: aileron produces yaw and rudder produces roll. That coupling is why balanced flight needs both controls together.

![Slip, balanced or skid: how does the ball show it?](diagram:slip-and-skid-ball "Ball to the inside of the turn: slipping, too little rudder; ball to the outside: skidding, too much. Step on the ball to centre it.")

The **balance ball** (the slip indicator) tells you whether the rudder and aileron are working together. With the ball in the centre the aeroplane is in balance. Ball displaced: "step on the ball", meaning apply rudder on the side the ball has gone to.

??? check "Check yourself: what is the further effect of left rudder in level flight, and why does it happen?"
    A roll to the left. The yaw moves the right wing forward faster than the left, so it makes more lift and rises; dihedral adds to it as the aeroplane skids. Uncorrected, the roll and the dropping nose develop into a spiral descent.

## What airspeed and power do to the controls

Every control surface works by making a force from the air flowing over it. That force depends on **dynamic pressure**, $\tfrac{1}{2}\rho V^2$, the same quantity that sets lift on the wing. So the speed of the air over a control surface decides how much a given deflection achieves.

**Airspeed.** At **high airspeed** the dynamic pressure is large. A small deflection makes a large force, so the controls are **more effective**, and they are **heavier** to move because the air is pressing harder on them. At **low airspeed** they are **less effective**: you move them further for less response, and they feel light and **sloppy**. That softness is one of the stall warnings in [RBKA 3.6](/notes/RBKA/3.6). Because the force goes with the square of the speed, halving the airspeed leaves only a quarter of the control force.

**Power and slipstream.** The propeller throws a fast, spiralling column of air back over the fuselage, the **slipstream**. It flows over the tailplane and the fin, so it increases the airspeed over the **elevator and rudder** whenever power is applied, even when the aeroplane itself is flying slowly. The **ailerons** are out on the wings, mostly **outside the slipstream**, so power does little for them. With the power off, in a glide or the flare, the elevator and rudder lose that boost and have only the aeroplane's own airspeed to work with.

![How do airspeed and power change what each control can do?](diagram:control-effectiveness-vs-airspeed "Slow and power off, everything is weak; power puts slipstream over the tail so the elevator and rudder work again, but not the ailerons; fast, all three are strong and heavy.")

This explains several things you will feel in the aeroplane. On the take-off roll the rudder works early, because the slipstream is over it before the aeroplane is moving fast. In a glide approach the elevator feels softer in the flare than in a powered approach, so you need a larger movement. And at low speed with high power, as in a go-around, the elevator and rudder are crisp while the ailerons are still weak.

### Power and airspeed change the pitch trim

Power changes do more than help the tail. On most single-engine trainers:

**Increasing power** pitches the nose **up**. The extra slipstream increases the airflow over the tailplane, which on a typical trainer increases its downward force, and the thrust line's position relative to the centre of gravity adds to the effect on many types. The increased power also **yaws** the nose: to the left on an aeroplane whose propeller turns clockwise seen from behind, because the spiralling slipstream strikes the left side of the fin, together with the propeller's other effects. You hold the nose with forward pressure and right rudder, then trim.

**Reducing power** does the opposite: the nose pitches **down** and yaws the other way.

**Airspeed** changes the trim too. An aeroplane trimmed for a given airspeed tends to return to it. Speed it up and it tends to pitch **up**, back towards its trimmed speed; slow it down and it tends to pitch **down**. So at every new airspeed you hold a new pressure on the column until you re-trim.

| Change | Typical effect (most single-engine trainers) | Trim needed |
|---|---|---|
| Power increased | Nose pitches **up** (thrust line, slipstream on the tailplane) and yaws (left with a propeller turning clockwise seen from behind) | Nose-down trim; right rudder |
| Power reduced | Nose pitches **down**, yaws the other way | Nose-up trim |
| Airspeed increased (by lowering the nose and/or adding power) | Aeroplane tends to pitch up towards its trimmed speed | Nose-down trim |
| Airspeed reduced | Aeroplane tends to pitch down towards its trimmed speed | Nose-up trim |

Your aeroplane may differ in the size of these changes; the POH and your instructor will tell you what to expect for your type.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    "More power makes all the controls more effective." Only the elevator and rudder, which sit in the slipstream. The ailerons are outside it and depend on airspeed alone. The exam asks which control is *least* helped by power at low speed: the ailerons.

??? check "Check yourself: you are gliding at 65 kt and add a burst of power. What happens to rudder effectiveness, and to the aileron effectiveness?"
    The rudder becomes more effective at once, because the slipstream increases the airflow over the fin. The ailerons barely change, because they are outside the slipstream and the aeroplane's airspeed has not yet changed.

## Trim: removing the pressure

You have seen that every change of power, airspeed or configuration changes the pressure you need on the controls to hold the attitude. Holding a steady push or pull for minutes on end is tiring, and a tired arm drifts, so the aeroplane wanders off height and speed. The **trim controls** exist to remove that sustained pressure.

**The purpose of trim** is to relieve the pilot of a steady control force so the aeroplane holds the selected attitude, and therefore the airspeed, with no pressure on the controls. That reduces workload and fatigue and makes accurate flying easier, because the aeroplane now wants to stay where you put it.

Most light aeroplanes do it with a **trim tab**, a small hinged surface on the trailing edge of the elevator, moved by a wheel or switch in the cockpit. The tab works backwards from what you might expect. For nose-up trim the elevator needs to be held **up**. The tab moves **down**; the airflow pushes up on it; and that upward force at the very back of the elevator holds the elevator's trailing edge up. The elevator stays in the nose-up position with no force from you. Other types use an adjustable tailplane or a spring in the control circuit, but the purpose is the same.

![How does a small tab hold a big elevator in place?](diagram:trim-tab "The tab moves opposite to the elevator: tab down, the air pushes it up, and that holds the elevator trailing edge up with no stick force.")

The technique follows from the purpose. **Attitude first, then trim**: select the attitude and power with the primary controls, let the speed settle, hold it, then trim out the remaining pressure until the aeroplane holds that attitude hands-off. Trim is not a primary flight control. You do not fly the aeroplane through a manoeuvre with the trim wheel, because trim is slow and removes the feel of the controls that tells you what the aeroplane is doing.

Some aeroplanes also have rudder trim, which removes the steady rudder pressure needed to hold balance at a given power and speed; on many trainers it is a fixed tab set on the ground.

??? check "Check yourself: what is the trim for, and why should you not use it to raise the nose in a climb entry?"
    Trim removes a sustained control pressure so the aeroplane holds the attitude you selected hands-off, reducing workload. You select the climb attitude with the elevator first, let the speed settle, then trim; using the trim wheel to fly the manoeuvre is slow and imprecise.

## Flap: lift, drag and attitude

Flap changes the wing itself, so it changes all three things the syllabus asks about: lift, drag and the attitude you fly at. The aerodynamics are in [RBKA 3.1](/notes/RBKA/3.1): lowering flap increases the wing's camber, so at the same angle of attack it makes more lift and more drag, and its maximum lift is higher, so the stall speed is lower.

![What does lowering flap do to the lift curve?](diagram:flap-effect-on-lift-curve "The whole curve moves up: more lift at every angle, a lower stall speed, the same lift at a smaller angle (nose lower), and more drag.")

What matters in the cockpit is what that does to the aeroplane at a constant airspeed.

**Lift.** Lowering flap at a given speed and angle of attack increases lift. If you change nothing else, the aeroplane rises. Raising flap reduces lift, and unless you raise the nose to compensate, the aeroplane **sinks**.

**Drag.** Lowering flap increases drag, so to hold the same speed level you need more power; without more power the speed falls or you must descend. Raising flap reduces drag.

**Attitude.** With flap down, the wing makes the same lift at a smaller angle of attack, so to hold the same speed in level flight the nose must be **lower**. Raising flap means the wing needs a larger angle again, so the nose must come **higher**.

**The immediate pitch change** when you move the flap depends on the type. Many high-wing trainers **pitch nose-up**, or "balloon", when flap is lowered, because lowering flap moves the wing's centre of pressure and changes the downwash over the tailplane. Raising flap usually brings a nose-down pitch and a sink. Your POH and your instructor will tell you what your aeroplane does.

| | Lowering flap | Raising flap |
|---|---|---|
| Lift (at the same speed and angle of attack) | Increases | Decreases (sink if not compensated) |
| Drag | Increases | Decreases |
| Stall speed | Decreases | Increases |
| Attitude to hold the same IAS level | **Lower** (more nose-down), because the cambered wing makes the lift at a smaller angle of attack | **Higher** (nose-up) to restore the angle of attack |
| Immediate pitch change | Depends on the type: many high-wing trainers **pitch nose-up ("balloon")** | Usually pitch nose-down and sink |

Flap also has a speed limit, because the extra lift it makes puts large loads on the flap and its mountings. The top of the white arc on the airspeed indicator, **VFE**, is the maximum speed with flap extended; the POH gives the figure for your type.

![Where are the flap speed limits on the airspeed indicator?](diagram:flap-speeds-vfo-vfe "The white arc runs from the full-flap stall speed up to VFE, the maximum speed with flap extended; slow down before you select flap.")

!!! example "Worked example: lowering the first stage of flap in level flight"
    Given: flying level at 80 kt, below VFE, in a high-wing trainer. You select the first stage of flap.

    1. Lift increases at the same angle of attack: the aeroplane **balloons** upward and the nose pitches up.
    2. To hold height, **lower the nose** slightly: the flapped wing needs a smaller angle of attack for the same lift.
    3. Drag has increased, so **add a little power** to hold 80 kt.
    4. Hold the new attitude, let the speed settle, then **re-trim**.

    The aeroplane is now flying level at the same speed with a lower nose, a lower stall speed and a higher power setting than before.

Now the reverse, before you open the solution. You are going around from a low approach with full flap down and full power set. What happens each time you raise a stage of flap, and what do you do?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    Each stage raised reduces lift at the same angle of attack, so the aeroplane **sinks**, and the **stall speed rises**, so your margin above the stall shrinks. Raise the flap in stages, at a safe speed and height, with climb power already set. As each stage comes up, raise the nose a little to restore the angle of attack, let the speed build, and re-trim.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Raising **full flap at low speed close to the ground** is dangerous: lift drops and the stall speed rises at the same moment. Raise flap in stages, at a safe speed and height, after climb power is set.

## Bringing it together

Back to the go-around at Jandakot. You are on final at 65 kt with full flap, trimmed nose-up, low power, and a vehicle moves onto the runway. Full power: the slipstream floods the tail, the elevator and rudder come alive, the nose pitches up hard and yaws left. You hold the attitude with firm forward pressure and keep straight with right rudder, because you expected exactly that. The ailerons are still soft, so you keep the wings level with larger movements than usual and rudder to balance each one. Positive climb: raise the first stage of flap, the aeroplane sinks a little and the stall speed rises, so you raise the nose slightly and let the speed build. Then the rest of the flap in stages. With the speed settled at the climb speed, you trim out the forward pressure, and the aeroplane climbs away hands-off.

## Key points

- **3.2.1** Elevator: pitch about the lateral axis, then airspeed changes (nose up, slower). Ailerons: roll about the longitudinal axis, then adverse yaw away from the roll, then yaw towards the lower wing as it slips. Rudder: yaw about the normal axis, then roll the same way. Uncorrected, both aileron and rudder lead to a spiral descent; rudder with aileron gives balanced flight.
- **3.2.2** Controls are more effective and heavier at high airspeed, weaker and sloppy at low airspeed. Power puts slipstream over the elevator and rudder, not the ailerons. More power typically pitches the nose up and yaws it left; less power pitches it down. A trimmed aeroplane tends to return to its trimmed speed.
- **3.2.3** Trim removes sustained control pressure so the aeroplane holds the selected attitude hands-off, reducing workload and fatigue. Attitude first, then trim; trim is not a primary control.
- **3.2.4** Lowering flap increases lift and drag, lowers the stall speed and needs a lower nose attitude at the same IAS (many high-wing trainers balloon first). Raising flap reduces lift and drag, raises the stall speed, causes a sink and needs a higher nose attitude.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Expect an axis question (ailerons: **longitudinal**, elevator: **lateral**, rudder: **normal**), a further-effect question (rudder gives roll the same way; aileron gives **adverse yaw** away from the roll first), a slipstream question (the **ailerons** are least helped by power), a power-change question (more power, nose **up**), a trim-purpose question (to **relieve sustained control pressure**, not to fly the aeroplane) and a flap question (lowering flap: lift and drag **increase**, nose **lower** at the same IAS; raising flap: **sink**, nose higher).
