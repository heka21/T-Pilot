## What the exam expects (RBKA 3.5)

- **3.5.1** Describe a balanced turn.
- **3.5.2** Describe the terms "g", wing loading and load factor.
- **3.5.3** State the effect of increasing bank angle in a level turn on (a) the stall IAS, including how fast it rises with bank, and (b) the load factor on the structure and the risk of damage if limits are exceeded.
- **3.5.4** List reasons for avoiding steep turns shortly after take-off and during a glide, particularly on approach.
- **3.5.5** Explain why an aeroplane in balanced level turns at low level may appear to slip or skid when turning downwind or into wind.
- **3.5.6** Given the level-flight stall speed, determine the stall speed and load factor at 45 and 60 degrees of bank.

## Why this matters

You turn on every flight: onto crosswind after leaving Jandakot, onto base and final, and in the 45 and 60 degree steep turns of the training area. A turn is also where most stall-spin accidents start, because it quietly raises the stall speed while the nose and the airspeed look normal. Knowing where the extra lift comes from tells you why you need back pressure and power, why the stall speed climbs with bank, why the structure has a limit, and why a low turn in a strong wind can fool your eyes. The exam asks for the two load factors by heart, a stall-speed calculation, the ball, and why turns stay shallow low down.

## How an aeroplane turns: tilting the lift

Anything that moves in a circle needs a sideways force towards the centre of the curve. A car gets it from its tyres gripping the road; without it the car carries straight on. That inward force is the **centripetal force**. An aeroplane has no road to push against: the only large force it can point sideways is the lift from its wings.

So you bank. Lift always acts at right angles to the wings, so when the wings tilt, lift tilts with them. Now lift has two jobs, and it helps to split it into two parts. The **vertical component** points straight up and holds the aeroplane up against its weight. The **horizontal component** points towards the centre of the turn and is the centripetal force that curves the flight path. Bank more and the horizontal part grows, so the aeroplane turns harder.

![Why does a level turn need more lift than level flight?](widget:bank-angle "The vertical part of lift always equals weight; tilting lift means total lift, and so load factor, must grow with bank.")

Here is the consequence that drives the whole lesson. If you simply roll into a bank and do nothing else, lift has the same size as before but is now tilted, so its vertical part is *smaller* than the weight. The aeroplane starts to sink. To hold height, the vertical component must still equal the weight, which means the total lift must become **larger than the weight**. The steeper the bank, the more the lift is tilted, and the larger the total lift has to be to keep the same vertical part.

How do you get more lift at the same airspeed? In the lift formula ([BAKC 4.2](/notes/BAKC/4.2)) the only thing you can change at a fixed speed is the lift coefficient, by raising the **angle of attack**. That is the **back pressure** you hold in every level turn.

### Why a turn needs more power

More lift costs drag. **Induced drag**, the drag that comes with making lift, grows with the square of the lift at a given speed, so in a 60 degree level turn, with twice the lift, it is roughly four times as large. Leave the throttle alone and the aeroplane slows. In a gentle turn the loss is a few knots; in a steep turn you must **add power** to hold the speed, and in a very steep turn even full power may not be enough, so the aeroplane must descend to hold speed.

### Rate and radius: what bank and speed do to the turn

Two numbers describe how hard you are turning. The **rate of turn** is how many degrees of heading you change each second. The **radius of turn** is the size of the circle you fly. Both depend on only two things, bank angle and true airspeed:

- **More bank** gives a bigger horizontal force for the same aeroplane, so the turn is **faster and tighter**: higher rate, smaller radius.
- **More speed** at the same bank means the aeroplane is harder to bend round, so the turn is **slower and wider**: lower rate, bigger radius. The radius grows with the square of the speed, so doubling the speed makes the circle four times as wide.

To give you a feel for the sizes, at 90 kt the radius is about 380 m at 30 degrees of bank, about 220 m at 45 degrees and about 125 m at 60 degrees. A full 360 degree turn at 45 degrees and 90 kt takes about 30 seconds. Weight does not appear: a heavy and a light aeroplane at the same speed and bank fly the same circle.

![How do bank and speed change the size of the turn?](diagram:turn-radius-vs-bank-and-speed "At 90 kt about 380 m at 30 degrees, 220 m at 45 and 125 m at 60; at 120 kt every circle is about 1.78 times as wide.")

![How do bank, lift, rate of turn and the ball fit together in a turn?](widget:balanced-turn "A steeper bank gives a bigger horizontal part of lift, so the aeroplane turns faster and tighter; the ball shows whether yaw matches bank.")

??? check "Check yourself: you roll into a 45 degree bank and change nothing else. What happens, and what two inputs prevent it?"
    The lift is tilted, so its vertical component is now less than the weight and the aeroplane descends. Back pressure raises the angle of attack so total lift grows until its vertical part equals the weight again, and extra power replaces the speed lost to the higher induced drag.

## The balanced turn

Banking curves the flight path, but the nose also has to swing round at the same rate as the path, which is yaw. In a well-flown turn the tail follows the nose exactly along the curve and the airflow meets the aeroplane from dead ahead. That is a **balanced turn** (also called a **coordinated turn**): the rate of yaw matches the angle of bank, there is no sideways airflow over the fuselage, and the passengers are pressed straight down into their seats rather than sideways.

Ailerons alone do not give a balanced turn. As you roll in, the down-going aileron makes more drag than the up-going one and yaws the nose out of the turn: **adverse yaw** ([RBKA 3.2](/notes/RBKA/3.2)), cured by rudder in the direction of the turn as you roll. Once the bank is set, most light aeroplanes need only a little rudder to stay balanced.

### Slip and skid, and the ball

When yaw and bank do not match, the aeroplane moves partly sideways through the air. There are two ways to get it wrong.

A **slip** is too much bank for the rate of yaw (usually not enough rudder). The tilted lift pulls the aeroplane inwards faster than the nose is coming round, so it slides sideways and down **towards the inside of the turn**, the low wing.

A **skid** is too much rudder for the bank. The nose is being yawed round faster than the bank can curve the path, so the aeroplane slides **towards the outside of the turn**, like a car whose rear end steps out on a wet corner.

You detect both with the **balance ball**, a ball in a curved, liquid-filled tube that responds to the sideways force you feel in your seat. In a balanced turn it sits in the centre. In a slip it falls to the inside, the low wing side; in a skid it is flung to the outside, the high wing side. The correction is the same for both: **step on the ball**, meaning press the rudder pedal on the side the ball has gone to.

![Which way is the ball, and which rudder fixes it?](diagram:slip-and-skid-ball "In a slip the ball sits on the inside (low wing), in a skid on the outside: step on the rudder on the ball's side.")

| Condition | Cause | Ball | Correction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Slip | Too much bank, too little rudder | Inside (low wing) | Rudder towards the low wing, or less bank |
| Balanced | Yaw matches bank | Centred | None |
| Skid | Too much rudder for the bank | Outside (high wing) | Less rudder in the turn direction |

Near the ground a skid is the worse error: the inside wing moves more slowly and is partly blanketed by the fuselage, so at the stall it drops first, towards the ground ([RBKA 3.6](/notes/RBKA/3.6)).

### The overbanking tendency

Once established, the aeroplane does not always stay at the bank you set. The outer wing flies around a bigger circle than the inner wing in the same time, so it moves faster through the air and makes a little more lift. That lift difference tries to roll the aeroplane further into the turn. In a shallow turn the aeroplane's natural lateral stability is stronger and it tends to roll back towards level, so you hold a little aileron into the turn. In a medium turn the two roughly cancel and the aeroplane stays where you put it. In a steep turn the outer wing's extra speed wins and the bank tends to keep increasing, the **overbanking tendency**, so you hold a little aileron *against* the turn. Left alone, a steep turn steepens, the nose drops and it begins to wind into a spiral dive.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The exam's distractor for a slip is "the ball is displaced to the outside". In a slip the ball falls to the **inside**, towards the low wing, because the aeroplane is sliding down and inwards. The rule "step on the ball" works for both cases, so learn where the ball goes and the correction follows.

??? check "Check yourself: in a left turn the ball is out to the right. Slip or skid, and what do you do?"
    A skid: the ball is on the outside of the turn, so there is too much left rudder for the bank. Step on the ball, which here means easing off the left rudder (adding right rudder pressure) until the ball centres.

## g, load factor and wing loading

You have already met the idea under another name. When the wing makes more lift than the aeroplane weighs, everything in the aeroplane is pressed down harder: your body into the seat, the engine onto its mounts, the wings upwards against their attachments. The number that measures this is the **load factor**, written $n$:

$$n = \frac{\text{lift}}{\text{weight}}$$

In straight and level flight, lift equals weight, so $n = 1$. In a level turn at bank angle $\theta$, the vertical component of lift is $L\cos\theta$, and it must equal the weight. Rearranging gives the formula the exam uses:

$$n = \frac{1}{\cos\theta}$$

**g** is the same number expressed as multiples of the force of gravity. "Pulling 2 g" means a load factor of 2: you, your passenger and every part of the structure feel twice as heavy as on the ground, and an 80 kg pilot presses into the seat as if they weighed 160 kg ([PHFC 2.9](/notes/PHFC/2.9) covers g effects on the body).

**Wing loading** is weight divided by wing area, for example kilograms per square metre. A 1,000 kg aeroplane with a 16 square metre wing has a wing loading of 62.5 kg per square metre in level flight. In a manoeuvre the wing supports $n$ times the weight, so at 2 g it carries the equivalent of 125 kg on every square metre. A more heavily loaded wing needs a higher angle of attack or a higher speed to make its lift, which links load factor to the stall.

??? check "Check yourself: what is the load factor in straight and level flight, and in a level 60 degree turn?"
    1 in level flight, because lift equals weight. In a level 60 degree turn it is $1/\cos 60° = 1/0.5 = 2$: the wing must make twice the aeroplane's weight in lift so that its vertical half still equals the weight.

## Bank angle and the stall speed

Now link this to [RBKA 3.6](/notes/RBKA/3.6). A wing stalls at its critical angle of attack, and the level-flight stall speed is the speed at which the wing, at that angle, makes lift equal to weight. In a turn the wing must make $n$ times the weight. At the critical angle it can only do that by flying faster. Because lift grows with the square of the speed, the speed only needs to rise by the **square root** of the load factor:

$$V_{s,\text{turn}} = V_{s,\text{level}} \times \sqrt{n}$$

The shape of this curve is what the syllabus means by "the rate of increase of stall speed with bank". At small bank angles the cosine barely changes, so the load factor, and with it the stall speed, rises slowly: 30 degrees adds only about 7 percent. Beyond 45 degrees the cosine falls away quickly towards zero and the load factor races upwards: from 60 to 75 degrees the stall speed rises by more than it did from 0 to 60. A level turn at 90 degrees of bank is impossible, because no amount of lift tilted on its side can hold the aeroplane up.

![How fast do load factor and stall speed rise with bank?](diagram:stall-speed-vs-bank "Both curves are gentle to 45 degrees and then climb steeply: 60 degrees doubles the load factor, and by 75 degrees the load factor passes the +3.8 g normal-category limit.")

| Bank | Load factor $n$ | $\sqrt{n}$ | Increase in stall speed |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0° | 1.00 | 1.00 | 0 percent |
| 30° | 1.15 | 1.07 | about 7 percent |
| 45° | **1.41** | 1.19 | about **19 percent** |
| 60° | **2.00** | 1.41 | about **41 percent** |
| 75° | 3.86 | 1.97 | about 97 percent |

!!! example "Worked example: stall speed and load factor at 45 and 60 degrees"
    Given: level-flight stall speed 50 kt.

    1. Load factor at 45 degrees: $n = 1/\cos 45° = 1/0.707 = \mathbf{1.41}$.
    2. Stall speed at 45 degrees: $50 \times \sqrt{1.41} = 50 \times 1.19 = \mathbf{59.5\ kt}$, about 60 kt.
    3. Load factor at 60 degrees: $n = 1/\cos 60° = 1/0.5 = \mathbf{2.0}$.
    4. Stall speed at 60 degrees: $50 \times \sqrt{2} = 50 \times 1.41 = \mathbf{70.7\ kt}$, about 71 kt.

    If you fly a 60 degree steep turn at 80 kt, your margin over the stall has shrunk from 30 kt to about 9 kt. A little too much back pressure takes the rest.

Try this one before opening the solution: an aeroplane's level-flight stall speed is 58 kt. Find the load factor and stall speed at 45 and at 60 degrees of bank.

??? solution "Show the solution"
    At 45 degrees: $n = 1/\cos 45° = 1.41$; $\sqrt{1.41} = 1.19$; $58 \times 1.19 = 69.0$, so **69 kt** at **1.41 g**.

    At 60 degrees: $n = 1/\cos 60° = 2.0$; $\sqrt{2} = 1.414$; $58 \times 1.414 = 82.0$, so **82 kt** at **2 g**.

    The 45 degree turn added 11 kt to the stall speed; the extra 15 degrees to 60 added another 13 kt. The penalty grows faster than the bank.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The load factor at 60 degrees is 2, but the stall speed does **not** double: it rises by the square root of 2, about 41 percent. Options of "doubles" or "100 percent" are written for candidates who forget the square root. The opposite slip is to multiply by the load factor at 45 degrees (1.41) instead of its square root (1.19).

## Bank angle and the structure

The wing does not care whether the extra lift is for a turn, a pull-up or a gust: the spars, attachments and skin carry $n$ times the weight. The designer builds the airframe for a **limit load factor**, the greatest load it must carry in service without any permanent deformation. For light aeroplanes the usual certification limits are **+3.8 g for the normal category, +4.4 g for the utility category and +6 g for the aerobatic category** (check the POH for your aircraft: it states the category and the limits, and some aeroplanes are certified in more than one category at different weights).

Above the limit lies a safety margin. The **ultimate load** is normally **1.5 times the limit load**, 5.7 g for a normal-category aeroplane. Between limit and ultimate the structure may **deform permanently** (wrinkled skin, bent ribs or spars, popped rivets); beyond ultimate it may fail. A bent wing may look fine from the ground and fail later at a lower load.

![How much g can each category take?](diagram:category-load-limits "Limit loads +3.8, +4.4 and +6 g; the ultimate load is 1.5 times the limit. A 75 degree level turn already pulls about 3.86 g, past the normal-category limit.")

Put the turn formula against those limits. A level turn at 60 degrees is 2 g, comfortably inside. At 75 degrees the load factor is $1/\cos 75° = 1/0.259 = 3.86$, already over the normal-category limit of 3.8 g. An over-banked level turn can reach the limit load without feeling violent, and a hard pull out of a spiral dive can exceed it in a second. A suspected overstress must be reported and the aeroplane inspected before it flies again. The full flight envelope, and why the manoeuvring speed protects you, is in [RBKA 3.8](/notes/RBKA/3.8).

??? check "Check yourself: a pilot pulls 4.5 g in a normal-category aeroplane and lands safely. Why can the aeroplane not simply fly again?"
    4.5 g is above the 3.8 g limit load but below the 5.7 g ultimate load, so the structure probably did not break but may have been permanently deformed. Hidden damage could fail at a lower load later, so it must be reported and inspected before further flight.

## Why steep turns are avoided low down

Everything above comes together near the ground, where you have the least height to recover from a mistake.

### Shortly after take-off

After take-off the aeroplane is slow, near its best rate of climb speed $V_y$ or best angle speed $V_x$. A steep turn raises the stall speed towards that climb speed, so the margin shrinks to a few knots. The turn also costs climb: tilted lift is not holding the aeroplane up, and the extra induced drag uses power that would otherwise climb, so obstacles may not be cleared. You are low, so a stall, a wing drop or the start of a spin leaves no height to recover. If the engine fails in a steep turn the nose must come down at once, and the instinctive steep, slow turn back to the runway has killed many pilots. You may also meet the wake turbulence of the aeroplane that departed ahead. So climb straight ahead to a safe height and keep climbing turns shallow, as your instructor and the POH direct.

### In a glide, especially on approach

In a glide you have no power to add. When you bank, the vertical component of lift falls, and the only way to hold the airspeed is to lower the nose, so the **rate of descent increases**, sharply as the bank steepens. On approach you have little height to give away. The gliding speed is already low, so the raised stall speed of the turn eats into a small margin. And an overshooting base-to-final turn tempts the wrong fix: rudder to hurry the nose round (a skid) and back pressure to hold the nose up (more load factor), the stall-spin chain of [RBKA 3.6](/notes/RBKA/3.6). Accept the overshoot, correct gently, or go around.

??? check "Check yourself: why does the rate of descent increase in a gliding turn, even when you hold the same airspeed?"
    In a bank only the vertical component of lift opposes the weight, so it is smaller than in a straight glide. Without power to make up the difference, the aeroplane descends faster, and the steeper the bank the larger the increase.

## Low-level turns in a strong wind: slip and skid that are not there

The aeroplane flies in the **air mass**. If the whole body of air is moving at 25 kt from the west, the aeroplane is carried along with it, and a turn that is balanced relative to the air (ball centred, airspeed steady) is a perfectly normal turn whatever the wind is doing. The wind does not change the aerodynamics of the turn at all.

What the wind changes is how the aeroplane moves over the ground. At low level, say 500 ft along the coast near Lancelin in a strong sea breeze, you see the ground sliding past, and the drift produces two convincing illusions.

**Turning from into wind to downwind.** As you swing through the crosswind part of the turn, the wind is on the outside of the turn and blows the aeroplane sideways towards the inside of it. Against the ground, the aeroplane seems to be **slipping inwards**. At the same time the groundspeed rises from airspeed minus the wind to airspeed plus the wind, so the ground appears to rush past faster and faster.

**Turning from downwind to into wind.** Now the wind is on the inside of the turn during the crosswind part and drifts the aeroplane outwards. Against the ground it seems to be **skidding outwards**, and the groundspeed falls, so the aeroplane feels slow.

The danger is reacting to what you see. On the turn downwind, the rushing ground tempts you to raise the nose and to add bottom rudder against the apparent slip. Raising the nose bleeds real airspeed towards a stall speed the turn has already raised; the rudder turns an apparent slip into a real skid. Together they stall the inside wing at low level. On the turn into wind, the feeling of slowness may make you lower the nose towards the ground. Fly the turn by the **airspeed indicator and the balance ball**, which measure what the air is doing to the aeroplane.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The exam asks what the pilot should do when a low-level downwind turn appears to slip. The answer is nothing different: if the ball is centred and the airspeed is correct, the turn is balanced. Options that add rudder or raise the nose describe the illusion working.

## Bringing it together

You are practising steep turns over the Mundijong training area in an aeroplane with a level-flight stall speed of 50 kt and a POH limit of +3.8 g. You roll into 60 degrees at 90 kt with rudder against adverse yaw, then power. The load factor is 2, the stall speed about 71 kt, so you have 19 kt in hand, and the induced drag has roughly quadrupled. Halfway round the bank creeps towards 70 degrees and the nose drops: the overbanking tendency. You check it with opposite aileron rather than pulling harder, because at 75 degrees a level turn already passes 3.8 g. The ball drifts outward, a skid, so you ease off the inside rudder. Back in the Jandakot circuit the same physics demands gentle turns after take-off and onto final, with the ball and the ASI, not the rushing ground, as your references in the sea breeze.

## Key points

- **3.5.1** A balanced turn has the yaw matching the bank, no sideways airflow and the ball centred. A slip puts the ball on the inside (too much bank), a skid on the outside (too much rudder): step on the ball.
- **3.5.2** Load factor $n$ is lift divided by weight; "g" expresses it as multiples of gravity; wing loading is weight per unit of wing area and effectively rises with load factor. In a level turn $n = 1/\cos\theta$.
- **3.5.3** As bank increases, the stall IAS rises with $\sqrt{n}$, slowly at first and steeply beyond 45 degrees; the structure carries $n$ times the weight, and beyond the limit (+3.8 g normal, +4.4 g utility, +6 g aerobatic; check the POH) it may deform, beyond 1.5 times the limit it may fail.
- **3.5.4** Avoid steep turns after take-off (slow, raised stall speed, reduced climb, no height to recover, engine failure) and in a glide (rate of descent rises sharply, small speed margin, the skidding final turn).
- **3.5.5** In a strong wind at low level, a balanced turn appears to slip when turning downwind and skid when turning into wind, because of drift over the ground. Fly by the ASI and the ball.
- **3.5.6** At 45 degrees: $n = 1.41$, stall speed times 1.19. At 60 degrees: $n = 2$, stall speed times 1.41.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Learn the two numbers: **45 degrees: load factor 1.41, stall speed up 19 percent. 60 degrees: load factor 2, stall speed up 41 percent.** Multiply the level-flight stall speed by 1.19 or 1.41. Expect questions on the ball (slip inside, skid outside), the structural limit (+3.8 g normal category, permanent deformation above it), steep turns low down, and the apparent slip when turning downwind, where the answer is to trust the instruments.
