## What the exam expects (RBKA 2.1)

- **2.1.1** Describe how you use a manual mixture control on a piston engine driving a fixed-pitch propeller: when to lean, how to lean by RPM, and when to enrich.
- **2.1.2** State how engine (carburettor) icing shows itself in an aeroplane with a fixed-pitch propeller, and how carburettor heat confirms and clears it.

## Why this matters

On a flight from Jandakot to Northam at 6,500 ft the engine is working in air about a fifth thinner than at the aerodrome, and unless you move the red knob it is burning fuel as if it were still at sea level. That costs you endurance you may have planned on, and it fouls the spark plugs. On the way home, a humid winter morning over the Darling Scarp and a long low-power descent are exactly the conditions that grow ice in the carburettor, with the outside air at 15 degrees. Both problems announce themselves on the same instrument, the tachometer, and both are cured by a control you move with one hand. This is the operating side of the engine you met in [BAKC 3.1](/notes/BAKC/3.1) and [BAKC 3.4](/notes/BAKC/3.4); the exam asks what you do with the mixture in each phase of flight, what the RPM does as you lean, and how to read the RPM when you pull the carburettor heat.

## Why a fixed-pitch propeller makes the tachometer your power gauge

Both elements of this subtopic say "fixed-pitch propeller", and the reason is worth getting straight before anything else. A **fixed-pitch propeller** has blades set at one angle, bolted to the crankshaft. The engine and the propeller turn together, and the only way for the propeller to turn faster is for the engine to make more power, or for the load on the propeller to fall (as it does when you lower the nose and speed up).

So at a steady airspeed, **RPM rises and falls directly with engine power**. Anything that changes the power the engine makes, whether it is the mixture, ice in the carburettor or hot air from the carburettor heat, shows up as a change in RPM. On an aeroplane with a constant-speed propeller the governor would hold the RPM constant and the same changes would show on the manifold pressure gauge instead. That is a PPL topic; in this lesson the tachometer is the instrument that tells you everything.

The one condition: hold the airspeed constant. Let the nose drop while leaning and the RPM rises because you have sped up, not because the mixture is better, so leaning and carburettor heat checks are done in steady, trimmed flight.

## What the mixture control is for

A piston engine burns a mixture of fuel and air, and it runs best within a narrow band of fuel/air ratio, roughly **1:15 by weight** for complete combustion. Too much fuel for the air (a **rich** mixture) and some of the fuel goes out of the exhaust unburnt. Too little (a **lean** mixture) and the burn is slow, hot and eventually ragged.

The carburettor meters fuel by the **volume** of air flowing through its venturi: the pressure drop in the venturi draws fuel from the float chamber in proportion to the airflow. It is set up so that with the mixture control fully forward, **full rich**, the engine gets a slightly rich mixture at sea level at high power. The extra fuel is deliberate. Fuel that evaporates in the cylinder absorbs heat, so a rich mixture helps keep the cylinders cool when the engine is working hardest, at take-off and in the climb.

Now climb. The air gets thinner: each litre that passes through the venturi carries less oxygen by weight. But the carburettor is still metering fuel by volume, so it delivers roughly the same amount of fuel into air that weighs less. The mixture becomes **richer** with height. By the time you are cruising at 6,500 ft, a full-rich mixture is well richer than the engine needs.

![What happens as you lean the mixture from full rich?](diagram:mixture-rich-to-lean "Power peaks near the correct mixture and falls away on both sides; too rich runs cool, wastes fuel and fouls plugs, too lean runs hot and invites detonation.")

An over-rich mixture has symptoms you can learn to recognise: slightly rough running, a loss of power (a lower RPM than the throttle setting should give), high fuel consumption, cooler-than-normal temperatures, and sooty, fouled spark plugs that show up later as a rough magneto check. The cure is to reduce the fuel flow so that the ratio is right again. That is **leaning**, and the **mixture control** (usually a red knob or lever) is the device that does it, by restricting the fuel reaching the main jet.

Leaning is not just about economy: a correctly leaned engine runs smoother and keeps its plugs clean. The POH cruise fuel flows that your flight plan uses for endurance normally assume a leaned engine.

??? check "Check yourself: you climb from a sea-level aerodrome to 7,500 ft without touching the mixture. Is the mixture now too rich or too lean, and why?"
    Too rich. The carburettor meters fuel by the volume of air passing through it, and at 7,500 ft each volume of air weighs less, so the same fuel meets less air by weight. You lean to restore the right ratio.

## When to lean, and when to go back to full rich

The general pattern follows from two ideas: the mixture gets richer as the air thins, and the engine needs extra fuel for cooling at high power. Your POH sets the actual rules for your type, and where it differs from the table below, the POH wins.

| Phase | Mixture | The reason |
|---|---|---|
| Start, taxi, run-up | Full rich (some POHs allow leaning for taxi at high-elevation aerodromes) | Cool, reliable running; the magneto and carburettor heat checks are done at the setting you will take off with |
| Take-off and climb at high power | Full rich, unless the POH permits leaning at high density altitude | The extra fuel cools the cylinders at high power |
| Cruise at or below the POH limit for leaning (commonly 75% power) | **Leaned** by the method below | The mixture has become rich with height; lean to restore it |
| Descent | Enrich progressively as you descend | The air gets denser, so a leaned mixture becomes leaner still |
| Pre-landing checks | Full rich | Ready for a go-around at full power, when you need the cooling fuel |
| Shutdown | Idle cut-off (fully back) | Stops the engine with no fuel left in the cylinders |

Two rows deserve a word. The **descent** is the mirror image of the climb: as the air thickens, the same metered fuel meets more air, so a mixture leaned for 6,500 ft becomes progressively **leaner** as you come down. If you do nothing, by the time you add power in the circuit it may be lean enough to run rough or overheat. So you enrich in steps as you descend, and go to **full rich** in the pre-landing checks. A go-around is the moment the engine goes to full power in a hurry, and it needs the full-rich mixture for that.

At **shutdown**, pulling the mixture fully back to **idle cut-off** stops fuel at the jet, so the engine runs the cylinders dry and stops with nothing left to fire if someone moves the propeller.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The exam reverses the direction: "as the aeroplane climbs the mixture becomes leaner". It does not. Less dense air with the same fuel gives a **richer** mixture, so you **lean** in the climb or cruise and **enrich** in the descent. If you remember only one direction, remember "thin air, rich mixture".

## Leaning by RPM, step by step

With a fixed-pitch propeller and no exhaust gas temperature gauge, the RPM method is how you find the right mixture. It works because engine power, and so RPM, peaks at the best-power mixture and falls away on both sides of it. You are using the tachometer to find the top of the curve in the diagram above.

Here is the procedure, with what the engine is doing at each step.

1. **Establish cruise.** Set the altitude, set cruise power with the throttle (at or below the POH limit for leaning, commonly 75 percent), and let the speed settle and trim. The engine is running rich and the RPM is a little lower than it could be. You need a steady airspeed so that every RPM change from here is caused by the mixture.
2. **Move the mixture control slowly towards lean**, watching the tachometer. As fuel flow falls, the surplus fuel that was cooling the burn and going out unburnt is removed. The mixture moves towards the best-power ratio, combustion improves, and the **RPM rises slightly**.
3. **Notice the peak.** The RPM reaches a **maximum** and stops rising. The engine is now at about the best-power mixture: the most power for this throttle setting.
4. **Keep going a fraction further and watch for the fall.** Leaner than the peak, there is no longer enough fuel for all the oxygen. The burn slows, power falls, the **RPM drops** and the engine begins to **run rough** as some cylinders, which never get exactly the same mixture as the others, start to misfire.
5. **Enrich at the first sign of RPM drop or roughness**, until the RPM is back at the peak and the engine is smooth, then **a little richer** as your POH directs. This is "rich of peak": you give up a sliver of economy to keep a margin against running lean, because the cylinders furthest from the carburettor run leaner than the average.
6. **Re-lean after any significant change of power or altitude.** The right setting depends on both. A climb needs further leaning; a descent needs enrichment.

![What does the tachometer do as you lean the mixture?](diagram:leaning-by-rpm "RPM rises from 2350 to a peak of 2400, then falls and the engine roughens: come back to the peak and a little richer.")

If an **exhaust gas temperature (EGT) gauge** is fitted, the POH method is usually to lean to peak EGT and then enrich by a specified number of degrees: the same idea of finding the peak and stepping back to the rich side.

!!! example "Worked example: leaning in the cruise at 6,500 ft"
    Given: cruising at 6,500 ft at a steady airspeed, full rich, tachometer reading 2350 RPM.

    1. Lean slowly: the RPM rises from 2350 to **2400**.
    2. Keep leaning: the RPM stops rising at **2400** (the peak), then drops back to **2370** and the engine roughens.
    3. Enrich: the RPM comes back to about **2400** and the engine is smooth.
    4. Enrich slightly further, as the POH directs: the engine is now **just rich of peak**.

    The aeroplane now burns noticeably less fuel per hour than at full rich, which your flight plan may already assume, and the engine is running smoothly with a margin against overheating.

Now picture the same engine an hour later. You have descended from 6,500 ft to 3,500 ft for the arrival without touching the mixture, and the engine is running a little rough with the RPM slightly lower than usual for the throttle setting. Before opening the solution, decide what has happened and what you do.

??? solution "Show the solution"
    The mixture was leaned for the thinner air at 6,500 ft. Descending into denser air, the same fuel flow now meets more air by weight, so the mixture has become **too lean**: past the peak on the lean side, which is why the RPM is down and the engine is rough. Enrich: move the mixture control forward until the engine is smooth and the RPM is back up, then re-lean for 3,500 ft if you are cruising there, or go full rich if you are about to join the circuit.

### What happens if you get it wrong

**Too lean at high power** is the dangerous one. A lean mixture burns hotter, and at high power the cylinders are already hot. Cylinder head temperature climbs, and the unburnt part of the charge can explode all at once instead of burning smoothly from the spark plug: **detonation**. It hammers the piston, shows as rough running and a sharp rise in cylinder head temperature, and can damage the engine within a short time. This is why you never lean on a climb at high power unless the POH says so, and why the mixture goes full rich before a go-around.

![How does detonation differ from normal combustion?](diagram:detonation-vs-normal-combustion "A lean mixture at high power lets the charge explode instead of burning smoothly; the remedy is to enrich, reduce power and cool the engine.")

**Too lean at any power** gives rough running and a loss of power, and eventually the engine stops. **Too rich** wastes fuel, reducing your **endurance** below what you planned, and fouls the spark plugs, which shows up as a rough magneto check.

??? check "Check yourself: while leaning by RPM, the tachometer rises, peaks, then starts to fall and the engine roughens. What is the engine telling you at each stage?"
    The rise means surplus fuel is being removed and the mixture is approaching the best-power ratio. The peak is that best-power mixture. The fall and roughness mean the mixture is now too lean, so you enrich back to the peak and slightly richer, as the POH directs.

## How carburettor ice forms

Now the second element: ice in the engine's induction system, and how you know it is there. Start with where it forms.

Air entering the carburettor speeds up through the **venturi**, a narrowing of the passage. Faster air has lower pressure, and lower pressure means lower temperature. At the same place, fuel sprays into the airflow and evaporates, and evaporation takes heat from the air around it (the same reason a wet hand feels cold in a breeze). Together, the pressure drop and the fuel evaporation can cool the air by **up to about 20 to 30 degrees**. If the air going in is humid, the moisture in it condenses and freezes on the walls of the venturi and on the **throttle butterfly**, the valve above the venturi that your throttle lever opens and closes.

![Where does ice form inside the carburettor?](diagram:carburettor-ice-locations "Throttle ice on the partly closed butterfly is the most common and most dangerous; fuel evaporation and the pressure drop together cool the air by 20 to 30 degrees.")

That cooling is why **carburettor ice forms on warm days**. With the outside air at 20 degrees, the air in the venturi can be below freezing. The risk is highest in **high humidity**: visible moisture, rain, or a small spread between temperature and dew point. A humid morning after rain, or flying near cloud, can produce ice across a wide range of outside temperatures, **roughly −10 to +30 degrees**. Fuel-injected engines have no venturi or butterfly of this kind and do not get carburettor ice, which is why this element is specific to carburettor engines.

The risk also depends on throttle position. At **low power**, during a descent or a glide approach, the butterfly is nearly closed. The gap the air squeezes through is small, the local pressure drop is greatest, and a thin layer of ice blocks a large share of a small opening. Low power is also when the engine produces least heat around the carburettor. That is why the descent and the approach are the classic times for carburettor ice.

## How ice shows itself with a fixed-pitch propeller

Ice in the carburettor narrows the passage the air has to flow through. It does exactly what closing the throttle would do: less mixture reaches the cylinders and the engine makes less power. It also disturbs the mixture, because the fuel nozzle and the venturi no longer have the shape they were designed with. With a fixed-pitch propeller, those changes give you these indications, usually in this order:

1. **A gradual, unexplained drop in RPM**, with the throttle not moved. This is the first sign, and it is easy to miss because it is slow: a few tens of RPM over several minutes.
2. **A loss of performance.** If you are holding altitude, the **airspeed falls**. If you are holding airspeed, the aeroplane **loses altitude**, or the rate of climb falls.
3. **Rough running**, as the ice disturbs the mixture. Left alone, this progresses towards engine stoppage.

With a constant-speed propeller the first sign would be a drop in manifold pressure instead, because the governor holds the RPM. That is the reason the element says "fixed pitch".

The danger is in the first item. A pilot who notices the RPM has crept down often pushes the throttle forward to put it back. The RPM recovers for a while, but the ice keeps growing behind a more open butterfly. Repeat that a few times and the throttle is fully open with nothing in reserve.

![Why does opening the throttle hide carburettor ice?](diagram:chasing-the-rpm "Each extra bit of throttle buys the RPM back while the ice keeps building; at full throttle there is nothing left, and the engine stops.")

!!! warning "Common trap"
    An unexplained RPM drop in a fixed-pitch aeroplane is **carburettor ice until proven otherwise**. The wrong answer is "increase the throttle to restore the RPM": that hides the symptom while the ice grows. The right answer is full carburettor heat.

??? check "Check yourself: you are cruising at constant throttle on a humid morning and notice the RPM has slowly fallen by about 100. You are holding altitude. What else would you expect to see, and what do you suspect?"
    A falling airspeed, because the engine is producing less power and you are holding height. Possibly some rough running. A gradual, unexplained RPM drop at constant throttle in humid air is the classic first sign of carburettor ice.

## Carburettor heat: confirming and clearing the ice

The **carburettor heat** control replaces the normal filtered intake air with hot air taken from a muff around the exhaust. The hot air melts ice that has formed and stops more forming. It also does two other things that are the key to reading the tachometer: hot air is **less dense**, so the engine makes **less power**; and the same fuel meets less air by weight, so the mixture becomes **richer**. Both make the RPM fall when you apply heat, whether or not there is any ice.

That means applying full heat always gives an RPM drop. What tells you whether there was ice is what the RPM does next, and what it does when you return to cold. This is a decision you make by watching the tachometer through three stages.

![Ice or no ice: how does the RPM tell you after you apply full heat?](diagram:carburettor-heat-decision "Ice: a drop, roughness, a rise while the heat is on, and higher RPM than before when you return to cold. No ice: a small drop that stays, then back to the original.")

| | Ice present | No ice |
|---|---|---|
| Immediately on applying full heat | RPM drops (less dense air, richer mixture) | RPM drops slightly |
| While the heat stays on | RPM then **rises** as the ice melts; the engine may run rough briefly as the water passes through | RPM stays at the lower value |
| On returning to cold | RPM rises to **higher than before** heat was applied | RPM returns to the original value |

Work through the "ice present" column and every row has a reason. The first drop is the hot air itself. The roughness is the melted ice going through the engine as water: unpleasant, but it means the heat is working. The rise while the heat is still on is the passage opening up as the ice melts, giving back the power the ice had stolen. And the final reading on cold is **higher than before you started**, because the RPM you had before applying heat was already reduced by ice. Clearing it has restored the power you had lost without noticing.

With no ice there is nothing to melt and nothing to give back: the RPM drops with the hot air, stays down, and returns to exactly where it was.

Some practical rules come with this, and the POH for your type governs how and when to use the heat:

- **Use full heat**, not partial, unless the POH says otherwise. Partial heat without a carburettor air temperature gauge can warm the air *into* the icing range rather than out of it.
- **Leave it on long enough to clear the ice**, typically some tens of seconds or more, until the RPM stops rising. Do not return to cold at the first rough running: that roughness is the sign the heat is working.
- **Before a long low-power descent or glide approach**, most POHs call for full carburettor heat applied *before* you reduce power, while the engine is still hot enough to supply heat to the muff.
- **Return to cold before you need full power**, for a go-around or a take-off, unless the POH says otherwise: hot air means less power.

!!! example "Worked example: confirming carburettor ice in the cruise"
    Given: cruise at constant throttle; RPM normally 2400, now crept down to 2300.

    1. Apply full carburettor heat: the RPM falls to **2200** (hot, less dense air), and the engine runs roughly for a few seconds.
    2. Keep the heat on: the RPM climbs to **2350** as the ice melts.
    3. After a further 30 seconds, select cold: the RPM settles at **2400**.

    The final reading is higher than the 2300 you had before applying heat. That confirms carburettor ice was present, and it has been cleared. Keep an eye on the RPM: the conditions that made the ice have not gone away, so you may need the heat again.

Now one to read yourself before you open the solution. On a dry, clear afternoon you notice what might be a small RPM loss. You apply full heat: the RPM drops by about 75 and stays there, smooth, for 30 seconds. On returning to cold it comes back to the same reading you started with. Was there ice?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    **No ice.** The drop on applying heat is only the effect of hot, less dense air and a richer mixture. There was no rise while the heat was on, because there was nothing to melt, and the RPM returned to the original value, not a higher one, on cold. If the original "loss" persists, look for another cause (a mixture setting after a change of altitude, or a magneto problem) rather than ice.

??? check "Check yourself: why does the RPM end up higher than before when you return to cold after clearing carburettor ice?"
    Because the reading before you applied heat was already reduced by the ice you had not yet noticed. Melting it restores the airflow and the power the ice had taken, so the RPM on cold returns to what the engine should have been making all along.

## Bringing it together

A winter morning flight from Jandakot to Northam and back, 14 degrees with a dew point of 12 after overnight rain: high humidity. Mixture full rich for take-off and the climb. Level at 6,500 ft, you set cruise power, trim, and lean by RPM: up to the peak, a little past until the RPM sags, back to the peak and a touch richer. Cruising home at constant throttle, the RPM has drifted down 100 and the airspeed is a few knots low. You do not touch the throttle. Full heat: the RPM drops, the engine coughs, then the RPM climbs, and back on cold it is above where it was. That was ice. Before the descent you apply full heat while the engine is still at cruise power, then reduce power and enrich in steps on the way down. Pre-landing checks: mixture full rich, ready for a go-around, and carburettor heat as the POH directs so that full power is there if you need it.

## Key points

- **2.1.1** Thin air with the same metered fuel makes the mixture richer with height: lean in the cruise (at or below the POH power limit), enrich in the descent, full rich for take-off, climb and the pre-landing checks, idle cut-off to stop. Leaning by RPM: lean slowly, the RPM rises to a peak, then falls and the engine roughens; enrich back to the peak and slightly richer, and re-lean after any change of power or height. Too lean at high power risks detonation; too rich wastes fuel and fouls plugs.
- **2.1.2** With a fixed-pitch propeller, carburettor ice shows as a gradual unexplained RPM drop at constant throttle, then loss of airspeed or height, then rough running. Full carburettor heat: RPM drops, may run rough, then rises as the ice melts, and is higher than before on cold. No ice: a small drop that stays, then back to the original.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Expect a question on the direction of mixture change with height (climbing makes it **richer**), one on the RPM sequence while leaning (rise, peak, fall: return to the peak and slightly rich), one on the pre-landing mixture setting (**full rich**), one on the first sign of carburettor ice in a fixed-pitch aeroplane (**gradual RPM drop**) and one that describes the RPM after carburettor heat and asks whether there was ice. "Higher than before on cold" means ice was present; "back to the original" means there was none. Carburettor ice can form at outside temperatures up to about 30 degrees in humid air, and is most likely at low power.
