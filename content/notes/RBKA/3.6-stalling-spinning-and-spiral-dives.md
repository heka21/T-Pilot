## What the exam expects (RBKA 3.6)

- **3.6.1** Describe the symptoms when approaching the stall and the characteristics of the stall itself.
- **3.6.2** Explain what using aileron near and in the stall does, and why an aeroplane stalls at different speeds.
- **3.6.3** State the effect (increase, decrease or nil) on the level-flight stall IAS of power, flap, vertical gusts and wind shear, manoeuvres, weight, frost and ice, and altitude.
- **3.6.4** Describe the aerodynamic principles of stall recovery.
- **3.6.5** Describe manoeuvres in which the aeroplane stalls at an attitude that looks nothing like the "normal" stalling attitude.
- **3.6.6** Tell a spin from a spiral dive and describe the standard recovery from each.

## Why this matters

Most fatal accidents in light aeroplanes that begin in the air are loss-of-control accidents, and most of those are a stall the pilot did not see coming, usually low and slow near an aerodrome. The classic one is the turn from base onto final at Jandakot or Murrayfield on a gusty afternoon: a little overshoot, a little rudder to tighten the turn, a little back pressure to hold the nose up, and the wing lets go at 600 ft with the nose near the horizon. Understanding the stall is what breaks that chain, because once you see that a wing stalls at an *angle*, not at a *speed* or an *attitude*, every one of the exam's questions in this subtopic answers itself. The exam tests it with recall (the symptoms in order, the spin recovery) and with numbers (stall speed in a turn or at a different weight).

## What a stall actually is

Start with the wing in cruise. Air meets the wing at a small angle, flows smoothly over the top surface and leaves at the trailing edge. The angle between the wing's **chord line** (the straight line from leading edge to trailing edge) and the **relative airflow** (the air coming at the wing, always exactly opposite to the wing's own path through the air) is the **angle of attack**. Lift grows with angle of attack: tilt the wing more and it produces more lift at the same speed.

That works up to a point. Somewhere around **15 to 18 degrees** for a typical light aeroplane wing, the air can no longer follow the curve of the upper surface. It separates, first near the trailing edge and then further forward, leaving a turbulent wake over the top of the wing. Lift falls sharply and drag rises sharply. That angle is the **critical angle of attack**, and the wing is **stalled**.

![How does lift change with angle of attack, and where does it stop?](diagram:cl-vs-angle-of-attack "Lift grows in a straight line with angle of attack until the critical angle, about 16 degrees; beyond it the flow separates and lift collapses.")

Two things follow, and the exam tests both again and again:

- The stall happens at the **critical angle of attack, every time**. Weight, speed, attitude and power change *when* you reach that angle; they never change the angle itself.
- Because the relative airflow comes from wherever the aeroplane is going, not from the horizon, the wing can be past the critical angle with the nose **above the horizon, on it, or below it**. The stall is not an attitude.

![Can you stall with the nose below the horizon? Try it](widget:attitude-and-angle-of-attack "Exceed about 16 degrees between the chord line and the relative airflow and the streamers break away, whatever the attitude or the airspeed.")

### What you feel on the way in

In straight and level flight at a fixed weight, the only way to reach the critical angle is to slow down: at a lower speed the wing must fly at a higher angle to make the same lift. So a level-flight stall arrives with a set of symptoms in a predictable order, and the exam expects you to know the order:

1. **Low and decreasing airspeed**, with a progressively higher nose attitude as you hold height.
2. **Sloppy controls.** The controls move further for less effect because less air is flowing over them, and the airflow noise drops.
3. **The stall warning**, a horn or light driven by a small vane on the leading edge that lifts as the point where the airflow divides moves back under the nose of the wing. It is set to trigger about **5 to 10 kt** above the stall.
4. **Buffet**, a shake through the airframe and the control column, because the turbulent air separating from the wing is striking the tailplane.

![What happens to the airflow, and what do you feel, as the wing approaches the stall?](diagram:stall-sequence "The flow breaks away from the rear of the wing first and spreads forward; the warnings arrive in order: low speed, sloppy controls, stall warning, buffet.")

### What the stall itself is like

At the stall the **nose drops**, because the centre of pressure moves and the tail can no longer hold the nose up against the collapsing lift. If one wing stalls before the other, which is common because wings are never perfectly matched and any yaw makes one wing fly faster than the other, that wing drops: a **wing drop**. The rate of descent is high. The aeroplane may show a tendency to roll further. The controls still work, but weakly, and the aeroplane will not climb or hold height however hard you pull: lift is now less than weight, and more back pressure makes the angle of attack larger and the stall deeper.

??? check "Check yourself: what single thing determines whether a wing is stalled?"
    The angle of attack. The wing stalls when the angle between the chord line and the relative airflow exceeds the critical angle, about 15 to 18 degrees on a light aeroplane. Airspeed, attitude, weight and power only change the conditions under which that angle is reached.

??? check "Check yourself: in what order do the stall symptoms arrive in a level-flight stall?"
    Low and decreasing airspeed with a rising nose attitude, then sloppy controls and a quieter airflow note, then the stall warning about 5 to 10 kt above the stall, then buffet, then the nose or a wing drops.

## Why the stall speed is not one number

Your POH quotes a stall speed, say 50 kt with flaps up. That number is true in exactly one condition: level flight, maximum weight, power off, flaps up. Change anything and it moves. To see why, recall the lift formula from [BAKC 4.2](/notes/BAKC/4.2):

$$L = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho V^2 S\, C_L$$

Lift depends on air density ($\rho$), the square of the speed, the wing area and the lift coefficient $C_L$, which rises with angle of attack up to its maximum, $C_{L\max}$, at the critical angle. In level flight lift must equal weight. The stall speed is simply the slowest speed at which the wing can still make that much lift, and that is the speed at which $C_L$ has to be $C_{L\max}$:

$$V_s = \sqrt{\frac{2W}{\rho\, S\, C_{L\max}}}$$

Read the formula as a recipe for what moves the stall speed. **More lift needed** (more weight, or a load factor above 1) pushes $V_s$ up. **More lift available at a given angle** (flap raising $C_{L\max}$, or slipstream) pushes $V_s$ down. And because the pitot-static system and the ASI also respond to $\tfrac{1}{2}\rho V^2$, the *indicated* stall speed does not change with density at all; that is the point of the altitude row further down.

### Load factor: the exam's favourite calculation

In a level turn the wing has two jobs: hold the aeroplane up and pull it round the turn. Lift is tilted, so its vertical part must still equal weight, which means total lift must be bigger than weight. The ratio of lift to weight is the **load factor**, $n$, and for a level turn at bank angle $\theta$:

$$n = \frac{1}{\cos\theta}$$

The lift needed has gone up by $n$, and because speed appears squared in the lift formula, the stall speed goes up by the **square root** of $n$:

$$V_{s,\text{turn}} = V_s \sqrt{n}$$

![Why does the stall speed rise in a turn, and how fast?](diagram:stall-speed-vs-bank "Beyond 45 degrees the curve steepens fast: 45 degrees costs 19 percent, 60 degrees costs 41 percent, 75 degrees nearly doubles the stall speed.")

The three bank angles the exam loves:

| Bank | Load factor $n = 1/\cos\theta$ | $\sqrt{n}$ | Stall speed rises by |
|---|---|---|---|
| 30° | 1.15 | 1.07 | about 7 percent |
| 45° | 1.41 | 1.19 | about 19 percent |
| 60° | 2.00 | 1.41 | about 41 percent |

![Explore bank angle, load factor and stall speed](widget:bank-angle)

!!! example "Worked example: stall speed in a level 45 degree turn"
    Given: level-flight stall speed 55 kt, bank 45 degrees.

    1. Load factor: $n = 1/\cos 45° = 1/0.707 = \mathbf{1.41}$.
    2. Square root: $\sqrt{1.41} = \mathbf{1.19}$.
    3. Stall speed in the turn: $55 \times 1.19 = \mathbf{65\ kt}$ (65.4).

    A 45 degree turn has moved the stall 10 kt closer. If you were flying the turn at 70 kt you had 15 kt in hand; now you have 5, and a gust or a little extra back pressure takes the rest.

### Weight works the same way

The same square-root rule applies to weight, because weight is the lift the wing must make in level flight:

$$V_{s,\text{new}} = V_{s,\text{old}} \sqrt{\frac{W_\text{new}}{W_\text{old}}}$$

!!! example "Worked example: stall speed at a lighter weight"
    Given: the POH stall speed is 55 kt at the maximum weight of 1,100 kg. You are flying solo with half tanks at 900 kg.

    1. Weight ratio: $900 / 1100 = \mathbf{0.818}$.
    2. Square root: $\sqrt{0.818} = \mathbf{0.905}$.
    3. Stall speed today: $55 \times 0.905 = \mathbf{50\ kt}$ (49.7).

    Lighter means a lower stall speed, which is why a solo student finds the aeroplane floats further in the flare than it did with the instructor on board: the POH approach speed was set for the heavy case.

Try one yourself before opening the solution: an aeroplane stalls at 46 kt at 750 kg. What is its stall speed at 900 kg?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    Weight ratio $900/750 = 1.20$; $\sqrt{1.20} = 1.095$; $46 \times 1.095 = 50.4$, so **about 50 kt**. Twenty percent more weight added about 10 percent to the stall speed: the square root is why the percentage is roughly halved.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The exam asks for the *indicated* stall speed. Density height changes the true airspeed at which the wing stalls but not the indicated airspeed, because the ASI measures the same dynamic pressure the wing feels. "Altitude" in the list of factors is therefore **nil** for IAS. The trap answer is "increases".

??? check "Check yourself: why does the stall speed rise by only about 41 percent when the load factor doubles?"
    Because lift is proportional to the square of the speed. Doubling the lift needed only requires the speed to rise by the square root of two, 1.41. The rule is always stall speed times the square root of the load factor (or of the weight ratio).

## Ailerons near the stall

Picture the wing dropping at the stall. Instinct says roll it back up with aileron: control column to the right if the left wing has dropped. That moves the **left aileron down**. A down-going aileron increases the camber of that part of the wing and, with it, the local angle of attack. On a wing already at or past the critical angle, more angle of attack means a deeper stall, less lift and more drag on exactly the wing you were trying to lift. The dropped wing drops further, the extra drag yaws the aeroplane towards it, and that combination of a stalled wing and yaw is the recipe for a spin.

![A wing drops at the stall: why not pick it up with aileron?](diagram:wing-drop-and-recovery "The down-going aileron pushes that wing further past the critical angle. Rudder holds the wings level; unstall first, then roll level with aileron.")

So near the stall you keep the wings level with **rudder**, which yaws the aeroplane so that the dropping wing speeds up a little and the rising wing slows, and you leave the ailerons roughly neutral. Once the wing is unstalled the ailerons work normally again and you roll level with them. The order matters and the exam tests it: **unstall, then roll**.

??? check "Check yourself: a wing drops at the point of stall. What do you do with the ailerons, and why?"
    Keep them neutral and use rudder to stop further yaw and wing drop, then reduce the angle of attack. Aileron to pick the wing up would lower the aileron on the dropped wing, increasing its angle of attack, deepening the stall on that side and adding the yaw that can start a spin.

## The seven factors, one by one

The syllabus names seven variables and wants, for each, increase, decrease or nil for the level-flight stall IAS. Rather than memorise the table, see each one as a change either to the lift the wing must make or to the lift it can make at the critical angle.

**Power: decreases.** With power on, two things help. The propeller slipstream flows over the inner wing faster than the free airstream, so that part of the wing makes more lift at a given indicated speed. And with the nose high at the stall, a share of the thrust points upwards and carries part of the weight, so the wing needs to make less lift. Both lower the speed at which the critical angle is reached. A power-on stall happens at a lower IAS, with a higher nose attitude and often a sharper wing drop.

**Flap: decreases.** Lowering flap increases the camber of the wing, which raises the lift coefficient at every angle of attack and raises $C_{L\max}$. More lift available at the critical angle means the stall arrives at a lower speed, which is the whole point of flap for landing. The critical *angle* with flap down is a little lower, but the stall *speed* is lower too, and the exam asks about speed.

![What does flap do to the lift curve?](diagram:flap-effect-on-lift-curve "With flap down the curve sits higher at every angle, so the wing makes the lift it needs at a lower speed; the stall speed falls.")

**Vertical gusts and wind shear: increases.** An up-gust changes the direction of the relative airflow: suddenly the air is coming from below as well as from ahead, so the angle of attack jumps with no change in attitude or in the airspeed reading. If you were already near the critical angle, the gust takes you past it. Wind shear works through speed instead: a sudden loss of headwind leaves the aeroplane, with its inertia, moving through the air more slowly, so the IAS drops towards the stall with the attitude unchanged. Either way the stall arrives at a higher indicated speed than the book figure, which is why you add a gust margin to the approach speed.

![What does a decreasing headwind do to the airspeed on approach?](diagram:wind-shear-on-approach "As the headwind falls away, inertia keeps the groundspeed, so the indicated airspeed drops towards the stall with the attitude unchanged.")

**Manoeuvres: increases.** Any turn, pull-up or pull-out of a dive raises the load factor, the wing has to make more than the weight, and the stall speed rises by the square root of the load factor, as worked above.

**Weight: increases.** More weight is more lift to make, and the stall speed rises by the square root of the weight ratio.

**Frost and ice: increases.** Even a thin layer of frost, the sort you find on a wing left outside overnight at Northam or Cunderdin in July, roughens the surface and changes the shape of the leading edge. The airflow separates earlier, so $C_{L\max}$ falls and the critical angle itself is reduced. The wing stalls at a higher speed and with less warning, and ice adds weight on top. Every trace of frost comes off before flight.

**Altitude: nil.** At altitude the air is thinner, so the wing must fly through it faster to make the same lift, and the true airspeed at the stall is higher. But the ASI measures dynamic pressure, exactly what the wing feels, and reads the same at the stall whatever the density. The indicated stall speed does not change.

The recap, now that each row has a reason:

| Factor | Level-flight stall IAS | The reason in one line |
|---|---|---|
| Power on | Decreases | Slipstream adds lift; a share of thrust carries weight |
| Flap down | Decreases | Higher $C_{L\max}$: more lift at the critical angle |
| Vertical gust, wind shear | Increases | Gust raises the angle of attack; shear drops the IAS |
| Manoeuvres | Increases | Load factor: more lift needed, $V_s\sqrt{n}$ |
| Weight | Increases | More lift needed, $V_s\sqrt{W_2/W_1}$ |
| Frost and ice | Increases | Lower $C_{L\max}$ and a lower critical angle, plus weight |
| Altitude | Nil | The ASI reads the dynamic pressure the wing feels |

??? check "Check yourself: power on and flap down both lower the stall IAS, but for different reasons. What are they?"
    Power on: the slipstream speeds up the airflow over the inner wing and the upward share of thrust carries some weight, so the wing needs to make less lift. Flap down: higher camber raises the lift coefficient at every angle, so the wing makes the lift it needs at a lower speed.

## Recovering: the only cure

Everything about stall recovery follows from one principle: **the wing unstalls when the angle of attack is reduced below the critical angle, and nothing else will do it.** Power does not unstall a wing. Levelling the wings does not unstall a wing. Only moving the control column forward, which lowers the nose relative to the flight path and reduces the angle of attack, does.

The standard recovery, in the order the exam wants:

1. **Reduce the angle of attack.** Ease the control column forward until the buffet stops and the controls firm up. You are trading height for angle; the height loss is the price of the recovery and the reason you recover promptly.
2. **Apply full power** to accelerate and to minimise the height loss, keeping straight with rudder against the yaw from the power increase.
3. **Level the wings with aileron**, now that the wing is unstalled and the ailerons work properly.
4. **Recover to the climb attitude** and climb away with the minimum height loss.

The forward movement is often small. At the first buffet a relaxation of the back pressure is usually enough; a fully developed stall with a wing drop needs a definite forward movement. Pushing to a steep nose-down attitude wastes height.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The exam offers "apply full power" and "level the wings" as first actions. Both are wrong. The first action in any stall recovery is to reduce the angle of attack. Power reduces the height loss and aileron comes after the wing is flying again.

## Stalls that do not look like stalls

Because the stall is an angle and not an attitude, there are manoeuvres in which the wing reaches the critical angle with the nose nowhere near the "stalling attitude" you practised at height. The syllabus wants you to recognise them.

**Steep turns and pull-ups.** The load factor is high, so the stall speed is high. In a 60 degree turn an aeroplane with a 50 kt book figure stalls at 71 kt, and the nose may be only a little above the horizon when it happens. The buffet is the warning; the attitude tells you nothing.

**Pulling out of a dive, or out of a spiral dive, too hard.** At high speed a sharp pull generates a large load factor in an instant. The wing can reach the critical angle with the nose well *below* the horizon: an **accelerated stall**, with little warning because the speed looked so healthy.

**The skidding turn onto final.** You have overshot the centreline turning base to final. Steepening the bank low down feels wrong, so you add rudder to hurry the nose round instead. The aeroplane skids; the inner wing is now moving more slowly through the air and partly blanketed by the fuselage, and you hold a little back pressure to keep the nose up in the turn. The inner wing reaches the critical angle first and drops, towards the ground, from a few hundred feet, with the nose near the horizon. This is the classic stall-spin accident, and the reason instructors are so insistent about balance in the circuit.

**Wind shear on approach.** On a gusty day the headwind falls away in the last few hundred feet. The IAS drops, the angle of attack rises as the aeroplane sinks, and the wing can stall at a perfectly normal approach attitude.

The common thread: **attitude is not a stall cue.** Airspeed relative to the configuration and load factor, the stall warning and the buffet are.

??? check "Check yourself: why is a skidding turn onto final so dangerous?"
    The pilot is low, slow and holding back pressure in a turn, so the stall speed is already raised by load factor. The skid slows the inner wing, which reaches the critical angle first and drops towards the ground. The nose is near the horizon, so the attitude gives no warning, and there is no height to recover.

## Spin or spiral dive

Both are steep, rotating descents that can follow a mishandled stall or a loss of control in cloud, and both end badly if you apply the wrong recovery. The exam wants you to tell them apart and to give the recovery for each.

A **spin** is **autorotation**. Both wings are stalled, but one more deeply than the other. The more stalled wing makes less lift and more drag, so it drops and drags back; the other wing, less stalled, makes more lift and rolls the aeroplane further; and the rolling raises the angle of attack of the down-going wing even more, which keeps it stalled. The aeroplane rolls and yaws continuously about a near-vertical path. The airspeed is **low and roughly steady**, because a stalled wing produces so much drag that the aeroplane cannot accelerate. A spin needs two ingredients: a **stall** and **yaw**. Remove the yaw and the stall, and it stops.

A **spiral dive** is **not a stall at all**. The wings are flying. The aeroplane is in a steep descending turn, the nose is low, and because the wing is not stalled the aeroplane accelerates: the airspeed is **high and increasing fast**, and the load factor builds as the pilot pulls. The danger is overspeed and structural failure, not loss of lift.

![Spin or spiral dive: how do you tell, and how do you recover?](diagram:spin-vs-spiral-dive "Look at the ASI: low and steady is a spin (opposite rudder, stick forward); high and rising is a spiral dive (power off, roll level, then ease out).")

| | Spin | Spiral dive |
|---|---|---|
| Wings | Both stalled, one more than the other | Flying, not stalled |
| Airspeed | Low and roughly steady | High and increasing |
| Load factor | Low | High and building |
| Rate of descent | High | Very high |
| What kills | Hitting the ground still spinning | Overspeed and overstress |
| Recovery | Opposite rudder, stick forward | Roll level, then ease out |

**Standard spin recovery** (the POH procedure for your type overrides any generic one):

1. **Throttle closed**: power flattens the spin and delays recovery.
2. **Ailerons neutral**: aileron in a spin can deepen it.
3. **Full opposite rudder** against the direction of rotation, to stop the yaw.
4. **Control column centrally forward** until the rotation stops: this unstalls the wings.
5. **Centralise the rudder** and **ease out of the dive**, without pulling hard enough to stall again.

**Standard spiral dive recovery:**

1. **Throttle closed** to stop the speed building.
2. **Roll the wings level** with aileron and coordinated rudder.
3. **Ease out of the dive** gently, keeping the load factor down.
4. **Restore power** once the speed is under control.

Notice the recoveries are nearly opposite. In a spin you push to unstall; in a spiral dive the wing is not stalled, so pushing just steepens the dive and adds speed. In a spiral dive you roll level first and then pull; in a spin, pulling keeps the wing stalled. The one instrument that tells you which you are in is the **airspeed indicator**.

??? check "Check yourself: the aeroplane is rotating and descending steeply. The ASI shows a high and rising speed. Spin or spiral dive, and what do you do first?"
    A spiral dive: a spinning aeroplane's airspeed stays low because the stalled wings produce so much drag. Close the throttle, roll the wings level with aileron and rudder, then ease out of the dive gently. Do not push (that is spin recovery) and do not pull before the wings are level (that tightens the spiral).

## Bringing it together

A gusty afternoon at Jandakot, 15 kt down the runway gusting 25. You are turning base onto final on 24 at 70 kt in a 1,000 kg aeroplane whose book stall speed is 50 kt. The bank is 30 degrees, so the load factor is 1.15 and the stall speed has already risen 7 percent, to about 54 kt. The sea breeze has drifted you through the centreline, and your hand wants a little more rudder and a little more back pressure. Trace what each would do: rudder skids the turn and slows the inner wing; back pressure raises the load factor and the angle of attack. A gust lull then drops the IAS by 10 kt in a second. You are now at 60 kt indicated with a stall speed somewhere in the high fifties, 600 ft above the highway. The right answers come from this lesson: keep the turn balanced, hold the attitude for 70 kt plus a gust margin and accept the overshoot, roll out and correct on final, or go around. If the wing does let go, the first movement is the control column forward, not the throttle and not the ailerons.

## Key points

- **3.6.1** A wing stalls when it exceeds the critical angle of attack, about 15 to 18 degrees, at any speed and any attitude. Approaching a level stall: low and decreasing airspeed, sloppy controls, stall warning 5 to 10 kt early, buffet, then the nose or a wing drops.
- **3.6.2** Aileron to lift a dropped wing lowers that wing's aileron, raises its angle of attack and deepens the stall; use rudder, unstall, then roll. The stall speed moves because the lift needed, or the lift available at the critical angle, moves: $V_s$ scales with $\sqrt{n}$ and with $\sqrt{W_2/W_1}$.
- **3.6.3** Stall IAS: power on down, flap down, gusts and wind shear up, manoeuvres up, weight up, frost and ice up, altitude nil.
- **3.6.4** Recovery: reduce the angle of attack first, then full power, then level the wings, then climb away with minimum height loss.
- **3.6.5** Steep turns, hard pull-outs, the skidding base-to-final turn and wind shear stall the wing at attitudes that look nothing like a practice stall. Attitude is not a stall cue.
- **3.6.6** Spin: stalled, low steady airspeed; recover with throttle closed, ailerons neutral, full opposite rudder, stick forward. Spiral dive: not stalled, high rising airspeed; recover with throttle closed, roll level, ease out. The ASI tells them apart.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Expect one calculation (stall speed in a turn or at a new weight: multiply by the square root), one "which factor has no effect" (altitude, for IAS), one on the order of recovery actions (angle of attack first) and one that distinguishes a spin from a spiral dive by airspeed. If a question describes a wing drop at the stall, the answer involving aileron is the wrong one.
