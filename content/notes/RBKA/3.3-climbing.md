## What the exam expects (RBKA 3.3)

- **3.3.1** Say whether the rate of climb and the angle of climb go up or down when each of these changes: (a) weight; (b) power; (c) airspeed, flown away from the recommended speed; (d) flap; (e) headwind or tailwind component, and wind shear; (f) bank angle; (g) altitude and density altitude.

## Why this matters

On a cool morning at Jandakot with only you on board, the aeroplane leaps off and the VSI shows a healthy climb. Take the same aeroplane out of Northam at 2 pm in February with three people and full tanks, in a climbing turn to avoid the town, and the VSI may show half that, with the trees at the end of the runway looking much closer than they did. Nothing is wrong with the engine. Every item on the syllabus list has moved against you at once: weight, density height and bank angle. This lesson builds on [BAKC 4.3](/notes/BAKC/4.3), which taught rate and angle of climb, Vx and Vy, and converting between them. Here you apply that to each factor the syllabus names, so that you can predict the answer instead of memorising seven rows of a table. The exam asks, factor by factor, "increase or decrease?", with a calculation or two on wind.

## The forces in a steady climb

Start with the aeroplane climbing steadily along a straight path, tilted up from the horizon by the climb angle $\gamma$ (gamma). Four forces act. **Lift** acts at right angles to the flight path. **Drag** acts back along the flight path. **Thrust** acts forward along it. **Weight** acts straight down, towards the centre of the earth, whatever the flight path is doing.

Because the path is tilted, weight is no longer at right angles to it. Split weight into two parts. One part, $W\cos\gamma$, acts across the path, and lift balances it. The other part, $W\sin\gamma$, acts **backwards along the path**, in the same direction as drag.

![What forces act on an aeroplane in a steady climb?](diagram:forces-in-a-climb "Part of the weight acts back along the path, so thrust must exceed drag: the excess thrust is what sets the climb angle, and lift is a little less than weight.")

Two results follow, and the first surprises most students.

**In a steady climb, lift is slightly less than weight.** Lift only has to balance $W\cos\gamma$, and since $\cos\gamma$ is less than 1, lift is less than weight. For the shallow climb of a light aeroplane, a few degrees, the difference is tiny, but the direction matters: the wing is not what lifts the aeroplane up the hill.

**Thrust does the climbing.** Along the path, thrust must overcome drag *and* the backward part of the weight:

$$T = D + W\sin\gamma \quad\Rightarrow\quad \sin\gamma = \frac{T - D}{W}$$

The surplus of thrust over drag, the **excess thrust**, is what holds the aeroplane on its climbing path. The more excess thrust for each kilogram of aeroplane, the steeper the climb. When there is no excess thrust, thrust only balances drag and the aeroplane flies level.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    "In a climb, lift is greater than weight." It is not. In a steady climb lift is slightly **less** than weight, because part of the weight is carried along the path by thrust. Lift exceeds weight only for a moment, while you are pitching up into the climb.

## Excess thrust and excess power: angle and rate

The angle of climb is set by excess thrust. The rate of climb, how many feet you gain each minute, is the speed along the path multiplied by $\sin\gamma$:

$$\text{rate of climb} = V\sin\gamma = \frac{V\,(T - D)}{W}$$

Thrust times speed is power, so $V(T - D)$ is the power available from the engine and propeller minus the power needed just to fly level at that speed: the **excess power**. **Angle is set by excess thrust; rate is set by excess power**, each divided by the weight.

Both depend on airspeed, and both have a best speed. For a propeller aeroplane, excess thrust peaks at a fairly low speed, a little above the stall: **Vx**, the best angle of climb speed. Excess power, which multiplies that surplus by the speed, keeps rising for a while beyond Vx and peaks higher: **Vy**, the best rate of climb speed. Vx is always lower than Vy.

![Why are Vx and Vy different speeds?](diagram:excess-thrust-and-power-vs-airspeed "The biggest gap between thrust and drag comes at a low speed, Vx; multiply by speed to get power and the biggest gap moves faster, to Vy.")

![Where on the climb curve are Vx and Vy?](diagram:climb-rate-vs-airspeed "Vy is the top of the rate curve; Vx is where the steepest line from the origin touches it, always at a lower speed.")

The rest of this lesson uses one simple test for every factor on the list: **does it change the excess thrust, the excess power, or the weight they have to lift?** Anything that reduces thrust or power, or adds drag, or adds weight, reduces both the rate and the angle. Wind is the one exception, and it gets its own section.

??? check "Check yourself: what is the difference between excess thrust and excess power, and which sets the rate of climb?"
    Excess thrust is thrust minus drag; excess power is that surplus multiplied by the airspeed. Excess power, divided by weight, sets the rate of climb. Excess thrust, divided by weight, sets the angle.

## Weight

Add weight, say a passenger and full tanks. Two things happen, and both work against the climb.

First, the bigger weight is divided into the same surplus. $\sin\gamma = (T - D)/W$: a larger $W$ underneath gives a smaller angle, and the same applies to the rate.

Second, the surplus itself shrinks. A heavier aeroplane needs more lift, which at a given speed means a larger angle of attack, which means more **induced drag**. Drag goes up, so $T - D$ goes down.

So more weight **decreases both the rate and the angle of climb**. Less weight increases both. That is why the climb you see solo is not the climb you will get with passengers, and why the POH climb chart has a weight scale.

!!! example "Worked example: planning the climb at a heavy weight"
    Given: the POH gives a sea-level climb rate of 700 fpm at 900 kg and 560 fpm at the maximum weight of 1,100 kg. Today you have three people and full fuel, close to maximum weight.

    1. Weight today: close to **1,100 kg**.
    2. Rate to plan on at sea level: **560 fpm**, not the 700 fpm you see when flying light.
    3. Loss compared with the light aeroplane: $700 - 560 = \mathbf{140\ fpm}$, a fifth of the climb.

    Plan on about 560 fpm, then reduce it further for a hot day or a high aerodrome, as the density altitude section shows.

## Power

Opening the throttle increases thrust, and with it the excess thrust and the excess power. **More power increases both the rate and the angle of climb**; less power reduces both. For a normal climb in a fixed-pitch trainer you use full power, or the climb power your POH specifies, because the climb is limited by the surplus available. With the throttle partly closed, the surplus is smaller, and below a certain power there is none: the aeroplane will only fly level, or descend.

## Airspeed away from the recommended speed

The POH gives you two climb speeds because each is the top of its own curve. At **Vy** the excess power is greatest, so the rate is greatest. At **Vx** the excess thrust is greatest, so the angle is greatest. Fly any other speed and you are below the top of the curve you care about, so **flying away from the recommended speed decreases the rate and the angle**.

Look at the two sides separately.

**Faster than Vy.** More of the power goes into overcoming **parasite drag**, which rises with the square of the speed. Less is left over for climbing: the rate falls, and because you are also covering more ground, the angle falls further.

**Slower than Vx.** You are now on the back of the drag curve, where **induced drag** is large and rising as you slow. The excess thrust shrinks, so the angle falls, and the rate falls too. You are also close to the stall, with the engine working hard at an airspeed too low to cool it properly. There is nothing to gain by being slower than Vx.

Between Vx and Vy you trade one for the other: Vx gives the best angle with a slightly lower rate; Vy gives the best rate with a slightly flatter angle. Which you use depends on whether the problem is distance (an obstacle) or time (reaching height).

![When do you climb at Vx and when at Vy?](diagram:vx-vs-vy "Vx gives the most height per distance (to clear an obstacle); Vy the most height per minute. At each minute mark the Vy aeroplane is higher.")

!!! warning "Common trap"
    "Raising the nose and flying slower will steepen the climb." Only down to Vx. Below Vx the angle gets worse as well as the rate, and the stall gets closer. The steepest climb is at Vx, not at the slowest speed you can fly.

## Flap

Flap increases lift at a given angle of attack, but it increases drag too, and in the climb it is the drag that matters. More drag means less excess thrust and less excess power, so **extending flap generally decreases both the rate and the angle of climb**.

Some POHs specify a small flap setting for take-off from a short or soft strip. That is not because the climb is better with flap; it is because the flapped wing makes enough lift to fly at a lower speed, so the **ground roll** is shorter and the aeroplane is airborne sooner. Once you are airborne with a small take-off flap setting, the POH tells you the speed and the point at which to raise it. Full flap, used for landing, adds a lot of drag and seriously reduces the climb, which is why it comes up in stages early in a go-around.

??? check "Check yourself: the POH allows a small flap setting for a short-field take-off. Does that mean flap improves the climb?"
    No. The small flap setting lets the wing lift off at a lower speed, shortening the ground roll. The extra drag of the flap still reduces the excess thrust and power, so the rate and angle of climb are generally lower than with the flap up.

## Bank angle

To turn while climbing, you bank, and the lift tilts with the wings. Only the vertical part of the lift now supports the aeroplane, so to keep climbing the total lift must be larger. The ratio of lift to weight is the **load factor**: about 1.15 in a 30 degree bank, 1.41 at 45 degrees.

![How much more lift does a banked aeroplane need?](widget:bank-angle "The vertical part of lift must still support the weight, so total lift grows with bank: 1.15 times at 30 degrees, 1.41 at 45.")

More lift at the same speed means a larger angle of attack and more **induced drag**. Drag rises, so the excess thrust and excess power fall, and some of the lift you are making is now turning the aeroplane rather than supporting it. **Increasing the bank angle decreases both the rate and the angle of climb.** A gently banked climbing turn costs little; a steep climbing turn can use up almost all of the surplus. That is why climbing turns after take-off are kept shallow, and why the climbing turn away from rising ground is a poor way to escape it.

## Altitude and density altitude

As you climb the air gets thinner. A normally aspirated engine, one without a supercharger or turbocharger, breathes less air with each stroke and makes less power: roughly **3 percent less for every 1,000 ft** of density height. The propeller, biting thinner air, makes less thrust. Thrust and power available fall, while the drag at a given indicated airspeed stays about the same.

![How much power is left at full throttle as you climb?](diagram:power-loss-with-altitude "Roughly 3 percent less per 1,000 ft: by 7,000 to 8,000 ft a typical trainer can only produce about 75 percent power.")

So **increasing altitude or density altitude decreases both the rate and the angle of climb**. It does so gradually as you climb, and it does so at the aerodrome on a hot day. The aeroplane performs according to **density altitude**, the height in the standard atmosphere at which the air is as thin as it is here today ([BAKC 6.2](/notes/BAKC/6.2)). An inland aerodrome a few hundred feet above sea level can have a density altitude of 3,500 ft or more on a 40 degree day, and the climb will be what the POH gives for that height, not for the aerodrome's elevation.

The two climb speeds move as the surplus shrinks. As altitude increases, **Vx (IAS) increases slightly and Vy (IAS) decreases**, until at the **absolute ceiling** they meet and the rate of climb is zero: at that height there is no surplus at any speed, and the aeroplane can only just fly level. Because the climb rate dwindles towards zero near the absolute ceiling, a more practical limit is defined: the **service ceiling**, where the rate of climb has fallen to **100 fpm**.

??? check "Check yourself: why does a hot day reduce the rate of climb even at a sea-level aerodrome?"
    Hot air is less dense, so the density altitude is above the elevation. The engine makes less power and the propeller less thrust in thinner air, so the excess power and excess thrust are smaller. Both rate and angle fall.

## Wind: the angle over the ground, not the rate

Every factor so far changed the excess thrust, the excess power or the weight. Wind changes none of them. The aeroplane climbs through the air mass, and the whole air mass is moving over the ground. Relative to the air, the climb is the same in any steady wind: the **rate of climb is unchanged**, and so is the angle through the air. The VSI shows the same reading.

What changes is the path **over the ground**, which is the one that matters for trees and hills. A **headwind** reduces the groundspeed, so each minute of climbing covers less ground: the path over the ground is **steeper**. A **tailwind** increases the groundspeed and **flattens** it.

To put numbers on it, convert the rate into a gradient using the groundspeed, as in [BAKC 4.3](/notes/BAKC/4.3):

$$\text{gradient (ft per NM)} = \frac{\text{rate of climb (fpm)} \times 60}{\text{groundspeed (kt)}}$$

!!! example "Worked example: the same climb in three winds"
    Given: rate of climb 500 fpm at 70 kt TAS. Compare still air, a 15 kt headwind and a 15 kt tailwind.

    1. Still air, groundspeed 70 kt: $500 \times 60 \div 70 = \mathbf{429\ ft\ per\ NM}$.
    2. 15 kt headwind, groundspeed $70 - 15 = 55$ kt: $500 \times 60 \div 55 = \mathbf{545\ ft\ per\ NM}$.
    3. 15 kt tailwind, groundspeed $70 + 15 = 85$ kt: $500 \times 60 \div 85 = \mathbf{353\ ft\ per\ NM}$.

    The VSI shows 500 fpm in all three. Into the headwind you are about 190 ft higher after each nautical mile than with the tailwind, which is why you take off into wind.

![Does wind change the climb?](diagram:climb-gradient-and-wind "Same 500 fpm on the VSI, three different paths over the ground: 545 ft per NM into a 15 kt headwind, 429 in still air, 353 with a 15 kt tailwind.")

Now try one with a stronger wind before you open the solution. An aeroplane climbs at 650 fpm at 75 kt TAS. Work out the gradient over the ground in still air, with a 20 kt headwind and with a 20 kt tailwind, and say what the VSI shows in each.

??? solution "Show the solution"
    1. Still air, groundspeed 75 kt: $650 \times 60 \div 75 = 39{,}000 \div 75 = \mathbf{520\ ft\ per\ NM}$.
    2. 20 kt headwind, groundspeed $75 - 20 = 55$ kt: $650 \times 60 \div 55 = 39{,}000 \div 55 = \mathbf{709\ ft\ per\ NM}$.
    3. 20 kt tailwind, groundspeed $75 + 20 = 95$ kt: $650 \times 60 \div 95 = 39{,}000 \div 95 = \mathbf{411\ ft\ per\ NM}$.

    The VSI reads **650 fpm in all three**. The headwind adds almost 190 ft per NM and the tailwind takes away more than 100: the stronger the wind compared with the airspeed, the bigger the effect.

### Wind shear: when the wind changes suddenly

A *steady* wind does not change the rate. A wind that *changes* suddenly, **wind shear**, does, for as long as the change lasts, because of inertia.

Suppose you are climbing out at Vy into a 20 kt headwind and the headwind suddenly drops to 5 kt, as it can below a gust front or when you climb out of the shelter of trees into a different wind layer. The aeroplane's mass keeps it moving over the ground at the same speed for a moment. With 15 kt less headwind, the air is now flowing past it 15 kt more slowly: the **airspeed drops**. With less airspeed you have less lift and the aeroplane is off its best climb speed, so the **rate and the angle of climb decrease** until it has re-accelerated, and to get back to Vy you must lower the nose and give up some of the climb. Close to the ground on climb-out that can be dangerous. A sudden increase in tailwind does the same.

![What does a sudden loss of headwind do after take-off?](diagram:wind-shear-on-climb-out "Inertia holds the groundspeed, so losing 15 kt of headwind takes 15 kt off the airspeed: the climb flattens until you lower the nose and regain Vy.")

The opposite shear, a **headwind that suddenly increases** (or a tailwind that decreases), raises the airspeed for a moment and gives a temporary bonus: the rate and angle **increase** briefly, until the aeroplane settles back to its trimmed speed.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The exam asks "what does a headwind do to the rate of climb?" The answer is **no change**: rate is measured relative to the air. A headwind increases the *angle over the ground*. Only a *change* in wind, wind shear, alters the rate, and only for as long as the aeroplane takes to re-accelerate.

??? check "Check yourself: climbing out at Vy, you notice the IAS suddenly drop by 10 kt with the attitude unchanged and the power at full. What has probably happened, and what does it do to the climb?"
    A sudden loss of headwind (or gain of tailwind): wind shear. Inertia kept the groundspeed, so the airspeed dropped. The rate and angle of climb fall until the aeroplane re-accelerates, and you must lower the nose to recover Vy.

## Bringing it together

Back to Northam in February: 40 degrees on the ground, three people on board and full tanks, close to maximum weight, with a light tailwind on the into-wind runway because the sock is swinging. Run the list. **Weight** near maximum: both rate and angle down; the POH figure for the heavy aeroplane, not the one you remember from solo flights. **Density altitude** thousands of feet above the elevation: less power and thrust, both down again. **Wind**: if it is a tailwind on climb-out, the rate is unchanged but the angle over the trees is flatter, so take the other runway or wait. **Flap**: the POH take-off setting shortens the roll, but clean it up as the POH directs once airborne. **Airspeed**: Vx until the trees are below you, then Vy, never slower. **Bank**: keep the turn after take-off shallow, because every degree of bank comes out of a surplus you no longer have. Done properly the departure is uneventful. Done the way you would do it on a cool morning solo, it is not.

## Key points

- **3.3.1** In a steady climb, lift is slightly less than weight and thrust does the climbing: the angle is set by excess thrust and the rate by excess power, each divided by weight. Then, for each factor:
    - (a) **Weight** up: rate and angle both **decrease** (more induced drag, more weight to lift).
    - (b) **Power** up: rate and angle both **increase**.
    - (c) **Airspeed** away from Vy or Vx: rate and angle both **decrease**; slower than Vx is worse in every way.
    - (d) **Flap** extended: rate and angle generally **decrease** (more drag); take-off flap shortens the ground roll, not the climb.
    - (e) **Headwind**: rate **unchanged**, angle over the ground **increases**; **tailwind**: rate unchanged, angle over the ground decreases. **Wind shear** with a loss of headwind: rate and angle decrease temporarily; a gain of headwind gives a temporary increase.
    - (f) **Bank** increased: rate and angle both **decrease** (load factor, more induced drag).
    - (g) **Altitude or density altitude** up: rate and angle both **decrease** (less power and thrust); Vx rises and Vy falls until they meet at the absolute ceiling; the service ceiling is where the climb rate is 100 fpm.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    One rule answers six of the seven rows: anything that adds **weight** or **drag** or takes away **power** (load, flap, bank, height, heat, a speed away from Vx or Vy) reduces **both** rate and angle. Wind is the odd one out: a steady headwind or tailwind leaves the **rate unchanged** and changes only the **angle over the ground**. Expect one calculation converting fpm and groundspeed to feet per nautical mile: multiply the rate by 60 and divide by the groundspeed.
