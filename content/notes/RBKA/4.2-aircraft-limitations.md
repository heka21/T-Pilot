## What the exam expects (RBKA 4.2)

- **4.2.1** Explain the flap operating speed (V<sub>FO</sub>) and the flap extended speed (V<sub>FE</sub>).

## Why this matters

Flap is the one part of a light aeroplane's structure you move in flight, and it is built to work only at low speed. Every circuit at Jandakot has you selecting it while slowing down and talking to the tower, and the easy mistake is to select it a few knots too fast. Do that often enough and the tracks wear, the flap distorts, or one side jams. The exam asks for the two flap speeds, what to do when they differ, and where they sit on the airspeed indicator.

## Why speed limits exist

Every force the air puts on the aeroplane, lift on the wing, drag on the airframe, load on a control surface, grows with **dynamic pressure**, the pressure of the air brought to rest against the aeroplane:

$$q = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho V^2$$

Speed appears **squared**: 10 percent faster and the air pushes about 21 percent harder; twice as fast, four times as hard. Strength costs weight, so the designer decides the highest speed at which each part has to work, builds it strong enough for that speed with a margin, and no stronger. That speed becomes a **limitation**.

Flaps are the clearest case. They are only needed at low speed, for take-off and landing, so building them and their attachments strong enough for cruise would waste weight. They are designed for a low speed, and that speed is the flap limit.

## The flap extended speed, V<sub>FE</sub>

The **flap extended speed**, V<sub>FE</sub>, is the **maximum speed at which the aeroplane may be flown with the flaps extended**. With flap out, the flap, its tracks, its hinges and its operating mechanism, and the rear part of the wing that carries them, are designed for the loads up to V<sub>FE</sub>. Above it those loads exceed the design limits.

Many aeroplanes have **more than one V<sub>FE</sub>**: a higher one for the first stage of flap and a lower one for full flap. The reason is that a fully extended flap sticks further into the airflow at a steeper angle, so it takes more load at any given speed. The POH lists each figure.

!!! example "Worked example: how much extra load is a small overspeed?"
    Given: a hypothetical aeroplane with a full-flap V<sub>FE</sub> of 95 kt. In a rushed approach you select full flap at 112 kt.

    1. Speed ratio: $112 / 95 = \mathbf{1.18}$.
    2. Load scales with the square: $1.18^2 = \mathbf{1.39}$.
    3. Extra load on the flap and its attachments: about **39 percent** above the most they were designed to carry in normal service.

    Seventeen knots does not feel like much on the dial. To the flap structure it is more than a third more load.

![How fast does the load on the flap grow above VFE?](diagram:flap-load-vs-speed "Load goes with the square of speed: 112 kt is 1.18 times a 95 kt VFE, and 1.18 squared is 1.39, about 39 percent over the design load.")

### What happens above V<sub>FE</sub>

Above V<sub>FE</sub> the flap surface can **bend or buckle**, the **tracks, rollers and hinges** can deform or crack, the **operating mechanism** (cable, pushrod, torque tube or electric motor) is loaded beyond its rating, and the rear spar and ribs that carry the flap loads into the wing take more than intended.

Aerodynamically, extended flap moves the wing's centre of pressure rearward and makes the wing twist and pitch nose-down more strongly, and that twisting load also grows with the square of the speed. Many POHs give a **lower limit load factor with flap extended** than with flap up; check the POH for your type. So a high-speed, flap-down pull-up combines two overloads.

The damage may not be symmetrical. If one flap distorts or one side of the mechanism yields, the two flaps can end up at different angles: **asymmetric flap**, which rolls the aeroplane towards the side with less flap, and may be more than the ailerons can hold at low speed ([RBKA 3.8](/notes/RBKA/3.8)). In the worst case a flap can separate from the aeroplane.

An overspeed with flap extended, even one that seems to have done no harm, is a defect: write it up in the maintenance release so that an engineer can inspect the flap system before the next flight.

??? check "Check yourself: why does full flap often have a lower VFE than the first stage of flap?"
    A fully extended flap projects further into the airflow at a steeper angle, so at any given speed it carries more load. To keep the load within what the structure was designed for, the speed must be lower.

## The flap operating speed, V<sub>FO</sub>

The **flap operating speed**, V<sub>FO</sub>, is the **maximum speed at which the flaps may be moved**, extended or retracted. It is about the flap *in motion*, not the flap in position.

Why would moving the flap need a lower speed than holding it out? While the flap travels, the mechanism has to drive it against the air load, and on some designs the motor or linkage is rated for less force than the locked structure can carry. On those aeroplanes the speed for operating the flap is set lower than the speed for flying with it set.

On **many light aeroplanes V<sub>FO</sub> and V<sub>FE</sub> are the same figure**, and the POH quotes only V<sub>FE</sub>. Where they differ, **V<sub>FO</sub> is the lower one**, and the order of operations follows from the definitions:

1. Slow to **V<sub>FO</sub> or below** before you select flap.
2. Once the flap is extended, **V<sub>FE</sub>** is the limit for flying with it out.
3. To retract the flap, you must again be at **V<sub>FO</sub> or below** while it moves.

![What is the difference between VFO and VFE?](diagram:flap-speeds-vfo-vfe "VFO is the most you may be doing while the flap moves; VFE is the most you may fly with it out. When they differ, VFO is the lower one.")

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The exam gives you a V<sub>FO</sub> lower than V<sub>FE</sub> and a speed between the two, and asks what you may do. At that speed you may **fly with the flap already extended** (you are below V<sub>FE</sub>) but you may **not move it**, extend or retract, until you are at or below V<sub>FO</sub>. The trap answers either forbid flying with flap at that speed or allow selecting it.

Try one before opening the solution. The same hypothetical POH gives V<sub>FO</sub> 85 kt, V<sub>FE</sub> 105 kt for the first stage of flap and V<sub>FE</sub> 95 kt for full flap. You are at 100 kt with the first stage of flap extended. Are you within limits, and may you select full flap?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    You are within limits: 100 kt is below the first-stage V<sub>FE</sub> of 105 kt, so you may fly with that flap out. You may not select full flap yet, for two reasons: 100 kt is above V<sub>FO</sub> (85 kt), so the flap may not be moved at all, and it is above the full-flap V<sub>FE</sub> (95 kt), so you could not fly with full flap even once it was out. Slow to 85 kt or below, which satisfies both, then select full flap. Once it is out you may fly with it up to 95 kt, but you must be back at 85 kt or below to retract it.

## Finding the flap speeds on the airspeed indicator

You will not have the POH open on short final, so the key limits are painted on the airspeed indicator as coloured arcs and a line, each end a defined speed.

![Where are the flap speeds on the airspeed indicator?](diagram:asi-colour-arcs "The white arc is the flap operating range: VSO at the bottom, VFE at the top.")

**The white arc: the flap operating range.** Its bottom is **V<sub>SO</sub>**, the stall speed in the landing configuration (full flap, power off) at maximum weight. Its top is **V<sub>FE</sub>**, the maximum flap extended speed. The white arc is where flap may be used. Where a type has more than one V<sub>FE</sub>, the top of the white arc is normally the full-flap figure, and the others are in the POH and often on a placard. V<sub>FO</sub>, where it differs, is generally not marked on the dial.

**The green arc: the normal operating range.** Its bottom is **V<sub>S1</sub>**, the stall speed in a specified configuration, normally flap up, at maximum weight. Its top is **V<sub>NO</sub>**, the maximum structural cruising speed. Up to V<sub>NO</sub> the structure is designed to take the gusts of rough air, so the green arc is where you may fly in any air.

**The yellow arc: the caution range.** It runs from V<sub>NO</sub> to V<sub>NE</sub>, and you may fly in it only in **smooth air**, and then with caution, because a strong gust at those speeds can overstress the structure ([RBKA 3.8](/notes/RBKA/3.8)).

**The red line: V<sub>NE</sub>**, the never-exceed speed. Beyond it the margins for flutter and structural strength are not assured.

**What is not on the dial.** **V<sub>A</sub>**, the manoeuvring speed, is not marked, because it changes with weight; you find it in the POH. V<sub>FO</sub>, where separate, is also only in the POH.

![What does each colour on the airspeed indicator mean?](diagram:asi-colour-markings "White arc VSO to VFE for flap, green arc VS1 to VNO for normal operation, yellow VNO to VNE for smooth air only, red line VNE; VA is not marked.")

The white arc starts below the green because flap lowers the stall speed (V<sub>SO</sub> below V<sub>S1</sub>), and ends below it because the flap is designed for a lower speed than the airframe (V<sub>FE</sub> below V<sub>NO</sub>). In the overlap you may fly clean or with flap.

??? check "Check yourself: what speeds mark the two ends of the white arc, and what does each mean?"
    The bottom is V<sub>SO</sub>, the stall speed in the landing configuration at maximum weight. The top is V<sub>FE</sub>, the maximum speed at which the aeroplane may be flown with flap extended.

## How the POH presents limitations

Section 2 of the POH, **Limitations**, holds the figures, and they are limits, not advice. Its **airspeed limitations** table lists V<sub>NE</sub>, V<sub>NO</sub>, V<sub>A</sub> (often at more than one weight) and V<sub>FE</sub> for each flap stage, with V<sub>FO</sub> if it is separate, usually in both indicated and calibrated airspeed. An **airspeed indicator markings** table gives the speed at each end of each arc. The section also gives the load factor limits, flap up and flap down, and the cockpit **placards**, which may repeat the flap speeds beside the flap lever. Use the indicated airspeed figures, since those are what the ASI shows, and the POH for your aeroplane: it wins over any textbook figure, this lesson included.

## Using flap within its limits

**On the approach.** Get the speed into the white arc, and below V<sub>FO</sub> where it is separate, **before** you reach for the flap lever. The common error is selecting flap in the green or yellow arc during a rushed descent: slow down first, then flap, then trim. If you select the first stage at its V<sub>FE</sub> and then want more flap, check the speed again, because the next stage may have a lower limit.

**On a go-around.** You are low, slow and fully configured. If you raise all the flap at once, the wing loses the extra lift that flap was providing and its stall speed jumps up towards the clean figure, V<sub>S1</sub>, while you may still be near the full-flap approach speed. The aeroplane **sinks**, close to the ground, and may approach the stall.

![What does flap do to the lift curve?](diagram:flap-effect-on-lift-curve "With flap down the wing makes more lift at every angle and stalls at a lower speed; take the flap away and the stall speed rises.")

So the go-around is flown in steps, in the order and at the speeds the POH gives:

1. **Full power** and **climb attitude**, together: the power arrests the descent and the attitude stops the speed rising.
2. **Retract the first stage of flap**, usually the high-drag stage from full to an intermediate setting, once the aeroplane is climbing. This removes most of the drag while keeping most of the extra lift.
3. **Accelerate** and, once you are climbing positively at a safe speed above the stall speed for the next configuration, **retract the remaining flap in stages**.

![Why raise the flap in stages on a go-around?](diagram:go-around-flap-retraction "All the flap at once removes its lift while you are slow and low, and the aeroplane sinks; power and attitude first, then flap a stage at a time.")

Watch the other limit as well: at full power with flap still down, the aeroplane can accelerate quickly past V<sub>FE</sub> if the attitude is too low. Hold the climb attitude and keep the speed in the white arc until the flap is up.

??? check "Check yourself: why is flap raised in stages on a go-around rather than all at once?"
    Raising all the flap at once removes its extra lift, so the stall speed jumps up towards the clean figure while the aeroplane is still slow and close to the ground, and it sinks. Raising it in stages, the high-drag stage first, keeps most of the lift while the aeroplane accelerates to a safe speed for the next configuration.

## Bringing it together

You join downwind at Jandakot at 100 kt in the hypothetical aeroplane above: V<sub>FO</sub> 85 kt, V<sub>FE</sub> 105 kt for the first stage and 95 kt for full flap. You reduce power and wait for the needle to pass below 85 kt before touching the flap, because V<sub>FO</sub> governs every movement. On short final the tower sends you around. Full power and climb attitude first; then, because the flap may only move below 85 kt, you hold the attitude firmly and raise the first stage as soon as you are climbing, the rest at the POH speed. Let the nose drop and the speed run to 100 kt, and you would be inside V<sub>FE</sub> but unable to retract the flap without slowing again.

## Key points

- **4.2.1 V<sub>FE</sub>** is the maximum speed with flap extended, the top of the white arc; above it, air loads, growing with the square of speed, exceed what the flap, its tracks and the wing structure were designed for. Types may have a V<sub>FE</sub> for each flap stage.
- **4.2.1 V<sub>FO</sub>** is the maximum speed at which the flap may be moved, extended or retracted. Often it equals V<sub>FE</sub>; where it differs it is lower: slow to V<sub>FO</sub> to move the flap, then V<sub>FE</sub> is the limit for flying with it out.
- **4.2.1 On the dial and in the POH:** white arc V<sub>SO</sub> to V<sub>FE</sub>, green V<sub>S1</sub> to V<sub>NO</sub>, yellow V<sub>NO</sub> to V<sub>NE</sub>, red line V<sub>NE</sub>; V<sub>A</sub> and V<sub>FO</sub> are in POH section 2, not on the dial. Raise flap in stages on a go-around.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    If a question gives V<sub>FO</sub> lower than V<sub>FE</sub>, you must be at or below V<sub>FO</sub> to move the flap, and may then fly up to V<sub>FE</sub> with it extended. The top of the white arc is V<sub>FE</sub> and the bottom is V<sub>SO</sub>; the top of the green arc is V<sub>NO</sub>, not V<sub>FE</sub>.
