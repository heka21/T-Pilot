## What the exam expects (RBKA 3.7)

- **3.7.1** Describe situations that may cause an aeroplane to "wheelbarrow" and the recommended pilot action.
- **3.7.2** Describe the effect of a crosswind on high-wing and low-wing aeroplanes during taxi, take-off and landing.
- **3.7.3** List the advantages of taking off and landing into wind.
- **3.7.4** Compare a flapless approach with a flap approach: attitude in the descent, approach path angle, threshold and touchdown speeds, and landing roll.
- **3.7.5** Describe the effect of wind shear (wind gradient) and ground effect on aerodynamic and flight characteristics, and how to identify them.

## Why this matters

Most training accidents happen in the few minutes near the ground, and few are dramatic: a wing tip lifted by a gust while taxiing at Jandakot, a nosewheel collapsed by a fast flat landing, a swing off the runway at Northam in a gusty crosswind, an aeroplane that sinks short as the headwind dies in the last 200 ft. Each is physics the pilot did not anticipate. Perth's afternoon sea breeze guarantees a crosswind or a gusty headwind on many training days. The exam asks you to describe each effect and the right response, so this lesson builds each one from the forces involved.

## Wheelbarrowing

A tricycle-undercarriage aeroplane is designed to roll on its **main wheels**, which sit just behind the centre of gravity. That arrangement is directionally stable on the ground: if the aeroplane starts to swing, the weight ahead of the main wheels tends to keep it rolling straight, the way a shopping trolley pushed from behind runs true.

Now imagine the aeroplane rolling fast with the nose held down. The wing is still lifting, so the main wheels carry very little weight, and the elevator presses the nose onto the runway. The aeroplane is balanced on its **nosewheel**, *ahead* of the centre of gravity: **wheelbarrowing**. Like a wheelbarrow, it pivots on a front wheel ahead of the weight, which is unstable. The aeroplane becomes hard to steer, any crosswind or brake input can swing it violently (a **ground loop**), and the nose gear, which is not built to carry that load at that speed, can be damaged or collapse.

![Why is an aeroplane balanced on its nosewheel unstable?](diagram:wheelbarrowing-pivot "With the main wheels light, the nosewheel ahead of the CG becomes the pivot and any swing grows; ease back to put the weight on the mains.")

Situations that cause it:

- **Landing too fast** and flying or pushing the aeroplane onto the runway in a flat or nose-down attitude, nosewheel first or all three wheels together. The excess speed means the wing is still lifting strongly after touchdown.
- **Holding forward pressure** on the control column during the take-off roll or the landing run at high speed, for example to "hold it down" for a better lift-off.
- A **crosswind** take-off or landing where forward elevator is held with excess speed, which adds a sideways force to an already unstable aeroplane.

The recommended action follows from the cause: get the weight back onto the main wheels.

- **On landing:** close the throttle if any power is on, and **ease the control column back** to raise the nose. That reduces the load on the nosewheel and lets the main wheels take the weight. If the speed is high or the runway remaining is short, **go around** rather than trying to force the aeroplane to stay down.
- **On take-off:** relax the forward pressure and hold the elevator about neutral, so the main wheels carry the weight until rotation.

Prevention is better than either: arrive at the threshold at the correct speed and land **main wheels first** in a nose-up attitude, holding the nosewheel off until it lowers naturally.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Pushing forward to "stick the aeroplane to the runway" after a fast landing is what causes wheelbarrowing, not the cure. The exam's correct option always involves back pressure (or a go-around), never forward pressure or harder braking.

??? check "Check yourself: why does a tricycle aeroplane become directionally unstable when it wheelbarrows?"
    The weight is being carried on the nosewheel, which is ahead of the centre of gravity, instead of on the main wheels behind it. A pivot ahead of the weight is unstable, like a wheelbarrow: any swing grows instead of correcting itself.

## What a crosswind does to the aeroplane

A crosswind does two things to any aeroplane, and every technique is a response to one of them.

**Weathercocking.** The fin and the side of the fuselage behind the main wheels act like the tail of a weathervane. Wind from the side pushes on that area and swings the tail downwind, so the **nose yaws into wind**. On the ground the main wheels are the pivot, and most of the side area is behind them, so the tendency is strong at low speed when the rudder has little airflow to fight back with.

**The into-wind wing lifting.** Wind from one side tends to **raise the upwind wing** and roll the aeroplane away from the wind. There are three reasons. The sideways airflow meets the upwind wing at a higher effective angle of attack because of **dihedral**, the upward tilt of the wings, which makes that wing produce more lift. The fuselage partly shields the downwind wing from the wind. And on a high-wing aeroplane the wind gets underneath the exposed upwind wing and pushes it up.

![What does a crosswind try to do on take-off and landing, and how do you counter it?](diagram:crosswind-takeoff-and-landing "The wind lifts the upwind wing and weathercocks the nose into wind: hold aileron into wind, keep straight with rudder; land crabbed or wing-down.")

### Taxiing in a strong wind

Taxiing slowly, the wind dominates the airflow over the controls, so you set them where the wind itself holds the aeroplane down.

**Wind from ahead** (anywhere in the front quadrants): hold the **aileron into the wind**, control column towards the side the wind is coming from. That raises the upwind aileron, which spoils some lift on the upwind wing and lets the wind press down on it. Hold the elevator **neutral** in a tricycle aeroplane.

**Wind from behind** (the rear quadrants): hold the **aileron away from the wind**, which lowers the upwind aileron, and the elevator **forward**. With the wind coming from behind, it strikes the top of the down-going aileron and the down-going elevator and presses them down, holding the upwind wing and the tail on the ground. The memory aid is **"dive away from the wind"**.

![Where do you hold the controls when taxiing in a strong wind?](diagram:crosswind-taxi-control-positions "Wind from ahead: stick into the wind, elevator neutral. Wind from behind: stick away from the wind and forward.")

A **high-wing** aeroplane is the more vulnerable when taxiing. Its wing is high and exposed, the wind gets under the upwind wing easily, and a strong gust from the rear quarter can lift it and tip the aeroplane onto the downwind wing tip. Taxi slowly, hold the controls firmly in position, and turn with care when the wind will be from behind. A **low-wing** aeroplane's wing is low and partly shielded by the ground, so it is less prone to lifting, but its wing tips are closer to the ground and to taxiway lights, signs and other aeroplanes.

### Taking off in a crosswind

At the start of the roll the aeroplane is slow, the controls are weak, and both crosswind effects are at their strongest. Begin with **full aileron into wind** to hold the upwind wing down, and keep straight with **rudder** against the weathercocking yaw. As speed builds, the ailerons become more effective and the same deflection would roll the aeroplane towards the wind, so you **reduce the aileron progressively** to keep the wings level. At rotation, lift off cleanly and positively so the aeroplane does not skip sideways on the runway as it becomes light, then turn into wind just enough to **crab**, heading slightly into wind, so that the aeroplane tracks along the extended centreline.

The rolling tendency is stronger in a high-wing aeroplane, whose wing is more exposed.

### Landing in a crosswind

On final the aeroplane is flying in the air mass, which is moving sideways across the runway, so you must point slightly into wind to track down the centreline. There are two ways to deal with that at touchdown, and many pilots combine them.

The **crab method**: fly the approach heading into wind with the wings level, tracking the centreline. Just before touchdown, use rudder to yaw the nose into line with the runway and aileron to stop the drift that starts the moment you do. Too early and the aeroplane drifts sideways; too late and it lands sideways, loading the undercarriage sideways and possibly swinging.

The **wing-down method** (a sideslip): lower the upwind wing just enough to stop the drift, and hold opposite rudder to keep the nose aligned with the runway. The aeroplane is then slipping into wind at exactly the rate the wind is pushing it away. It touches down on the **upwind main wheel first**, then the other main wheel, then the nosewheel. After touchdown keep increasing the aileron into wind as the aeroplane slows, so the wind cannot lift the upwind wing.

Here the difference between the two wing positions works the other way. A **low-wing** aeroplane has **much less wing-tip and flap clearance** when the upwind wing is lowered, so an over-steep wing-down attitude or a touchdown with bank risks a tip or flap strike. A high-wing aeroplane has generous clearance.

Every POH states a **maximum demonstrated crosswind**: the largest crosswind component flown during certification, by a test pilot. It is not a hard limit, but treat it as one until you have the experience.

### Working out the crosswind

To compare the wind with the demonstrated crosswind you need its **crosswind component**, the part of the wind blowing straight across the runway, and its **headwind component**, the part blowing straight down it. If the angle between the wind direction and the runway direction is $\theta$:

$$\text{crosswind} = V \sin\theta \qquad \text{headwind} = V \cos\theta$$

![How much of the wind is across the runway, and how much down it?](widget:crosswind-component "Thirty degrees off the runway already puts half the wind across it; compare the crosswind with the POH's demonstrated figure.")

!!! example "Worked example: crosswind and headwind components"
    Given: runway 24, wind 280 degrees at 20 kt (both magnetic).

    1. Angle between wind and runway: $280 - 240 = \mathbf{40°}$.
    2. Crosswind: $20 \times \sin 40° = 20 \times 0.643 = \mathbf{12.9\ kt}$.
    3. Headwind: $20 \times \cos 40° = 20 \times 0.766 = \mathbf{15.3\ kt}$.

    Compare 13 kt with your POH's demonstrated crosswind before accepting the runway, and expect the wind from the right: the into-wind wing to hold down is the right one.

Rules of thumb let you check in your head: a wind **30 degrees** off the runway puts **half** of it across, 45 degrees about seven-tenths, and 60 degrees about nine-tenths.

Now try one with a twist. The tower at Jandakot is using runway 24 and the wind is 100 degrees at 12 kt. Work out the components for runway 24, then for runway 06.

??? solution "Show the solution"
    Runway 24: the angle between 240 and 100 is 140 degrees, more than 90, so the wind has a component from behind. Crosswind $12 \times \sin 140° = 12 \times 0.643 = 7.7$ kt; along-runway component $12 \times \cos 140° = 12 \times (-0.766) = -9.2$ kt, a **tailwind of about 9 kt**. Runway 06: the angle is $100 - 60 = 40$ degrees, so the crosswind is the same **7.7 kt** but there is a **headwind of 9.2 kt**. The crosswind is identical; the choice of end decides whether 9 kt helps or hurts you, which is the next section.

??? check "Check yourself: taxiing a high-wing aeroplane with a strong wind from the right rear quarter, where do you hold the controls?"
    Aileron away from the wind (control column to the left, so the right, upwind, aileron is down) and elevator forward. Wind from behind presses on the top of the lowered aileron and elevator, holding the upwind wing and the tail down.

## Why take off and land into wind

The aeroplane lifts off and touches down at a fixed **airspeed**. Into a headwind, part of that airspeed is supplied by the wind before you have moved, so the **groundspeed** at lift-off and touchdown is lower. Everything good about taking off and landing into wind follows from that one fact.

- **Shorter take-off and landing distances.** The energy the aeroplane must build up on take-off, or get rid of on landing, depends on the square of the groundspeed, so even a modest headwind gives a large saving.
- **Steeper climb-out and approach paths over the ground.** The rate of climb or descent in feet per minute is the same, but you cover less ground each minute, so obstacles at the end of the runway are cleared more easily and the approach can come over them more steeply.
- **Better directional control**, with no drift and no weathercocking to fight.
- **Control effectiveness earlier** in the take-off roll, because airspeed, and with it the authority of the rudder and ailerons, builds up before the groundspeed does.
- **Less wear** on tyres and brakes, and less energy to absorb if something goes wrong, so less damage and injury in an overrun or collision ([BAKC 2.5](/notes/BAKC/2.5) covers kinetic energy).

!!! example "Worked example: what the wind does to the touchdown energy"
    Given: approach speed 65 kt, wind 15 kt straight down the runway. Compare landing into it with landing downwind.

    1. Into wind: groundspeed $65 - 15 = \mathbf{50\ kt}$.
    2. Downwind: groundspeed $65 + 15 = \mathbf{80\ kt}$.
    3. Energy ratio: $(80/50)^2 = 1.6^2 = \mathbf{2.56}$.

    Landing downwind in a 15 kt wind, the brakes must absorb more than two and a half times the energy, over a runway that the aeroplane reaches at 30 kt more groundspeed. That is how tailwind landings become overruns.

![Why does a little extra groundspeed cost so much?](diagram:kinetic-energy-speed-squared "Energy goes with the square of speed: 10 percent faster carries 21 percent more energy, double the speed four times as much.")

On a sloping country strip, the wind is not the only factor: an upslope shortens the landing and lengthens the take-off, and a wet or grass surface lengthens both. [RBKA 4.1](/notes/RBKA/4.1) works through slope and surface; with a light wind the slope may decide the direction, but with any real wind, into wind usually wins.

??? check "Check yourself: does a headwind change the rate of climb after take-off?"
    No. The rate of climb in feet per minute depends on the aeroplane's power and drag in the air mass. A headwind lowers the groundspeed, so the same rate of climb gives a steeper path over the ground and better obstacle clearance.

## Flapless and flap approaches compared

Flap can fail, so you will practise flapless landings. The comparison starts with what flap does to the wing. Lowering flap increases the wing's camber, so the wing makes more lift at every angle of attack and has a higher maximum lift coefficient. Flap also adds a lot of drag.

![What does flap do to the lift curve?](diagram:flap-effect-on-lift-curve "With flap down the curve sits higher at every angle, so the wing makes the lift it needs at a smaller angle of attack and stalls at a lower speed.")

**(a) Attitude in the descent.** With flap, the wing makes the lift it needs at a *smaller* angle of attack, so the nose sits lower and you see the runway clearly over it. Without flap, the wing needs a higher angle of attack for the same lift, so the **nose attitude is higher** and the forward view is poorer.

**(b) Approach path angle.** Flap's drag lets the aeroplane descend steeply without gaining speed: drag is doing the work that the throttle would otherwise do. Without flap the aeroplane is "slippery", with a better lift-to-drag ratio, so it descends on a **flatter, shallower path** at a given speed and power setting. To fly a normal path it needs less power, and the approach starts further out on a longer final.

**(c) Threshold and touchdown speeds.** Approach speeds are set as a margin above the stall speed in the landing configuration, typically about 1.3 times it. Without flap the stall speed is higher, so the **threshold and touchdown speeds are higher**. Use the POH's flapless approach speed.

**(d) Landing roll.** A higher touchdown speed means more energy to dissipate, and because energy goes with the square of speed, a few knots make a large difference. The clean wing also **floats** further in ground effect before it settles. The **landing roll is longer**, and the whole landing distance is longer still.

| | Flapless approach | Approach with flap |
|---|---|---|
| (a) Attitude in the descent | Higher nose, poorer view | Lower nose, better view |
| (b) Approach path angle | Flatter (shallower) | Steeper |
| (c) Threshold and touchdown speeds | Higher | Lower |
| (d) Landing roll | Longer | Shorter |

The flapless landing has one more trap: the attitude at touchdown is already high, so flaring too high or too hard can strike the tail on the runway. Plan a longer final, use the flapless speed, and fly the aeroplane onto the runway with less flare.

??? check "Check yourself: why is the nose attitude higher on a flapless approach at the correct approach speed?"
    Without flap the wing makes less lift at each angle of attack, so it must fly at a higher angle of attack to support the weight. A higher angle of attack on the same approach path means a higher nose attitude.

## Wind shear, wind gradient and gusts

**Wind shear** is a change in wind speed or direction over a short distance, vertically or horizontally. The aeroplane flies relative to the air, but it has **inertia**: when the wind changes suddenly, the aeroplane keeps its groundspeed for a moment, so its *airspeed* changes by the amount the wind changed.

The most common form near the ground is the **wind gradient**. Friction with the surface slows the wind close to the ground, so on a windy day the wind may be 25 kt at 500 ft and 10 kt at the runway. Trees, hangars and buildings upwind make it worse and more abrupt.

![How does the wind change as you descend towards the runway?](diagram:wind-gradient-on-approach "Friction slows the wind near the ground, so the headwind on final weakens as you descend; the airspeed decays and the aeroplane sinks below the path unless you add power.")

Follow an approach into that wind. As you descend, the headwind falls away. Inertia keeps the groundspeed, so the **indicated airspeed drops**, lift falls, and the aeroplane **sinks below the approach path** with no change in attitude or power. If you do nothing, you **undershoot**, and if the speed was already low, the drop can take you to the stall. The opposite shear, a headwind that increases or a tailwind that dies away, raises the airspeed: the aeroplane **balloons** above the path and then **floats** and overshoots.

| Shear on approach | Immediate effect | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Headwind decreasing, or tailwind increasing | IAS falls, aeroplane sinks, nose drops | Undershoot; risk of stall |
| Headwind increasing, or tailwind decreasing | IAS rises, aeroplane balloons | Overshoot, float |

![What happens when the headwind dies away near the ground?](diagram:wind-shear-on-approach "The groundspeed stays for a moment, so the IAS falls and the aeroplane sinks below the path: add a speed margin and be ready with power.")

On take-off the same physics catches you in the climb: climbing out of a strong headwind into a weaker one, or through a sea-breeze front into a tailwind, drops the airspeed just when you are low and slow. Hold the climb speed, be ready to lower the nose, and delay departure near a thunderstorm, whose outflow produces the most violent shear of all.

**Identify** wind shear by its signature: the airspeed and the rate of descent change suddenly with no change of attitude or power. Expect it in strong or gusty winds, behind buildings and trees, along a sea-breeze front, and near showers and thunderstorms.

**Gusts** are rapid, random fluctuations in wind speed, each one a small shear. The usual technique in gusty conditions is to **add half the gust factor** to the normal approach speed, where the gust factor is the difference between the steady wind and the gusts. Check the POH and your instructor's guidance for your type.

!!! example "Worked example: approach speed in a gusty wind"
    Given: normal approach speed 65 kt; Jandakot ATIS wind 15 kt gusting 25 kt, close to the runway direction.

    1. Gust factor: $25 - 15 = \mathbf{10\ kt}$.
    2. Half the gust factor: $10 \div 2 = \mathbf{5\ kt}$.
    3. Approach speed: $65 + 5 = \mathbf{70\ kt}$.

    The extra 5 kt means that when a gust dies away on short final, the airspeed drops back to about the normal figure instead of towards the stall. Expect a slightly longer landing roll as the price.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Loss of headwind on approach means loss of airspeed and an undershoot; gain of headwind means gain of airspeed and a float. The exam often reverses them in the distractors. Remember that the aeroplane's inertia keeps the *groundspeed*, so the airspeed changes by the change in the wind.

## Ground effect

As the aeroplane comes within about **one wingspan** of the surface, the ground starts to interfere with the airflow around the wing, and the effect is strongest within about half a span. A wing making lift pushes air downwards behind it (**downwash**) and sheds a vortex from each tip; both are the source of **induced drag**. Near the ground, there is no room for the downwash to go down and the tip vortices are squashed. The result: **induced drag falls**, and the wing makes **more lift** at a given angle of attack. Parasite drag is not affected.

![What does the ground do to the wing in the last few feet?](diagram:ground-effect "Near the runway the downwash and tip vortices are squashed, so induced drag falls: a float on landing, and an early lift-off that may not climb.")

That changes how the aeroplane flies in the last few feet:

- **On landing** the aeroplane **floats**, especially if it arrives too fast, because it is suddenly more efficient and loses speed slowly. A fast flapless approach floats the longest. Floating uses runway, and the temptation to push the aeroplane onto the runway leads straight to wheelbarrowing.
- **On take-off** the aeroplane may **lift off below its normal speed** and fly happily at a few feet, then sink back or climb poorly when it climbs out of ground effect and the induced drag returns. If it was pulled off too early, it may be unable to climb over obstacles. Accelerate in ground effect to the proper climb speed before climbing; the soft-field technique uses ground effect deliberately to get the wheels out of the mud as early as possible.
- **Pitch and airspeed indications** can change slightly as the downwash at the tailplane changes.

**Identify** ground effect by a floating, cushioned feel in the flare and a reluctance to settle, or by an aeroplane that leaves the ground early but will not climb away.

??? check "Check yourself: an aeroplane lifts off early on a soft strip but will not climb past about 10 ft. What is happening, and what should the pilot do?"
    It is flying in ground effect, where induced drag is reduced, at a speed too low to climb once the ground effect is lost. Hold it level in ground effect and let it accelerate to the climb speed before climbing.

## Bringing it together

A summer afternoon at Jandakot: the sea breeze is in at 18 kt gusting 26, from about 30 degrees off runway 24 on the right. Before taxiing you work the components: about half the wind, 9 kt, is across the runway, comfortably inside the POH's demonstrated figure, and the headwind is about 16 kt. Taxiing out to the threshold with the wind behind you, you hold the stick away from the wind and forward. On the roll you start with full right aileron, reduce it as the speed builds, and lift off cleanly before setting up a crab. Back in the circuit, the gust factor is 8 kt, so you fly final 4 kt faster than normal. Passing 300 ft the headwind dies and the airspeed sags; you add power at once rather than raising the nose. Over the threshold you lower the right wing to stop the drift, hold left rudder to keep straight, and touch down on the right main wheel. The aeroplane floats a little in ground effect, and you hold it off with back pressure rather than pushing it down, keeping weight off the nosewheel, adding right aileron as it slows.

## Key points

- **3.7.1** Wheelbarrowing is weight on the nosewheel with a lifting wing, from a fast flat landing or forward pressure. Ease the column back (close the throttle), or go around; prevent it with the right speed and a main-wheels-first touchdown.
- **3.7.2** A crosswind weathercocks the nose into wind and lifts the upwind wing. High-wing aeroplanes are more exposed when taxiing and on the roll; low-wing aeroplanes have less tip and flap clearance in a wing-down landing. Taxi with stick into wind from ahead, away from the wind and forward from behind.
- **3.7.3** Into wind gives lower groundspeed, shorter distances, steeper paths over obstacles, better directional control and less energy to absorb.
- **3.7.4** Flapless: higher nose attitude, flatter path, higher threshold and touchdown speeds, longer landing roll and more float.
- **3.7.5** A decreasing headwind on approach drops the IAS and causes an undershoot; an increasing one causes a float. Add half the gust factor. Ground effect reduces induced drag within about a wingspan, causing float on landing and early, unsustainable lift-off on take-off.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Expect questions on taxi control positions (wind from behind: stick away from the wind and forward), the low-wing aeroplane's tip clearance in a wing-down landing, the advantages of into wind (pick the one that is not), the flapless approach (higher attitude, flatter path, higher speeds, longer roll), and wind gradient or ground effect. Ground effect reduces **induced** drag, not parasite drag.
