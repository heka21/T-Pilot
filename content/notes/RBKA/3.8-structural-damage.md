## What the exam expects (RBKA 3.8)

- **3.8.1** Describe what structural damage, including a bird strike, does to (a) the stall characteristics and (b) the controllability of the aeroplane.

## Why this matters

An aeroplane is designed around a smooth, correctly shaped wing and a structure that has never been bent. Take either away and the numbers in the POH stop being true. Bird strikes are the damage you are most likely to meet: waterbirds and raptors work the wetlands around Jandakot, galahs and corellas flock over country strips in the wheatbelt, and most strikes happen in the climb-out and approach, low and slow, exactly when you have the least margin. The other common source is the pilot: a hard pull out of a dive, a fast entry into turbulence, or a heavy landing can bend the airframe without breaking anything you can see. The exam asks what damage does to the stall and to the handling, and what you do about it, and every answer comes from understanding how the wing makes lift and how the structure carries it.

## How an aeroplane gets damaged

Damage reaches the airframe in a handful of ways, and they fall into two families.

**Damage from outside.** A **bird strike** is the most common. The usual impact points are the parts that face forward: the wing and tailplane **leading edges**, the **windscreen**, the **propeller** and spinner, and the engine air intake. Strikes cluster on take-off, climb, approach and landing, near coasts, rivers, wetlands, rubbish tips and aerodromes with large grass areas. **Hail** dents every upward- and forward-facing surface at once. **Ground handling** accidents, a wing tip against a hangar door or a tug against the tail, and **contact with an obstacle** such as a fence post or a tree on a short strip complete the list. **Ice and frost** are not damage in the usual sense, but they act like it: they change the shape and roughness of the wing in exactly the way a dent does.

**Damage from loads.** Every part of the structure is designed to carry a certain load. Ask more of it and it bends or breaks. This is **overstress**, and the pilot usually causes it: by pulling too much g, by flying too fast in turbulence, or by landing heavily. Because overstress so often leaves no obvious mark, it needs its own section.

## Overstress and the flight envelope

Recall from [RBKA 3.5](/notes/RBKA/3.5) that the **load factor**, $n$, is lift divided by weight: the number of "g" the structure is carrying. The designer builds the airframe to a **limit load factor** for its category. For a normal-category aeroplane that is **+3.8 g and −1.52 g** ([BAKC 6.3](/notes/BAKC/6.3)); for utility **+4.4 g**, for aerobatic **+6 g**. The **ultimate load** is normally **1.5 times the limit**. Check the POH for your aeroplane: it states the category and the limits. Between limit and ultimate the structure may bend permanently; beyond ultimate it may fail.

![What load can each category carry, positive and negative?](diagram:load-factor-limits "Normal category: +3.8 and -1.52 g limit, 5.7 g ultimate. Between limit and ultimate the structure bends permanently; beyond ultimate it breaks.")

### The stall protects you, up to a point

There is a natural ceiling on how much g you can pull: the wing cannot make more lift than it makes at the critical angle of attack. At low speed that ceiling is low. From the lift formula, the most lift the wing can make rises with the square of the speed, so the greatest load factor available at airspeed $V$ is

$$n_{\max} = \left(\frac{V}{V_s}\right)^2$$

where $V_s$ is the level-flight stall speed. At the stall speed itself you can pull only 1 g. At twice the stall speed you can pull 4 g before the wing stalls. Somewhere in between, the stall ceiling reaches the limit load. That speed is the **manoeuvring speed**, $V_A$:

$$V_A = V_s \times \sqrt{n_{\text{limit}}} = V_s \times \sqrt{3.8}\ \text{(normal category)}$$

Below $V_A$, if you pull the control column fully back, the wing stalls before it can reach 3.8 g: the stall acts as a safety valve. Above $V_A$, the same full pull reaches the limit load *before* the wing stalls, and the structure takes it.

The graph that shows all of this is the **V-n diagram**, or flight envelope: load factor up the side, airspeed along the bottom. The stall curve rises from the left; the limit load is a flat line across the top; the two meet at $V_A$. To the right the envelope is closed off by $V_{NE}$, the never-exceed speed.

![Where does the stall protect the structure, and where does it not?](diagram:v-n-diagram "Left of VA the stall curve is below +3.8 g, so a full pull stalls first; right of VA a pull or gust can reach the limit load before the stall.")

!!! example "Worked example: manoeuvring speed at maximum weight"
    Given: level-flight stall speed 52 kt at maximum weight, normal category (+3.8 g).

    1. Square root of the limit load factor: $\sqrt{3.8} = \mathbf{1.949}$.
    2. Manoeuvring speed: $V_A = 52 \times 1.949 = \mathbf{101\ kt}$.

    Below about 101 kt a full, abrupt pull stalls the wing before it can overstress the structure. Above it, the same input, or a sharp gust, can exceed 3.8 g.

### Why $V_A$ falls as the aeroplane gets lighter

This is the exam's favourite twist, and most candidates guess wrong. A lighter aeroplane has a lower stall speed (stall speed goes with the square root of weight, [RBKA 3.6](/notes/RBKA/3.6)). At any given airspeed, the lighter aeroplane is therefore further above its stall speed, so its wing can make more g before it stalls. The stall curve on the V-n diagram moves to the left, and it reaches the 3.8 g line at a **lower speed**. Put the other way: the wing's strength is fixed, and a lighter aeroplane needs less lift to reach the same g, so it gets there sooner.

![Why does VA fall when the aeroplane is lighter?](diagram:va-vs-weight "Lighter, the stall curve moves left and meets the +3.8 g limit sooner: 101 kt at maximum weight, about 90 kt at 80 percent of it.")

!!! example "Worked example: manoeuvring speed at a lighter weight"
    Given: the aeroplane above has $V_A$ 101 kt at its maximum weight of 1,100 kg. Today it weighs 880 kg.

    1. Weight ratio: $880 / 1100 = \mathbf{0.80}$.
    2. Square root: $\sqrt{0.80} = \mathbf{0.894}$.
    3. Manoeuvring speed today: $101 \times 0.894 = \mathbf{90\ kt}$ (90.3).

    Flown solo with light fuel, the aeroplane is protected by the stall only below about 90 kt, not the 101 kt you might remember from the POH. Many POHs publish $V_A$ at more than one weight for exactly this reason; use the figure for the weight you are at.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    "$V_A$ increases as weight decreases" is the distractor. It decreases. A lighter aeroplane stalls at a lower speed, so at any speed it can pull more g before the stall saves it, and it reaches the limit load at a lower speed.

### Gusts load the structure too

A gust does to the wing what a pull on the control column does. An up-gust suddenly adds an upward component to the relative airflow, which raises the angle of attack with no change of attitude, and the extra lift arrives as extra load factor. How much extra depends on how fast you are going: the lift increment from a given gust grows **roughly in proportion to the airspeed**. Fly through the same rough air at 120 kt instead of 90 kt and every bump hits the structure about a third harder. A lighter aeroplane also feels each gust more, because the same increase in lift is a larger fraction of its weight.

That is why the green arc on the ASI ends at $V_{NO}$, the maximum structural cruising speed, and the yellow arc above it is for **smooth air only**: up to $V_{NO}$ the structure is designed to take the certification gusts; above it, it is not. In turbulence, slow to the POH's turbulence penetration speed, usually close to $V_A$, so that a strong gust stalls the wing momentarily rather than overstressing it. [RBKA 4.2](/notes/RBKA/4.2) covers the arcs in detail.

??? check "Check yourself: why does flying below VA protect the structure from a full control input?"
    Below $V_A$ the most lift the wing can make before it stalls is less than 3.8 times the weight. A full pull stalls the wing first, and the stall caps the load factor below the limit.

### After a suspected overstress or a heavy landing

Overstress may leave no visible sign from the cockpit, and even on the ground the damage may be hidden inside the wing or behind fairings. Signs worth looking for are wrinkled or buckled skin (especially near the wing roots, the tail and the firewall), popped or loose rivets, cracked paint at joints, doors or panels that no longer fit, fuel or oil leaks, and, after a heavy landing, a bent or splayed undercarriage leg, a damaged tyre or wheel, or a scraped propeller tip or tail. Not seeing any of these does not mean the aeroplane is undamaged.

So the rule is simple: if you think you may have exceeded the limit load, or landed heavily enough to wonder, **say so**. Enter it as a defect in the maintenance release and do not fly the aeroplane again until a licensed engineer has inspected it and signed it off ([PFRA 2.9](/notes/PFRA/2.9)). The next pilot will not know what the aeroplane went through unless you tell them.

## What damage does to the stall

The wing's maximum lift depends on the air following its upper surface as far as possible before it separates, and that depends above all on a smooth, correctly shaped **leading edge**. The leading edge is where the airflow accelerates hardest around a tight curve, and a small change there has a large effect: the air has to follow the curve, and any dent, tear or lump trips it into turbulence. Picture a bird strike that has pushed in a section of the left leading edge.

**The stall speed increases.** Behind the dent the airflow separates earlier, at a lower angle of attack. The maximum lift coefficient falls, and the critical angle itself is reduced. The wing therefore stalls at a **higher speed** than the POH figure, and possibly by a large margin, because there is no way of knowing how much the damage has cost. Damage also adds **drag**, so the aeroplane needs more power to hold a given speed and climbs less well.

![What does a dented leading edge do to the airflow and the stall?](diagram:damaged-leading-edge-flow "The dent trips the flow so it separates early: lower maximum lift at a smaller critical angle, so the stall comes at a higher speed than the POH says.")

**The stall becomes asymmetric.** If only one wing is damaged, that wing reaches its reduced critical angle first. At the stall it drops sharply while the other wing is still flying: a pronounced **wing drop** and a strong tendency to **spin**, at a speed you would have thought was safe. The same is true at higher load factors: a damaged wing in a turn stalls first and earlier than normal.

**The warnings may fail.** The stall warning vane or reed is set for an undamaged wing; it triggers when the airflow at one point on the leading edge reaches a certain angle. If the damaged area stalls first, the warning may come late or not at all. The buffet, which comes from the separated wake striking the tail, may also come later or feel different. The warnings you have learned to trust in [RBKA 3.6](/notes/RBKA/3.6) can no longer be relied on.

The conclusion for the pilot: the published stall speeds and the stall warning are no longer valid. **Fly faster than normal, avoid high angles of attack, and avoid steep turns**, which raise the stall speed further.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The exam may offer "the stall speed is unchanged because the critical angle is a property of the aerofoil". The point is that damage *changes* the aerofoil. A damaged leading edge separates the flow earlier, reduces the maximum lift and the critical angle, and raises the stall speed, usually on one side only.

??? check "Check yourself: a bird has dented the right wing's leading edge. Describe the likely stall."
    It will come at a higher speed than the book figure, the right wing will stall first and drop, possibly with a tendency to spin, and the stall warning may sound late or not at all. Keep the speed well up and avoid steep turns.

## What damage does to controllability

The second half of the element asks how damage changes your ability to control the aeroplane. Think of the controls as surfaces that need clean airflow, intact hinges and free cables or pushrods to work, and of the aeroplane as something that flies straight only because it is symmetrical.

**Damage to a control surface or its linkage.** A dented aileron, elevator, rudder or trim tab, a bent hinge or a damaged cable can **reduce control authority**, cause a control to **jam**, or allow the surface to **vibrate**. A loose or damaged surface can **flutter**: an oscillation that grows rapidly with speed and can tear the surface off. Any new vibration after damage is a reason to slow down.

**Asymmetric damage.** A damaged wing makes less lift and more drag than the other, so the aeroplane wants to **roll and yaw** towards the damaged side, and you must hold aileron and rudder against it. How much you need depends on speed, in two competing ways. At **low speed** the controls themselves become weak, because their power depends on the airflow over them, while the lift loss on the damaged wing becomes relatively worse as the angle of attack rises: there comes a speed at which **full control deflection can no longer hold the wings level**. At high speed the controls are powerful, but the asymmetric forces and the risk of flutter and further damage grow. That is why the answer is a moderate speed found by test, not "as slow as possible" or "as fast as possible".

![How does control effectiveness change with airspeed?](diagram:control-effectiveness-vs-airspeed "At low airspeed, power off, all three controls are weak and sloppy; with speed they become effective and heavier.")

**Damaged flaps.** A flap damaged by a bird may jam, or may extend on one side only or further on one side than the other. **Asymmetric flap** makes more lift on the side with more flap, so the aeroplane rolls towards the side with less, worst at low speed when the ailerons are weakest, and it can be more than the ailerons can hold. If the flaps are damaged or you are unsure, a **flapless approach** is generally safer: leave them where they are.

**A damaged windscreen** can let the bird in. It can injure the pilot, reduce visibility, and add noise and wind blast that make communication and concentration hard. **A damaged propeller** causes severe vibration, because a propeller blade out of balance shakes the whole engine on its mounts; reduce power at once, and if the vibration is severe the engine may have to be shut down before it tears itself from its mounting.

**Structural deformation** from overstress or a strike can change the aeroplane's rigging, so the trim changes and the aeroplane handles differently from what you expect.

??? check "Check yourself: why might an aeroplane with a damaged wing be controllable at 80 kt but not at 60 kt?"
    The ailerons and rudder lose authority as the speed falls, while the damaged wing loses lift relative to the good one as the angle of attack rises. At some speed full control can no longer balance the asymmetry. That speed sets the minimum for the approach.

## Flying a damaged aeroplane home

The sequence of actions follows from everything above: keep flying, find out what you have, and then plan an approach that keeps well clear of the new, unknown limits.

1. **Fly the aeroplane.** Maintain control and a safe airspeed. Do not slow down.
2. **Assess** vibration, engine indications, control response, and what you can see of the damage.
3. If controllability is in doubt and height permits, carry out a **controllability check** at a safe height: configure the aeroplane as you intend to land, then slow down gradually until control starts to become difficult (for example, the aeroplane cannot be held level with substantial aileron) or the speed approaches the stall. That speed is the minimum safe speed for the damaged aeroplane. Plan the approach with a good **margin above it**.
4. **Avoid changing the configuration**, particularly flap, unless you must. If the flap is damaged, a flapless approach may be safer.
5. Fly a **flatter, wider, faster** approach with gentle bank angles, to a long runway if one is available, and land as soon as practicable.
6. Declare an emergency, **MAYDAY** or **PAN PAN** as appropriate ([PAKC 3.7](/notes/PAKC/3.7)).
7. After landing, report the damage and have the aircraft inspected before further flight.

!!! example "Worked example: choosing an approach speed after a bird strike"
    Given: after a bird strike on climb-out, the left wing leading edge is dented. Normal approach speed 65 kt.

    1. Climb to a safe height, 3,000 ft, and lower the flap you intend to land with.
    2. Slow gradually, watching for the point where control becomes difficult. At **65 kt** full right aileron is needed to stop a roll to the left: this is the minimum controllable speed today.
    3. Add a margin above that minimum: fly the approach at about **75 to 80 kt**.
    4. Plan for the consequences: the longest runway available, gentle bank, no stall warning trusted, and a longer landing roll from the higher speed.

    The normal approach speed would have put you exactly at the edge of control, with no margin for a gust or a turn.

![How do you choose an approach speed for a damaged aeroplane?](diagram:controllability-check "The speed where full control only just holds it (65 kt in the example) is the minimum; fly the approach well above it, about 75 to 80 kt.")

### Reporting

A bird strike is a **routine reportable matter**: a written report to the **ATSB** within 72 hours ([RFRC 2.7](/notes/RFRC/2.7); verify against the current Transport Safety Investigation Regulations). Serious damage or injury makes it an immediately reportable matter, by phone as soon as practicable. Separately, any damage or suspected overstress goes into the maintenance release as a defect, and the aeroplane stays on the ground until an engineer has cleared it.

## Bringing it together

You depart Jandakot's runway 06 on a still morning and pass through a flock of ibis at 300 ft. There is a thump on the left wing and the aeroplane yaws left; you hold it with rudder, keep the climb speed and keep climbing straight ahead. At 2,000 ft over the training area you can see a dent in the left leading edge, and the aeroplane needs right aileron to fly level. Think through what this lesson tells you. The left wing will stall first, at a speed above the book figure, without reliable warning. The aileron you are already holding will run out at some lower speed. You make a PAN PAN call, then do the controllability check in the landing configuration and find the limit at 65 kt, so you plan 75 to 80 kt, a long, flat final to the longest runway, gentle turns, no flap changes once set, and a longer landing roll. On the ground you report the strike to the ATSB, write it up in the maintenance release, and leave the aeroplane for the engineer.

## Key points

- **3.8.1 (a) Stall:** damage to a wing, especially its leading edge, separates the flow earlier, so the stall speed rises above the book figure, the damaged wing stalls first and drops with a spin tendency, and the stall warning may come late or not at all.
- **3.8.1 (b) Controllability:** damaged surfaces, hinges or cables reduce authority, jam or flutter; asymmetric damage must be held with aileron and rudder and becomes uncontrollable below some speed; asymmetric flap can exceed the ailerons; a damaged propeller vibrates and needs power reduced.
- **3.8.1 Response:** fly the aeroplane, keep the speed up, check controllability at a safe height in the landing configuration, approach well above that speed with gentle turns and no configuration changes, and report it.
- **3.8.1 Overstress:** above $V_A = V_s\sqrt{3.8}$ a full pull or a gust can exceed the limit load; $V_A$ falls as weight falls. A suspected overstress or heavy landing is a defect: write it up and have it inspected before further flight.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    The exam answer to structural damage is almost always: the stall speed **rises** and one wing may stall first, so keep the speed **up**, avoid steep turns and configuration changes, and establish a safe approach speed with a **controllability check** at a safe height. If flap is damaged, a flapless approach is generally safer. If $V_A$ appears, remember that it **decreases** with weight.
