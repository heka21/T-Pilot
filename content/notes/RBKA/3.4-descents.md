## What the exam expects (RBKA 3.4)

- **3.4.1** State what happens to the rate of descent, the angle of descent and the nose attitude when you change (a) the power or (b) the flap while holding the indicated airspeed constant.
- **3.4.2** State what a headwind or tailwind does to the glide path and the glide distance over the ground.
- **3.4.3** Explain why gliding at any indicated airspeed other than the recommended glide speed reduces the distance you can cover in still air.

## Why this matters

Every circuit ends in a descent, and on every approach you are solving the same problem: arrive at the runway threshold at the right speed and the right height. The tools are the throttle, the flap lever and the attitude, and this lesson is about what each one does. The other descent you train for is the one with no engine. Over the wheatbelt east of the scarp, with a field ahead and a wind blowing, the questions are which field you can reach and how to fly the glide to get there. The instinct to raise the nose to stretch the glide is strong and wrong, and the exam tests it. Expect "increase, decrease or no change" questions on power, flap and wind, and a calculation or two on glide distance.

## The forces in a glide

Start with the simplest descent: the engine at idle, the aeroplane gliding down a straight path at a steady speed. There is no thrust. Three forces act: **lift**, at right angles to the flight path; **drag**, back along the flight path; and **weight**, straight down.

With no thrust, something must keep the aeroplane moving forward against the drag. That something is weight. Because the path is tilted downwards, part of the weight acts **forwards along the path**, exactly the way part of your weight carries a bicycle down a hill without pedalling. In a steady glide that forward part of weight balances the drag, and the rest of the weight, across the path, is balanced by lift. Put another way, lift and drag together make a resultant that is exactly equal and opposite to the weight.

![What keeps a gliding aeroplane moving with no thrust?](diagram:forces-in-a-glide "Lift and drag together balance the weight; the glide angle is set by drag over lift, so the flattest glide is at the best L/D.")

Write the glide angle as $\gamma$. Along the path, $W\sin\gamma = D$; across it, $W\cos\gamma = L$. Divide one by the other and the weight cancels:

$$\tan\gamma = \frac{D}{L}$$

That is the most important line in this lesson. **The steepness of the glide depends only on the ratio of drag to lift.** Less drag for the lift you are making gives a flatter glide. The flattest glide of all comes when drag is smallest compared with lift, at the best **lift/drag ratio** (L/D) of [RBKA 3.1](/notes/RBKA/3.1). An aeroplane with an L/D of 9 glides 9 ft forward for every foot it loses.

Notice what is *not* in that line: weight. That fact answers a favourite exam question, and you will meet it again below.

## A descent with power: the same picture, plus thrust

Now add some power, as you do on a normal approach. Thrust acts forward along the path, so it shares the job of balancing drag with the forward part of the weight. Less of the weight is needed for that job, so the path can be less steep:

$$W\sin\gamma = D - T$$

More thrust, smaller $\sin\gamma$, a shallower path. Add enough thrust to equal the drag and $\gamma$ is zero: you are flying level. Add more and the path tilts up: you are climbing. A powered descent, level flight and a climb are the same balance of forces with different amounts of thrust.

That gives you the first half of element 3.4.1.

### Power at a constant IAS

Hold the indicated airspeed constant and change the power.

**Power increased.** The extra thrust means the path can be shallower for the same speed: the **angle of descent decreases**. A shallower path at the same speed means the aeroplane loses fewer feet each minute: the **rate of descent decreases**. And to stop the extra thrust from simply accelerating the aeroplane, you must raise the nose to hold the speed: the **attitude is higher**.

**Power reduced.** The reverse: with less thrust the aeroplane needs a steeper path to keep its speed up. The **angle and rate of descent increase**, and you lower the nose to hold the IAS: the **attitude is lower**.

| Change | Rate of descent | Angle of descent | Attitude |
|---|---|---|---|
| Power **increased** | **Decreases** | **Decreases** (shallower) | **Higher** (nose raised to hold the IAS) |
| Power **reduced** | **Increases** | **Increases** (steeper) | **Lower** (nose lowered to hold the IAS) |

This is the reason for the technique you are taught on approach: **attitude controls the airspeed, power controls the rate of descent**. With the attitude held for the approach speed, the throttle sets how steeply you come down and so where on the runway the path ends: your aim point. If you are getting low, the answer is power, with the attitude adjusted to hold the speed. If you are getting high, take power off. Raising the nose instead of adding power, when low, slows the aeroplane and, as you will see below, steepens the path.

??? check "Check yourself: on final at a constant 70 kt, you see that you are getting low. What do you do with the power and the attitude, and what happens to the rate of descent?"
    Add power and raise the nose slightly to hold 70 kt. The extra thrust lets the path be shallower at the same speed, so the rate of descent decreases and the path flattens towards the aim point. Raising the nose without power would only cost speed.

## Flap at a constant IAS

Now hold the power and the IAS constant and lower flap.

Flap adds **drag**, and in the glide equation more drag means a larger $D/L$ and so a **steeper path**. The best L/D with flap down is lower than with flap up ([RBKA 3.1](/notes/RBKA/3.1)), so the glide angle steepens. At the same airspeed down a steeper path, the aeroplane loses more height each minute: the **rate of descent increases**.

The attitude change is the more interesting part, because two things add together. First, flap increases the wing's camber, so it makes the lift it needs at a **smaller angle of attack**: the nose comes down relative to the flight path. Second, the flight path itself is now steeper. Both lower the nose, so with flap down the **nose attitude is noticeably lower**. That lower attitude is useful: it gives you a much better view of the runway over the nose on a flapped approach.

| Change | Rate of descent | Angle of descent | Attitude |
|---|---|---|---|
| Flap **lowered** (more flap) | **Increases** | **Increases** (steeper) | **Lower** (nose down) |
| Flap **raised** | Decreases | Decreases (flatter) | Higher |

![At the same IAS, what do power and flap do to the descent?](diagram:powered-descent-vs-glide "Power flattens the path and raises the nose; flap steepens it and lowers the nose. Attitude holds the speed, power sets the rate of descent.")

Look at the diagram as a summary of 3.4.1. The glide with no power and no flap is in the middle. Power lifts the path and the nose. Flap drops them both. On a real approach you use both together: flap to set a steep, controllable approach path with a good view, then small power changes to fine-tune where that path meets the runway.

!!! example "Worked example: correcting a glide approach"
    Given: a glide approach (power at idle) at 65 kt. Halfway down final, the aim point is moving down the windscreen: you are too high.

    1. Lower a further stage of flap while holding the attitude for **65 kt**.
    2. The extra drag steepens the path: the **angle of descent increases**.
    3. To hold 65 kt you lower the nose: the **attitude is lower**.
    4. On the VSI the **rate of descent increases**, and the aim point stops moving down the windscreen.

    Flap was the right tool for "too high" because power was already at idle. Had you instead been too low, the answer would have been **power**, not back pressure, while holding 65 kt.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    "Lowering flap at constant IAS raises the nose." It does not. More flap at the same speed needs a **lower** nose: the cambered wing makes the lift at a smaller angle of attack, and the path is steeper. (Many high-wing trainers pitch up for a moment when the flap first goes down, but the attitude you then hold for the same IAS is lower.)

??? check "Check yourself: you lower full flap at a constant 65 kt with the power unchanged. Rate of descent, angle of descent, attitude: up or down?"
    Rate of descent up, angle of descent up (steeper), attitude down. The extra drag lowers the L/D so the path steepens, and the cambered wing needs a smaller angle of attack, so the nose comes down on both counts.

## Gliding at the right speed

Now the engine has stopped, and the only question is how far you can go. In still air the answer comes straight from the glide angle. If the aeroplane glides L/D feet forward for every foot it descends, then from a height $h$:

$$\text{still-air glide distance} = h \times \frac{L}{D}$$

The ratio L/D is not a fixed property of the aeroplane. It depends on the angle of attack you fly the wing at, and it is greatest at one angle, about **4 degrees** on a basic low-speed wing ([RBKA 3.1](/notes/RBKA/3.1)). At a given weight, that angle corresponds to one indicated airspeed: the **recommended glide speed** (best glide speed) in your POH. Fly that speed and you get the best L/D, the flattest path and the greatest still-air distance.

!!! example "Worked example: still-air glide distance at the best L/D"
    Given: best L/D 9:1; engine failure at 3,000 ft AGL; gliding at the recommended speed in still air.

    1. Distance in feet: $3000 \times 9 = \mathbf{27{,}000\ ft}$.
    2. Convert to nautical miles: $27{,}000 \div 6076 \approx \mathbf{4.4\ NM}$.
    3. If mishandled at a speed giving only 7:1: $3000 \times 7 = 21{,}000$ ft, $21{,}000 \div 6076 \approx \mathbf{3.5\ NM}$.

    Flying the wrong speed has cost almost a nautical mile, which can be the difference between the good field and the one with the fence across it.

Try one before you open the solution: an aeroplane with a best L/D of 8:1 has an engine failure at 2,500 ft AGL. How far can it glide in still air?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    1. Distance in feet: $2500 \times 8 = \mathbf{20{,}000\ ft}$.
    2. Convert to nautical miles: $20{,}000 \div 6076 = \mathbf{3.3\ NM}$.

    That is the best case, at the recommended glide speed. Any other speed gives a lower L/D and a shorter glide.

### Why any other speed glides less far

Recall the drag curve from [BAKC 4.2](/notes/BAKC/4.2). Total drag is the sum of two kinds that behave in opposite ways. **Parasite drag**, the drag of pushing the aeroplane's shape through the air, rises with the square of the speed. **Induced drag**, the price of making lift, is greatest at low speed and high angle of attack, and falls as speed rises. Their sum is a U-shaped curve with its lowest point where the two are equal. That lowest point is the best L/D, and it is the best glide speed.

![How does total drag change with airspeed, and where is it least?](diagram:drag-curves-vs-airspeed "Parasite drag rises and induced drag falls with speed; total drag is least where they are equal: the speed for best L/D and the best glide.")

Now move away from that point in either direction.

**Faster than the best glide speed.** You lower the nose; the angle of attack is smaller, so induced drag falls, but parasite drag rises much faster. Total drag is larger for the same lift, so L/D falls and the glide steepens. You arrive at the ground sooner and nearer.

**Slower than the best glide speed.** You raise the nose; parasite drag falls, but the larger angle of attack brings much more **induced drag**. Again total drag is larger, L/D falls, and the glide steepens. You are also closer to the stall, in exactly the situation, low and with no engine, where a stall is least survivable.

Either way, drag is greater for the lift produced, so the aeroplane comes down more steeply and covers **less distance** in still air. The best glide speed is the bottom of the drag curve, and there is no speed either side of it that does better.

![Why does any other speed glide less far?](diagram:glide-speed-off-best "Slower or faster, L/D falls from 9:1 towards 7:1: from 3,000 ft that is about 3.5 NM instead of 4.4.")

### Why raising the nose does not stretch a glide

The field is a little further than you hoped, and every instinct says pull the nose up to make it. Here is what actually happens. For a few seconds the aeroplane does rise, or at least stops sinking: you are trading airspeed for height, converting kinetic energy into potential energy, just as a ball thrown upwards slows as it rises.

![Where does the height come from when you raise the nose?](diagram:energy-trade-zoom "Raising the nose without power trades speed for height, once: the total energy stays the same and only the share between speed and height changes.")

But that trade is a one-off, and it is not free: you have used up speed you had no way of replacing. Once the speed has decayed below the best glide speed, the aeroplane settles into a steady glide at the new, slower speed, with its higher induced drag and lower L/D. The path is now steeper than before. The few feet you gained in the zoom are soon lost, and from then on you are losing distance with every foot of height. The only way to get the greatest distance is to hold the best glide speed from the start, by attitude, and accept the path it gives you. If the field is out of reach at the best glide speed, it is out of reach: choose another one early.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    "Raise the nose to stretch the glide" is the classic distractor. Slower than the best glide speed, induced drag rises, the L/D falls and the glide **steepens**; you also move closer to the stall. Hold the best glide speed.

### Weight changes the speed, not the angle

Go back to $\tan\gamma = D/L$: weight is not in it. The best L/D occurs at the same angle of attack whatever the weight, so the **best glide angle and the still-air glide distance do not change with weight**.

What changes is the speed at which that angle of attack is reached. A heavier aeroplane needs more lift, so it must fly faster to reach the best L/D angle: the **best glide speed is higher when heavy** and lower when light, by the square root of the weight ratio. The heavy aeroplane goes down the same slope, only faster, so it has a higher rate of descent and reaches the ground sooner, at the same spot. The POH glide speed is usually for maximum weight; light, the right speed is a little lower.

![How does weight change the best glide speed but not the best L/D angle?](diagram:best-ld-and-critical-angle-speeds "About 4 degrees gives the best glide at every weight: 70 kt heavy, about 63 kt light. The angle stays; the speed moves.")

??? check "Check yourself: two identical aeroplanes, one heavy and one light, glide from the same height at their own best glide speeds in still air. Which goes further, and which arrives first?"
    They go the same distance, because the best L/D, and so the glide angle, does not depend on weight. The heavy one flies a faster best glide speed down the same slope, so it has a higher rate of descent and arrives first.

## Wind and the glide

The aeroplane glides **relative to the air**. Its airspeed, its rate of descent and its glide angle through the air are set by the forces above, and a steady wind does not change any of them. What a wind does is move the whole body of air, with the aeroplane in it, across the ground. So the **time** to reach the ground from a given height is the same in any wind, because the rate of descent is the same. The distance over the ground is that time multiplied by the **groundspeed**, and the groundspeed is what the wind changes.

Into a **headwind** the groundspeed is lower, so in the same time the aeroplane covers less ground: the glide path over the ground is **steeper** and the **glide distance over the ground is reduced**. With a **tailwind** the groundspeed is higher: the path over the ground is **flatter** and the distance is **increased**. The rate of descent is unchanged in both.

| Wind | Glide path over the ground | Glide distance over the ground | Rate of descent |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Headwind** | **Steeper** | **Reduced** | Unchanged |
| **Tailwind** | **Flatter** | **Increased** | Unchanged |

To calculate it: work out the time to the ground from the height and the rate of descent, then multiply by the groundspeed.

!!! example "Worked example: glide distance in a 20 kt wind"
    Given: gliding at 65 kt TAS from 2,000 ft AGL with a rate of descent of about 670 fpm.

    1. Time to the ground: $2000 \div 670 \approx \mathbf{3\ min}$.
    2. Still air: $3 \times 65 \div 60 = \mathbf{3.25\ NM}$.
    3. 20 kt headwind, groundspeed 45 kt: $3 \times 45 \div 60 = \mathbf{2.25\ NM}$.
    4. 20 kt tailwind, groundspeed 85 kt: $3 \times 85 \div 60 = \mathbf{4.25\ NM}$.

    The same aeroplane from the same height can reach a field 4 NM downwind but not one 3 NM upwind. The VSI reads about 670 fpm in every case.

![Can you reach the field? Try height, glide ratio and wind](widget:glide-range-vs-wind "A headwind shortens the distance over the ground and a tailwind stretches it; the rate of descent and the still-air distance do not change.")

Now a variant to try before opening the solution. An aeroplane glides at 70 kt TAS with a rate of descent of 700 fpm, from 2,800 ft AGL. How far does it glide over the ground in still air, into a 15 kt headwind, and with a 15 kt tailwind?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    1. Time to the ground: $2800 \div 700 = \mathbf{4\ min}$, in every case.
    2. Still air, groundspeed 70 kt: $4 \times 70 \div 60 = \mathbf{4.67\ NM}$.
    3. 15 kt headwind, groundspeed $70 - 15 = 55$ kt: $4 \times 55 \div 60 = \mathbf{3.67\ NM}$.
    4. 15 kt tailwind, groundspeed $70 + 15 = 85$ kt: $4 \times 85 \div 60 = \mathbf{5.67\ NM}$.

    Each 15 kt of wind for 4 minutes moves the landing point by exactly 1 NM: $15 \times 4 \div 60 = 1$. The wind shifts the whole circle of reachable ground downwind.

That last line is a useful way to picture it. In still air the ground you can reach is a circle around you. A wind slides the whole circle downwind by the wind speed times the glide time. A field **downwind** is reachable from further away, though you will land with a high groundspeed; a field **upwind** must be closer. Wherever you can, land into wind: the low groundspeed at touchdown matters more in a forced landing than anywhere else.

![Which fields can you reach with the wind blowing?](diagram:glide-circle-and-wind "The wind slides the whole glide circle downwind (1 NM for 15 kt over 4 minutes); its size does not change.")

A refinement found in some textbooks: in a strong headwind, flying slightly **faster** than the best glide speed improves the distance over the ground, because it shortens the time the headwind has to push you back; in a tailwind, slightly **slower** lets the wind carry you further. The exam focus is the basic effect in the table, and for practical flying the best glide speed in the POH is the speed to fly.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    "A headwind increases the rate of descent." It does not: the rate of descent is set by the aeroplane's glide through the air and is unchanged by a steady wind. A headwind reduces the **groundspeed**, which steepens the path over the ground and reduces the **distance**.

??? check "Check yourself: gliding at the best glide speed, you turn from a tailwind into a headwind. What happens to the rate of descent and the glide distance over the ground?"
    The rate of descent is unchanged, because the glide through the air has not changed. The groundspeed falls, so the path over the ground steepens and the distance you can cover over the ground is reduced.

## Bringing it together

Cruising at 3,000 ft AGL over farmland east of York, the engine stops. You hold the attitude for the best glide speed and trim: no other speed will give you more distance, and raising the nose would only steepen the path. The wind is 15 kt from the west. You have about four minutes in the air whatever you do. Westward, into the wind, the reachable distance shrinks; eastward it grows, but you would land fast downwind. You choose a large paddock a little south-west, well inside the into-wind range, so you arrive high rather than low. On base you are clearly high, so you lower flap in stages: each stage steepens the path, lowers the nose and increases the rate of descent, all at the same IAS. Had you been low, there would have been no power to add and no amount of back pressure would have helped. That is why you chose a field within easy reach, and why the extra height was something to manage with flap rather than a problem.

## Key points

- **3.4.1** At a constant IAS: more power reduces the rate and angle of descent and raises the nose; less power increases them and lowers the nose. More flap increases the rate and angle of descent and lowers the nose; less flap does the reverse. On approach, attitude holds the speed and power sets the rate of descent.
- **3.4.2** The aeroplane glides through the air, so a steady wind does not change the rate of descent or the time to the ground. A headwind lowers the groundspeed: steeper path over the ground, less distance. A tailwind raises it: flatter path, more distance. Distance = time to the ground × groundspeed.
- **3.4.3** In a glide, $\tan\gamma = D/L$: the flattest glide is at the best L/D, which occurs at one angle of attack and so at one IAS, the recommended glide speed. Faster brings more parasite drag, slower more induced drag; either way L/D falls, the glide steepens and the still-air distance (height × L/D) shrinks. Raising the nose does not stretch a glide. Weight changes the best glide speed, not the angle or the still-air distance.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    For power and flap at constant IAS, think "power flattens and raises the nose; flap steepens and lowers it". For wind, the **rate of descent is unchanged** and only the distance over the ground changes. For glide distance, multiply the height by the L/D and divide by 6,076 for nautical miles, or multiply the time to the ground by the groundspeed. Any option that stretches a glide by raising the nose, or says weight changes the glide distance, is wrong.
