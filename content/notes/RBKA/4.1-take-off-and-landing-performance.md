## What the exam expects (RBKA 4.1)

- **4.1.1** State whether each of the following increases or decreases take-off, landing and take-off climb performance: (a) runway slope, (b) a wet runway surface, (c) a slushy runway surface.

## Why this matters

The performance charts in your POH are drawn for a level, dry, paved runway. Very few of the strips you will use once you leave the training circuit are all three. Farm strips in the wheatbelt slope, sometimes noticeably, and many are gravel or grass; winter rain soaks grass strips across the south-west for weeks; and a summer thunderstorm can leave standing water on a sealed runway for an hour. Each of these moves the take-off and landing distances, sometimes by more than the margin you allowed. [BAKC 6.2](/notes/BAKC/6.2) dealt with the air (density height and wind); this lesson deals with the ground. The exam asks a simple question for each factor, increase or decrease, but the answer is only reliable if you can see the force behind it.

## What decides the distances

Start with what happens on a take-off roll. The aeroplane starts at rest and must reach its lift-off speed. What accelerates it is the **net force** along the runway: propeller thrust, minus aerodynamic drag, minus the **rolling resistance** of the wheels on the surface. The bigger the net force, the faster the aeroplane accelerates, and the shorter the run. The net force on a light aeroplane is only a modest fraction of its weight, so anything that takes even a small bite out of it has a noticeable effect on the distance.

A landing roll is the same problem in reverse. The aeroplane arrives at its touchdown speed and must be brought to rest. What slows it is aerodynamic drag, rolling resistance and, above all, the **braking force** from the tyres gripping the surface. The bigger the decelerating force, the shorter the roll.

Slope and surface change these forces in different ways, which is why they can affect take-off and landing in opposite directions. Hold that picture and every row of the table at the end of this lesson becomes something you can work out rather than memorise.

The third quantity the syllabus names, the **take-off climb**, is what happens after lift-off: the climb gradient from the end of the take-off distance over whatever lies beyond the runway. Once the wheels leave the ground the surface no longer acts on the aeroplane, so slope and surface affect the climb only indirectly: by where it starts, and by what the ground ahead is doing.

## Runway slope

### What the slope does to the forces

Imagine pushing a trolley up a ramp. Part of its weight now acts backwards along the ramp, and you have to push harder to accelerate it. An aeroplane on an upsloping runway is the same: a component of its weight acts down the slope, against the thrust. For the gentle slopes of real runways that component is very nearly the weight multiplied by the slope as a fraction, so on a **2 percent** upslope about 2 percent of the aeroplane's weight is pulling it backwards.

Two percent of the weight sounds small, but remember that the net accelerating force is itself only a fraction of the weight. Losing 2 percent of the weight to the slope takes a real share of what was accelerating you, and as a rule of thumb a **2 percent upslope can add around 10 percent to the take-off distance**. On a downslope the same component acts forwards and helps the thrust.

![What does a 2 percent upslope take away on the take-off roll?](diagram:slope-force-on-runway "About 2 percent of the weight acts back down the slope, a real share of the modest net force accelerating you: about 10 percent more take-off distance.")

### Take-off

**Uphill: take-off distance increases.** The slope force opposes the thrust throughout the run, so the aeroplane accelerates more slowly and needs more runway to reach lift-off speed.

**Downhill: take-off distance decreases.** Gravity adds to the thrust, so the aeroplane accelerates faster and lifts off sooner.

### Landing

**Uphill: landing distance decreases.** Now the slope force acts against the aeroplane's motion, adding to the brakes and the drag. The aeroplane slows faster and the landing roll is shorter.

**Downhill: landing distance increases.** The slope force pushes the aeroplane along the runway, working against the brakes. Downhill landings also tend to **float**, because the runway falls away beneath the aeroplane in the flare. Combine a downslope with a tailwind and you have the classic overrun.

![Why land uphill and take off downhill?](diagram:runway-slope-take-off-and-landing "Uphill, part of the thrust goes into climbing the slope (about +10 percent take-off distance for 2 percent), but the slope helps you stop when landing.")

### Take-off climb

Once airborne, the aeroplane climbs at the same rate and the same gradient through the air whether the runway sloped or not: slope has **no effect on the aeroplane's own climb**. What changes is the ground. Taking off uphill, the aeroplane lifts off later, and the terrain beyond the runway usually keeps rising, so the climb gradient **relative to the rising ground ahead is reduced** and obstacle clearance suffers. Taking off downhill, the aeroplane lifts off earlier and the ground beyond usually falls away, which **helps obstacle clearance**.

### Slope or wind?

On a sloping strip with a light wind, the usual choice is to **land uphill and take off downhill**: each uses the slope to help. When the wind is stronger, the two compete, and the wind usually wins unless the slope is steep. The CASA take-off and landing charts include a slope correction, and the POH may too; use them to compare the two directions with the actual wind rather than guessing.

!!! example "Worked example: upslope on a farm strip"
    Given: the chart gives a take-off distance of 520 m for today's weight, density height and wind on a level dry runway. The strip has a 2 percent upslope in the direction you must use, and 600 m is available.

    1. Rule-of-thumb correction for a 2 percent upslope: $+10\%$.
    2. Corrected take-off distance: $520 \times 1.10 = \mathbf{572\ m}$.
    3. Margin: $600 - 572 = \mathbf{28\ m}$, about 5 percent of the runway.

    That is too thin to accept. Wait for a cooler part of the day, reduce the weight, or use the other direction if the wind allows. Where the chart or POH gives its own slope correction, use that figure instead of the rule of thumb.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Slope works **opposite ways** for take-off and landing. The exam pairs them to catch candidates who learn "uphill is bad": uphill lengthens the take-off but **shortens** the landing; downhill shortens the take-off but **lengthens** the landing.

??? check "Check yourself: does a downhill take-off change the aeroplane's rate of climb once it is airborne?"
    No. Once airborne the slope no longer acts on the aeroplane, so its rate and gradient of climb through the air are unchanged. What improves is clearance over the ground, because the aeroplane lifts off sooner and the terrain beyond usually falls away.

## A wet runway surface

Water changes two things: how hard the wheels can **grip** the surface, which governs braking and steering, and how hard the wheels have to **push** to roll through it, which governs acceleration. On a wet sealed runway the first matters most; on wet grass both do.

### Wet sealed runway

**Landing distance increases.** A film of water between tyre and bitumen acts as a lubricant and reduces the friction available for braking, often by a large amount. The brakes can only stop the aeroplane as hard as the tyres can grip, so the landing roll grows, often by **15 percent or more**. Brake harder than the grip allows and the wheels lock and skid, which stops you even less well and costs directional control. Crosswind control on the ground is also reduced, because the same lack of grip makes it harder for the tyres to resist the sideways push of the wind.

If there is **standing water**, the tyre may ride up on a wedge of water and lose contact with the runway altogether: **aquaplaning** (hydroplaning). An aquaplaning tyre has almost no braking and no steering, and the aeroplane can slide off the side of the runway in a crosswind.

**Take-off distance: little change.** A thin film of water adds little rolling resistance on a sealed surface, so the take-off run hardly changes, and braking does not matter on a normal take-off. (A rejected take-off is another story: the stopping distance is longer.)

![How much crosswind is too much on a wet runway?](widget:crosswind-component "On a wet runway there is less grip to hold the aeroplane straight, so leave more margin below the POH's demonstrated crosswind.")

### Wet grass

**Take-off distance increases.** Grass already has more rolling resistance than bitumen. When it is wet and the soil underneath softens, the wheels sink in and have to push through the surface, so the rolling resistance rises further, the net accelerating force falls, and the take-off run lengthens.

**Landing distance increases.** Wet grass is slippery, the way a wet lawn is under your shoes, so braking is poor and the landing roll is longer. The extra rolling resistance helps a little, but not enough to make up for brakes that barely grip.

![How does a real take-off chart handle grass and slope?](workbook:fig5 "What to notice: the surface panel (fourth from the top, on the sideways page) has lines for hard sealed, short dry grass, short wet or long dry grass, and long wet grass; the slope panel above it applies the runway slope the same way.")

### Take-off climb

Water has **no direct effect on the climb** once the aeroplane is airborne. But on wet grass the aeroplane lifts off further down the runway, so the climb begins later and with less distance to the obstacles beyond: **obstacle clearance is reduced**.

!!! example "Worked example: landing on a wet sealed runway"
    Given: the chart gives a landing distance of 450 m on a dry sealed runway for today's conditions. A shower has just passed and the runway is wet. The runway is 550 m long.

    1. Rule-of-thumb wet correction: at least $+15\%$.
    2. Corrected landing distance: $450 \times 1.15 = \mathbf{518\ m}$ (517.5).
    3. Margin: $550 - 518 = \mathbf{32\ m}$.

    A margin that small leaves nothing for a fast approach or a long float. Wait for the runway to dry, or divert to a longer one. Where the POH gives a wet-runway factor, use it in place of the rule of thumb.

Try one before opening the solution: with no surface correction the take-off distance from a grass strip is 480 m. After heavy winter rain the strip is soft and wet. Which way does the take-off distance go, and what happens to the landing distance on the same strip?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    Both **increase**. The take-off run lengthens because soft, wet grass adds rolling resistance and the aeroplane accelerates more slowly, so it will need more than 480 m. The landing roll lengthens because the brakes barely grip on wet grass. The POH's grass and wet-surface notes give the factors to apply; if it gives none for a soft, waterlogged strip, treat that as a reason not to use it.

??? check "Check yourself: why does a wet sealed runway lengthen the landing but barely change the take-off?"
    Landing depends on braking, and water lubricates the tyre-surface contact so the brakes can stop the aeroplane less hard. Take-off depends on acceleration, and a thin film of water adds little rolling resistance on a sealed surface.

## A slushy runway surface

**Slush** is a mixture of water and snow or ice. You will not meet it often in Western Australia, but the same physics applies to any deep, soft contaminant on the runway: **standing water** after a thunderstorm, deep **mud** on a waterlogged strip. The exam treats slush as the most severe surface of all, and the reasons show why.

### Take-off

A wheel rolling through slush has to push it out of the way, a drag called **displacement drag**, and the spray it throws up strikes the underside of the aeroplane and the flaps, called **impingement drag**. Unlike ordinary rolling resistance, both **grow with speed**. As the aeroplane accelerates, the slush drag climbs while the propeller's thrust falls away, so the net accelerating force shrinks just when it matters most. **The take-off distance increases greatly**, and the aeroplane may stop accelerating short of lift-off speed and be unable to take off at all.

![Why can slush stop an aeroplane reaching lift-off speed?](diagram:slush-drag-vs-thrust "Slush drag grows with speed while thrust falls; where they meet, short of lift-off speed, the aeroplane stops accelerating.")

### Landing

On landing, slush and standing water bring the worst of both worlds. The tyres may **aquaplane** and lose contact with the runway, removing braking and steering together, and when they do grip, the grip is very poor. **The landing distance increases greatly**, and directional control is uncertain. The slush thrown up by the wheels can also **damage the flaps** and the underside of the aeroplane.

### Take-off climb

Once airborne the aeroplane's own climb is **unchanged**, but it starts much further along the runway, if it starts at all, so obstacle clearance is reduced. Slush packed into the wheel fairings and on the flaps adds weight and drag too.

Most light-aeroplane POHs give **no figures** for slush or standing water. That absence is itself the answer: the manufacturer has not shown that the aeroplane can operate from such a surface, and you should not try.

??? check "Check yourself: why can slush stop an aeroplane reaching lift-off speed, when a long dry runway never would?"
    Slush drag grows with speed, while propeller thrust falls with speed. At some speed below lift-off the two can balance, and the aeroplane stops accelerating however much runway is left.

## The whole picture

Now the table that the exam is built on. Each row is the result of the forces described above, so you should be able to explain every entry.

| Condition | Take-off distance | Landing distance | Take-off climb |
|---|---|---|---|
| Uphill | Increases | Decreases | Reduced relative to rising ground |
| Downhill | Decreases | Increases | Unchanged, terrain falls away |
| Wet sealed | Little change | Increases | Unchanged |
| Wet grass | Increases | Increases | Unchanged but starts later |
| Slushy or waterlogged | Increases greatly | Increases greatly | Unchanged but starts later |

![Slope and surface: longer or shorter?](diagram:runway-slope-and-surface-effects "Slope works opposite ways for take-off and landing; wet and slushy surfaces lengthen both, and slush can stop you reaching lift-off speed at all.")

These factors add to the ones from [BAKC 6.2](/notes/BAKC/6.2). A hot afternoon at a high, sloping, wet grass strip stacks density height, slope and surface penalties one on top of another, and the corrections multiply.

## Bringing it together

In July you plan a trip to a friend's farm strip near York: 700 m of grass with a 2 percent slope down towards the east, and a westerly of 5 kt forecast after a week of rain. For landing, the light wind and the slope agree: land uphill towards the west, into wind, and let the slope help the brakes on wet grass. For the departure, the light wind and the slope disagree. Taking off downhill to the east, the slope helps acceleration and the ground falls away beyond the strip, but you accept a 5 kt tailwind. Taking off uphill to the west, you have the headwind, but the upslope adds about 10 percent and the hill beyond reduces your climb margin over the ground. You work both through the POH charts with the wet-grass correction applied, and take the direction with the bigger margin. You also walk the strip first: if it is soft and waterlogged, with the wheels sinking in, no chart figure applies, and the right decision is to wait for it to dry.

## Key points

- **4.1.1 (a)** Slope: uphill lengthens the take-off and shortens the landing; downhill does the reverse (about 10 percent extra take-off distance for a 2 percent upslope, rule of thumb). The climb through the air is unchanged; clearance over rising ground is reduced, over falling ground improved.
- **4.1.1 (b)** Wet: a wet sealed runway lengthens the landing (often 15 percent or more) with little effect on the take-off; wet grass lengthens both. The climb is unchanged but may start later.
- **4.1.1 (c)** Slush or standing water: greatly lengthens both take-off and landing, may prevent lift-off, risks aquaplaning and flap damage. No POH figures means do not operate.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    The exam asks "increase or decrease" for each factor. Slope works opposite ways for take-off and landing: uphill is bad for take-off and good for landing. Wet and slushy surfaces are bad for both, and slush is the worst. If an option says a surface condition changes the aeroplane's rate of climb once airborne, it is wrong: the climb starts later, but the aeroplane climbs the same.
