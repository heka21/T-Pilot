## What the exam expects (POPC 2.6)

- **2.6.1** Describe the situations in which a pilot would want to know the equi-time point or the point of no return, and recognise one when the exam describes it.
- **2.6.2** With a constant cruise level and TAS, find where the equi-time point lies between two points, in still air and with wind.
- **2.6.3** Given the fuel on board and the groundspeeds, work out which of continuing to the destination, returning to departure or diverting to an alternate needs the least fuel, reserves included, and which options are available at all.

## Why this matters

Fly east from Jandakot towards Kalgoorlie and after Merredin the wheatbelt gives way to salt lakes and scrub, with a handful of strips 50 nm or more apart. Fly up the coast towards Geraldton, or out to the Abrolhos, and there are stretches where the only flat ground is the beach or the sea. On legs like these two questions sit in the back of a good pilot's mind the whole way: if something goes wrong now, is it quicker to go on or to go back, and have I still got the fuel to go back at all? The first question is answered by the **equi-time point**, the second by the **point of no return**, and both are worked out on the ground before you go, when the arithmetic is easy. The third skill in this subtopic is the in-flight version: something has changed, you have three choices, and you need to know in two minutes which ones your fuel allows. The exam asks for the ETP as a number, asks you to tell the ETP from the PNR, and gives you a fuel comparison to complete.

## Two questions, two points

Picture yourself an hour and a half out of Jandakot on the way to Kalgoorlie, with an easterly wind on the nose. Two different kinds of trouble could come up.

In the first, a passenger becomes ill, or the oil pressure starts to sag, or smoke drifts from behind the panel. Fuel is not the issue; **time** is. You want to be on the ground as soon as possible, and the question is simply whether the destination ahead or the aerodrome behind is closer in minutes. The point on your track where the two are exactly equal is the **equi-time point** (ETP): from there it takes the **same time** to continue to the destination as to return to the departure point. Before the ETP, turning back is quicker; after it, continuing is quicker. The same idea works between any two aerodromes, for example two alternates either side of a long leg, but the exam's version is departure and destination.

In the second, the destination's weather has gone bad: a TAF amendment brings thunderstorms to Kalgoorlie, or the cloud on the horizon is lower than forecast and you will not get through. Now the question is not which is quicker but whether you can **get back at all** with your fixed reserve still in the tanks. The furthest point along the track from which you can return to the departure aerodrome with the reserve intact is the **point of no return** (PNR). Before it, returning is an option. After it, you are committed: whatever happens ahead, you will be landing somewhere ahead, because you no longer have the fuel to come home.

The difference is the whole of element 2.6.1. The ETP is about **time** and does not care how much fuel you have. The PNR is about **fuel** and moves every time your fuel changes.

When do you need them? Whenever going on and going back are both real options and neither is obviously right:

- a long leg over country with no usable aerodromes between departure and destination, which describes much of inland WA;
- a leg over water, or along a remote coast, where a forced landing would be a ditching;
- a destination with a marginal TAF, where the plan is "go and look, and turn back if it is not good enough";
- a flight planned with little fuel margin, where you want to know in advance where the last chance to turn back is;
- any flight carrying a passenger whose health might turn, where the time to the nearest aerodrome matters.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The exam's favourite distractor swaps the definitions. The **ETP** is about **equal time** to two places and is independent of fuel. The **PNR** is the furthest point from which you can **return with the reserve intact**, so it depends on fuel. If an option says "the PNR is the point where the time to go on equals the time to go back", it is describing the ETP.

??? check "Check yourself: you load an extra 30 L before departure. Does the ETP move? Does the PNR move?"
    The ETP does not move: it depends only on the distance and the two groundspeeds, and fuel is not part of it. The PNR moves further from departure, because more fuel means more endurance for the out-and-back trip.

## The equi-time point in still air

Start with the simplest case: no wind, a constant cruise level and a constant TAS. Your groundspeed is then the same in both directions, because with no wind groundspeed equals TAS whichever way you point.

Now think about what "equal time" means when the speed is the same both ways. Time is distance divided by speed. If the speed is the same, equal times need equal distances. The point where the distance on equals the distance back is the **midpoint** of the track. So in still air the ETP is **halfway**, whatever the distance and whatever the TAS.

That is element 2.6.2 as the MOS states it, and it is worth pausing on why it feels obvious: in still air the aeroplane does not care which way it is going. The interesting part is what happens when it does.

??? check "Check yourself: in still air, a 260 nm leg is flown at 120 kt TAS. Where is the ETP, and would it move if the TAS were 100 kt?"
    At 130 nm, the midpoint. In still air the groundspeed is the same both ways, so equal times need equal distances; changing the TAS changes both groundspeeds equally and the ETP stays halfway.

## Why wind moves the ETP into wind

Add a headwind on the way out. Your groundspeed outbound, **GS on**, drops below the TAS; if you turned round, the same wind would become a tailwind and your groundspeed home, **GS home**, would rise above the TAS.

![What does a headwind or tailwind do to your groundspeed?](diagram:groundspeed-headwind-tailwind "The same TAS gives a low groundspeed into wind and a high one downwind: the trip home with the wind behind you is faster than the trip on.")

Now stand at the midpoint. The distances on and back are equal, but you would fly home faster than you would fly on. So at the midpoint, going back is quicker. To make the times equal you must move further along the track, adding distance to the fast trip home and taking it off the slow trip on, until the extra distance home exactly balances its higher speed. The ETP has moved **towards the destination**, which is the direction the wind is coming from. Reverse the wind and the same reasoning moves the ETP back towards departure, again towards where the wind comes from. Hence the rule: **the ETP moves into wind**.

![Where does the ETP sit with a headwind, a tailwind and no wind?](diagram:etp-pnr-geometry "Still air puts the ETP at the midpoint; a headwind outbound slides it towards the destination; a tailwind outbound slides it back towards departure.")

### Deriving the formula

Call the total distance $D$ and the distance from departure to the ETP $x$. From the ETP the distance back home is $x$ and the distance on to the destination is $D - x$. The ETP is where the two times are equal:

$$\frac{x}{GS_\text{home}} = \frac{D - x}{GS_\text{on}}$$

Cross-multiply:

$$x \times GS_\text{on} = (D - x) \times GS_\text{home} = D \times GS_\text{home} - x \times GS_\text{home}$$

Collect the $x$ terms on the left:

$$x \times (GS_\text{on} + GS_\text{home}) = D \times GS_\text{home}$$

$$\text{ETP distance from departure} = \frac{D \times GS_\text{home}}{GS_\text{on} + GS_\text{home}}$$

Read the formula as a check on the reasoning. In still air $GS_\text{home} = GS_\text{on}$, the fraction is $\tfrac{1}{2}$ and the ETP is at $D/2$. With a headwind outbound $GS_\text{home}$ is the larger of the two, the fraction is more than a half, and the ETP is beyond the midpoint. The groundspeed **home** goes on top; that is the one detail to get right.

The formula has a built-in **mental check**: at the ETP the two times must be equal. Once you have $x$, divide it by $GS_\text{home}$ and divide $D - x$ by $GS_\text{on}$. If the two times do not agree, you have put the wrong groundspeed on top.

!!! example "Worked example: ETP with a 20 kt headwind outbound"
    Given: track 180 nm, TAS 110 kt, wind a 20 kt headwind outbound (so a 20 kt tailwind on the way home).

    1. Groundspeed on: $110 - 20 = \mathbf{90\ kt}$.
    2. Groundspeed home: $110 + 20 = \mathbf{130\ kt}$.
    3. ETP from departure: $\dfrac{180 \times 130}{90 + 130} = \dfrac{23{,}400}{220} = \mathbf{106\ nm}$ (106.4).
    4. Check, time home from the ETP: $106.4 \div 130 = \mathbf{0.82\ h}$, about 49 min.
    5. Check, time on from the ETP: $(180 - 106.4) \div 90 = 73.6 \div 90 = \mathbf{0.82\ h}$, about 49 min.

    The ETP is 16 nm beyond the 90 nm midpoint. If trouble comes at 100 nm, turning back is still the quicker way to the ground, even though the destination is only 80 nm away.

!!! example "Worked example: ETP on a 200 nm leg with a 10 kt headwind"
    Given: track 200 nm, TAS 110 kt, 10 kt headwind outbound.

    1. Groundspeed on: $110 - 10 = \mathbf{100\ kt}$.
    2. Groundspeed home: $110 + 10 = \mathbf{120\ kt}$.
    3. ETP from departure: $\dfrac{200 \times 120}{100 + 120} = \dfrac{24{,}000}{220} = \mathbf{109\ nm}$ (109.1).
    4. Check: on, $(200 - 109.1) \div 100 = 90.9 \div 100 = \mathbf{0.91\ h}$; home, $109.1 \div 120 = \mathbf{0.91\ h}$. Equal, so the right groundspeed is on top.

    A 10 kt wind moves the ETP 9 nm from the midpoint; the 20 kt wind in the first example moved it 16 nm on a shorter leg. The stronger the wind relative to the TAS, the further the ETP moves.

Now try one in the other direction before opening the solution: a 240 nm leg, TAS 120 kt, with a **15 kt tailwind** outbound. Where is the ETP, and on which side of the midpoint?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    Groundspeed on $= 120 + 15 = 135$ kt; groundspeed home $= 120 - 15 = 105$ kt. ETP $= \dfrac{240 \times 105}{135 + 105} = \dfrac{25{,}200}{240} = 105$ nm from departure, **15 nm short of the 120 nm midpoint**. Check: home, $105 \div 105 = 1.0$ h; on, $135 \div 135 = 1.0$ h. The tailwind outbound is a headwind on the way home, so the ETP moves back towards departure, into wind.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Two errors account for most wrong ETP answers. Putting $GS_\text{on}$ on top instead of $GS_\text{home}$ gives the mirror-image answer, the same distance on the wrong side of the midpoint. And forgetting that the ETP moves **into** wind: with a headwind outbound it is **beyond** halfway, not short of it. The time check catches both.

??? check "Check yourself: why does the ETP move towards the destination when there is a headwind outbound?"
    Because the trip home would be downwind and therefore faster. At the midpoint returning would take less time than continuing, so the point of equal time has to be further out, where the longer distance home balances its higher groundspeed.

## The point of no return depends on endurance

The ETP needed only distances and groundspeeds. The PNR needs one more thing: how long the aeroplane can stay in the air on the fuel it is allowed to use. That figure is the **safe endurance** for the out-and-back, written $E$: the fuel on board, less the taxi allowance, less the fixed reserve and any holding you insist on keeping for the return, divided by the fuel flow. The reserve is taken out first because it is not yours to spend; the PNR is defined as the point from which you return **with the reserve intact**.

Think about flying out to the PNR and straight back. The fuel you burn going out plus the fuel you burn coming back must exactly equal the fuel available. At a constant fuel flow, fuel is proportional to time, so the same statement in time is: the **time out plus the time back equals the endurance**. And the distance out to the PNR is the same as the distance back from it. Those two facts fix the PNR.

### Deriving the formula

Let $t_\text{out}$ be the time from departure to the PNR and $t_\text{home}$ the time back. Then:

$$t_\text{out} + t_\text{home} = E$$

$$GS_\text{out} \times t_\text{out} = GS_\text{home} \times t_\text{home} \quad \text{(same distance both ways)}$$

From the second line, $t_\text{home} = t_\text{out} \times GS_\text{out} \div GS_\text{home}$. Put that into the first:

$$t_\text{out} \times \left(1 + \frac{GS_\text{out}}{GS_\text{home}}\right) = E \quad\Rightarrow\quad t_\text{out} = \frac{E \times GS_\text{home}}{GS_\text{out} + GS_\text{home}}$$

That is the **time to the PNR**. Multiply by the groundspeed out to get the distance:

$$\text{PNR distance from departure} = \frac{E \times GS_\text{out} \times GS_\text{home}}{GS_\text{out} + GS_\text{home}}$$

The mental check this time is fuel, not time: the time out and the time back must add up to $E$.

![How is the fuel available split between the trip out to the PNR and the trip home?](diagram:pnr-fuel-out-and-back "Fuel out plus fuel back uses everything except the reserve; with a headwind outbound most of it is spent going out.")

!!! example "Worked example: PNR with 2.5 hours of fuel available"
    Given: TAS 110 kt, 10 kt headwind outbound, so $GS_\text{out} = 100$ kt and $GS_\text{home} = 120$ kt. Safe endurance after the taxi allowance and the reserve: $E = 2.5$ h.

    1. PNR distance: $\dfrac{2.5 \times 100 \times 120}{100 + 120} = \dfrac{30{,}000}{220} = \mathbf{136\ nm}$ (136.4).
    2. Time to the PNR: $136.4 \div 100 = \mathbf{1.36\ h}$, about **82 min** after setting course.
    3. Time back from the PNR: $136.4 \div 120 = \mathbf{1.14\ h}$, about 68 min.
    4. Check: $1.36 + 1.14 = \mathbf{2.50\ h}$, the whole endurance.

    If you have not turned back by about 82 minutes after setting course, the return is no longer an option with the reserve intact. In still air the same fuel would have taken you to $2.5 \times 110 \div 2 = 137.5$ nm: the headwind pulled the PNR about a mile closer to home.

That last observation is worth a second look, because it is the counter-intuitive part. A tailwind outbound does **not** push the PNR further out. Swap the groundspeeds (120 kt out, 100 kt home) and the formula gives exactly the same 136 nm, because $GS_\text{out}$ and $GS_\text{home}$ appear symmetrically. What changes is the time: at 120 kt out you reach it after $136.4 \div 120 = 1.14$ h, about 68 minutes, sooner than with the headwind. Any wind, from either direction, brings the PNR closer than in still air, because you always spend longer flying into the wind on one leg than you save flying with it on the other.

Now try one before opening the solution: usable fuel 117 L after taxi, fixed reserve 18 L, fuel flow 36 L/h, TAS 100 kt, 20 kt headwind outbound. How far out is the PNR, and how long after setting course do you reach it?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    Fuel available $= 117 - 18 = 99$ L, so $E = 99 \div 36 = 2.75$ h. $GS_\text{out} = 80$ kt, $GS_\text{home} = 120$ kt. PNR $= \dfrac{2.75 \times 80 \times 120}{80 + 120} = \dfrac{26{,}400}{200} = 132$ nm. Time to the PNR $= 132 \div 80 = 1.65$ h, **99 min**. Check: time back $= 132 \div 120 = 1.1$ h; $1.65 + 1.1 = 2.75$ h, the whole endurance.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The endurance in the PNR formula is the fuel you may **spend**, not the fuel in the tanks. Take out the taxi fuel and the fixed reserve (and any holding you want to keep for the return) before dividing by the fuel flow. Using total fuel puts the PNR too far out, which is the one direction in which the error can hurt you.

??? check "Check yourself: with the same wind, why does the PNR move but the ETP stay put when you carry more fuel?"
    The PNR is set by fuel out plus fuel back equalling the fuel available, so more fuel lets you go further before turning. The ETP compares two times to two places from your position and has no fuel term in it at all.

## Diversions: which option needs the least fuel?

Element 2.6.3 is the in-flight problem. You are somewhere along the track; something has changed; you have three options and a fixed amount of fuel. The exam wants you to find, for each option, how much fuel it needs including the reserve, and so which options are available and which needs the least.

### The decision sequence

Before any arithmetic, the order of thinking matters, because the arithmetic only answers part of the question.

1. **Fly the aeroplane.** Hold height and heading while you think; a diversion begun in a hurry often ends up off track and lost.
2. **Decide what kind of problem it is.** If it is time-critical (illness, a failing engine, smoke), the nearest aerodrome in **time** wins and the ETP tells you which way that is. If it is weather or fuel, the fuel comparison decides.
3. **List the options:** continue to the destination, return to departure, divert to a suitable alternate.
4. **Work the fuel for each**, as below, and strike out any that would eat into the reserve.
5. **Check the survivors** against the things fuel does not cover: the weather at the aerodrome, the daylight left, the runway and its suitability for your aeroplane, and whether you can get fuel there.
6. **Commit**, turn onto the new heading, and work out a new ETA.
7. **Tell someone**: a radio call to Centre or the FIS, and amend your flight notification or SARTIME so nobody starts a search for you at the original destination.

![What happens if you do not amend your SARTIME after a diversion?](diagram:sartime-timeline "If the SARTIME passes without being cancelled or amended, the search and rescue system starts alerting, whatever the reason you landed somewhere else.")

### The fuel comparison table

For each option the method is the same three steps, and setting them out as a table keeps the arithmetic honest:

1. **Time:** distance divided by the groundspeed for that option, times 60 for minutes.
2. **Fuel:** time multiplied by the fuel flow.
3. **Add the fixed reserve** (30 minutes) **and any holding** that aerodrome requires, and compare the total with the fuel on board.

Any option whose total exceeds the fuel on board is **not available**: flying it would mean landing with less than the reserve. Among the options that remain, the one needing the least fuel is usually the best, subject to step 5 of the sequence.

!!! example "Worked example: three options from a position 100 nm out"
    Given: you are 100 nm from departure with 80 nm to go to the destination; an alternate lies 45 nm away off to the side. Groundspeeds: on to the destination 85 kt (headwind), back to departure 125 kt, to the alternate 100 kt. Fuel flow 36 L/h, fixed reserve 18 L (30 minutes), 55 L on board.

    1. Proceed to destination: $80 \div 85 \times 60 = \mathbf{56\ min}$; fuel $36 \times 56.5 \div 60 = \mathbf{34\ L}$; plus reserve **52 L**.
    2. Return to departure: $100 \div 125 \times 60 = \mathbf{48\ min}$; fuel $36 \times 48 \div 60 = \mathbf{29\ L}$; plus reserve **47 L**.
    3. Divert to alternate: $45 \div 100 \times 60 = \mathbf{27\ min}$; fuel $36 \times 27 \div 60 = \mathbf{16\ L}$; plus reserve **34 L**.

    | Option | Time | Fuel | Plus reserve | Fuel on board 55 L? |
    |---|---|---|---|---|
    | Proceed to destination | 80 ÷ 85 × 60 = 56 min | 34 L | 52 L | Yes, 3 L spare |
    | Return to departure | 100 ÷ 125 × 60 = 48 min | 29 L | 47 L | Yes, 8 L spare |
    | Divert to alternate | 45 ÷ 100 × 60 = 27 min | 16 L | 34 L | Yes, 21 L spare |

    The alternate needs the least fuel. Returning is cheaper than continuing despite the greater distance, because of the wind. If the destination then required 30 minutes of holding (18 L), proceeding would need 70 L and would be impossible with the reserve intact, and the decision is made for you.

Notice what the wind did in that example. The destination was 20 nm closer than home, yet going home cost 5 L less, because the groundspeed home was 40 kt higher. That is the same effect that moves the ETP into wind, showing up in fuel instead of time. And with a constant fuel flow, least fuel and least time are the same answer; they part company only when holding is added to one option.

![Which options leave the reserve intact?](diagram:diversion-fuel-ladder "Each option's trip fuel, holding and reserve stacked against the fuel on board: any bar that crosses the line is not available.")

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The fixed reserve is never used for holding or for the diversion itself. Add holding **on top** of the reserve for any aerodrome whose weather requires it, and compare the total with the fuel on board. An answer that "uses the reserve to hold" is the distractor.

??? check "Check yourself: why can returning to departure need less fuel than continuing to a destination that is closer?"
    Fuel depends on time, and time depends on groundspeed as well as distance. With a headwind outbound the groundspeed home is much higher, so a longer distance home can take less time, and therefore less fuel, than a shorter distance into wind.

## Bringing it together

A summer morning: Jandakot to Kalgoorlie, about 295 nm, solo with 150 L usable. The forecast gives a 25 kt easterly, a direct headwind for the trip out, and your TAS is 110 kt, so $GS_\text{on} = 85$ kt and $GS_\text{home} = 135$ kt. Fuel flow 32 L/h, taxi 3 L, fixed reserve 30 minutes at 32 L/h, 16 L. These distances are illustrative: measure your own off the chart.

**On the ground.** ETP: $\dfrac{295 \times 135}{85 + 135} = \dfrac{39{,}825}{220} = 181$ nm from Jandakot. Check: home $181 \div 135 = 1.34$ h; on $(295 - 181) \div 85 = 114 \div 85 = 1.34$ h. PNR: fuel available $150 - 3 - 16 = 131$ L, so $E = 131 \div 32 = 4.09$ h; PNR $= \dfrac{4.09 \times 85 \times 135}{220} = 213$ nm, which you reach $213 \div 85 = 2.5$ h after setting course. You write both on the flight log, with the times, beside the track.

**In flight.** At 210 nm, about 25 nm past Southern Cross, the fuel log shows 68 L. Kalgoorlie's TAF is amended: TEMPO thunderstorms, so 60 minutes of holding is now required there. You are past the ETP, so for a time-critical problem Kalgoorlie would be the place; this one is a fuel problem, so out comes the table.

![When does a TEMPO group require holding fuel?](diagram:taf-change-groups "TEMPO is a spell of 30 to 60 minutes, which is why it brings 60 minutes of holding; INTER, under 30 minutes, brings 30.")

!!! example "Worked example: the Kalgoorlie diversion"
    Given: 210 nm from Jandakot, 85 nm to Kalgoorlie, Southern Cross 25 nm behind you on the reverse track. Groundspeeds on 85 kt, home 135 kt. Fuel flow 32 L/h, reserve 16 L, 68 L on board. Kalgoorlie requires 60 minutes of holding.

    1. Continue: $85 \div 85 = \mathbf{60\ min}$, 32 L; holding 60 min, 32 L; plus reserve: $32 + 32 + 16 = \mathbf{80\ L}$. More than 68 L: **not available**.
    2. Return to Jandakot: $210 \div 135 \times 60 = \mathbf{93\ min}$; fuel $32 \times 1.556 = \mathbf{50\ L}$; plus reserve **66 L**. Available, **2 L** spare.
    3. Divert to Southern Cross: $25 \div 135 \times 60 = \mathbf{11\ min}$; fuel $32 \times 0.185 = \mathbf{6\ L}$; plus reserve **22 L**. Available, **46 L** spare.

    Southern Cross needs the least fuel by a wide margin. Returning is technically possible, but with 2 L spare you are only 3 nm short of the PNR you calculated on the ground, which is exactly what the planning predicted.

Before you turn, step 5: check ERSA for Southern Cross's runway and whether fuel is available there, confirm the weather is not affected by the same storms, and note the daylight left. Then turn, call Melbourne Centre with the diversion, and amend the SARTIME. The ETP told you where speed would have pointed you; the PNR told you, before you ever left the ground, that this was the region where going home would become marginal; the table made the decision in a couple of minutes.

??? check "Check yourself: in the Kalgoorlie scenario you were past the ETP. Why did that not settle the decision?"
    Because the ETP answers only the time question, which matters for a sick passenger or a failing engine. This was a fuel problem: the holding required at Kalgoorlie made continuing impossible with the reserve intact, so the fuel comparison, not the ETP, decided.

## Key points

- **2.6.1** The ETP is where the time to go on equals the time to go back; use it for time-critical problems. The PNR is the furthest point from which you can return with the reserve intact; use it when the destination may become unusable. Both matter on long legs over remote country or water, to doubtful destinations, and with thin fuel margins.
- **2.6.2** In still air the ETP is at the midpoint. With wind it moves into wind: $\text{ETP} = D \times GS_\text{home} \div (GS_\text{on} + GS_\text{home})$, and the times on and back must come out equal. The PNR is $E \times GS_\text{out} \times GS_\text{home} \div (GS_\text{out} + GS_\text{home})$ with $E$ the endurance after reserves; any wind brings it closer than in still air.
- **2.6.3** For each option: time from distance and groundspeed, fuel from time and flow, then add the 30-minute reserve and any holding. Reject any option that exceeds the fuel on board; choose the least-fuel survivor after checking weather, daylight and the aerodrome. Then tell someone and amend the SARTIME.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Expect an ETP calculation with wind, a question that tests whether you know the ETP is about time and the PNR about fuel, and a three-option fuel table. For the ETP, put the groundspeed **home** on top and check that the two times agree. For the fuel table, work time, then fuel, then add the reserve and any holding on top, and read the question for whether it wants the options that are *available* or the one that needs the *least* fuel. Avoid rounding until the last step.
