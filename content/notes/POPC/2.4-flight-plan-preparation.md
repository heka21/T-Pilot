## What the exam expects (POPC 2.4)

- **2.4.1** Apply the pilot in command's duty to get a weather and operational briefing before planning a VFR flight.
- **2.4.2** For a given route: choose the visual charts (**2.4.2a**); list the operations for which a meteorological and operational briefing is mandatory (**2.4.2b**); list the weather services and how to get them (**2.4.2c**); and apply the flight notification requirements for VFR flights and the preferred ways to submit one (**2.4.2d**).
- **2.4.3** From an aerodrome forecast, decide whether holding or an alternate is required; if so, nominate a suitable alternate (**2.4.3a**) and work out the extra fuel for holding or for the flight to the alternate (**2.4.3b**).

## Why this matters

On a weekday evening you sit down to plan Saturday's flight from Jandakot to Geraldton: two hours up the coast, past the restricted areas around Pearce and Lancelin, to an aerodrome whose weather you cannot see from Perth. Everything that keeps that flight safe is decided at the kitchen table, before the engine starts. You need the forecasts and the NOTAMs, the right charts for each part of the route, somebody who will start a search if you do not turn up, and enough fuel not just to get there but to cope if the forecast says Geraldton might be under low cloud when you arrive. The exam turns each of those into a question, and the last one into a calculation: given a TAF and an ETA, is holding or an alternate required, and how many litres does it add? This lesson works through the planning in the order you do it, and ends with a complete fuel plan.

## The briefing: what you must get, and when

The rule starts from a simple idea. The pilot in command is the one person who will be in the aeroplane when the weather and the airspace do whatever they do, so the pilot in command must find out about both beforehand. Before any flight you must obtain the **weather and operational information** reasonably necessary for the safe conduct of the flight.

The Part 91 rules make a **meteorological briefing** (the forecasts for the route and the destination) **mandatory** for flights **beyond the vicinity of the departure aerodrome** (more than 50 nm, or any flight to another aerodrome) and for any flight where the weather could affect it; good practice is a briefing for every flight (Part 91 MOS chapter 7; verify the current wording). In practice that means every cross-country you will fly as a private pilot.

The forecasts must be **current** when you study them, and if you studied them **more than an hour before departure** you refresh them before you go ([PMTC 2.7](/notes/PMTC/2.7) gives the detail). The reason is that forecasts are amended. The TAF you read at 0700 can have been replaced by 0830 with a new TEMPO group for the very period you will arrive in.

The **operational briefing** is the other half: the **NOTAMs** for the departure aerodrome, the destination, the alternates and the route; the **AIP Supplements**; and any **head-office NOTAMs**. These carry the temporary changes that override ERSA and the charts: a runway closed for works, a restricted area activated for an exercise, an AWIS out of service ([POPC 2.3](/notes/POPC/2.3)).

??? check "Check yourself: you collected the forecasts at 0715 and are about to depart at 0840. What must you do, and why?"
    Obtain the forecasts again and review them, because more than an hour has passed. A TAF can be amended at any time, and an amendment could add a deterioration in your arrival period that the earlier copy did not show.

## Where the weather comes from

The Bureau of Meteorology produces the products, and Airservices and the Bureau deliver them. Knowing both halves answers the exam's "where would you get…" questions.

**The products.** The Bureau issues the **GAF** (graphical area forecast) and the **GPWT** (grid point wind and temperature forecast) for the route, the **TAF** for each aerodrome, the **METAR** and **SPECI** observations, and the **SIGMET** and **AIRMET** warnings of hazardous weather ([RMTC 2.2](/notes/RMTC/2.2) decodes them).

**How you get them.** Before flight, from the **NAIPS** briefing system (web or app) as a location or route briefing, from the **BoM aviation weather** site, or by phone from the Airservices briefing office. In flight, from the **FIS** on the area frequency, which can give you the latest TAF, METAR and SIGMET on request, and from the **ATIS** and **AWIS** broadcasts at aerodromes along the way.

![What does each group of a TAF say?](diagram:taf-anatomy "Aerodrome, issue time, validity, wind, visibility and cloud, then the change groups, then temperatures and QNH.")

??? check "Check yourself: halfway to Geraldton you want the latest Geraldton TAF. Who do you ask, and how?"
    Flight Information Service, on the area frequency for your position (from ERSA NAV/COMM or the chart). FIS can read you the latest TAF, METAR and any SIGMET on request.

## Charts for the route

Each visual chart is drawn for a job, and the job is set by how much detail you need at that stage of the flight.

The **WAC** (world aeronautical chart, 1:1,000,000) covers the whole country in sheets and is the chart for navigating across open country by its terrain, towns and roads. Select the WAC(s) covering the whole route, using the **WAC index on the PCA**. The **VNC** (visual navigation chart) adds the airspace detail that matters near controlled airspace: the steps of Class C, the restricted areas, the reporting points; you need it for any part of the route near controlled airspace. The **VTC** (visual terminal chart) is the large-scale chart for departure and arrival in a terminal area, with the lanes of entry, the VFR routes and the approach points. And you carry **ERSA** for the aerodromes.

![What does each visual chart show?](diagram:chart-features-panel "The WAC for terrain across open country, the VNC for airspace near controlled areas, the VTC for the terminal area in detail.")

For a flight from Jandakot to Kalgoorlie that is the **Perth VTC** for departure, the **Perth VNC** through the Perth and Pearce airspace, and the **Perth and Kalgoorlie WACs** for the rest.

??? check "Check yourself: for Jandakot to Geraldton, which chart would you use to plan the departure from the Perth terminal area, and which for the open country north of Lancelin?"
    The Perth VTC for the departure, because it shows the terminal area lanes and approach points at the scale you need, then the Perth VNC for the airspace beyond. North of the controlled airspace the WAC covering the route takes over, with the VNC kept handy where restricted areas lie near the track.

## Flight notification: who knows you are out there

Every safe outcome after a forced landing starts with someone noticing you are missing. The flight notification rules exist so that someone always will.

A VFR flight is not required to submit a flight plan unless it will operate in controlled airspace or wants SAR cover, but the pilot in command must leave some form of **search and rescue watch**. There are three:

- a **flight notification**, a flight plan lodged with Airservices;
- a **SARTIME** lodged with Airservices: the time by which you will report arrival, after which the search and rescue system acts;
- a **flight note** left with a **responsible person**, who will notify SAR if the aircraft is overdue.

The **preferred methods of submission are electronic**: **NAIPS** (web or the app) for flight plans and SARTIMEs, then by phone to the Airservices briefing office, and in flight by radio to the FIS. A flight into or through **controlled airspace** needs a flight plan (full details, or an abbreviated plan by radio) so that ATC has the details; a flight plan is also the easiest way to get a SARTIME.

![How does a SARTIME work, and what happens if you forget it?](diagram:sartime-timeline "Lodge it with a margin after your ETA, extend it before it expires if delayed, cancel it on landing; otherwise the alerting phases begin.")

The notification is a promise, so it has to stay true. **Amend it** if the flight changes (ETA, route, aircraft), and **cancel the SARTIME on arrival**. A SARTIME you forget to cancel starts a search for an aeroplane that is safely in its hangar.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    "A VFR flight does not need a flight plan" is true but incomplete. It still needs a SAR watch of some kind: a flight notification, a SARTIME or a flight note with a responsible person. And entering controlled airspace does need a flight plan.

## The arrival test: is holding or an alternate needed?

Now the calculation the exam builds on. You are planning to arrive at an aerodrome you cannot see, and the only evidence about its weather is a forecast. The question the rules ask is: does that forecast give you enough confidence of a visual arrival, and if not, what is your way out?

### The window and the minima

The forecast is tested over a **window**, not at the ETA alone: **from 30 minutes before to 60 minutes after the ETA**. The window allows for arriving early, for arriving late, and for the forecast's timing being a little out.

Within that window, the decision uses the **alternate minima for VFR**. The destination TAF must show:

- cloud **not more than scattered below 1,500 ft** above the aerodrome;
- visibility **at least 8 km**;
- no probability of **fog, dust, thunderstorms** or other phenomena below the minima;
- and the **crosswind** within limits.

(Part 91 MOS chapter 7 and AIP ENR 1.1; verify the current figures. [PFRA 2.4](/notes/PFRA/2.4) explains each.)

![Which change groups touch my arrival?](diagram:taf-arrival-window "Geraldton, ETA 0400: the window runs 0330 to 0500, the TEMPO covers all of it, and its BKN012 and 4000 m both fail the alternate minima.")

### What a failure costs

If a forecast condition in the window fails that test, what you must carry depends on **how long** the forecast says the bad weather will last. Short-lived bad weather can be waited out; permanent bad weather cannot.

- a **TEMPO** deterioration (fluctuations under 60 minutes) requires **60 minutes of holding fuel**;
- an **INTER** deterioration (under 30 minutes) requires **30 minutes of holding fuel**;
- a **PROB** condition is treated according to the group it qualifies (a PROB30 TEMPO of fog requires the TEMPO holding or an alternate);
- a **permanent** condition below the minima (FM or BECMG to below the minima, or the base conditions) requires an **alternate aerodrome**, which must itself meet the minima for the arrival period, with fuel to fly from the destination to it;
- **no forecast** available: an alternate, or the special provisions for departing without one ([PMTC 2.7](/notes/PMTC/2.7)).

![How long does each change group last?](diagram:taf-change-groups "FM is a permanent change; TEMPO spells last up to an hour and INTER fluctuations under 30 minutes. Test every group that touches your window.")

The logic of the holding figures is that you carry enough fuel to outlast the worst single spell the forecast describes: a TEMPO spell can last up to an hour, an INTER fluctuation less than half an hour. For a TEMPO or INTER you may instead nominate an alternate; a permanent deterioration leaves you no holding option, because waiting does not help.

![Holding fuel or an alternate: what does each failure cost?](diagram:alternate-decision-flow "How long the bad weather lasts decides it: INTER 30 minutes of holding, TEMPO 60, a permanent change an alternate; the fixed reserve is on top of all of them.")

**The fuel figures.** Holding fuel is calculated at the **holding fuel flow** from the POH (or the cruise flow if none is given); alternate fuel is the fuel for the diversion distance at cruise consumption. The **fixed reserve** (30 minutes for day VFR in an aeroplane below 5,700 kg; Part 91 MOS chapter 19, table 19.02(2) item 1; verify) is on top of all of it. The fixed reserve is the fuel you plan never to use; holding and alternate fuel are fuel you have planned that you might.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Testing the ETA instead of the window. An ETA of 0400 with a TEMPO starting at 0430 looks clear; but the window runs to 0500, so the TEMPO is in it and holding or an alternate is required. Work out the window first, then lay every change group against it.

### The Geraldton example

!!! example "Worked example: TEMPO at Geraldton"
    Given: ETA Geraldton 0400 UTC. TAF: 15012KT 9999 SCT030, TEMPO 0300/0600 4000 SHRA BKN012. Holding flow 28 L/h; cruise flow 36 L/h; AVGAS 0.72 kg/L.

    1. Window: 30 minutes before to 60 minutes after the ETA: **0330 to 0500 UTC**.
    2. Base conditions: SCT030 and 10 km or more pass the minima.
    3. TEMPO: between 0300 and 0600, which covers the whole window, broken cloud at 1,200 ft and 4 km visibility, **both below the minima**.
    4. Requirement for a TEMPO: **60 minutes** of holding fuel.
    5. Holding fuel: $1\ \text{h} \times 28\ \text{L/h} = \mathbf{28\ L}$, which is $28 \times 0.72 = \mathbf{20\ kg}$.
    6. Fixed reserve, 30 minutes at the same flow: $0.5 \times 28 = \mathbf{14\ L}$, carried as well.

    Or, instead of holding, nominate an alternate: Dongara, 25 minutes away, if its TAF meets the minima for your arrival there. Alternate fuel: $\frac{25}{60} \times 36 = \mathbf{15\ L}$. Carry whichever you choose, plus the reserve, and record the decision on the plan.

(The TAF times above are as the original note gives them, hours UTC. A current TAF writes a change period with the day first, as in the diagrams: a TEMPO from 0300 to 0600 on the 3rd would read TEMPO 0303/0306.)

![How do litres become kilograms?](diagram:units-conversion-ladder "AVGAS at 0.72 kg per litre: multiply litres by 0.72 for kilograms.")

### Choosing an alternate

An alternate is not any aerodrome near the destination. It has to give you a better chance of a visual arrival than the destination does, and it has to be usable. So, using the ERSA checks of [POPC 2.3](/notes/POPC/2.3):

- its own forecast must **meet the alternate minima** for your arrival period **there**, which is later than your destination ETA by the diversion time;
- it should not be under the **same weather**: an aerodrome 15 nm down the coast, inside the same band of low cloud, is no way out;
- it must be **usable**: no PPR you cannot get on the day, a runway long enough on the chart, in daylight for the time you would arrive;
- and close enough that the **alternate fuel** is reasonable.

Dongara works in the Geraldton example if its TAF is clear for its own window, because it is short, has a usable runway, and a shower passing Geraldton may well miss it. If the deterioration were a wide band of coastal fog, an inland aerodrome would be the better choice.

Now try one before opening the solution: your ETA at a coastal destination is 0230 UTC, and its TAF has **FM0300 3000 DZ BKN008** (a permanent change from 0300 UTC). Your chosen alternate is 40 minutes away and its TAF is clear. Cruise flow 36 L/h. Is holding allowed, and how much extra fuel do you carry for the deterioration?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    Window: **0200 to 0330 UTC**. The FM change at 0300 falls inside it and brings 3,000 m and broken cloud at 800 ft, below the minima. It is **permanent**, so holding is not an option: an **alternate** is required. Alternate fuel $= \frac{40}{60} \times 36 = $ **24 L**, on top of the trip fuel and the fixed reserve. Check that the alternate's TAF meets the minima for your arrival there, about 0310 UTC plus the window around it.

??? check "Check yourself: why does a TEMPO allow holding fuel but an FM change to below the minima does not?"
    Holding works only if the bad weather will end while you wait. A TEMPO spell lasts up to an hour, so 60 minutes of fuel outlasts it. An FM change is permanent for the rest of the forecast period, so waiting does not bring the weather back: you need somewhere else to land.

## A complete fuel plan

The holding and alternate figures are only part of a fuel plan. Put the whole thing together in the order you would write it on the plan, and compare the total with what the tanks hold.

!!! example "Worked example: fuel plan with an INTER at the destination"
    Given: trip time 1 h 45 min; cruise flow 36 L/h, also used for holding because the POH gives no holding flow; the destination TAF has an INTER below the minima across your window; fixed reserve 30 minutes; usable fuel 110 L.

    1. Trip fuel: $1.75\ \text{h} \times 36\ \text{L/h} = \mathbf{63\ L}$.
    2. Holding for an INTER, 30 minutes: $0.5 \times 36 = \mathbf{18\ L}$.
    3. Fixed reserve, 30 minutes: $0.5 \times 36 = \mathbf{18\ L}$.
    4. Fuel required: $63 + 18 + 18 = \mathbf{99\ L}$, plus the taxi allowance from your POH or operator (not included here).
    5. Margin: $110 - 99 = \mathbf{11\ L}$, which at 36 L/h is $\frac{11}{36} \times 60 = \mathbf{18\ min}$ of flight.

    The flight is legal with full usable fuel, but the margin is only 18 minutes before the taxi allowance, and a headwind stronger than forecast eats it quickly. If the forecast had been a TEMPO instead, holding would be 36 L and the total 117 L: more than the tanks hold, so you would plan an alternate within range, or a fuel stop, or a later arrival outside the TEMPO.

![What makes up the fuel required?](diagram:fuel-plan-ladder "Trip 63, holding 18 and reserve 18 make 99 L, leaving 11 L of the 110 L; a TEMPO's 36 L of holding would need 117 L, more than the tanks hold.")

Keep figures unrounded until the end; the exam's fuel policy says so, and a total rounded down at an intermediate step can turn a flight that does not fit into one that appears to.

??? check "Check yourself: in the fuel plan above, could you use the 18 L fixed reserve to cover a longer hold if the INTER showers lingered?"
    No. The fixed reserve is the fuel you plan to land with and never use; holding and alternate fuel are separate and on top of it. If the hold goes on until only the reserve is left, you divert or land without delay.

## Bringing it together

Friday evening, Jandakot to Geraldton tomorrow, ETA 0400 UTC (1200 WST). You open NAIPS: the GAF shows showers along the coast, the Geraldton TAF has the TEMPO above, and the NOTAMs show an exercise activating a restricted area near Lancelin. You get out the Perth VTC for the departure, the Perth VNC for the Pearce and Lancelin airspace, and the WAC for the coast to Geraldton. The arrival window, 0330 to 0500, sits inside the TEMPO, so you choose: 28 L of holding or 15 L to Dongara. Dongara's TAF is clear, so you nominate it, add 15 L and the 14 L reserve to the trip fuel, and check the total against the tanks. You lodge a flight plan on NAIPS with a SARTIME, because you will cross controlled airspace on the way out. Saturday morning at 0700 WST you check the TAF again, since your first look was the evening before; it has not changed. On arrival you cancel the SARTIME from the apron. Every element of the subtopic was a step in one plan.

## Key points

- **2.4.1** The pilot in command must obtain the weather and operational information for the flight: forecasts for the route and destination, NOTAMs, AIP Supplements and head-office NOTAMs; refresh forecasts studied more than an hour before departure.
- **2.4.2a** WACs for the route (from the WAC index on the PCA), the VNC near controlled airspace, the VTC for terminal areas, and ERSA.
- **2.4.2b** A meteorological briefing is mandatory beyond the vicinity of the departure aerodrome and whenever the weather could affect the flight (verify the current Part 91 MOS wording).
- **2.4.2c** The BoM's GAF, GPWT, TAF, METAR/SPECI, SIGMET and AIRMET, from NAIPS, the BoM aviation site and the briefing office before flight, and from FIS, ATIS and AWIS in flight.
- **2.4.2d** Every VFR flight needs a SAR watch: flight notification, SARTIME or a flight note with a responsible person; a flight plan for controlled airspace. Submit electronically through NAIPS first. Amend it if things change; cancel the SARTIME on arrival.
- **2.4.3a** Test the destination TAF over 30 minutes before to 60 minutes after the ETA against the VFR alternate minima. An alternate must meet the minima for its own arrival period, not share the destination's weather, and be usable.
- **2.4.3b** TEMPO below the minima: 60 minutes holding; INTER: 30 minutes; permanent: an alternate with the fuel to reach it. Holding at the POH holding flow (or cruise), alternate fuel at cruise, and the fixed reserve (30 minutes, day VFR; verify) on top of all of it.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    The arrival window is 30 minutes before to 60 minutes after ETA. TEMPO needs 60 minutes of holding, INTER 30; a permanent deterioration needs an alternate that itself passes the test. The 30-minute fixed reserve is never used for holding or diversion. In a fuel calculation, write each line with its unit (trip, holding or alternate, reserve, taxi), round only at the end, and compare the total with usable fuel.
