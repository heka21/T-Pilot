## What the exam expects (POPC 2.3)

- **2.3.1** Use every item of ERSA information that bears on a VFR flight by day: find it, decode it, and turn it into a decision about the flight.

## Why this matters

In [BAKC 5.2](/notes/BAKC/5.2) you learned to read an ERSA entry: the runway line, the PRD listing, the UTC hours. That was reading. The PPL asks for something harder, which is using it. Plan Jandakot to an unfamiliar strip in the Wheatbelt or up the coast towards Kalbarri, and ERSA is the only briefing you will get on the place before you are overhead it: whether you are allowed to land, which runway the afternoon wind will favour, whether that runway is long enough on a hot day, which way the circuit goes, what frequency everyone is on, and whether the restricted area beside your track will be active when you pass. The exam reflects that. It hands you an extract for an aerodrome you have never heard of and asks what you must do before arriving, which runway to use, or what a remark means for you. This lesson gives you a fixed order for working any entry, and shows how each item feeds a decision you already know how to make.

## The book and what each part is for

**ERSA**, the En Route Supplement Australia, is the permanent picture of places and facilities. Its sections are worth knowing by what you use them for, because a question will often name the information and expect you to know where it lives.

| Section | Content | What a VFR pilot uses it for |
|---|---|---|
| Introduction and legend | Abbreviations, the layout of an aerodrome entry, the aerodrome diagram symbols | Decoding entries |
| GEN | General information: conversion tables, the wind component table, sunrise and sunset tables for the major aerodromes, flight planning codes and forms, SARTIME and flight notification procedures, fuel availability codes, the remote areas and the survival equipment requirements, the ATIS and AWIS systems, phone numbers | Planning tools in the exam |
| FAC (facilities) | The aerodrome entries: location, elevation, variation, operator and PPR, runway table, diagram, lighting (PAL frequency and operation), obstacles, communications (CTAF, tower, ATIS, AWIS), navigation aids, fuel, local traffic regulations (circuit direction, noise abatement, helicopter operations, gliding), remarks | Every destination and alternate: runway length and surface for the performance charts, elevation for pressure height, frequencies, circuit direction, hours, fuel |
| PRD | Prohibited, restricted and danger areas with limits, hours, authority and status | Route planning and clearances |
| NAV/COMM | Navigation aids and communications facilities | Frequencies for the FIS areas, aids on the route |
| EMERG | Emergency procedures | In flight; see [RFRC 2.7](/notes/RFRC/2.7) and the PFRA 2.7 note |
| Runway distances supplement | TORA, TODA, ASDA, LDA for aerodromes with declared distances | TODA and LDA for the performance charts |

Two features of the whole book shape how you read it. **Times are UTC**, so that one book serves every time zone; in Western Australia you add 8 hours. And **runway lengths and widths are metres**, while elevations and heights are feet. Most careless ERSA errors are one of those two.

The other thing to hold on to is what ERSA is not. It is amended on a cycle, and anything that changed since, or that is temporary, comes by **NOTAM** or AIP Supplement and overrides it ([PFRA 2.1](/notes/PFRA/2.1)). So every ERSA check ends with the NOTAMs for the aerodrome and the route.

![Which document answers which question?](diagram:where-to-find-it "Facts about places are in ERSA; today's changes are in NOTAM; rules about how you fly are in Part 91 and its MOS.")

??? check "Check yourself: you want to know whether AVGAS is available at your destination and how to pay for it. Where do you look, and what do you check afterwards?"
    The aerodrome's FAC entry in ERSA, which gives the fuel types and the payment arrangements (the fuel codes are explained in GEN). Then the NOTAMs, because a fuel outage is exactly the kind of temporary change that does not appear in ERSA.

## A fixed order for any aerodrome

An aerodrome entry has a dozen blocks, and a pilot who reads them in whatever order the eye falls on them misses one. A pilot who works the same seven questions every time does not. The order follows the flight: can I go there at all, where will I land, how do I join, what is there, what is around it, when, and what if it goes wrong.

![What is in each block of an ERSA aerodrome entry?](diagram:ersa-entry-anatomy "Header with elevation, variation and time zone; runway table with dimensions in metres, surface, slope and lighting; communications and aids; remarks and local traffic regulations.")

1. **Can I use it?** PPR, operating hours, the aerodrome category, any restriction on aircraft types, fuel and the hours it is available.
2. **Runways.** Which runway suits the forecast wind (use the wind component table in GEN), its length and width in metres (and the TODA and LDA if declared), surface and slope, for the take-off and landing charts; lighting if arrival is near last light (PAL activation and the standby frequency).
3. **Procedures.** Circuit height and direction for that runway (right-hand circuits are stated), noise abatement, non-standard joining, parachuting or gliding activity, helicopter operations, the CTAF frequency, whether radio is mandatory (certified or registered aerodrome), reporting points.
4. **Services.** AWIS or ATIS frequency and phone, navigation aids, fuel type and payment method.
5. **Airspace.** Any PRD areas or control zones around the aerodrome, their hours and the controlling authority, from the PRD section and the chart.
6. **Time.** Convert the ERSA hours and the restricted area hours from UTC to WST and compare with your ETA; check sunrise and sunset for the last light rule.
7. **Emergency.** The nearest alternate from FAC, and the EMERG section for the SAR arrangements.

The rest of this lesson works through the steps that need more than a look-up, using the invented aerodrome in the diagram, **Example Creek**: elevation 1,050 ft, variation 1 degree west, runway 06/24 at 1,200 m sealed and lit with a slope of 0.5 percent up to the east, runway 12/30 at 800 m gravel and unlit, CTAF, AWIS, PPR, right-hand circuits on runway 24.

## Can I go there at all?

Before you spend time on runways, find out whether you may land. **PPR**, prior permission required, means the operator wants a phone call first, and the number is in the header. Landing without it is a breach of the operator's conditions, and the call often tells you what ERSA cannot: the strip was graded on Tuesday, the sheep are in the paddock beside the threshold, the fuel bowser is out. Look also for **operating hours**, **restrictions on aircraft type or weight**, and the **fuel** block: the type, whether it is available at all hours or only when the operator is there, and how you pay. A destination with no fuel changes your fuel plan for the leg home, so it feeds straight into [POPC 2.4](/notes/POPC/2.4).

## Choosing the runway: wind, length and surface

This is where ERSA meets the performance work of [BAKC 6.2](/notes/BAKC/6.2), and where the exam can join two subtopics in one question.

### Which runway for the wind?

A runway's designator is its **magnetic** direction to the nearest 10 degrees, with the last zero dropped. A forecast wind in a TAF or GAF is given in degrees **true**. To compare them, convert the wind to magnetic with the variation in the ERSA header: **variation west, magnetic best** (add); **variation east, magnetic least** (subtract). At Example Creek the variation is only 1 degree, but the habit is what matters: elsewhere it is larger. Then find the angle between the wind and each runway, and split the wind into a **headwind component** and a **crosswind component**. ERSA GEN carries a wind component table for this; the arithmetic it tabulates is

$$\text{crosswind} = V \sin\theta \qquad \text{headwind} = V \cos\theta$$

where $V$ is the wind speed and $\theta$ the angle between the wind and the runway.

![Split any wind into headwind and crosswind for a runway](widget:crosswind-component "At 30 degrees off the runway half the wind is already across it; at 60 degrees nine-tenths is.")

!!! example "Worked example: choosing between the runways at Example Creek"
    Given: forecast wind 280 degrees true at 15 kt; variation 1 degree west; runways 06/24 (1,200 m sealed) and 12/30 (800 m gravel). Treat each runway as pointing at its designator times ten.

    1. Wind in magnetic: variation west, so add: $280 + 1 = \mathbf{281°M}$.
    2. Runway 24 (240°M): angle $281 - 240 = \mathbf{41°}$. Crosswind $15 \times \sin 41° = 15 \times 0.656 = \mathbf{9.8\ kt}$; headwind $15 \times \cos 41° = 15 \times 0.755 = \mathbf{11.3\ kt}$.
    3. Runway 30 (300°M): angle $300 - 281 = \mathbf{19°}$. Crosswind $15 \times \sin 19° = 15 \times 0.326 = \mathbf{4.9\ kt}$; headwind $15 \times \cos 19° = 15 \times 0.946 = \mathbf{14.2\ kt}$.

    Runway 30 is better aligned, but it is 400 m shorter and gravel. Runway 24 gives a 10 kt crosswind on a long sealed surface. Which you choose depends on two numbers that are not in ERSA: your aeroplane's demonstrated crosswind in the POH, and the landing distance from the chart for an 800 m gravel strip with a 14 kt headwind. ERSA gave you the inputs; the POH decides.

Now try one before opening the solution: the forecast changes to 200 degrees true at 20 kt. Which runway end is into wind, and what are its components?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    Wind in magnetic $= 200 + 1 = 201°$M. Runway 24 (240°M) is $39°$ off: crosswind $20 \times \sin 39° = 20 \times 0.629 = 12.6$ kt, headwind $20 \times \cos 39° = 20 \times 0.777 = 15.5$ kt. Runway 12 (120°M) is $81°$ off: almost a direct crosswind, $20 \times \sin 81° = 19.8$ kt, with only 3.1 kt of headwind. So **runway 24**, with about 13 kt across it; check that against the POH demonstrated crosswind, and remember that 24 has right-hand circuits.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Wind in a forecast is **true**; wind from a tower or ATIS is **magnetic**, and runway designators are magnetic. Converting an ATIS wind a second time, or forgetting to convert a forecast wind, moves the angle by the variation. Where the variation is small the error hides; in the exam it may not be.

### Is it long enough?

The runway table gives the physical runway: length and width in metres, surface, slope. Where the aerodrome has **declared distances**, the runway distances supplement gives four figures for each direction, and these are the ones the performance charts are compared against. Picture the runway from the start of the take-off roll:

- **TORA**, take-off run available: the length you can roll on.
- **TODA**, take-off distance available: TORA plus any **clearway** beyond the end, over which you may climb to the screen height. This is what you compare with the chart's **take-off distance** to 50 ft.
- **ASDA**, accelerate-stop distance available: TORA plus any **stopway**, where you could stop after an abandoned take-off.
- **LDA**, landing distance available: measured from the landing threshold. A **displaced threshold**, moved along the runway to clear an obstacle on the approach, makes the LDA shorter than the runway, which is why you compare the chart's **landing distance** with the LDA and not with the length in the runway table.

!!! example "Worked example: declared distances against the charts"
    Given: a hypothetical runway with TORA 1,100 m, TODA 1,250 m and LDA 950 m (its threshold is displaced). Your workbook chart gives a take-off distance to 50 ft of 1,020 m and a landing distance from 50 ft of 980 m for today's weight, pressure height and temperature.

    1. Take-off: compare the chart's take-off distance with TODA: $1{,}250 - 1{,}020 = \mathbf{230\ m}$ to spare.
    2. Landing: compare the chart's landing distance with LDA: $950 - 980 = \mathbf{-30\ m}$. The landing does **not** fit.

    The runway is long enough to leave but not to return to at this weight. The physical length would have hidden that: the displaced threshold took the margin away. Lighten the aeroplane, wait for cooler air, or choose another runway or aerodrome. Apply any factor your POH or the rules for your operation require on top of the chart figures ([BAKC 6.2](/notes/BAKC/6.2) explains what drives them).

![Which declared distance does each chart figure go against?](diagram:declared-distances "Take-off distance against TODA, which includes the clearway; landing distance against LDA, which starts at the displaced threshold: 230 m to spare leaving, 30 m short arriving.")

The **surface** and **slope** go into the same charts. Gravel and grass lengthen both distances; a slope helps one way and hurts the other. At Example Creek runway 24 runs downhill (it rises to the east and you take off towards the west), so a take-off on 24 is helped by the slope and a landing on 06 is too.

![Uphill or downhill: what does slope do to each distance?](diagram:runway-slope-take-off-and-landing "Taking off uphill, part of the thrust climbs the slope; landing downhill, gravity fights the brakes.")

??? check "Check yourself: a runway has a displaced threshold at one end. Which declared distance does it shorten, and which chart figure do you compare it with?"
    It shortens the LDA for landings in that direction, because the landing distance is measured from the threshold. Compare it with the landing distance from the chart. The take-off distances for that direction are normally not reduced, because you may usually still roll from the start of the pavement; the declared distances tell you for certain.

## Joining and operating: procedures and frequencies

Once the runway is chosen, ERSA tells you how the local traffic flows. The **local traffic regulations** and **remarks** are where an aerodrome's departures from the standard live, and they are where the exam finds its questions. The circuit is left-hand unless the entry says otherwise, so **right-hand circuits are stated** for the runways that have them: at Example Creek, runway 24. Look for a **circuit height** other than the standard, **noise abatement** areas to avoid (a town, a homestead, a stud farm), a **non-standard join**, and **gliding, parachuting or helicopter** activity that will share the circuit with you.

The **communications** block gives the **CTAF** at a non-controlled aerodrome, or the tower and ground frequencies and the hours of the tower at a controlled one; outside tower hours a controlled aerodrome becomes a CTAF and the entry says so. Note whether radio is mandatory (certified or registered aerodrome). The same block gives the **AWIS** or **ATIS** frequency and phone number, which is how you get the actual wind, QNH and temperature before you commit to a runway. **NAV/COMM** gives the area frequencies for the Flight Information Service along the route.

**Lighting** is the last runway item and matters by day only at the edge of it. If your arrival could slip towards last light, the lighting line tells you whether the runway is lit at all and, where it has **pilot activated lighting** (PAL), the frequency and the method of activation set out in ERSA. An unlit runway gives you no margin at all.

??? check "Check yourself: an entry lists right-hand circuits for one runway only. You are landing on the reciprocal end. Which way is the circuit?"
    Left-hand, the standard. A circuit direction remark applies to the runway it names; the other end of the same strip keeps the standard left-hand circuit unless the entry says otherwise.

## Around and above it: airspace and time

The aerodrome sits in airspace, and ERSA, with the chart, tells you what that airspace is doing. A control zone or a restricted area near the aerodrome shows on the chart; the **PRD** section gives a restricted area's **hours**, **controlling authority** and **conditional status** (RA1 plan through, RA2 plan to avoid, RA3 no clearance), as [BAKC 5.2](/notes/BAKC/5.2) set out.

![Is that airspace active? The pre-flight check](diagram:airspace-active-check "Chart for the boundaries, ERSA PRD for the hours and controlling authority, NOTAM for changes, then ask; without a clearance, stay out.")

Every time in that check, and every time in the FAC entry (tower hours, fuel hours, AWIS hours where limited), is **UTC**. The PPL step is to put them all on one timeline with your own ETA, in WST, add 8 hours, and watch for the day change when a UTC period crosses midnight.

![What does an ERSA time of 2200–0600 mean in Western Australia?](diagram:utc-to-wst-activity "Add 8 hours: 2200–0600 UTC is 0600–1400 WST.")

Then the daylight. As a day VFR pilot you must land before the end of daylight, so the last thing on the timeline is last light at the **destination**, not at your departure point ([PNVC 2.2](/notes/PNVC/2.2) works through the daylight graphs and their conversion). ERSA GEN carries sunrise and sunset tables for the major aerodromes, and the AIP daylight graphs cover everywhere else (verify the current layout of both); whatever source you use, convert it to WST and compare it with your latest arrival, including the time you might spend on a diversion.

??? check "Check yourself: the FAC entry for a controlled aerodrome gives tower hours that end at 1000 UTC. You plan to arrive at 1730 WST. What do you expect to find?"
    1000 UTC is 1800 WST, so the tower will still be open at 1730 WST and you need a clearance to enter the control zone. Arrive after 1800 WST and the entry's after-hours procedure, normally a CTAF, applies.

## When it goes wrong: alternates and EMERG

The last step is the one you hope not to use. Before you leave, pick the **nearest suitable alternate** from FAC and run the same checks on it in brief: can you land there, is the runway long enough, is there fuel. If the forecast requires an alternate, this is the one you will nominate and fuel for ([POPC 2.4](/notes/POPC/2.4)). The **EMERG** section, the pink pages, holds the emergency procedures you use in flight: radio failure, the emergency transponder codes, interception, and the **search and rescue** arrangements. GEN holds the **SARTIME and flight notification procedures** and, for flights into remote areas, the **survival equipment** requirements.

## Bringing it together

A Saturday in autumn, Jandakot to Example Creek, ETA 0300 UTC. Step 1: PPR, so you phone the operator on Friday; AVGAS is available, so you can plan to refuel there. Step 2: the TAF wind is 280 degrees true at 15 kt; with the 1 degree west variation that is 281 magnetic, giving 10 kt across runway 24 and 5 kt across runway 30. Your POH's demonstrated crosswind is comfortably above 10 kt, and the landing chart says 800 m of gravel is tight at your weight, so you plan runway 24: 1,200 m, sealed, downhill to the west. Step 3: the remarks say right-hand circuits on 24, so you plan to join on the right-hand downwind, and you note the CTAF. Step 4: you will call the AWIS on the way for the actual wind and the QNH. Step 5: a restricted area beside your track is listed 0000-0800 UTC Monday to Friday; it is Saturday, so it is not active unless a NOTAM says otherwise. Step 6: ETA 0300 UTC is 1100 WST, many hours before last light. Step 7: you pick an alternate within 30 minutes with a sealed runway and fuel. Then the NOTAMs, which tell you the AWIS is unserviceable; you will set QNH from the area forecast and read the wind off the windsock. Seven steps, one book, and nothing about the arrival is a surprise.

??? check "Check yourself: in the scenario, why did the NOTAM check come last, and what would you have missed without it?"
    Because NOTAMs carry what changed after ERSA was published, so they override it. Without them you would have planned on an AWIS that was not working; the same check catches a closed runway, an activated restricted area or a fuel outage.

## Key points

- **2.3.1 Where things are:** FAC for each aerodrome (header, runway table, communications, lighting, fuel, local traffic regulations and remarks), PRD for restricted areas, the runway distances supplement for TORA, TODA, ASDA and LDA, GEN for the tables, codes and SARTIME procedures, NAV/COMM for frequencies, EMERG for in-flight emergencies. Times UTC (WST is UTC + 8), lengths in metres, heights in feet.
- **2.3.1 How to use it:** work every entry in the same order: can I use it, runways, procedures, services, airspace, time, emergency; then the NOTAMs. Convert a forecast wind from true to magnetic before comparing it with runway designators; compare chart take-off distance with TODA and landing distance with LDA; note circuit direction by runway; put every UTC time and last light on one WST timeline with your ETA.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    ERSA times are UTC, runway dimensions are metres, elevations are feet. The runway table and the local traffic regulations are where most ERSA questions come from. Read the whole runway line, including slope direction, then the remarks for anything that applies to the runway in the question; a circuit direction remark applies only to the runway it names. When a question gives declared distances, take-off goes against TODA and landing against LDA.
