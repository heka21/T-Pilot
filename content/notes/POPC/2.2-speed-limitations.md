## What the exam expects (POPC 2.2)

- **2.2.1** Explain the normal operating speed V<sub>NO</sub>, the never exceed speed V<sub>NE</sub>, the manoeuvring speed V<sub>A</sub>, the turbulence penetration speed V<sub>B</sub>, the limit and design load factors, and the flap speeds V<sub>FO</sub> and V<sub>FE</sub>.
- **2.2.2** Describe the situations that can take an aeroplane past its speed and load factor limits.

## Why this matters

You have met every one of these speeds already: the load factors and V-speeds in [BAKC 6.3](/notes/BAKC/6.3) and the flap speeds in [RBKA 4.2](/notes/RBKA/4.2). The PPL syllabus repeats them because the flying changes. As a private pilot you cruise higher and further, descend from 8,500 ft into a terminal area with a schedule to keep, cross the Darling Scarp in an easterly with mountain waves rolling off it, and share circuits at Perth or Jandakot with aircraft whose wake can roll you. Those are the situations in which limits get broken, not in a calm circuit at a training aerodrome. The PPLA exam asks you to define each limit, but it also asks for the numbers behind V<sub>A</sub> and which way it moves with weight, and it gives you a scenario and asks what you should have done. This lesson recaps each limit in a sentence or two, then spends its time on the reasoning a PPL candidate is expected to have.

## The speed scale in one picture

Start where you start in the aeroplane: the airspeed indicator. Its colours carry most of the limits, and the ones that are not on the dial are the ones the exam likes to ask about.

![Which limits are on the dial, and which are not?](diagram:asi-colour-markings "White arc VSO to VFE, green VS1 to VNO, yellow VNO to VNE for smooth air only, red line VNE; VA, VB and VFO, where separate, are not marked.")

Read it from the top down.

The **red line** is the **never exceed speed**, V<sub>NE</sub>. Beyond it the designers have not shown the aeroplane to be free of **flutter**, a self-feeding oscillation of a control surface or wing that can destroy it in seconds, and the structure and controls may fail. It is a limit in any air, smooth or rough.

The **yellow arc** runs from V<sub>NO</sub> to V<sub>NE</sub>. It is the **caution range**: legal, but for **smooth air only**.

The **top of the green arc** is the **normal operating speed**, V<sub>NO</sub>, also called the **maximum structural cruising speed**. Up to it the structure is designed to take the gusts the certification standard assumes you will meet in turbulence. **Do not exceed V<sub>NO</sub> except in smooth air.**

The **white arc** is the flap range. Its top is the **flap extended speed**, V<sub>FE</sub>, the maximum speed with flap out; its bottom is V<sub>SO</sub>, the full-flap stall speed at maximum weight.

Four things are **not on the dial**: V<sub>A</sub>, V<sub>B</sub>, V<sub>FO</sub> where it differs from V<sub>FE</sub>, and the load factors. They live in section 2 of the POH and on placards. The reason V<sub>A</sub> is missing is the most examinable fact in this subtopic: it changes with weight, and a fixed mark on the dial could only be right at one weight.

??? check "Check yourself: the needle is just above the top of the green arc on a smooth, clear evening descent. Are you breaking a limit?"
    No. The yellow arc, from V<sub>NO</sub> to V<sub>NE</sub>, is permitted in smooth air. You are now relying on the air staying smooth, so the moment it is not, you slow back into the green.

## Load factors: limit and design

Every speed limit in this lesson protects either the structure against **load factor** or the airframe against something that grows with speed. Load factor, $n$, is lift divided by weight, measured in g: 1 g in level flight, $1/\cos\theta$ in a level turn at bank $\theta$, 2 g at 60 degrees ([BAKC 6.3](/notes/BAKC/6.3) builds this from first principles).

The **limit load factor** is the most the structure is designed to carry in service **without permanent deformation**. Up to it the airframe flexes and springs back. It depends on the certification category:

| Category | Positive limit | Negative limit | Design (ultimate), positive |
|---|---|---|---|
| Normal | +3.8 g | −1.52 g | 5.7 g |
| Utility | +4.4 g | −1.76 g | 6.6 g |
| Aerobatic | +6.0 g | −3.0 g | 9.0 g |

The **design (ultimate) load factor** is the limit multiplied by a safety factor of **1.5**. Between limit and ultimate the structure is not expected to break, but it may **bend permanently**; beyond the ultimate load it **may fail**.

![How far is a manoeuvre from the limits in each category?](diagram:category-load-limits "Limit 3.8, 4.4 and 6 g; ultimate 1.5 times that. A 60 degree turn is 2 g, a 75 degree turn about 3.86 g, already past the normal category limit.")

Two PPL-level points follow. First, the 1.5 margin is not yours to use. It absorbs scatter in materials and manufacture, corrosion, fatigue and the over-stresses of previous pilots you know nothing about. An aeroplane that has been taken to 4.5 g and landed safely is an aeroplane that needs an inspection, not one that has proved it is strong.

Second, many four-seat trainers carry **two categories** at once: utility at a reduced weight and normal at full weight (the loading systems in [POPC 2.1](/notes/POPC/2.1) have both envelopes). The structure is the same; what changes is the weight the limit load factor applies to. Lift is what the spar carries, so 4.4 g at the utility weight and 3.8 g at the higher normal weight put roughly comparable loads on the wing. Load the aeroplane above the utility weight and the lower normal-category limit applies to every manoeuvre you fly that day.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The **limit** load, not the ultimate load, is the one you must not exceed. "It didn't break at 5 g" in a normal category aeroplane means it went past 3.8 g into the range where the structure may now be permanently bent. Report it.

??? check "Check yourself: a training aeroplane is certified utility up to one weight and normal above it. With four people on board, which limit load factor applies, and why does the number go down?"
    The normal category limit, +3.8 g, because the aeroplane is above the utility weight. The wing structure carries lift, and at a higher weight each g needs more lift, so a lower load factor keeps the wing within the same design load.

## The manoeuvring speed, V<sub>A</sub>

The idea from [BAKC 6.3](/notes/BAKC/6.3) in one paragraph: the most load factor the wing can make before it stalls grows with the square of the airspeed, $n_\text{max} = (V/V_S)^2$. At low speed a full pull stalls the wing before it can overload anything. At high speed the wing can make more lift than the structure can carry. The speed where the two meet is V<sub>A</sub>, **the highest speed at which a full, abrupt control deflection will stall the wing before the limit load is reached**.

![Where does the stall stop protecting the structure?](diagram:v-n-diagram "The stall curve rises with the square of speed and meets the +3.8 g line at VA; right of VA a pull or a gust can reach the limit load before the wing stalls.")

At PPL level you are expected to produce the number. Set $n_\text{max}$ equal to the limit load and solve for the speed:

$$V_A = V_S \times \sqrt{n_\text{limit}}$$

For a normal category aeroplane $\sqrt{3.8} = 1.95$, so V<sub>A</sub> is just under twice the clean stall speed at the same weight.

!!! example "Worked example: V<sub>A</sub> at maximum weight and at 80 percent of it"
    Given: clean stall speed 55 kt at maximum weight; normal category, limit +3.8 g.

    1. Square root of the limit: $\sqrt{3.8} = \mathbf{1.949}$.
    2. V<sub>A</sub> at maximum weight: $55 \times 1.949 = \mathbf{107\ kt}$.
    3. At 80 percent of maximum weight the stall speed falls with the square root of the weight ratio: $\sqrt{0.8} = \mathbf{0.894}$.
    4. V<sub>A</sub> at 80 percent weight: $107 \times 0.894 = \mathbf{96\ kt}$.

    Solo with part fuel, the speed below which a full control input is safe is 11 kt lower than the placard figure for maximum weight.

Why lower and not higher? A lighter aeroplane needs less lift for each g. At any given speed its wing can therefore pull more g before it stalls, so it reaches the limit load at a lower speed. The structure has not got weaker; the stall has stopped protecting it sooner. Many POHs give V<sub>A</sub> at two or three weights for this reason. Use the figure for your weight, or the lower one.

Now try one before opening the solution: an aeroplane stalls clean at 60 kt at maximum weight and is limited to +3.8 g. What is V<sub>A</sub> at 75 percent of maximum weight?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    V<sub>A</sub> at maximum weight $= 60 \times 1.949 = 116.9$ kt. Weight factor $\sqrt{0.75} = 0.866$. V<sub>A</sub> at 75 percent weight $= 116.9 \times 0.866 = 101.3$, so **about 101 kt**. (Working through the stall speed gives the same answer: $60 \times 0.866 = 52.0$ kt, and $52.0 \times 1.949 = 101.3$ kt.)

Two limits on what V<sub>A</sub> promises. It covers **one full deflection in one axis**. Full aileron and full elevator together, or a full deflection followed by a rapid reversal, can overload parts of the structure even below V<sub>A</sub>. And it says nothing about the flap: with flap down the structure is protected by V<sub>FE</sub> and a lower flap-down load limit, not by V<sub>A</sub>.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    V<sub>A</sub> **decreases** as weight **decreases**. The distractor says it rises because "a light aeroplane is under less stress". The structure is the same; the wing reaches the limit load at a lower speed because it needs less lift per g.

## Gusts: V<sub>NO</sub> and the turbulence penetration speed V<sub>B</sub>

A gust loads the wing without any input from you. Fly into a rising current and the relative airflow briefly comes from below as well as ahead: the angle of attack jumps, and with it the lift. The extra angle depends on the gust's speed compared with your airspeed; the lift that angle produces depends on the square of your airspeed. Put the two together and **the extra load factor from a given gust grows in proportion to your airspeed**.

!!! example "Worked example: the same gust at two speeds"
    Given: a hypothetical aeroplane meets a sharp up-gust that adds 1.0 g at 100 kt. The same gust is met at 130 kt.

    1. Speed ratio: $130 \div 100 = \mathbf{1.3}$.
    2. Extra load factor at 130 kt: $1.0 \times 1.3 = \mathbf{1.3\ g}$.
    3. Total load factor at 130 kt: $1 + 1.3 = \mathbf{2.3\ g}$, against $\mathbf{2.0\ g}$ at 100 kt.

    Every knot you take off before the rough air reduces the hit. The relation is a rule of thumb for a given aeroplane and weight, not a figure from any POH; the point is the proportion.

![Why does the same gust hit harder at higher speed?](diagram:gust-load-vs-airspeed "Every gust line fans up from 1 g in proportion to airspeed: the design gust stays inside +3.8 g up to VNO and crosses it just beyond.")

That proportion is what the two gust speeds are built on. **V<sub>NO</sub>** is the highest speed at which the structure has been shown to take the design gust, so above it you are in smooth-air-only territory. **V<sub>B</sub>**, strictly the **design speed for maximum gust intensity**, is lower again: slow enough that the strongest design gust stalls the wing momentarily rather than overloading it, and fast enough to keep a margin above the stall so the aeroplane stays controllable while the airspeed jumps around. Where the POH publishes V<sub>B</sub> it is the turbulence penetration speed. Many light-aeroplane POHs do not; they give a **rough-air or turbulence penetration speed near V<sub>A</sub>** instead, and that is the speed you fly.

Why not simply fly as slowly as possible? Because the gust works both ways. A strong up-gust at a speed only a little above the stall will stall the wing, and a down-gust at that speed can leave you with too little margin to recover cleanly. The penetration speed is the compromise between overstress at high speed and loss of control at low speed. Hold the attitude, not the airspeed, and accept that the needle will swing.

??? check "Check yourself: V<sub>B</sub> is not published for your aeroplane. What speed do you fly in severe turbulence, and why not the bottom of the green arc?"
    The POH's turbulence penetration speed, which for light aeroplanes is near V<sub>A</sub> for the weight. The bottom of the green arc is too close to the stall: a strong gust or the airspeed swings in turbulence could stall the wing and leave too little margin to recover.

## The flap speeds, V<sub>FO</sub> and V<sub>FE</sub>

The flap, its tracks, hinges and operating mechanism, and the rear spar that carries them are built for the loads at approach speeds, and air load grows with the square of speed. Hence two flap limits ([RBKA 4.2](/notes/RBKA/4.2) builds them in full).

**V<sub>FE</sub>**, the **flap extended speed**, is the most you may fly with the flap out: the top of the white arc. A type may have a separate V<sub>FE</sub> for each flap stage, lower for the larger settings.

**V<sub>FO</sub>**, the **flap operating speed**, is the most you may be doing while the flap is moving, extending or retracting. On many light aeroplanes it equals V<sub>FE</sub> and is not quoted separately. Where it differs, it is lower, and it is not on the dial.

![Which flap speed governs moving the flap and which governs flying with it out?](diagram:flap-speeds-vfo-vfe "Slow to VFO before the flap moves, in either direction; once it is out, VFE is the limit for flying with it.")

The PPL point is the go-around and the busy arrival. On a missed approach at full power the aeroplane accelerates quickly, and the flap is still out. If the nose is allowed to drop, the speed can pass the full-flap V<sub>FE</sub> before you have the flap up, and if V<sub>FO</sub> is separate you cannot retract it either until you slow again. Hold the climb attitude and raise the flap in the stages the POH gives. The other trap is a fast arrival at a controlled aerodrome, asked to keep the speed up on final, and then selecting flap with the needle still in the green arc.

??? check "Check yourself: the POH gives V<sub>FO</sub> lower than V<sub>FE</sub>. You are between the two with flap already out. What may you do?"
    You may keep flying with the flap extended, because you are below V<sub>FE</sub>. You may not move it, in or out, until you are at or below V<sub>FO</sub>, because V<sub>FO</sub> is the limit for flap in motion.

## How private flying breaks the limits

The RPL list in [BAKC 6.3](/notes/BAKC/6.3) still holds: spiral dives and nose-low upsets, harsh pull-outs, steep turns and accelerated stalls at speed, turbulence above V<sub>A</sub>, the yellow arc in rough air, flap above its limits, power-on descents and over-controlling. The PPL syllabus asks you to see them in the flying you will now do. Each follows from the physics above.

**The long descent with cruise power on.** At the end of a cross-country you lower the nose from 8,500 ft towards Jandakot and leave the power where it was. The engine's thrust and the height you are trading both go into speed, and over a 15 minute descent the needle drifts through V<sub>NO</sub> without any single moment you notice. That is fine in smooth air. Then the descent takes you into the low-level turbulence of a hot afternoon, and you are taking every bump at a yellow-arc speed. The defence is planning: reduce power at the top of descent to hold a speed in the green, and slow further before the rough air, not after.

**Mountain waves over the scarp.** In a strong easterly the Darling Scarp can set up waves and rotor on its lee side, the coastal plain. The air can be smooth until it suddenly is not, and the vertical gusts are large. A cruise speed set in the smooth air ahead of the wave puts you above V<sub>A</sub> when the rotor arrives. Slow to the penetration speed when the forecast or the first bumps warn you.

![Where does the turbulence sit in a strong easterly?](diagram:rotor-in-the-lee-of-the-scarp "The air tumbles down the western face of the scarp onto the coastal plain; that rotor is where a cruise speed above VA meets its biggest gusts.")

**Wake turbulence at a controlled aerodrome.** Following a heavier aircraft at Perth, a vortex encounter can roll the aeroplane abruptly. The instinct is full opposite aileron and rudder, perhaps with a pull, which is exactly the full, multi-axis deflection that V<sub>A</sub> does not protect against, at an approach speed that may be above V<sub>FE</sub> for the stage you are about to select. Avoiding the wake by flight path is the real defence.

![How do you stay out of a heavier aircraft's wake?](diagram:wake-turbulence-avoidance "Taking off, lift off before the heavier aircraft's rotation point and climb above its path; landing, stay above its approach path and touch down beyond its touchdown point.")

**The spiral dive and the pull-out.** An unrecognised spiral, after inadvertent entry into cloud or a distraction on a long leg, builds speed fast with the nose low and the bank steep. The danger is in the recovery. At high speed the wing can make far more lift than the structure can carry, so a hard pull with the wings still banked can reach the limit load before anything stalls.

![Spin or spiral dive: how do you tell, and how do you recover?](diagram:spin-vs-spiral-dive "In a spiral the wings are not stalled and the speed is high and increasing; close the throttle, roll level, then ease out gently.")

!!! example "Worked example: how much g a spiral recovery can pull"
    Given: clean stall speed 50 kt at today's weight; the spiral has reached 130 kt; normal category, limit +3.8 g.

    1. Speed ratio to the stall: $130 \div 50 = \mathbf{2.6}$.
    2. Most load factor the wing can make before it stalls: $2.6^2 = \mathbf{6.8\ g}$.
    3. Compare: 6.8 g is above the **3.8 g limit** and above the **5.7 g ultimate** load.

    At this speed the stall gives no protection at all: a hard pull could bend or break the wing before the wing stalls. Close the throttle, roll level first so the lift points up, then ease out with gentle back pressure.

**Steep turns at speed.** A 60 degree turn is 2 g; a 75 degree turn is about 3.9 g, past the normal category limit. Tightening a steep turn at cruise speed, to avoid traffic or to keep a ground feature in sight, can get there faster than the g meter in your stomach suggests.

![How quickly does load factor climb with bank?](widget:bank-angle "Load factor creeps to 1.41 at 45 degrees, doubles by 60 and reaches the normal category limit near 75.")

??? check "Check yourself: why does the spiral recovery start with closing the throttle and rolling level, not with pulling back?"
    Because at high speed the wing can make more lift than the structure can carry, so a pull with the wings banked mainly tightens the turn and adds load factor. Closing the throttle stops the speed building, and rolling level points the lift upward so the pull-out needs the least g.

## Bringing it together

You are flying a four-seater back to Jandakot from Merredin with three friends on board, so the aeroplane is above its utility weight and the limit is the normal category's 3.8 g. The POH placard gives V<sub>A</sub> at maximum weight; at today's weight you are close to it, so the placard figure is about right. The forecast warned of a moderate easterly and turbulence below 3,000 ft west of the scarp. At the top of descent you reduce power so the speed stays in the green, and approaching the scarp you slow further, to the POH's turbulence penetration speed, before the first bump rather than after the third. Approach asks you to keep the speed up on a long final. You do, but you wait for the needle to come below V<sub>FO</sub> before the first stage of flap goes out, and you check it again before the next stage, because full flap has a lower V<sub>FE</sub>. Every limit in the lesson was in play, and none of them was broken, because each was anticipated.

??? check "Check yourself: on the same flight solo with half fuel, would you fly the turbulence penetration speed from the placard or a lower one, and why?"
    A lower one where the POH gives V<sub>A</sub> or the penetration speed by weight, because both fall with the square root of the weight. At a light weight the wing reaches the limit load at a lower speed, so the maximum-weight figure leaves you above the protected speed.

## Key points

- **2.2.1** V<sub>NE</sub> is the red line, never crossed (flutter, structural and control failure beyond it). V<sub>NO</sub> is the top of the green: design gust loads; above it, the yellow arc, smooth air only. V<sub>A</sub>, not on the dial, is the speed below which a full abrupt deflection stalls the wing before the limit load: $V_A = V_S\sqrt{n_\text{limit}}$, about $1.95\,V_S$ for 3.8 g, and **lower at lower weight**. V<sub>B</sub> is the turbulence penetration speed, near V<sub>A</sub> if not published. Limit load: normal +3.8 / −1.52 g, utility +4.4 / −1.76, aerobatic +6.0 / −3.0, no permanent deformation; design (ultimate) load 1.5 times the limit. V<sub>FE</sub> is the most with flap out (top of the white arc); V<sub>FO</sub> is the most while the flap moves, lower where it differs.
- **2.2.2** Limits are broken by power-on descents into rough air, mountain waves and turbulence taken above V<sub>A</sub>, wake encounters met with full multi-axis inputs, spiral dives recovered with a hard banked pull, steep turns tightened at speed, and flap at or near the go-around above its limits. The defence is to anticipate: slow down before the rough air, roll level and close the throttle before easing out.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Expect a V<sub>A</sub> calculation ($V_S \times \sqrt{3.8}$, then $\times\sqrt{W/W_\text{max}}$ for a lighter weight) and a "which way does V<sub>A</sub> move" question: it falls with weight. Have by heart the normal category +3.8 g and −1.52 g, the 1.5 safety factor, 2 g at 60 degrees, and which limits are on the dial (V<sub>NO</sub>, V<sub>NE</sub>, V<sub>FE</sub>) and which are not (V<sub>A</sub>, V<sub>B</sub>, V<sub>FO</sub>). The turbulence speed is the one to slow to before the rough air, not after. Your POH figures win over any in this lesson.
