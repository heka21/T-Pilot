## What the exam expects (POPC 2.5)

- **2.5.1** Work from a given package: a departure point and two landing points, a weather and operational briefing, the passengers and baggage to be carried, and the aeroplane's performance data.
- **2.5.2** From that package, complete a flight plan that chooses a safe VFR route and cruising levels, takes each level from the table of cruising levels, works out trip fuel, holding fuel, alternate fuel and the required reserves, keeps the aeroplane within its weight and balance limits, and gives the latest time you can depart.

## Why this matters

Everything you have learned for the PPL meets in one place: the night before a weekend trip, with a WAC on the table, the forecasts on a screen and three people wanting to come to the wheatbelt for lunch. Navigation, weather, air law, loading and performance each feed a number into the next, and a mistake early in the chain spoils everything after it. The PPL exam tests it the same way: one scenario, a page of data, and a run of questions from the cruising level for the first leg to the latest time you can start the engine. This lesson gives you the sequence, the reason for each step, and one complete plan worked from start to finish.

## What you are given, and what each piece is for

Element 2.5.1 is a list of inputs. Look at them as a planner does, by asking what question each one answers, because the exam package is large and only some of it matters for each question.

- **The departure place and two landing points** fix the geometry: tracks, distances, terrain and airspace for each leg, and the aerodromes whose forecasts, runways and daylight you need.
- **The weather and operational briefing** is the forecast package (the area forecast GAF, the grid point winds and temperatures GPWT, the TAFs for each landing point) plus NOTAMs and ERSA entries. It gives the winds, the cloud that limits your level, and the TAF tests.
- **The passengers and baggage** are the payload. They decide how much fuel you can carry.
- **The performance data** is the POH or the workbook charts: TAS and fuel flow at a power setting and level, the climb and descent figures, and the take-off and landing charts.

Read the question first, then go to the piece of the package it needs: cruising level needs the magnetic track and the cloud; minimum fuel needs the times, flows and TAFs; latest departure needs the times and the daylight table.

## The planning sequence

The order matters because each step uses the result of the one before: fuel needs the times, times need the groundspeeds, and groundspeeds need the level, because wind and TAS change with height.

1. **Route.** Tracks, distances and the lowest safe altitude for each leg.
2. **Weather.** Wind and temperature at the candidate levels, cloud and visibility along the route, the TAF test at each landing point.
3. **Cruising level** for each leg.
4. **Performance:** TAS, heading, groundspeed and time for each leg.
5. **Fuel:** the minimum fuel required, then the fuel you will carry.
6. **Weight and balance** with that fuel.
7. **Take-off and landing performance** at each aerodrome at those weights.
8. **Latest departure time.**
9. **Notification:** lodge the flight plan or SARTIME and brief the passengers.

Steps 5 and 6 are a loop: fuel adds weight, and taking weight out can mean less fuel.

## Choosing a safe route (2.5.2a)

Lay the route out on the WAC, with the VNC or VTC wherever controlled or restricted airspace is near your tracks. You want a line that is legal, navigable and safe.

Legal first. The route must stay clear of **controlled airspace you cannot enter**, of **active restricted areas**, and of **terrain you cannot clear** at the minimum heights. Leaving Jandakot eastbound, for example, you are under the Perth Class C steps, whose lower limits rise with distance from Perth; your level on the first leg is capped by the step above you until you are clear of them ([PFRA 2.6](/notes/PFRA/2.6)).

![Why does the controlled airspace above you cap your level?](diagram:airspace-cross-section "The Class C steps rise with distance from the major airport: close in, the floor above you is low, and a VFR flight without a clearance must stay below it.")

Navigable next. Pick tracks over **features you can map-read**: a highway, a railway, a town, a lake. A straight line across featureless farmland is shorter but harder to fly. Then measure each leg: **true track**, converted to **magnetic track** with the variation from the chart, and **distance** in nautical miles.

Safe last, and this gives you a number. For each leg find the **lowest safe altitude** (LSALT): the highest terrain or obstacle within the track tolerance either side of the leg, **plus 1,000 ft**, and never less than the VFR minimum heights. It is the floor under every cruising level you consider: below it, an error in navigation has no margin over the high ground.

![What are the minimum heights the route must respect?](diagram:minimum-heights "1,000 ft above the highest obstacle within 600 m over towns and gatherings, 500 ft above the highest feature within 300 m elsewhere: the absolute floor, which the LSALT sits well above.")

## Reading the weather into the plan

Take three things from the briefing for each leg.

The **wind and temperature at your candidate levels**, from the GPWT. The wind gives you heading and groundspeed; the temperature gives you the TAS. A headwind that strengthens with height can make a lower level faster.

The **cloud base and visibility** along the route, from the GAF. VFR means staying in VMC, and above 3,000 ft AMSL (or 1,000 ft above the ground, whichever is higher) that means 1,000 ft vertically clear of cloud as well as 5,000 m visibility ([RFRC 2.3](/notes/RFRC/2.3)). A forecast base of 5,000 ft therefore puts a lid at about 4,000 ft on any level you choose. Note any forecast turbulence or thunderstorms that would change the route altogether.

The **TAF at each landing point**, tested against the alternate minima for the arrival window ([POPC 2.4](/notes/POPC/2.4)). A TEMPO deterioration below the minima means **60 minutes of holding fuel**; an INTER means **30 minutes**; a permanent deterioration means an **alternate** that itself passes the test. These are answers you carry straight into the fuel step.

![How long is a TEMPO or INTER spell, and so how much holding does it require?](diagram:taf-change-groups "TEMPO spells last under an hour, so you carry 60 minutes of holding; INTER spells last under 30 minutes, so you carry 30.")

## Cruising levels (2.5.2a and b)

A cruising level has to satisfy several conditions at once: think of a window that closes from above and below.

From below: the level must be **at or above the LSALT** for the leg.

From above: it must keep you in **VMC**, which means 1,000 ft below the cloud base in most of the airspace you will use; it must be **below any CTA step** you have no clearance to enter; and the **oxygen rule above 10,000 ft** and the time you would spend up there make the high levels unattractive in an unpressurised trainer ([PHFC 2.12](/notes/PHFC/2.12)).

Inside that window, the **table of cruising levels** in AIP ENR 1.7 decides which levels you may use. Above 5,000 ft, a VFR flight on a **magnetic track of 000 to 179 degrees** flies **odd thousands plus 500 ft** (5,500, 7,500, 9,500); on **180 to 359 degrees** it flies **even thousands plus 500 ft** (6,500, 8,500). The reason is separation: IFR traffic uses the whole thousands, VFR sits 500 ft between them, and the east-west split puts opposite-direction VFR aircraft 1,000 ft apart. Below 5,000 ft the table does not bind, but using the same pattern above 3,000 ft is good practice for the same reason.

![Which half of the compass gets odd levels and which gets even?](diagram:vfr-cruising-levels "Eastbound magnetic tracks (000 to 179) take odd thousands plus 500; westbound (180 to 359) take even thousands plus 500.")

The table works on **magnetic track**, not heading (which includes drift) and not true track. The boundary is exact: 179 degrees is eastbound, 180 is westbound.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Using the true track or the heading to choose a level. On a track near north or south the difference can move you across the 000 or 180 boundary. Convert the true track to magnetic with the chart variation, then look at which half of the compass it falls in.

??? check "Check yourself: a leg has a magnetic track of 192 degrees, the LSALT is 3,400 ft and the cloud base is forecast at 9,000 ft. Is 7,500 ft a correct cruising level?"
    No. A track of 192 degrees magnetic is in the westbound half, so above 5,000 ft the level must be an even thousand plus 500: 6,500 or 8,500 ft. Of those, 8,500 ft is only 500 ft below the cloud base, so 6,500 ft is the one that also keeps 1,000 ft clear of cloud.

## Performance for each leg

With the level chosen, the POH gives you the **TAS** for your power setting at that pressure height and temperature. With the wind from the GPWT, the navigation computer gives you the **heading**, the **drift** and the **groundspeed** ([PNVC 2.4](/notes/PNVC/2.4)). The time for each leg is distance divided by groundspeed.

![Why is the heading different from the track?](diagram:heading-track-drift "The wind carries the aeroplane sideways, so you point the nose upwind of the track by the drift angle; the groundspeed comes out of the same triangle.")

A full plan splits each leg into **climb, cruise and descent**. The POH climb table gives the time, fuel and distance to the **top of climb** (TOPC), and you plan the **top of descent** (TOPD) so that you arrive at circuit height without a dive. Exam questions often simplify this with one groundspeed or one fuel flow per leg. Then add the legs for the **total flight time**.

## Fuel: building the minimum fuel figure (2.5.2c)

Fuel planning is a ledger. Each line answers a different "what if", always in the same order, so nothing is counted twice or left out. For a day VFR private flight in a piston aeroplane:

1. **Taxi and run-up fuel.** The POH allowance, typically 2 to 4 L. It is burned before take-off but must be in the tanks at engine start. On a trip with stops, allow it for every start.
2. **Trip fuel**: the fuel to fly the climb, cruise and descent to the destination at the POH fuel flows. Climb fuel is higher than cruise, which is why the POH tables it separately.
3. **Holding fuel**, where a TAF requires it: **60 minutes for a TEMPO, 30 minutes for an INTER**. It covers waiting overhead for a forecast spell of bad weather to pass.
4. **Alternate fuel**, where an alternate is required: the fuel from the destination to the alternate at cruise consumption. It covers the case where the weather does not improve at all.
5. **Fixed reserve**: **30 minutes** at the holding fuel flow (Part 91 MOS table 19.02 item 1; verify against the current MOS). It is the fuel you plan to land with and never plan to use. It is not available for holding or for a diversion; those have their own lines above it.
6. **Contingency or discretionary fuel.** A margin for a wrong forecast or a diversion round a shower. It is good practice, though not required for private VFR; many pilots carry 10 percent of the trip fuel.

Add lines 1 to 5 (and 6 if you carry it) to get the **minimum fuel required**. Compare it with the fuel you plan to carry; the difference is your margin. Then convert to mass for the loading sheet: avgas is about **0.72 kg per litre**, so multiply litres by 0.72. Keep the whole ledger in one unit, record it on the flight plan form, and do not round the intermediate steps.

![How do you turn litres into kilograms for the loading sheet?](diagram:units-conversion-ladder "Avgas weighs about 0.72 kg per litre: 100 L is about 72 kg.")

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Using the fixed reserve as holding fuel. The reserve is what you land with after everything else has gone wrong, including the hold. If the TAF calls for 30 minutes of holding, that is a separate 30 minutes on top of the 30-minute reserve, and the exam's distractor is the answer that counts only one of them.

??? check "Check yourself: why is the fixed reserve worked at the holding fuel flow rather than the cruise flow?"
    Because the reserve is time in the air with nowhere better to go, flown at the economical holding power setting rather than cruise. The ledger states it as 30 minutes at the holding flow, so the litres depend on that lower flow.

## Weight and balance with the fuel on board (2.5.2d)

Load the aeroplane in the loading system for its type ([POPC 2.1](/notes/POPC/2.1)): the empty weight, the pilot and passengers seat by seat, the baggage, and the fuel at 0.72 kg per litre. Then check, in order:

- the **take-off weight against MTOW**;
- the take-off weight against the **climb weight limit** from the take-off chart at the departure aerodrome's pressure height and temperature, which on a hot day at an inland strip can be lower than MTOW ([POPA 2.4](/notes/POPA/2.4));
- the **CG at zero fuel weight and at take-off weight**, both inside the envelope;
- the **landing weight at each landing point**, which falls as fuel burns.

![Where does the loaded aeroplane sit on the CG envelope?](widget:weight-and-balance "Add the fuel last and watch the point move: it must stay inside the envelope full and empty, and below the weight limit at the top.")

If the aeroplane is over a limit, something comes off: baggage, fuel (never below the minimum fuel required) or a passenger. Every change sends you back round the loop.

Then check **take-off and landing performance** with the workbook charts: is each runway long enough for the take-off weight there, and the landing distance available enough for the landing weight?

??? check "Check yourself: full tanks put the aeroplane 9 kg over MTOW with three people and their bags. What can you trade, and what limits the trade?"
    You can take out fuel, about 13 L for 9 kg, or 9 kg of baggage. The fuel can come out only as long as what is left still meets the minimum fuel required for the whole plan, reserves included; if it would not, the baggage or a passenger has to come out instead.

## The latest departure time (2.5.2e)

A day VFR flight must be on the ground **at least 10 minutes before last light** at the final landing point ([RFRC 2.3](/notes/RFRC/2.3)), with any holding allowed for. Last light comes from the daylight tables or graphs for the date and place ([PNVC 2.2](/notes/PNVC/2.2)). Work backwards from that deadline.

1. **Last light** at the final landing point, from the table.
2. Subtract **10 minutes**: the **latest arrival time**.
3. Subtract the **total flight time** of all the legs.
4. Subtract any **holding** you may have to do along the way.
5. Subtract the planned **ground time** at each intermediate stop.

The result is the **latest time of departure**. Check it against aerodrome and restricted area hours, then convert it to UTC for the flight plan form: in Western Australia **WST is UTC plus 8 hours**.

![How do you convert a WST time to UTC for the flight plan?](diagram:utc-and-wst-strip "WST is UTC plus 8, so subtract 8 hours from a WST time to get UTC.")

Use the last light at the place you will **finish**, not where you start: in Western Australia last light inland is earlier than at the coast on the same evening.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Leaving the holding or the ground time at the stops out of the backwards count. If the TAF makes you carry 30 minutes of holding fuel, it can also make you spend 30 minutes holding, and that half hour comes out of your daylight just as surely as it comes out of your tanks.

## A complete plan, worked through

Here is one plan from start to finish. The aeroplane and its figures are a fictional trainer, and the distances and terrain are illustrative: measure your own off the chart for a real flight.

The task: depart Jandakot, land at Cunderdin, then Northam, then return to Jandakot, all on one afternoon. Three people (pilot 80 kg, front passenger 75 kg, rear passenger 70 kg) and 20 kg of baggage. No fuel is available at the two stops.

The aeroplane: TAS 108 kt at the planned levels, cruise fuel flow 34 L/h, holding fuel flow 27 L/h, taxi allowance 2 L per start, usable capacity 160 L, basic empty weight 760 kg, MTOW 1,111 kg.

The briefing: wind 270 degrees true at 20 kt at all the levels you are considering; cloud base 7,000 ft; Cunderdin and Jandakot TAFs above the minima with no change groups; the Northam TAF has an INTER group with showers below the alternate minima over your arrival window. Last light at Jandakot is 1857 WST. You plan 30 minutes on the ground at Cunderdin and 40 minutes at Northam.

!!! example "Worked example: route, levels and times"
    Given: legs measured off the WAC. Jandakot to Cunderdin 74 nm, magnetic track about 068 degrees, LSALT 2,500 ft. Cunderdin to Northam 29 nm, magnetic track about 267 degrees. Northam to Jandakot 48 nm, magnetic track about 237 degrees. Groundspeeds from the computer for wind 270/20 at 108 kt TAS.

    1. Leg 1 level: track 068 is eastbound, so odd plus 500; **5,500 ft** is above the LSALT, 1,500 ft below the cloud, and is a level you climb to once the Perth steps are behind you.
    2. Leg 1 time: almost a tailwind, groundspeed 126 kt; $74 \div 126 \times 60 = \mathbf{35.2\ min}$.
    3. Leg 2 level: a short westbound leg below 5,000 ft; **3,500 ft**. Time into a direct headwind at 88 kt: $29 \div 88 \times 60 = \mathbf{19.8\ min}$.
    4. Leg 3 level: track 237 is westbound; below 5,000 ft the table does not bind, so **4,500 ft** keeps you below the steps on the way back into Jandakot and follows the even-plus-500 pattern. Groundspeed 91 kt; $48 \div 91 \times 60 = \mathbf{31.6\ min}$.
    5. Total flight time: $35.2 + 19.8 + 31.6 = \mathbf{86.6\ min}$.

    The tailwind gives you the first leg cheaply and charges for it on the way home: 77 nm in 51 minutes against 74 nm in 35.

!!! example "Worked example: the fuel ledger"
    Given: total flight time 86.6 min at 34 L/h; holding flow 27 L/h; Northam INTER requires 30 minutes of holding; no alternate required; three starts at 2 L each.

    1. Taxi: $3 \times 2 = \mathbf{6\ L}$.
    2. Trip fuel: $86.6 \times 34 \div 60 = \mathbf{49.1\ L}$.
    3. Holding for Northam: $30 \times 27 \div 60 = \mathbf{13.5\ L}$.
    4. Alternate: **0 L**.
    5. Fixed reserve: $30 \times 27 \div 60 = \mathbf{13.5\ L}$.
    6. Minimum fuel required: $6 + 49.1 + 13.5 + 0 + 13.5 = \mathbf{82.1\ L}$. With a 10 percent contingency ($4.9$ L) it is **87.0 L**.

    Holding and reserve are the same size here, but they are two separate lines and both are in the total.

!!! example "Worked example: loading and the fuel you can carry"
    Given: basic empty weight 760 kg; occupants 80, 75 and 70 kg; baggage 20 kg; MTOW 1,111 kg.

    1. Zero fuel weight: $760 + 80 + 75 + 70 + 20 = \mathbf{1{,}005\ kg}$.
    2. Room for fuel under MTOW: $1{,}111 - 1{,}005 = \mathbf{106\ kg}$, which is $106 \div 0.72 = \mathbf{147\ L}$. Full tanks (160 L, 115.2 kg) would put you 9.2 kg over.
    3. Choose 130 L: $130 \times 0.72 = \mathbf{93.6\ kg}$. Ramp weight $1{,}005 + 93.6 = \mathbf{1{,}098.6\ kg}$; after the first 2 L of taxi, take-off weight $1{,}098.6 - 1.4 = \mathbf{1{,}097.2\ kg}$, under MTOW.
    4. Margin over the minimum: $130 - 82.1 = \mathbf{47.9\ L}$, about 43 L more than the minimum with contingency.

    Then plot the CG at zero fuel and take-off weight, and check the climb weight limit and runway lengths.

!!! example "Worked example: the latest departure time"
    Given: last light at Jandakot 1857 WST; total flight time 86.6 min, taken as 87 min; holding 30 min; ground time 30 min at Cunderdin and 40 min at Northam.

    1. Latest arrival: $1857 - 10\ \text{min} = \mathbf{1847\ WST}$.
    2. Time to take off the clock: $87 + 30 + 30 + 40 = \mathbf{187\ min} = 3\ \text{h}\ 07\ \text{min}$.
    3. Latest departure: $1847 - 3{:}07 = \mathbf{1540\ WST}$.
    4. For the flight plan form: $1540 - 8\ \text{h} = \mathbf{0740\ UTC}$.

    Round the flight time up, not down: for a deadline a fraction of a minute counts as a whole one.

Now one to try yourself before opening the solution. A different day, a single destination with the same aeroplane: trip fuel for 1 hour 50 minutes at 34 L/h, one start at 2 L, no holding. The destination TAF has an FM group that brings cloud below the alternate minima for the whole arrival window, so you nominate an alternate 40 nm beyond it, flown at a groundspeed of 100 kt. What is the minimum fuel required, in litres and in kilograms?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    Trip: $110 \times 34 \div 60 = 62.3$ L. Alternate: $40 \div 100 \times 60 = 24$ min, so $24 \times 34 \div 60 = 13.6$ L at cruise flow. Reserve: $30 \times 27 \div 60 = 13.5$ L. Taxi 2 L. Minimum fuel required: $2 + 62.3 + 0 + 13.6 + 13.5 = \mathbf{91.4\ L}$, which is $91.4 \times 0.72 = \mathbf{65.8\ kg}$. A permanent deterioration needs an alternate, not holding, and the alternate fuel is flown at cruise consumption, not the holding flow.

??? check "Check yourself: in the worked plan, why did the INTER at Northam change the latest departure time as well as the fuel?"
    Because the holding fuel is there so that you can actually hold for up to 30 minutes, and if you do, you arrive at Jandakot 30 minutes later. The backwards count from last light has to include that possible delay, so departure moves 30 minutes earlier.

## Bringing it together

At 1300 on the day, a new TAF comes out for Jandakot with a TEMPO group of thunderstorms over your arrival time. The TAF test now requires **60 minutes of holding** at Jandakot: a new line of $60 \times 27 \div 60 = 27$ L in the ledger, which moves the minimum fuel from 82.1 L to 109.1 L, still inside the 130 L you planned to carry. The weight is unchanged. But the latest departure time does: 60 more minutes of possible holding moves it from 1540 to **1440 WST**, and if it is already 1500 when you have briefed, the plan as drawn is dead. Leave out Northam, with its INTER holding and ground time, to recover about 70 minutes, or go another day. Nothing in that decision needed a new skill, only the same ledger and backwards count rerun with one new input: exactly what the exam tests.

??? check "Check yourself: the passengers ask to bring an extra 15 kg of bags after the plan is complete. Which steps of the plan do you have to redo?"
    The weight and balance with the new zero fuel weight and CG, and the take-off and landing performance at the new weights. If the extra weight pushes you over MTOW you must take out fuel, and then recheck that what remains still meets the minimum fuel required; the route, levels and times stay as they were.

## Key points

- **2.5.1** Route, briefing, payload and performance data each answer a different question; go to the piece each question needs.
- **2.5.2 (a)** Route clear of airspace you cannot enter, active restricted areas and high terrain, over map-readable features; LSALT is the highest terrain or obstacle in the tolerance plus 1,000 ft.
- **2.5.2 (b)** Each level must be above the LSALT, in VMC, below any CTA step and below the oxygen levels; above 5,000 ft fly odd plus 500 on magnetic tracks 000 to 179 and even plus 500 on 180 to 359.
- **2.5.2 (c)** Ledger in order: taxi, trip, holding (60 minutes TEMPO, 30 minutes INTER), alternate at cruise flow, fixed reserve of 30 minutes at holding flow, and optional contingency; one unit, no rounding until the end, litres to kilograms at 0.72.
- **2.5.2 (d)** Check MTOW, the climb weight limit, the CG at zero fuel and take-off weight, and the landing weights; take out baggage or fuel (never below the minimum) and repeat.
- **2.5.2 (e)** Latest departure = last light at the final landing point, minus 10 minutes, minus the total flight time, minus any holding, minus the ground time at the stops; then convert to UTC.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Expect one scenario and a chain of questions on it. Keep the fuel ledger in the order taxi, trip, holding, alternate, reserve, and in one unit; holding and the 30-minute reserve are separate lines. Cruising levels: odd plus 500 eastbound (000 to 179 magnetic), even plus 500 westbound, chosen on magnetic track. Latest departure time works backwards from last light minus 10 minutes, through the total flight time including holding and stops. Have the conversion figures to hand: 0.72 kg per litre, and WST is UTC plus 8.
