## What the exam expects (POPC 2.7)

- **2.7.1** Say what the certificate of registration and the certificate of airworthiness are for.
- **2.7.2** From a flight scenario, work out the communication equipment, the normal equipment and the emergency equipment the aircraft must carry.
- **2.7.3** As pilot in command: **(a)** your responsibilities for the daily inspection; **(b)** your responsibilities for recording and reporting defects; **(c)** the maintenance a PPL or CPL holder may carry out; **(d)** from a copy of a maintenance release, decide whether it is valid, which classes of operation it allows, and which defects or endorsements are outstanding and whether they affect airworthiness.

## Why this matters

As a student you flew a club aeroplane that someone else had prepared, on a flight your instructor had approved, mostly within sight of Jandakot or Serpentine. As a private pilot you take that same aeroplane, or one you own a share of, to Kalbarri, Esperance or Rottnest with friends on board, and nobody else checks it. You decide whether the paperwork makes it legal, whether the equipment on board matches the flight you have planned, and what you may fix yourself when a bulb blows at a country strip. The RPL lesson ([BAKC 6.1](/notes/BAKC/6.1)) built the chain of documents and inspections; this lesson adds what the PPL exam asks on top: why the two certificates exist, how to extract the equipment list for a scenario, what maintenance you may do, and how to read a maintenance release line by line and give a verdict.

## What you already know from the RPL

[BAKC 6.1](/notes/BAKC/6.1) laid out the chain that tells you an aircraft is fit to fly today: a certificate of airworthiness that stays in force while the aircraft is maintained, a certificate of registration, the aircraft's own POH, and the **maintenance release**, the document you check before every flight. You learned the four questions you ask of the maintenance release (current in hours and date, daily certified today, no grounding defect, nothing due before you land), the difference between the certified daily inspection and the uncertified pre-flight, and how a defect is endorsed in Part 2 and locks the aircraft until an engineer deals with it.

![How do you know the aeroplane is serviceable today?](diagram:airworthiness-documents-flow "Every link in the chain points to the maintenance release: current in hours and date, daily certified, no grounding defect, nothing due before you land.")

None of that changes at PPL level. What changes is the depth: the exam gives you a maintenance release and asks for a verdict, gives you a route and asks for the equipment, and asks which jobs you may do yourself.

## Two certificates, two questions (2.7.1)

Think of a car: its registration papers say which car it is and who is responsible for it; its roadworthy inspection says it was safe when checked. An aircraft has the same split.

The **certificate of registration** records that the aircraft is entered on the **Australian civil aircraft register** with its **VH- marks**, and identifies the **registered operator**, the person legally responsible for it (CASR Part 47). It answers the question "**which aircraft is this, and who answers for it?**" It is the aircraft's identity document, and it is a legal document rather than an engineering one: it says nothing about whether the aircraft is in good condition. An **unregistered aircraft cannot be flown**.

The **certificate of airworthiness** answers a different question: "**was this aircraft built to an approved design, and is it kept to it?**" It certifies that the aircraft **conforms to its type design** and was **in a condition for safe operation when the certificate was issued** (CASR Part 21). It has no expiry date. Instead it **remains in force as long as the aircraft is maintained in accordance with the regulations and its approved maintenance program, and is operated within its flight manual**. Stop the maintenance, or operate outside the flight manual, and the certificate no longer means what it says.

The certificate of airworthiness is issued in a **category** that defines the operations permitted: **normal, utility or aerobatic** for standard aircraft, or a **special category** for experimental and limited aircraft. That is where the limits on what you may do with the aircraft start. An aircraft certificated in the normal category is not one to loop, whatever its owner thinks of its handling.

Neither certificate tells you that the aircraft is fit to fly **today**. That is the job of the maintenance release, the day-to-day evidence of continuing airworthiness. Together the three answer three questions: registration, **who** the aircraft is; certificate of airworthiness, that it was **built and is maintained** to standard; maintenance release, that it is **fit to fly now**.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Treating the certificate of airworthiness as proof that the aircraft is airworthy today. It certifies conformity to the type design and stays in force only while the maintenance and operating conditions are met. The document that shows current serviceability is the maintenance release.

??? check "Check yourself: an aircraft's maintenance release expired last week. Is its certificate of airworthiness still meaningful, and may it fly?"
    The certificate is in force only while the aircraft is maintained as the regulations and its maintenance program require. With the maintenance release expired that condition is not met, so the aircraft may not fly until it is maintained and released again.

## Working out the equipment for a flight (2.7.2)

The equipment an aircraft must carry is not one fixed list. The Part 91 MOS sets a base for the kind of operation (day VFR, night VFR, IFR) and then adds items according to **where** you fly: how high, over what, how far from the aerodrome, in which airspace. So the exam gives you a scenario and expects you to build the list. Work through it in four groups, in the same order every time.

### Normal equipment: flight and engine instruments

These are what you need to fly the aeroplane under day VFR at all. For a day VFR private flight in a light aeroplane:

- an **airspeed indicator**, to stay above the stall and below the structural limits;
- an **altimeter**, to fly the minimum heights, the cruising level and the circuit;
- a **magnetic compass**, the one direction reference that needs no power;
- a **clock or watch showing hours, minutes and seconds**, for navigation and timing;
- **engine instruments** and **fuel quantity indication**.

**Night and IFR** add lighting, attitude and heading instruments, a turn and slip indicator, and the navigation equipment for the route. None of these is required for day VFR.

### Communication equipment

Radio and transponder requirements follow from airspace and height, because they exist so that ATC and other aircraft can talk to you and see you.

- A **VHF radio** is required for flight **in controlled airspace**, at **certified and registered aerodromes**, at **CTAF aerodromes where radio is required**, and **above 5,000 ft**.
- A **transponder** is required in **Class A, C and E airspace and above 10,000 ft**, with **altitude encoding** (verify the current list in the Part 91 MOS and AIP).
- An **ADS-B transmitter** is required where the rules require it.

### Navigation equipment

The **charts and documents for the route** ([PFRA 2.3](/notes/PFRA/2.3)), and **navigation aids as needed for the airspace**. For day VFR the main item is current charts covering the whole route and ERSA.

### Emergency equipment

This is where the scenario details matter most, because each item answers a particular way of coming to grief.

- An **ELT** (emergency locator transmitter) for flights **beyond the vicinity (more than 50 nm) of the departure aerodrome**, with exemptions for some local flights. Beyond about 50 nm, a search could not quickly cover the area you might be in, so the aircraft must be able to tell rescuers where it is.
- A **first aid kit**.
- A **fire extinguisher** in the cabin for aircraft above a certain seat count (check the MOS for the figure).
- **Life jackets** for flight over water **beyond gliding distance of land**: if the engine stops there, the landing is a ditching.
- **Life rafts and signalling equipment** for **extended over-water flight**.
- **Survival equipment** (water, signalling equipment and the items prescribed) for flight over **designated remote areas**, where you might wait days for rescue.
- **Oxygen** for flight **above 10,000 ft beyond the time limit**, and **above the altitude where it is always required** ([PHFC 2.12](/notes/PHFC/2.12)).

![What keeps you alive after a forced landing in remote country?](diagram:survival-priorities "Protection, location, water, food, in that order; stay with the aircraft and use the ELT, which is why remote and distant flights must carry one.")

Each of the trigger words in a scenario points at an item: **height** (radio above 5,000 ft, transponder and oxygen above 10,000 ft), **airspace** (radio and transponder), **distance** from the departure aerodrome (ELT), **water** (life jackets, rafts), **remote area** (survival equipment). Read the scenario for those words, then walk the four groups.

!!! example "Worked example: equipment for a remote inland flight"
    Given: a private day VFR flight from Jandakot to Meekatharra, about 350 nm, three people on board, cruising at 9,500 ft in Class G airspace once clear of the Perth control zone and steps. Suppose the route crosses a designated remote area on the AIP map, and there is no water crossing.

    1. Normal: **airspeed indicator, altimeter, magnetic compass, clock or watch with seconds, engine instruments, fuel quantity indication.** Nothing extra: day VFR.
    2. Communication: **VHF radio**, because the cruise is above 5,000 ft (and departure is from a controlled aerodrome). **Transponder: not required by the list** for Class G below 10,000 ft; it would be if the route entered Class A, C or E or went above 10,000 ft.
    3. Navigation: **current charts for the whole route and ERSA.**
    4. Emergency: **ELT**, because the flight goes more than 50 nm from Jandakot. **Survival equipment** (water, signalling equipment and the prescribed items), for the remote area. **First aid kit.** **Fire extinguisher** if the seat count requires it. **No life jackets** (no water) and **no oxygen** (below 10,000 ft).

    The list followed the scenario word by word: 9,500 ft brought the radio, 350 nm the ELT, the remote area the survival kit, and what was absent (water, height above 10,000 ft) took items off.

Now try one in the other direction before opening the solution. A private day VFR flight from Jandakot across to Rottnest Island and back, about 20 nm each way, at 1,500 ft, two people on board, part of it over open water out of gliding distance of land. Which emergency items does the scenario add, and which does it not?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    **Life jackets**, because part of the flight is over water beyond gliding distance of land. **No ELT** on the distance test, because the flight stays within 50 nm of Jandakot. **No remote-area survival equipment** and **no oxygen**. The first aid kit (and a fire extinguisher if the seat count requires one) are carried as for any flight. A **VHF radio** is needed anyway, because Jandakot is in controlled airspace. Whether this counts as extended over-water flight for life rafts depends on the distance set in the MOS; check it.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Reading the ELT rule as "over remote areas" or "over water". The trigger in the rule as given is **distance from the departure aerodrome, more than 50 nm**. A short over-water hop can need life jackets and no ELT; a long trip over farmland can need an ELT and no life jackets.

??? check "Check yourself: you replan the Meekatharra flight at 11,500 ft to get above a cloud layer. Which items does that add?"
    A transponder with altitude encoding, because you are above 10,000 ft, and oxygen once your time above 10,000 ft passes the limit. The higher level removes nothing already on the list.

## The pilot in command's responsibilities (2.7.3a and b)

The responsibilities are those of [BAKC 6.1](/notes/BAKC/6.1), and at PPL level you meet more of them yourself.

**Daily inspections.** Before the first flight of the day, the pilot in command **confirms that a daily inspection has been certified** on the maintenance release. As a private pilot you **may perform and certify it yourself when you are authorised** to do so on the type. That authorisation is the difference between doing a careful walk-around and signing a daily: the daily is done to the written schedule, and your signature on the maintenance release tells every later pilot that day that it was done. Your own **pre-flight inspection** is still required before every flight and is never certified.

![How does the daily inspection differ from the pre-flight inspection?](diagram:daily-vs-preflight-inspection "Daily: once a day, to the schedule, certified on the maintenance release. Pre-flight: every flight, by you, not certified.")

**Recording.** The pilot in command **records the flight time** on the maintenance release, so that the hours on it stay true and the next pilot can tell how close the aircraft is to its next maintenance.

**Defects.** You **endorse defects in the maintenance release** (Part 2), with a clear description, the date and time in service, and your signature and licence number. A defect found in flight is endorsed after landing. The aircraft then stays on the ground until an engineer rectifies the defect or endorses it as a permissible unserviceability with any conditions.

**Reporting.** Separately, you **report defects affecting safety** under the safety reporting rules. The endorsement protects the next pilot of this aircraft; the report lets the system see the same fault across a fleet.

![What happens after you endorse a defect?](diagram:defect-endorsement-flow "The endorsement grounds the aircraft until an engineer rectifies it or endorses a permissible unserviceability with conditions.")

??? check "Check yourself: you are authorised to do daily inspections on the club aeroplane, and you certify one at 0800. You fly again at 1400. Which inspection do you do before the 1400 flight?"
    Only the pre-flight inspection. The daily you certified at 0800 is valid for the rest of the day, but the pre-flight is required before every flight, whoever did the daily.

## Maintenance you may do yourself (2.7.3c)

At a country strip on a Sunday, the navigation light bulb is dead and the oil is a litre low. There is no engineer for 200 km. Can you fix it?

For some jobs, yes. **CAR 1988 Schedule 8** lists the maintenance that the **holder of a pilot licence** may perform on a **Class B aircraft** (private and aerial work categories) that they are **entitled to fly**, provided they are **competent** to do it and **record it in the maintenance release or logbook**. The logic of the list is that every item is simple, needs no special tools and no engineering judgement, and can be checked by looking at it afterwards. The list includes:

- **replenishing** engine oil, hydraulic fluid and fuel;
- **replacing and servicing batteries**;
- **replacing tyres and tubes, bulbs, fuses and spark plugs** (spark plugs with the correct type and gapping);
- **cleaning and lubricating**;
- **replacing seats, seat belts and cabin furnishings**;
- **removing and refitting cowlings, fairings and inspection panels**;
- **replacing filters where no special tools are needed**;
- **the daily inspection**.

It **excludes** anything that needs a licensed engineer's judgement, special tools, or a test flight. A **CPL holder has the same Schedule 8 privileges**. Anything outside the list needs a **maintenance authority or a LAME** (licensed aircraft maintenance engineer).

So at the country strip you may top up the oil and replace the bulb, and you must record both. Rigging a control cable, adjusting the carburettor or changing a magneto is not on the list, whatever your mechanical skills.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    "Competent" and "recorded" are part of the privilege, not decorations on it. A job on the list done without the right parts, the right type of plug, or an entry in the maintenance release or logbook is not authorised maintenance.

??? check "Check yourself: which of these may a PPL holder do on their own private aeroplane: replace a blown landing light bulb, replace a spark plug with the correct type and gap, or adjust the idle mixture?"
    The bulb and the spark plug are on the Schedule 8 list, provided you are competent and record the work. Adjusting the idle mixture needs an engineer's judgement and possibly a ground run and test, so it is outside the list.

## Reading a maintenance release (2.7.3d)

The exam gives you a copy of a maintenance release and three questions: is it **valid**, what **classes of operation** does it allow, and what is **outstanding**, and does it affect airworthiness? Each question has a place on the document.

**Validity.** Look at the **issue date** and the **expiry**, stated as a date or time in service, for example **12 months or 100 hours, whichever comes first**. Compare the aircraft's **current total time** with the expiry time. Then look for **maintenance due before expiry**: an inspection due at a stated hour count, or an **airworthiness directive due by a date**. Once that point is passed the release is no longer valid, even though its own expiry is still ahead. Finally check that the **daily inspection has been certified for today**.

**Classes of operation.** The release states the **categories of operation** the aircraft is certified and maintained for: **private, aerial work, charter or air transport**. Maintenance is more demanding for the commercial categories, so the release can only allow the operations the aircraft has been maintained for. An aircraft maintained to the **private** category **cannot be used for charter**.

**Defects and endorsements.** Every entry in **Part 2** has one of three statuses:

- **rectified** and signed off by an engineer: no longer a defect;
- deferred as a **permissible unserviceability** (PU) with conditions, for example "landing light unserviceable, day VFR only": the aircraft may fly, but only within those conditions;
- **uncleared**: still open.

An **uncleared defect that affects airworthiness** means the aircraft is **not airworthy**. So does a **permissible unserviceability whose conditions you cannot meet** on the flight you have planned. That second point is the one the exam likes: a PU is not a general permission, it is a permission for flights that keep to its conditions.

!!! example "Worked example: a maintenance release verdict"
    Given: today is 4 October. The release was issued on 18 May this year at **1,876.3 hours**, valid for **100 hours or 12 months**, categories **private and aerial work**. Maintenance due: an airworthiness directive inspection at **1,950.0 hours**. The aircraft now shows **1,942.6 hours**. No daily is certified today, and you are authorised to do it. You plan a **1.6 hour** private flight from Northam, in Class G airspace at 3,500 ft. Part 2 shows:

    - left fuel gauge reading low: **rectified**, signed off by an engineer on 12 September;
    - transponder unserviceable: **PU**, "not to be flown in airspace where a transponder is required";
    - right brake soft and pulling: endorsed by a pilot **yesterday**, **not cleared**.

    1. Expiry by hours: $1876.3 + 100 = \mathbf{1{,}976.3}$ hours. By date: **18 May next year**.
    2. Maintenance due first: the AD at **1,950.0 hours**. Hours at the end of your flight: $1942.6 + 1.6 = \mathbf{1{,}944.2}$, below it. **Valid for this flight.**
    3. Daily: not yet done; you **inspect to the schedule and certify it** before flight.
    4. Classes: **private and aerial work**; your flight is private. **Allowed.**
    5. Fuel gauge: **rectified**, no effect. Transponder PU: Class G below 10,000 ft needs no transponder on the list, so **the condition is met**.
    6. Brake: **uncleared**, and brakes affect safe ground handling. **Not airworthy.**

    Verdict: you do not fly until an engineer has dealt with the brake. Everything else on the release was in order, which is exactly why it is worth reading to the end.

Now try one before opening the solution. A week later the brake has been rectified and signed off, and the aircraft shows 1,944.2 hours. You plan a round trip from Northam to Geraldton and back, **6.5 hours** in all, private, in Class G below 10,000 ft. Is the release valid for the whole trip, and does the transponder PU stop you?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    The AD inspection is due at 1,950.0 hours, and you would finish at $1944.2 + 6.5 = 1{,}950.7$ hours, 0.7 hours past it. The release is **not valid for the whole trip**: you could fly at most $1950.0 - 1944.2 = 5.8$ hours before the AD inspection. The transponder PU does not stop you, because Class G below 10,000 ft is not airspace where the list requires a transponder; but the AD does.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Stopping at the expiry line. The release's own expiry, 100 hours or 12 months, is rarely the first limit. An AD or inspection due at a lower hour count, or a date before expiry, comes first, and the question is always whether you will pass it **before you land**.

## Bringing it together

A Saturday trip: you and two friends from Jandakot to Kalbarri, about 280 nm, with the route along the coast and a short stretch over the water at Jurien Bay, cruising at 6,500 ft (a westbound track, so even plus 500). Before leaving home you check the documents: the aircraft is registered, its certificate of airworthiness is in the normal category, and the maintenance release shows 31 hours to run and no AD due inside them, against a 5.5 hour weekend. The categories include private.

Equipment, group by group. The day VFR instruments are fitted. At 6,500 ft you need the VHF radio; the transponder is not required by the list in Class G below 10,000 ft. Charts and ERSA for the whole coast. Kalbarri is more than 50 nm from Jandakot, so the ELT must be carried; the stretch over the water at Jurien Bay is within gliding distance of the shore at 6,500 ft, but you plan to drop lower along the coast on the way home, so you take life jackets for the three of you rather than replan the descent around them.

At the aeroplane you certify the daily, as you are authorised to, and do your pre-flight. At Kalbarri the left navigation light bulb has blown; replacing it is Schedule 8 maintenance, you have the bulb and are competent, so you fit it and record it. On the way home the alternator warning light flickers for twenty minutes. After landing you endorse it in Part 2 with the date, time in service, signature and licence number, record the flight time, and the aeroplane waits for an engineer. Every responsibility in the subtopic appeared in one weekend.

??? check "Check yourself: in that scenario, why could you fix the navigation light yourself but not the alternator warning?"
    Replacing a bulb is on the Schedule 8 list, with no special tools or judgement needed. A flickering alternator warning needs diagnosis by an engineer, so you endorse it as a defect and the aircraft is grounded until it is rectified or accepted as a permissible unserviceability.

## Key points

- **2.7.1** Registration: which aircraft, and who answers for it (VH- marks, registered operator). Certificate of airworthiness: conforms to type design, in force while maintained and operated within the flight manual, issued in a category. Maintenance release: fit to fly today.
- **2.7.2** Build the list from the scenario: day VFR instruments; radio by airspace and above 5,000 ft; transponder in Class A, C and E and above 10,000 ft; charts and documents; ELT beyond 50 nm, life jackets beyond gliding distance of land, rafts for extended over-water flight, survival equipment for remote areas, oxygen above 10,000 ft; first aid kit and, by seat count, a fire extinguisher.
- **2.7.3 (a)** Confirm the daily is certified before the first flight of the day, or do and certify it yourself when authorised; the pre-flight is still yours every flight.
- **2.7.3 (b)** Record the flight time, endorse defects in Part 2 with description, date, time in service, signature and licence number, and report defects that affect safety.
- **2.7.3 (c)** Schedule 8: oil, fuel and fluids, batteries, tyres, bulbs, fuses, plugs, cleaning, seats and belts, panels and simple filters, and the daily inspection, on a Class B aircraft you may fly, if competent, and recorded. Nothing needing an engineer's judgement, special tools or a test flight.
- **2.7.3 (d)** Validity: date, hours, maintenance or ADs due first, and today's daily. Classes: only those listed. Defects: rectified, PU with conditions you must meet, or uncleared; an uncleared defect affecting airworthiness, or a PU whose conditions you cannot meet, grounds the aircraft for your flight.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Registration says who the aircraft is; the certificate of airworthiness says it was built and is maintained to standard; the maintenance release says it is fit to fly today. Pilot maintenance is the Schedule 8 list: oil, fuel, tyres, bulbs, fuses, plugs, batteries, panels and cleaning, recorded in the release. For equipment questions, read the scenario for height, airspace, distance, water and remote area, then walk the groups. For a maintenance release, check what falls due first and whether it falls due before you land, and test every permissible unserviceability against the flight you have actually planned.
