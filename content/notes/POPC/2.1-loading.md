## What the exam expects (POPC 2.1)

- **2.1.1 (a)** Describe arm, moment, datum and station, and the index unit that loading systems use to scale moments.
- **2.1.1 (b)** Describe the centre of gravity and the CG limits, and why they form an envelope.
- **2.1.1 (c)** Describe empty weight, zero fuel weight and ramp weight.
- **2.1.1 (d)** Describe the maximum take-off and maximum landing weights.
- **2.1.1 (e)** Describe floor loading limits and how to check them.

## Why this matters

As a private pilot you will fly heavier and further than you did as a student: three passengers, bags and enough fuel for Jandakot to Kalbarri. Those loads sit near the limits, and the loading sheet in the POH is your only way of knowing before you start. In the PPLA exam you load an aeroplane with one of the CASA workbook systems, ALPHA, BRAVO or CHARLIE, and CASA lists "correct use of the various loading systems" among the most common PPLA errors. The physics is the lever from [BAKC 6.4](/notes/BAKC/6.4): weight times arm, add, divide. This lesson adds index units, the weight ladder from empty to ramp, floor loading, and a routine for each workbook system.

## From moments to index units (2.1.1a)

Start from what you already know. The **datum** is the reference plane all distances are measured from; the **arm** is the distance from the datum to an item's centre of gravity, positive aft; the **moment** is weight times arm, the item's turning effect about the datum; and a **station** is a place in the aeroplane identified by its arm, such as the front seats or the baggage compartment. [BAKC 6.4](/notes/BAKC/6.4) builds all of these from the playground seesaw, and the diagram below is its worked example.

![How do weight and arm combine into a moment?](diagram:moment-arm-seesaw "Each load pushes down at its arm; the total moment divided by the total weight gives the balance point.")

Now look at the size of the numbers. A 70 kg passenger sitting 3,300 mm behind the datum has a moment of

$$70 \times 3300 = 231{,}000\ \text{kg mm}$$

An empty aeroplane of nearly 700 kg has a moment approaching two million kilogram millimetres. Summing seven-digit numbers by hand in an exam room invites a dropped zero and a CG a metre out.

So the loading systems divide every moment by a fixed number and work with the result. That scaled moment is an **index unit** (IU). Loading system CHARLIE defines

$$1\ \text{IU} = 100\ \text{kg mm}$$

so the passenger's 231,000 kg mm becomes **2,310 IU**. Every item is divided by the same 100, so the balance is unchanged; multiply back by 100 for the CG in millimetres.

Different systems choose different scalings. CHARLIE uses 100 kg mm. BRAVO uses moment divided by 1,000 in inch-pounds. ALPHA and ECHO use index units read off graphs, with the scaling (and in ALPHA's case an offset that lets the empty aeroplane start at a negative number) built into the scales. The rule is simple: **always use the system's own definition**, printed on its instructions page, and never carry one system's scaling into another.

!!! example "Worked example: index units for fuel and baggage in CHARLIE"
    Given: CHARLIE, 1 IU = 100 kg mm, fuel arm 2,950 mm, baggage arm 4,210 mm, AVGAS SG 0.72. Load 120 L of fuel and 40 kg of baggage.

    1. Fuel mass: $120 \times 0.72 = \mathbf{86.4\ kg}$.
    2. Fuel moment: $86.4 \times 2950 = 254{,}880$ kg mm, so $254{,}880 \div 100 = \mathbf{2{,}548.8\ IU}$.
    3. Baggage moment: $40 \times 4210 = 168{,}400$ kg mm, so $168{,}400 \div 100 = \mathbf{1{,}684\ IU}$.

    These are exactly the figures CHARLIE's index unit table on page 14 gives for 120 L and 40 kg: the table is this arithmetic done in advance.

The same arithmetic runs backwards. Divide an item's index units by its weight and multiply by 100 and you recover its arm: CHARLIE's 7 kg of oil at 86.1 IU sits at $86.1 \times 100 \div 7 = 1{,}230$ mm, well forward near the engine. A rear passenger with an arm forward of the pilot means a misread line.

![How do litres become kilograms?](diagram:units-conversion-ladder "AVGAS at 0.72 kg per litre: 120 L is 86.4 kg. Litres to kilograms, multiply; kilograms to litres, divide.")

??? check "Check yourself: why do loading systems use index units instead of raw moments?"
    Raw moments in kilogram millimetres run to six and seven digits, which invites slips. Dividing every moment by the same constant (100 kg mm in CHARLIE) shrinks the numbers without changing the balance; multiply back at the end to get the CG.

## The centre of gravity and its envelope (2.1.1b)

The **centre of gravity** (CG) is the single point where the whole weight of the loaded aeroplane can be thought of as acting. It is found the same way in every system: total moment divided by total weight. With index units the scaling has to come back out, so CHARLIE's instruction page gives

$$\text{CG (mm aft of datum)} = \frac{\text{IU} \times 100}{\text{weight (kg)}}$$

The **CG limits** are the forward and aft positions between which the aeroplane has been shown to be stable and controllable. Forward of the forward limit the tail cannot raise the nose for the flare; aft of the aft limit the aeroplane loses longitudinal stability and may not recover from a stall or spin ([BAKC 6.4](/notes/BAKC/6.4)).

![What goes wrong with the CG outside each limit?](diagram:cg-position-effects "Forward: stable but nose heavy, higher stall speed, maybe not enough elevator to flare. Aft: unstable and over-sensitive, stall and spin recovery possibly impossible.")

On most aeroplanes the limits are not two fixed numbers. At higher weights the wing flies at a higher angle of attack and the tail has to work harder, so the **forward limit moves aft as weight rises**. Drawn on a graph of weight against CG position, the limits make a shape, the **CG envelope**. CHARLIE plots weight against CG in millimetres, BRAVO weight against moment, ECHO gross weight against total moment index, and ALPHA drops a line from its index scales onto the envelope. The test is always the same: **the point must lie inside the envelope**, at every stage of the flight.

CHARLIE and BRAVO have two envelopes, one per **category**: **normal**, and a smaller **utility** envelope that allows more manoeuvring (spins, steeper turns) for a lower maximum weight and a tighter CG range. A load can be legal in normal and not in utility, so the question tells you which applies.

![Load an aeroplane and watch the CG move on the envelope](widget:weight-and-balance "The point must stay inside the envelope at take-off and after the fuel has burned off; the forward limit tightens as weight rises.")

??? check "Check yourself: why does the forward CG limit often move aft at higher weights?"
    At higher weight the wing must make more lift and the tail must push down harder to balance a forward CG. Near the forward limit at high weight the elevator would run out of authority, especially in the flare, so the designers move the forward limit aft as weight increases, which makes the envelope slope.

## The weight ladder: empty, zero fuel, take-off, ramp and landing (2.1.1c and d)

A loading calculation builds the aeroplane up in layers, each with its own name and its own check.

**Empty weight** (basic empty weight) is the aeroplane as weighed, with its fixed equipment, **unusable fuel and full or undrainable oil**, as each workbook system specifies in its notes. It comes with its own moment or index units and is unique to that airframe: the exam gives it to you, and in real life it comes from the aeroplane's weight and balance schedule.

**Zero fuel weight** (ZFW) is the empty weight plus everything you load except usable fuel: oil if it is not already included, the occupants and the baggage. Many larger aeroplanes have a **maximum zero fuel weight** (MZFW; ECHO's is 2,630 kg) because people and bags in the fuselage bend the wing roots upward, while fuel in the wings does not.

**Take-off weight** is ZFW plus the fuel on board at the start of the take-off roll. It must not exceed the **maximum take-off weight** (MTOW), the structural limit set by the strength of the airframe: 1,115 kg (normal category) in CHARLIE, 1,000 kg (2,200 lb) in BRAVO, 1,633 kg in ALPHA, 2,950 kg in ECHO.

**Ramp weight** is the weight at engine start: take-off weight plus the taxi and run-up allowance. Some aeroplanes have a **maximum ramp weight** slightly above MTOW, so you can load taxi fuel on top of a full take-off load and burn down to MTOW before lining up.

**Landing weight** is take-off weight minus the fuel burned in flight. It must not exceed the **maximum landing weight** (MLW), the structural limit for the touchdown loads on the undercarriage and its attachments. In light trainers MLW often equals MTOW. In ECHO it is lower, 2,725 kg against an MTOW of 2,950 kg, so a full ECHO taking off at MTOW must burn 225 kg of fuel before it may land.

![Which weight is checked against which limit?](diagram:weight-ladder "Each rung adds a load and meets its own limit: zero fuel weight against MZFW, take-off against MTOW, landing against MLW.")

Fuel is the only load that changes in flight, and its tanks are rarely exactly at the CG. As it burns, the loading point slides along a line from the take-off point towards the zero fuel point: forward if the tanks are aft of the CG, aft if they are forward. So the workbook has you check the CG at **both ZFW and take-off weight**, the two ends of the line; if both are inside, the whole flight is. The landing point lies between them.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Checking only the take-off point. A load can be inside the envelope at take-off and outside it at ZFW, typically a light pilot with heavy baggage in the rear compartment and fuel tanks forward of the CG: as the fuel burns, the CG walks aft out of the envelope. The exam builds questions on exactly this.

??? check "Check yourself: which is heavier, the ramp weight or the take-off weight, and by how much?"
    The ramp weight, by the taxi and run-up fuel: it is the weight at engine start, and that fuel is burned before the take-off roll. Where an aeroplane has a maximum ramp weight above MTOW, the difference is that allowance.

## Floor loading limits (2.1.1e)

A baggage compartment has two separate limits, and they protect different things. The compartment's **maximum weight** (122 kg in CHARLIE, 54 kg in BRAVO, 55 kg and 155 kg for ECHO's compartments) protects the floor beams, the tie-down points and the compartment's contribution to the CG. The **floor loading limit** protects the floor panel itself from being punched through or buckled by a concentrated load.

Think of a stiletto heel on a timber floor: the same person in flat shoes does no harm, but on a heel the whole weight goes through a tiny area and dents the boards. A floor is rated for a maximum **weight per unit area**, in kilograms per square metre. ECHO's floor loading limit is **450 kg/m²**. To check an item:

$$\text{floor loading} = \frac{\text{weight (kg)}}{\text{contact area (m}^2)}$$

A dense item, such as a battery or a case of tinned food, can be well inside the compartment's weight limit and still exceed the floor loading. The remedy is to stand it on a board, which increases the contact area; the board's weight goes on the load sheet too.

!!! example "Worked example: a spare battery in the rear compartment"
    Given: a 30 kg battery with a base of 0.2 m by 0.15 m; floor loading limit 450 kg/m².

    1. Contact area: $0.2 \times 0.15 = \mathbf{0.03\ m^2}$.
    2. Floor loading: $30 \div 0.03 = \mathbf{1{,}000\ kg/m^2}$, more than twice the 450 kg/m² limit.
    3. Smallest area that keeps it legal: $30 \div 450 = \mathbf{0.067\ m^2}$.
    4. Stood on a board of 0.4 m by 0.3 m: area $0.12\ m^2$, loading $30 \div 0.12 = \mathbf{250\ kg/m^2}$, within the limit.

    The battery was always within the compartment's weight limit; only the floor loading made it illegal.

![How does a board make a heavy item legal?](diagram:floor-loading-board "The same 30 kg on four times the area: 1,000 kg/m² on its own base, 250 kg/m² on the board, against a 450 kg/m² limit.")

Now try one before opening the solution: a 40 kg box of survey equipment has a base of 0.25 m by 0.25 m. Does it meet a 450 kg/m² floor loading limit?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    Area $= 0.25 \times 0.25 = 0.0625$ m². Floor loading $= 40 \div 0.0625 = 640$ kg/m², above the 450 kg/m² limit. It needs at least $40 \div 450 = 0.089$ m² of contact area, for example a board 0.3 m by 0.3 m (0.09 m², about 444 kg/m²), and a larger board gives a sensible margin.

??? check "Check yourself: a heavy item is within the compartment's maximum weight. Why might it still be illegal to carry it as it is?"
    Because floor loading is weight per unit area, not total weight. A dense item with a small base can exceed the kilograms per square metre the floor is rated for; a board spreads it and brings the loading down.

## Working the CASA loading systems

Every workbook system is the same calculation in a different costume. Use one order every time:

1. **Read the system's instructions page.** Note its units (kilograms and millimetres, or pounds and inches), its index unit definition, the SG (0.72 kg per litre in all of them), the MTOW for the category, and the compartment limits.
2. **Start with the empty aeroplane** and its moment or index units, as given in the question.
3. **Add every load item except fuel**, each with its moment or index units from the system's table or graph. Total them: that is the **ZFW** line.
4. **Convert the fuel** from litres to kilograms (multiply by 0.72), or to pounds for BRAVO, and find its moment or index units. Add it: that is the **take-off weight** line.
5. **Check the weights**: take-off weight against MTOW, each compartment against its own limit, ZFW against MZFW where one exists.
6. **Find the CG** (or the plotting point) at ZFW and at take-off weight, and check **both** against the envelope.

Do not round until the end: a take-off weight 0.2 kg under MTOW is legal, but rounded up early it becomes illegal.

### CHARLIE: index units in kilograms and millimetres

CHARLIE is a four-seater worked entirely in arithmetic. The instructions are on page 13, the index unit tables for fuel (arm 2,950 mm), baggage (arm 4,210 mm), the two seat rows and oil on page 14, and the CG envelope on page 15. Its limits: MTOW **1,115 kg** normal category, **925 kg** utility, baggage compartment **122 kg**.

!!! example "Worked example: loading CHARLIE for four people and bags"
    Given: empty 687 kg, 19,522 IU; oil 7 kg, 86.1 IU; two people in row 1 totalling 160 kg; one person in row 2, 75 kg; baggage 40 kg; fuel 120 L. From the workbook: fuel 120 L = 86.4 kg = 2,548.8 IU; baggage 40 kg = 1,684 IU; row 1 at 160 kg about 4,400 IU; row 2 at 75 kg about 2,700 IU (read the real values from the workbook in the exam).

    | Item | Weight (kg) | Index units |
    |---|---|---|
    | Empty aeroplane | 687 | 19,522 |
    | Oil | 7 | 86.1 |
    | Row 1 | 160 | 4,400 |
    | Row 2 | 75 | 2,700 |
    | Baggage | 40 | 1,684 |
    | **Zero fuel weight** | **969** | **28,392.1** |
    | Fuel 120 L | 86.4 | 2,548.8 |
    | **Take-off weight** | **1,055.4** | **30,940.9** |

    1. ZFW: $687 + 7 + 160 + 75 + 40 = \mathbf{969\ kg}$; IU $= 19{,}522 + 86.1 + 4{,}400 + 2{,}700 + 1{,}684 = \mathbf{28{,}392.1}$.
    2. CG at ZFW: $28{,}392.1 \times 100 \div 969 = \mathbf{2{,}930\ mm}$.
    3. Take-off weight: $969 + 86.4 = \mathbf{1{,}055.4\ kg}$, under the 1,115 kg MTOW; IU $= 28{,}392.1 + 2{,}548.8 = \mathbf{30{,}940.9}$.
    4. CG at take-off: $30{,}940.9 \times 100 \div 1{,}055.4 = \mathbf{2{,}932\ mm}$ (2,931.7).
    5. Plot both points (969 kg at 2,930 mm and 1,055.4 kg at 2,932 mm) on the page 15 envelope and confirm both are inside.

    Both weights are legal and the baggage is well inside its 122 kg limit.

Notice what the two CG figures say. The fuel sits at 2,950 mm, a little **aft** of the 2,932 mm CG, so as it burns the CG creeps **forward**, from 2,932 mm at take-off to 2,930 mm with dry tanks. If you were asked for the landing CG after burning 60 L ($60 \times 0.72 = 43.2$ kg, $43.2 \times 29.5 = 1{,}274.4$ IU), it would be $(30{,}940.9 - 1{,}274.4) \times 100 \div (1{,}055.4 - 43.2) = 29{,}666.5 \times 100 \div 1{,}012.2 = 2{,}931$ mm: between the two ends, as it must be.

The seat-row figures also show the arms hidden in the table: $4{,}400 \times 100 \div 160 = 2{,}750$ mm for row 1 and $2{,}700 \times 100 \div 75 = 3{,}600$ mm for row 2. The row 2 passenger is 850 mm further aft than the front seats, which is why a heavy back-seat passenger moves the CG aft far more than the same person in front.

Now try the workbook's own CHARLIE example before opening the solution. It gives a ZFW of 1,014 kg with 30,060.1 IU, and a take-off weight of 1,114.8 kg with 33,033.7 IU after loading 140 L of fuel. Find both CGs, the fuel's index units, and comment on the take-off weight.

??? solution "Show the solution"
    CG at ZFW $= 30{,}060.1 \times 100 \div 1{,}014 = $ **2,964.5 mm**. CG at take-off $= 33{,}033.7 \times 100 \div 1{,}114.8 = $ **2,963.2 mm**. Both are within the envelope in the workbook. The fuel accounts for $33{,}033.7 - 30{,}060.1 = 2{,}973.6$ IU and $1{,}114.8 - 1{,}014 = 100.8$ kg, which is $140 \times 0.72$, at $2{,}973.6 \times 100 \div 100.8 = 2{,}950$ mm, the fuel arm. The take-off weight is **0.2 kg under** the 1,115 kg MTOW: legal, with no margin at all, and illegal if you had rounded up during the sum.

### BRAVO: pounds, inches and moment per thousand

BRAVO is a four-seater from the American tradition: weights in **pounds**, arms in **inches**, and moments divided by 1,000 (inch-pounds per thousand) to keep them small. The instructions and the worked example are on page 11, the loading graph and the CG envelope on page 12. Read each item's moment/1,000 from the loading graph, add weights and moments, and plot total weight against total moment at both ZFW and take-off weight.

Its limits: MTOW **1,000 kg (2,200 lb)** normal category, **841 kg (1,850 lb)** utility; cargo compartment **154 kg (339 lb)**; baggage **54 kg (120 lb)**; standard tanks **37 US gallons at 6 lb per gallon**. Expect to convert. Kilograms to pounds is multiply by 2.205. Litres to pounds is two steps in one, litres to kilograms (× 0.72) then kilograms to pounds (× 2.205), so multiply by **1.587**.

!!! example "Worked example: BRAVO's own figures"
    Given, from the workbook's page 11 example: empty weight 1,260 lb at 80 in; ZFW 1,950 lb with moment/1,000 of 174.88; fuel 140 L, entered as 221 lb at 91 in; take-off weight 2,171 lb, moment/1,000 194.99.

    1. Empty moment: $1{,}260 \times 80 = 100{,}800$ in lb, so moment/1,000 $= \mathbf{100.80}$.
    2. Fuel weight: $140 \times 1.587 = 222$ lb by the conversion above (the workbook's example rounds to **221 lb**). Moment: $221 \times 91 = 20{,}111$, so moment/1,000 $= \mathbf{20.11}$.
    3. Take-off totals: $1{,}950 + 221 = \mathbf{2{,}171\ lb}$; $174.88 + 20.11 = \mathbf{194.99}$.
    4. CG at ZFW, if you want it in inches: $174{,}880 \div 1{,}950 = \mathbf{89.7\ in}$. At take-off: $194{,}990 \div 2{,}171 = \mathbf{89.8\ in}$.
    5. Weight check: 2,171 lb is under the 2,200 lb normal category MTOW but over the 1,850 lb utility limit, so this load is **normal category only**.

    BRAVO's envelope is weight against moment, so you plot (1,950 lb, 174.88) and (2,171 lb, 194.99). The fuel at 91 in is just aft of the CG, so burning it barely moves the point.

### ALPHA: the graphical system

ALPHA is a six or seven-seat aeroplane with a single chart (page 10) that does the moment arithmetic by geometry. The instructions on page 9 set the order, and following it exactly is the whole skill.

The chart has a horizontal **index unit scale** across the top, then one horizontal scale per load station underneath it: row 1, row 2 (forward or aft facing), row 3, nose baggage, rear baggage and fuel. Each station's scale is marked in **kilograms per division** and carries an **arrow** showing which way that load moves the index. At the bottom is the **CG envelope**, with gross weight up the side and the 1,633 kg MTOW marked on it.

1. Mark the **basic empty weight index units** on the top scale, and enter the basic empty weight at the top of the weight column.
2. Enter each load item's weight in the column and **total them**: first to **ZFW**, then add the fuel for **take-off weight**.
3. On the envelope, draw **horizontal lines** at the ZFW and at the take-off weight.
4. From the empty index point, **drop vertically** to the first load scale. Move **left or right in the direction of the arrow** by the number of divisions for that item's weight: 154 kg on a scale of 77 kg per division is **2 divisions**. Mark the point.
5. Drop vertically from that mark to the next scale and repeat, down through every station to **rear baggage**.
6. From the rear baggage point, **drop a line straight down** through the envelope. Where it crosses the ZFW line is the zero fuel point; where it crosses the take-off line is the take-off point. Both must be inside the envelope, and the take-off weight must not exceed 1,633 kg. If either is outside, reorganise the load and start again.

Why does the fuel come last, and why is its scale not used for the vertical line? Because ALPHA's fuel scale sits just about at the CG, the take-off point lies on the same vertical as the zero fuel point; only the weight line differs.

The common errors: moving **the wrong way** along a scale (follow the arrow), **miscounting divisions** (77 kg per division is not 50), using the forward-facing scale for an aft-facing seat, and reading against the **wrong weight line**. A ruler and a sharp pencil prevent most of them.

??? check "Check yourself: in ALPHA, why are the ZFW and take-off points read on the same vertical line?"
    Because the vertical line carries the index after all the non-fuel loads, and the fuel effectively changes the weight without moving that line. The two points differ only in which horizontal weight line you read them against: ZFW before the fuel, take-off weight after.

### ECHO: the CPL system you may still meet

ECHO, a six-seater used mainly in the CPL exam, is in the same workbook. Instructions are on pages 16 and 17, the moment index chart is Figure 10 (page 18) and the CG chart Figure 11 (page 19). For each item, find its weight on Figure 10's vertical scale, go across to that station's line and drop down to read its **moment index**. Sum weights and indexes, then plot gross weight against total moment index on Figure 11: inside the shaded area, **at every stage of flight**, is correctly loaded.

Its limits are the fullest set in the workbook: MTOW **2,950 kg**, MLW **2,725 kg**, MZFW **2,630 kg**; compartments **55 kg** forward and in each wing locker, **155 kg** rear; floor loading **450 kg/m²**; fuel in the main tanks (arm 1,780 mm) and auxiliary tanks (2,800 mm). Its seats weigh **5 kg each** and may be removed, and a removed seat's weight and moment must be taken off the empty figures.

## Bringing it together

You are planning Jandakot to Rottnest and on to Busselton in a CHARLIE-type four-seater, with empty weight and index units from its own weight and balance schedule. You write the table in the system's order: empty, oil, row 1, row 2, baggage. The baggage is a heavy esky with a small base; weight divided by footprint says it needs a board, and the board goes on the sheet. You total to ZFW and find the CG with $\text{IU} \times 100 \div$ weight. Then fuel: litres times 0.72, its index units at the 2,950 mm arm, and the take-off totals, a few kilograms under the 1,115 kg MTOW. Both points go on the envelope; with the fuel aft of the CG the point moves forward as it burns, so the landing CG lies between the two you checked. A quarter of an hour on paper beats discovering the aft limit in the flare at Busselton.

## Key points

- **2.1.1 (a)** Datum, arm, station and moment as in BAKC 6.4. An **index unit** is a scaled moment: in CHARLIE 1 IU = 100 kg mm. Use each system's own definition, and multiply back to get the CG.
- **2.1.1 (b)** CG = total moment ÷ total weight (CHARLIE: IU × 100 ÷ weight). The limits form an **envelope**, with the forward limit moving aft as weight rises; check the category (normal or utility).
- **2.1.1 (c)** **Empty weight** includes unusable fuel and oil as the system states. **ZFW** is everything but usable fuel. **Ramp weight** is take-off weight plus the taxi fuel. Check the CG at ZFW and at take-off weight: fuel burn slides the point between them.
- **2.1.1 (d)** **MTOW** and **MLW** are structural limits (CHARLIE 1,115 kg normal; BRAVO 1,000 kg; ALPHA 1,633 kg; ECHO 2,950 kg and MLW 2,725 kg, MZFW 2,630 kg).
- **2.1.1 (e)** **Floor loading** = weight ÷ contact area, against a limit in kg/m² (450 kg/m² in ECHO). Spread dense items on a board and count the board.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    CASA lists "correct use of the various loading systems (ALPHA, BRAVO and CHARLIE)" among the most common PPLA errors. Read the system's instructions page every time, use its SG (0.72), its arms and its index unit definition, check the CG at ZFW and at take-off weight, and do not round until the end. In ALPHA follow each scale's arrow and count divisions carefully; in BRAVO convert litres to pounds with × 1.587; in CHARLIE remember the × 100.
