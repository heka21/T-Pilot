## What the exam expects (RFRC 2.4)

- **2.4.1** Find in the legislation the restrictions on carrying passengers on particular kinds of flight.
- **2.4.2** Apply the pilot in command's duties before flight (fuel and oil, refuelling, starting and running engines, briefing passengers) and in flight (operating the aircraft safely, the pilot in command's authority, and dropping things from the aircraft).
- **2.4.3** Recall the rules for each phase: before flight (locking devices, doors and hatches, tank caps, control checks, frost and ice, instruments, securing the empty control seat's harness before solo, the fuel drain check, passengers in control seats, infants and children) and in flight (who sits where, seat belts, seat adjustment, and who may handle the controls).

## Why this matters

The moment you sign for an aeroplane as pilot in command, everything about the flight becomes your legal responsibility: the fuel in the tanks, the gust lock you might have missed, the child in the back seat, the friend in the right seat who wants a go at the controls. Most of the rules in this subtopic were written after a specific accident: an aeroplane that took off with water in the fuel, a door that opened after lift-off, a seat that slid back on the take-off roll and pulled the pilot's hands off the controls. The exam asks them as recall ("seat belts must be worn when?") and as small scenarios ("may you carry a passenger on this flight?"). This lesson walks through a flight in order, from the passenger list to shutdown, and explains the reason behind each rule so that you can rebuild it under exam pressure.

## Who may be carried, and on which flights

Start with the question you face when a friend asks to come along: is this a flight on which I may carry anyone at all? A **passenger** is anyone on board who is not a crew member doing a job on the flight. The restrictions on carrying them come from two places, and knowing which is which is the skill the syllabus asks for: restrictions about **you** (your licence, your recency, your medical) are in **CASR Part 61** and **Part 67**; restrictions about **the flight and the aircraft** are in **CASR Part 91 and its MOS**.

The logic behind all of them is the same. A passenger has not chosen to accept the risks a pilot accepts, cannot judge them, and often cannot even see them. So passengers are kept off flights where the risk is unknown or higher than normal, and off flights flown by pilots who have not shown they can manage the normal risk.

- A **student pilot** flying solo must **not carry a passenger**. A student has not yet been assessed as competent to take responsibility for anyone else.
- A pilot must have made **3 take-offs and 3 landings in the previous 90 days** before carrying a passenger (CASR 61.395; see [RFRC 2.2](/notes/RFRC/2.2)).
- Passengers must not be carried on a **test flight** after maintenance, on a **ferry flight under a special flight permit** unless the permit allows it, or on any flight where the aircraft's certificate or the operation prohibits them. A test flight exists because nobody yet knows whether the aircraft is right; a special flight permit exists because the aircraft does not fully meet its normal standard.
- **Aerobatic flights** may carry passengers only where the aircraft, the pilot's qualifications and the briefing requirements for aerobatic passenger carriage are met.
- Under a **RAMPC** no more than **1 passenger**; under a **Basic Class 2** no more than **5 passengers**.
- A **flight review or flight test** is not a passenger flight: the person in the other seat is an instructor or examiner doing a job.

!!! example "Worked example: where would you look, and what does it say?"
    Three situations, each answered by asking whether the restriction is about the pilot or about the flight.

    1. *The club's aeroplane has just had its engine replaced and the engineer asks you to fly the check flight. Your partner would love to come.* This is about the flight: a **test flight after maintenance**. Part 91 says no passengers. Your partner waits on the ground.
    2. *You have not flown for four months and want to take your brother to Rottnest.* This is about you: **recent experience**, CASR Part 61 (61.395). You need 3 take-offs and 3 landings in the previous 90 days first.
    3. *An aeroplane is being flown to Kalgoorlie for repairs under a special flight permit. The owner's son wants a lift.* This is about the aircraft and the flight: a **ferry flight under a special flight permit**. Passengers only if the permit itself allows it, so you read the permit's conditions.

    The pattern: name what the restriction is about, then go to the Part that governs that thing.

??? check "Check yourself: during your flight review, is the instructor in the right seat a passenger?"
    No. A flight review or flight test is not a passenger flight; the instructor is there doing a job, so the passenger restrictions such as recent experience do not apply to their presence.

## Before flight: fuel, oil and refuelling

### Fuel and oil

Fuel exhaustion and fuel starvation remain among the most common causes of engine failure in light aeroplanes, and almost every one of them was preventable on the ground. So the rule puts the responsibility squarely on you: the pilot in command must ensure the aircraft carries **enough fuel and oil for the planned flight plus the required reserves**, of the **correct grade**, and that the **quantity has been checked**, not just read from the gauges.

Why not trust the gauges? Light aeroplane fuel gauges are notoriously inaccurate, especially near empty and on the ground with the wings not level. The physical check (a dipstick, a calibrated sight gauge, or a known quantity from full tanks) tells you what is really there; the gauges in flight then tell you whether it is going down as expected.

For a **VFR day flight in a piston aeroplane** the **fixed fuel reserve is 30 minutes** at normal cruise consumption (Part 91 MOS chapter 19; verify current text). The fixed reserve is fuel you plan never to use: it is there for the go-around, the diversion around a shower, the headwind that was stronger than forecast. A **fuel check** must be made **before departure and at intervals in flight**, comparing the fuel remaining with what the plan says should remain.

Correct grade matters as much as quantity. The wrong fuel can destroy a piston engine within minutes (see [BAKC 3.2](/notes/BAKC/3.2) on fuels and their colours).

### Refuelling

Refuelling puts a large quantity of flammable vapour next to a metal aeroplane that may be carrying a static charge. Each rule removes one way of igniting it:

- **No smoking or naked flames** within the refuelling zone: no ignition source.
- **Engines stopped**: no hot exhaust or ignition spark.
- **Aircraft and bowser bonded (earthed)** against static: the flowing fuel builds up a static charge, and bonding lets it equalise through a wire instead of a spark at the nozzle.
- **A fire extinguisher at hand**.
- **Passengers off the aircraft**, or supervised under the conditions the rules allow.
- **No radar or HF transmissions**: these can induce currents strong enough to spark.

![What must be true before fuel flows?](diagram:refuelling-safety-zone "What to notice: each rule removes one ignition source; the bonding lead lets static equalise through a wire instead of sparking at the nozzle.")

After refuelling, the **caps are secured** and a **fuel drain check** is made for **water and the correct fuel**.

![What should a fuel sample look like?](diagram:fuel-sample-colours "AVGAS 100LL is blue; water sits as clear globules at the bottom; a clear sample from an AVGAS aeroplane means water or jet fuel, so do not fly.")

!!! warning "Common trap"
    "The gauges show full" is not a fuel check. The rule asks you to **verify the quantity** physically before flight, then use the gauges in flight to monitor it.

## Before flight: starting and the passenger briefing

### Starting and ground operation of engines

A propeller turning at idle is almost invisible and will kill anyone who walks into it. The starting rules all aim at keeping people away from the propeller and the aeroplane under control:

- **Nobody near the propeller**, and the area **behind** the aeroplane clear of people and loose objects that the slipstream could blow about.
- **Brakes set or chocks in**, so the aeroplane does not move when the engine fires.
- Call **"clear prop"** loudly, look, and wait for any response before starting.
- **Do not run the engine inside a hangar**.
- **A pilot must be at the controls whenever the engine is running**. An engine started by hand-swinging, or running for a check, is never left unattended.

### The passenger briefing

Your passenger has never seen the inside of this aeroplane before. If something goes wrong, the thirty seconds you spend before start-up are all they will have to go on. So the rule is that before take-off the pilot in command must **brief passengers** on:

- the **use of seat belts and harnesses**, and when they must be worn;
- the **location and operation of the exits**;
- the **smoking rules**;
- the **location of emergency equipment** (fire extinguisher, life jackets if carried);
- the **brace position**;
- that they **must follow the pilot's instructions**.

A **verbal briefing** is standard in a light aeroplane. A good one is practical: show them how the door latch works, have them fasten and unfasten the harness themselves, point to the extinguisher.

![What must the passenger briefing cover?](diagram:passenger-briefing "Six items: belts, exits, smoking, emergency equipment, the brace position, and obeying the pilot.")

??? check "Check yourself: why are aircraft and bowser bonded before refuelling begins?"
    Fuel flowing through the hose builds up a static charge. Bonding connects the aircraft and the bowser so the charge equalises through the wire instead of jumping as a spark at the filler, in a cloud of fuel vapour.

## The pre-flight checks the law requires

The walk-around in your POH is a checklist. Behind several of its items is a legal requirement, each one there because pilots have died without it. Taking them in the order you meet them at the aeroplane:

- **Locking devices removed**: control locks, gust locks, pitot covers, engine blanks. A control lock left in means the controls do not move; a pitot cover left on means no airspeed indication. Both have caused accidents within seconds of lift-off.
- **Doors and hatches secured**. A door opening after take-off is a known cause of accidents, not because the door itself is dangerous (most light aeroplanes fly acceptably with a door ajar) but because the noise and distraction make pilots stop flying the aeroplane.
- **Tank caps** fitted and secure after refuelling. An unsecured cap lets the low pressure over the wing siphon fuel out in flight.
- **Flight controls tested** for **full, free and correct movement** before take-off. *Correct* is the word that catches people: after maintenance, a control can be rigged to move freely in the wrong direction.
- **Frost, ice and snow removed** from the wings, tail and control surfaces. Even a thin layer of frost roughens the wing enough to cut lift and raise the stall speed, so the aeroplane must be **clean** before flight. A winter morning at Narrogin or York can leave frost on wings that looked dry the night before.
- **Instruments checked** for serviceability: altimeter set and reading correctly, gyros erect and set, ASI at zero.
- **Unoccupied control seat harness secured** before a **solo flight in a dual-control aircraft**, so it cannot foul the controls. A loose harness buckle can jam behind a control column or drop into the rudder pedals.
- **Fuel system inspection**: a **drain check for water and contamination at each drain point** before the **first flight of the day** and **after each refuelling**, **allowing the fuel to settle first**. Water is heavier than fuel and sinks to the lowest point, the drain; if you sample straight after refuelling, the water is still mixed through the tank and the sample looks clean.

![Which walk-around items does the law require?](diagram:preflight-legal-checks "What to notice: locks off, doors and caps secure, frost gone, controls full, free and correct, instruments checked, the empty seat's harness secured for solo, and every drain point sampled once the fuel has settled.")

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Draining straight after refuelling is the classic mistake. Water needs time to settle to the drain points; sample too early and you get clean fuel while the water is still in suspension, waiting to reach the engine.

??? check "Check yourself: why does the control check say full, free and correct movement, not just full and free?"
    Because a control can move fully and freely in the wrong direction if it has been misrigged, for example after maintenance. Checking that each surface moves the right way for each input catches that before take-off.

## Who sits where: control seats, infants and children

### Passengers in a control seat

In a training aeroplane the right seat has a full set of controls. A passenger sitting there can, by accident or curiosity, push the column or a rudder pedal at the worst moment. So **control seats** may be occupied only by a **pilot** or by a person the **pilot in command permits**, who **must not interfere with the controls**. A passenger in a control seat must be **briefed to keep clear of the controls**: feet flat on the floor away from the pedals, hands in the lap, nothing stowed where it can jam the column.

### Infants and children

The default is simple: **every occupant must have a seat and a seat belt**. The one exception is an **infant under a prescribed age**, who may be carried on an adult's lap restrained by an **infant seat belt attached to the adult's belt**. **Children** must be properly restrained and **seated where they cannot reach the controls**. A curious six-year-old in the right seat can reach the mixture knob faster than you can stop them.

??? check "Check yourself: a family friend wants to sit in the right seat of your two-seater and 'just watch'. What must happen before start-up?"
    The pilot in command must permit it, and brief the friend to keep hands and feet clear of the controls and not to touch anything unless asked. A person in a control seat must not interfere with the controls.

## During flight: seats, belts and the controls

### Occupation of seats and seat belts

Once airborne the rules keep everyone where they can be protected. **Each person must occupy a seat with a seat belt or harness**, and the **pilot in command must occupy a control seat with the harness fastened at all times**.

**Seat belts must be worn by everyone**:

- during **take-off and landing**;
- during **flight below 1,000 ft above ground**;
- in **turbulence**;
- **whenever the pilot in command directs**.

The pattern is the phases where something sudden is most likely and where there is least time to react: close to the ground, and in rough air. Near the ground there is no time to brief, and in turbulence an unrestrained head hits the cabin roof.

![When must seat belts be worn?](diagram:seat-belts-in-flight "Take-off, landing, any time below 1,000 ft above the ground, in turbulence, and whenever the pilot in command says so.")

### Seat adjustment

**Seats must be adjusted and locked before take-off and landing**, so a seat cannot slide back on the take-off run. When a seat slides back under acceleration, the pilot's hands pull back on the control column as they go, raising the nose at low speed: the recipe for a stall just after lift-off. Check the seat lock pin is engaged by trying to move the seat after you adjust it.

### Who may handle the controls

**Only a pilot qualified on the type, or a student under instruction, may handle the controls**, and the pilot in command **must not permit an unauthorised person to operate the controls**. Letting a friend "have a go" in cruise is the scenario the exam has in mind: the friend is not a pilot and not a student under instruction, so the answer is no, however benign the moment looks.

## The pilot in command: authority and safety

All of the rules above put duties on the pilot in command. The other half of the bargain is **authority**. The **pilot in command** has **final authority** over the aircraft and everyone in it, and is responsible for the **safety of the aircraft, passengers and cargo** and for **compliance with the rules**. Passengers and crew must obey the pilot in command's **lawful instructions** (CASR Part 91 Subpart 91.D; verify current regulation numbers).

Authority and responsibility come together because one cannot work without the other. You cannot be responsible for the safety of a flight if a passenger can overrule you, and you should not have authority over people's lives without being answerable for how you use it.

In an **emergency** the pilot in command may **deviate from a rule to the extent necessary for safety**, and must **report the deviation**. If an engine runs rough at 800 ft over a town, you may descend below the populous-area minimum height to make a field; afterwards you report what you did and why.

### Dropping articles

**Nothing may be dropped or thrown from an aircraft in flight that could endanger people or property**, unless done in accordance with a **CASA approval** or the specific rules for the activity. A bottle thrown from 1,500 ft arrives at the ground at lethal speed; even paper can foul another aircraft's propeller. Approved activities such as supply dropping, firebombing and parachuting have their own rules.

??? check "Check yourself: a passenger refuses to fasten their seat belt for landing. What gives you the right to insist?"
    The pilot in command's authority: passengers must obey the pilot's lawful instructions, and seat belts must be worn for landing. As pilot in command you are responsible for their safety and for compliance, and the law gives you the authority to match.

## Bringing it together

A winter Saturday at Murrayfield. Your passenger is a colleague and her four-year-old daughter. Before the flight you confirm your own currency (Part 61): five landings last month, so passengers are allowed. The aeroplane was refuelled last night, so this morning, with the fuel settled, you drain each point, find clean blue fuel, and dip the tanks to confirm the quantity covers the flight plus the 30-minute reserve. There is frost on the tailplane, so you wait for the sun to clear it. Walk-around: gust lock and pitot cover off, caps secure, doors latched. The daughter sits in the back in her own seat with her own belt, where she cannot reach the controls; her mother sits in front, briefed to keep clear of the controls, and you brief both on belts, the door, no smoking, the extinguisher, the brace position and doing what you ask. You call "clear prop", check behind, start with the brakes on, and test the controls full, free and correct. Seats locked, belts on for take-off, and on in the climb until well above 1,000 ft. Halfway round, the mother asks if she can steer for a minute. She is not a pilot or a student under instruction, so you explain, kindly, that the law says no.

??? check "Check yourself: name two rules from this lesson that exist because of what happens at the moment of acceleration on the take-off roll."
    Seats must be adjusted and locked, because a seat sliding back pulls the pilot's hands back on the column; and seat belts must be worn for take-off. Doors secured is a third: a door opening at lift-off distracts the pilot at the worst moment.

## Key points

- **2.4.1** No passengers for a student pilot solo, without 3 take-offs and landings in 90 days, on a test flight after maintenance, or on a special-permit ferry flight unless the permit allows; aerobatic passengers only when all the conditions are met; 1 passenger on a RAMPC, 5 on a Basic Class 2. Pilot restrictions are in Part 61 and 67, flight restrictions in Part 91 and its MOS.
- **2.4.2** Before flight: enough fuel and oil of the right grade, physically checked, with a 30-minute fixed reserve for day VFR in a piston aeroplane; refuel with no ignition source, engines stopped, bonded, extinguisher ready; start with nobody near the propeller and a pilot at the controls; brief passengers on belts, exits, smoking, emergency equipment, brace and obeying the pilot. In flight: the pilot in command has final authority and responsibility, may deviate in an emergency and reports it; nothing dropped that could endanger anyone without approval.
- **2.4.3** Before flight: locks off, doors and caps secure, controls full, free and correct, frost removed, instruments checked, the empty seat's harness secured for solo, fuel drained after settling at the first flight of the day and after refuelling, passengers in control seats briefed, a seat and belt for everyone except a lap-held infant. In flight: everyone seated and belted for take-off, landing, below 1,000 ft, in turbulence and when directed; seats locked; only qualified pilots or students under instruction on the controls.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    The passenger briefing list (belts, exits, smoking, emergency equipment, brace, obey the pilot) and the seat belt rule (take-off, landing, below 1,000 ft, turbulence, when directed) are easy marks. Fuel: 30 minutes fixed reserve for a day VFR piston aeroplane, and a physical fuel quantity check before flight, not just the gauges. For "may you carry a passenger?" scenarios, ask what the restriction is about (the pilot, the aircraft or the flight) and the answer follows. Verify the current Part 91 regulation numbers, because the exam may cite them.
