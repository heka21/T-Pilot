## What the exam expects (RFRC 2.3)

- **2.3.1** Recall and apply the rules of the air; the rules on and near an aerodrome including turns after take-off; take-off and landing separation at a non-controlled aerodrome; smoking restrictions; VFR and VMC for aeroplanes below 10,000 ft; altimetry below 10,000 ft.
- **2.3.2** State the rules on drugs and alcohol, including the minimum time between drinking and departure, and on temporary medical unfitness.
- **2.3.3** Recall what steady green and red lights, and green, red and white flashes, mean when directed at an aircraft.
- **2.3.4** Recall the minimum heights over populous areas and elsewhere.
- **2.3.5** State the limits on acrobatic flight and on flight over public gatherings.
- **2.3.6** Recall the requirement to land before the end of daylight.

## Why this matters

These are the rules you fly by on every flight, from the moment you decide whether last night's drinks allow you to fly at all, to the height you cross the Darling Scarp, to who gives way when you and another aeroplane arrive at Serpentine's circuit together. Most of them exist because someone died without them, and most of them have a logic you can reconstruct once you see it: every avoiding turn goes the same way, every minimum height is a glide-clear or see-and-avoid margin, every light signal pairs a colour with a meaning you can guess. The exam tests this subtopic almost entirely by recall of specific figures, so by the end you need a short list by heart: 8 hours, 500 ft, 1,000 ft within 600 m, 3,000 ft, 600 m and 1,800 m, 5,000 m and 1,500 m and 1,000 ft, 10 minutes. This lesson gives each one its reason so that it sticks. Every figure here is from the cited source as it stood when this lesson was written; confirm each against the current VFRG, because these are the ones CASA adjusts.

## Rules of the air: who gives way

Two aeroplanes in the same bit of sky need a rule both pilots know without talking. The rules of the air are that rule, and they have one organising idea: **when in doubt, turn right**, and the less manoeuvrable aircraft has priority.

The pilot in command is always responsible for avoiding a collision, whatever clearance has been given and whatever the other aircraft ought to be doing. Right of way does not excuse you from looking out and giving way if the other pilot has not seen you.

- **Converging** at about the same level: the aircraft that has the other on its **right** gives way. Think of a roundabout: traffic from your right has priority. Among different types, power-driven heavier-than-air aircraft give way to airships, gliders and balloons, and to any aircraft towing something or carrying a sling load, because those can manoeuvre least.
- **Head-on**, or nearly so: **both** alter heading to the **right**. If both turn the same way they pass clear; the rule only works because everyone turns right.
- **Overtaking:** the aircraft being overtaken has right of way. The overtaking aircraft alters heading to the **right** and stays clear until well past, whether it is climbing, descending or level.
- **Landing:** an aircraft landing or on final approach has right of way over aircraft in flight and on the ground. When two aircraft are both approaching to land, the **lower** one has right of way, but it may not use that to cut in front of or overtake an aircraft on final.
- **Emergency:** an aircraft in distress has right of way over everything.
- **On the ground:** aircraft on the manoeuvring area give way to aircraft taking off or landing. Converging, give way to the one on your right; head-on, both turn right. Do not fly or taxi so close to another aircraft as to create a collision hazard, and no formation flight without prior arrangement between the pilots.

![Who gives way to whom?](diagram:right-of-way-rules "Every avoiding turn is to the right; the aircraft with the other on its right, the overtaker and the higher one on approach give way.")

??? check "Check yourself: two aeroplanes are converging at the same level. One sees the other at its two o'clock. Which gives way, and which way does it turn?"
    The one that sees the other on its right gives way, and it turns right to pass behind. The one with the other on its left holds its heading and speed, while still watching in case the other pilot has not seen it.

## On and around the aerodrome

An aerodrome without a tower works because everyone flies the same pattern in the same direction at the same height. The rules make that pattern law.

Join and fly the circuit in the **published direction**: **left-hand** unless the ERSA entry or the ground signals show right-hand circuits for that runway. All turns in the circuit are in the circuit direction. Standard circuit height is **1,000 ft above aerodrome level** for most light aeroplanes, with 500 ft for very low performance types and 1,500 ft for high performance aircraft, as the Part 91 MOS table sets out. Take off and land into wind on the runway in use, conform to the pattern of the other traffic, keep a lookout, and vacate the runway promptly after landing.

![What does the standard circuit look like, and when may you turn?](diagram:circuit-pattern "Upwind straight ahead, the first turn at or above 500 ft, downwind at 1,000 ft above the aerodrome; all turns left unless published otherwise.")

**Turns after take-off** have their own rule, because the aeroplane is low, slow and heavy and the circuit below is full of traffic: climb straight ahead to at least **500 ft above aerodrome level** before turning, unless a turn is needed to avoid terrain or to follow a published procedure, and then turn in the circuit direction. Five hundred feet is the height from which a recovery from a mishandled turn still has a chance.

At a **non-controlled aerodrome** you make the standard calls on the CTAF: taxiing, entering the runway, inbound, joining, turning base, and anything else safety needs. The calls are how other pilots build a picture of where you are.

![How do you join the circuit from overhead?](diagram:overhead-join "Cross overhead above circuit height, descend on the dead side, join crosswind at circuit height.")

??? check "Check yourself: how high must you be before the first turn after take-off, and what are the exceptions?"
    At least 500 ft above the aerodrome. The exceptions are a turn needed to avoid terrain or to follow a published departure procedure; the turn is then still made in the circuit direction.

## Separation for take-off and landing at a non-controlled aerodrome

Without a tower you are your own controller for runway spacing, and the rules set the minimum. The logic is runway occupancy and wake: the aircraft ahead must be clear of the runway, or clear of your path, before you commit.

You must not **take off** until a preceding aircraft using the same runway:

- has **landed and vacated** the runway, or
- has taken off and **crossed the upwind end** of the runway or **commenced a turn**, or
- if the runway is **longer than 1,800 m**, is airborne and at least **1,800 m** ahead of your intended lift-off point, or
- if **both** aircraft have a maximum take-off weight **under 2,000 kg**, is airborne and at least **600 m** ahead of your intended lift-off point.

You must not **land** until a preceding aircraft using the same runway has **vacated** it and is taxiing clear, or has taken off and crossed the upwind end or commenced a turn, or is airborne the same **1,800 m** or **600 m** ahead of your intended touchdown point under the same conditions.

![When may you take off behind another aircraft at a non-controlled aerodrome?](diagram:takeoff-separation "Vacated, past the upwind end or turning, or airborne far enough ahead: 600 m if both are under 2,000 kg, 1,800 m on a runway longer than 1,800 m.")

The 600 m figure is the one you use most. Two training aeroplanes at Murrayfield are both under 2,000 kg, so once the one ahead is airborne and 600 m down the runway you may roll. If the one ahead is a 2,500 kg twin, the 600 m rule is off and you wait for it to cross the upwind end or start its turn.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The 600 m rule needs **both** aircraft under 2,000 kg, not just yours. And the 1,800 m rule depends on the runway being longer than 1,800 m, not on the distance alone.

??? check "Check yourself: you are number two for take-off behind a 1,100 kg aeroplane that has just lifted off. When may you start your take-off roll?"
    Once it is airborne and at least 600 m ahead of your intended lift-off point, because both aircraft are under 2,000 kg; or earlier if it has crossed the upwind end or started a turn. If your own aeroplane were over 2,000 kg the 600 m option would not apply.

## Smoking

Short and absolute: smoking in an aircraft is prohibited during **take-off, landing and refuelling**, whenever the no-smoking signs are on, and whenever the pilot in command prohibits it. Nobody smokes anywhere near an aircraft being refuelled. Fuel vapour, oxygen and an open flame are the reasons.

## VFR and visual meteorological conditions

The **visual flight rules** rest on a bargain: you may fly without an air traffic control separation service because you can **see and avoid** other aircraft and terrain. The **visual meteorological conditions** (VMC) are the minimum weather in which that bargain holds. You need enough **flight visibility** to see another aircraft in time to turn away, and enough **distance from cloud** that an aircraft popping out of a cloud (an IFR aircraft, which is allowed to be in there) does not appear in front of you with no time to react.

To fly VFR below 10,000 ft you must stay in VMC, navigate by visual reference to the ground or water (below 2,000 ft above the ground or water; above that, by other approved means), and meet these minima:

| Airspace below 10,000 ft | Flight visibility | Distance from cloud |
|---|---|---|
| Class C and E | 5,000 m | 1,500 m horizontally, 1,000 ft vertically |
| Class D | 5,000 m | 600 m horizontally, 1,000 ft above and 500 ft below cloud |
| Class G, above 3,000 ft AMSL or 1,000 ft AGL (whichever is higher) | 5,000 m | 1,500 m horizontally, 1,000 ft vertically |
| Class G, at or below 3,000 ft AMSL or 1,000 ft AGL (whichever is higher) | 5,000 m | Clear of cloud and in sight of ground or water |

![What are the VMC minima below 10,000 ft?](diagram:vmc-minima-by-airspace "5,000 m visibility everywhere; 1,500 m and 1,000 ft from cloud, except low in Class G, where clear of cloud and in sight of the ground is enough.")

Why the low Class G row is relaxed: close to the ground, in uncontrolled airspace, there is no IFR traffic descending out of cloud on an instrument approach into your path, and the ground itself is your reference. Why the controlled-airspace rows are not: in Class C, D and E there are IFR aircraft in cloud, and the margins give you time to avoid one that emerges. Class D gets smaller margins because the tower knows where the IFR traffic is and keeps it apart from the VFR circuit by procedure. Above 10,000 ft the visibility requirement rises to 8 km, because closing speeds are higher. In a control zone you also need a clearance from the tower, and the **special VFR** provisions allow lower visibility only with a clearance.

![Where does each class of airspace sit?](diagram:airspace-cross-section "Class C and D control zones from the surface, Class C and E steps above, Class G underneath; the VMC row you need depends on which one you are in.")

??? check "Check yourself: you are at 2,500 ft in Class G over flat ground near Pinjarra, 1,000 m horizontally from a cloud. Are you in VMC?"
    Yes, provided the flight visibility is 5,000 m or more and you are clear of cloud and in sight of the ground. At or below 3,000 ft AMSL (or 1,000 ft AGL, whichever is higher) in Class G the cloud requirement is clear of cloud, not 1,500 m. Climb into Class C or E and 1,000 m from cloud would not be enough.

## Altimetry below 10,000 ft

Every altimeter in the area must agree, or vertical separation means nothing. The altimetry rules make them agree by giving everyone the same datum.

- **Before take-off** set the **aerodrome QNH** from the tower, ATIS or AWIS. If none is available, set the altimeter to the aerodrome elevation and the subscale shows the local QNH (see [BAKC 6.2](/notes/BAKC/6.2)).
- **In flight below the transition altitude of 10,000 ft** fly on **QNH**: either the local QNH of a station within **100 nm**, or the **area QNH** from the area forecast, which is valid for the period of the forecast (area QNH is issued for **3-hour** periods).
- **At or above the transition layer**, from 10,000 ft up to **FL110**, set **1013 hPa** and fly flight levels; descending, set QNH when leaving FL110. The RPL does not take you there, but the exam may ask.
- **Serviceability:** with the correct QNH set the altimeter must read the aerodrome elevation within **100 ft** for VFR, or it is unserviceable.

**Cruising levels.** Below 10,000 ft the **hemispherical rule** keeps opposite-direction traffic 1,000 ft apart. A VFR flight above 5,000 ft on a **magnetic track of 000 to 179 degrees** flies **odd thousands plus 500 ft** (5,500, 7,500, 9,500); on **180 to 359 degrees**, **even thousands plus 500 ft** (6,500, 8,500). Below 5,000 ft any altitude may be flown, subject to minimum heights and good practice. "Odd" and "east" go together: east is the first half of the compass.

![Which VFR cruising levels go with which magnetic track?](diagram:vfr-cruising-levels "Eastbound half (000 to 179): odd thousands plus 500. Westbound half (180 to 359): even thousands plus 500.")

??? check "Check yourself: you are cruising VFR at 7,500 ft from Perth towards Kalgoorlie on a magnetic track of 095 degrees. Which QNH do you fly on, and is 7,500 ft a correct level?"
    Below 10,000 ft you fly on QNH: the local QNH of a station within 100 nm, or the area QNH from the forecast for the current 3-hour period. Track 095 is in the 000 to 179 half, so odd thousands plus 500: 7,500 ft is correct.

## Alcohol, drugs and medical fitness

The alcohol rules are two: a **time** and a **level**. You must not act as a crew member within **8 hours** of consuming alcohol, nor while under its influence; and for aviation safety-sensitive activities the blood alcohol limit is **0.02 grams per 100 mL**, which in practice means none. CASA and the police can test. Note that 8 hours is a minimum, not a guarantee: the liver removes roughly one standard drink an hour, so a heavy night can leave you over 0.02 well after 8 hours.

![Does 8 hours guarantee you are clear?](diagram:alcohol-and-blood-levels-timeline "About one standard drink leaves per hour and nothing speeds it up; after a heavy night 8 hours is not enough.")

Drugs: you must not fly under the influence of any drug that impairs your ability, and that includes prescription and over-the-counter medicines. A decongestant that makes you drowsy counts. Check any medication with a DAME (designated aviation medical examiner) before flying.

**Temporary medical unfitness.** If you are temporarily unfit, through injury, illness, surgery, pregnancy complications or a condition that needs medication, you must not fly. If the condition lasts more than a short period or is medically significant you must tell a DAME or CASA, who decide when you may fly again. Fitness is your call every day, and the medical certificate does not override a head cold.

??? check "Check yourself: a pilot finished their last drink at 2200 and plans to depart at 0700. Legal?"
    Nine hours have passed, so the 8-hour minimum is met, but the pilot must also be under 0.02 and not under the influence. If the night involved several drinks they may still be over the limit at 0700. The 8 hours is a floor, not a clearance.

## Light signals

If your radio fails in a control zone, or a tower needs to talk to an aircraft without one, it uses a signal lamp. The colours follow a pattern: **green is permission, red is prohibition, white is "go back", and a flash is a weaker version of a steady light.**

| Signal | To an aircraft in flight | To an aircraft on the ground |
|---|---|---|
| Steady green | Cleared to land | Cleared for take-off |
| Steady red | Give way to other aircraft and continue circling | Stop |
| Green flashes | Return for landing (a clearance to land will follow) | Cleared to taxi |
| Red flashes | Aerodrome unsafe, do not land | Taxi clear of the landing area in use |
| White flashes | Land at this aerodrome and proceed to the apron (after landing) | Return to your starting point on the aerodrome |
| Red pyrotechnic | Notwithstanding any previous instruction, do not land for the time being | |

Acknowledge in flight by **rocking the wings** by day or **flashing the landing light** at night; on the ground by moving the ailerons or rudder, or flashing lights at night.

??? check "Check yourself: a steady red light is directed at you on final. What does it mean, and what do you do?"
    Give way to other aircraft and continue circling. Go around, stay in the circuit and watch for the next signal; a steady green will clear you to land. On the ground the same steady red means stop.

## Minimum heights

Two heights, two reasons. Over people, you must be high enough to glide clear if the engine fails; elsewhere, you must be high enough not to endanger or disturb anyone on the ground.

- **Over a populous area** (a city, town or other populous area, and over a public gathering): not below **1,000 ft above the highest obstacle within a 600 m radius** of the aircraft. Note the datum: the highest obstacle within 600 m, not the ground. Over Perth's city centre that puts the floor well above 1,000 ft AMSL.
- **Anywhere else:** not below **500 ft above the highest feature or obstacle within a 300 m radius** of the point directly below the aircraft. Over flat open country or water that is 500 ft above the surface.

![How low may you fly over a town, and elsewhere?](diagram:minimum-heights "Over a populous area the 1,000 ft is measured from the highest obstacle within 600 m, not from the ground.")

The exceptions: taking off and landing, operations approved by CASA (low-level training in approved areas), a forced landing, and flight needed to avoid weather or terrain. Whatever the height, you must always be able to glide clear of a populous area and land without undue hazard if the engine fails.

??? check "Check yourself: what is the minimum height over a town, and from where is it measured?"
    1,000 ft above the highest obstacle within a 600 m radius of the aircraft, not 1,000 ft above the ground. Elsewhere the minimum is 500 ft above the highest feature or obstacle within 300 m of the point below you.

## Aerobatics and public gatherings

**Acrobatic (aerobatic) flight** is permitted only in an aircraft certified for it, in day VMC, by a pilot who holds the aerobatic endorsement or is under instruction, **not below 3,000 ft above the ground** unless CASA has approved a lower height, and never over a populous area or a public gathering. Passengers may only be carried on an aerobatic flight if the rules for passenger carriage during aerobatics are met.

**Flight over a public gathering** (a sporting fixture, a race meeting, a concert, an air show crowd) comes under the populous-area rule: 1,000 ft above the highest obstacle within 600 m. Air displays over gatherings require CASA approval.

## Landing before the end of daylight

A VFR flight by a pilot without a night VFR rating must be planned so that the aircraft is on the ground at least **10 minutes before the end of daylight** (last light) at the destination or alternate, after allowing for any holding. First light and last light are published for each location in the daylight and darkness graphs in the AIP and online, and they depend on the date and the place. Western Australia's east-west spread makes this bite: last light at Kalgoorlie is well before last light at Perth on the same evening, and a flight planned on Perth's figure arrives in the dark.

??? check "Check yourself: you are planning to arrive at Bunbury with last light at 1915 local. By when must you be on the ground?"
    By 1905 local, 10 minutes before last light, with any holding you might need allowed for before that. If the plan does not achieve it, leave earlier or land short.

## Bringing it together

A Saturday in May: Jandakot to Bunbury and back. The night before, you stop at one glass at 2100 because you brief at 0800; 8 hours is comfortably met and so is the limit. At the aeroplane you set the ATIS QNH and the altimeter reads within 100 ft of the 99 ft elevation. Departing 24L you climb straight ahead to 500 ft before the left turn. Clear of the zone you climb to 4,500 ft on a track of about 175 degrees magnetic: above 5,000 ft you would need an odd thousand plus 500, but you are below it, so any altitude meeting the minimum heights will do, and you fly on the Perth QNH until you are more than 100 nm from it, then area QNH. Over farmland near Harvey the floor is 500 ft; over Bunbury itself it is 1,000 ft above the highest obstacle within 600 m. Scattered cumulus with tops near 3,500 ft and 8 km visibility: at 4,500 ft in Class G above 3,000 ft AMSL you need 1,500 m horizontally and 1,000 ft vertically from cloud, so you hold 1,000 ft above the tops or weave 1,500 m around the taller ones. At Bunbury you join the published circuit, give way to the aircraft lower on approach, and on departure wait for the Jabiru ahead to be 600 m down the runway before you roll. Last light is 1800, so you plan wheels down at Jandakot by 1750 at the latest. Every rule in the lesson has appeared, and none of them needed a tower.

## Key points

- **2.3.1** Turn right to avoid; give way to the aircraft on your right, to the one you are overtaking, to the lower one on approach and to anything less manoeuvrable. Circuits left-hand unless published, 1,000 ft, no turn below 500 ft after take-off. Behind another aircraft: wait until it has vacated, crossed the upwind end or turned, or is airborne 600 m ahead (both under 2,000 kg) or 1,800 m ahead (runway over 1,800 m). No smoking during take-off, landing or refuelling. VMC below 10,000 ft: 5,000 m visibility, 1,500 m and 1,000 ft from cloud (Class D: 600 m, 1,000 ft above, 500 ft below), or clear of cloud in sight of the ground low in Class G. Below 10,000 ft fly on QNH (local within 100 nm, or area QNH); above 5,000 ft odd plus 500 on tracks 000 to 179, even plus 500 on 180 to 359.
- **2.3.2** 8 hours bottle to throttle and 0.02 g/100 mL; no impairing drugs; do not fly while temporarily unfit, and tell a DAME or CASA if it lasts.
- **2.3.3** Green permits, red prohibits, white sends you back; flashes are the weaker version. Steady green: land or take off. Steady red: circle or stop.
- **2.3.4** 1,000 ft above the highest obstacle within 600 m over a populous area; 500 ft above the highest feature or obstacle within 300 m elsewhere.
- **2.3.5** Aerobatics: certified aircraft, endorsed pilot, day VMC, not below 3,000 ft AGL, never over a populous area or gathering. Gatherings: the populous-area rule.
- **2.3.6** On the ground at least 10 minutes before last light, holding included.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    The RPLA questions in this subtopic are mostly straight recall, so have the figures by heart: 8 hours, 1,000 ft within 600 m and 500 ft within 300 m elsewhere, 3,000 ft for aerobatics, 500 ft before turning after take-off, 600 m and 1,800 m, 5,000 m and 1,500 m and 1,000 ft, 10 minutes before last light, odd plus 500 eastbound. Read the condition in each question carefully: "both aircraft under 2,000 kg" and "runway longer than 1,800 m" are where the distractors live. Confirm each figure against the current VFRG, because these are the figures CASA changes when it updates Part 91.
