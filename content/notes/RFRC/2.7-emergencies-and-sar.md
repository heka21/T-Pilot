## What the exam expects (RFRC 2.7)

- **2.7.1** Explain what an aircraft is signalling when it switches its navigation or landing lights on and off intermittently.
- **2.7.2** Distinguish an accident from an incident.
- **2.7.3** Work out what must be reported after an incident or accident, to whom, how and how soon.
- **2.7.4** Explain what a SARTIME is and how you use one.
- **2.7.5** Name the document that holds the emergency procedures.

## Why this matters

Most flights end exactly as planned, and this subtopic is about the ones that do not. A radio failure on the way into Jandakot, a bird strike on climb-out from Northam, a precautionary landing in a paddock east of Merredin with the nearest farmhouse 20 km away: in each, what happens next depends on systems you set up before the flight and on rules you know without looking up. Western Australia makes the search and rescue part of this unusually real. Much of the state is empty enough that if you went down without a SARTIME or a flight note, nobody would start looking for a long time. The exam asks short recall questions about the definitions, the reporting rules, the SARTIME and where to find the procedures. This lesson gives each one its reason, because in an emergency you act on understanding, not memorised wording.

## Flashing lights: "I have to land"

Imagine you are holding short at a country aerodrome, waiting to line up, and an aeroplane on a wide final starts switching its **landing lights on and off**, over and over. It has made no radio call. What is it telling you?

An aircraft that **repeatedly switches its landing lights or navigation lights on and off** is signalling that it is **in difficulty and compelled to land**, for example with a **radio failure**, and it **needs the aerodrome to make way for it**. The pilot cannot tell you by radio, so the lights do the talking. If you see it, **give way and expect the aircraft to land**: stay clear of the runway, and if you are in the circuit, extend or leave the pattern to make room.

The signal works because it is unmistakable. Steady lights are normal; landing lights going on and off repeatedly are not something anyone does by accident.

Context still matters, because lights are used for other signals too. **Flashing the landing lights** is also the **night acknowledgement of a light signal from the tower** (see the light signals in [RFRC 2.3](/notes/RFRC/2.3)), and **intercepted aircraft use lights** as part of the **interception signals in ERSA EMERG**. A single flash in reply to a green light from the tower is an acknowledgement; repeated irregular switching on approach is a request for the runway.

??? check "Check yourself: why would a pilot use the lights rather than the radio to signal that they must land?"
    Usually because the radio has failed. The lights are the remaining way to tell the aerodrome and other traffic that the aircraft is in difficulty and needs to land, so they should give way.

## Accident or incident

After something goes wrong, the first question the system asks is how serious it was, because that decides how fast it must be reported and whether it will be investigated. The two categories are defined in the **transport safety legislation** (the Transport Safety Investigation Act 2003 and Regulations 2021; verify current text), and the definitions turn on outcomes, not causes.

### Accident

An **accident** is an occurrence associated with the operation of an aircraft, **between the time a person boards with the intention of flight and the time everyone has disembarked**, in which:

- a person is **killed or seriously injured**; or
- the aircraft sustains **serious damage or structural failure** affecting its strength, performance or flight characteristics and normally requiring major repair; or
- the aircraft is **missing or completely inaccessible**.

Notice the time window. An occurrence with an engine running on the apron but nobody aboard intending to fly is outside it. Notice also that the three outcomes are about **people**, the **airframe**, and the **aircraft's whereabouts**.

Some damage is common, local and repairable, and does not by itself make an occurrence an accident: **damage limited to the engine, propeller, wheels, tyres, brakes, fairings, small dents or puncture holes in the skin**. A propeller strike on landing that bends the propeller tips is serious and must be reported, but if nothing else was damaged and nobody was hurt it does not meet the accident definition on its own.

### Incident and serious incident

An **incident** is **any other occurrence**, other than an accident, associated with the operation of an aircraft that **affects or could affect the safety of operation**. The definition is wide on purpose: the point of reporting incidents is to learn from the near things before they become the real things.

Between the two sits the **serious incident**: one in which **an accident nearly occurred**. Examples are a **near collision**, a **runway incursion**, and **fuel exhaustion short of an accident**. Nobody was hurt and nothing was broken, but only luck separated it from an accident.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The word "serious" is not a measure of how frightening it felt. A **serious incident** is one where an **accident nearly occurred**. An **accident** requires an actual outcome: death or serious injury, serious damage or structural failure, or a missing aircraft. A hair-raising near miss with no damage is a serious incident, not an accident.

??? check "Check yourself: a nosewheel tyre bursts on landing and the aeroplane stops on the runway with no other damage and nobody hurt. Accident or incident?"
    An incident. Damage limited to wheels and tyres does not by itself make an occurrence an accident, and nobody was injured. It still affected the safety of operation, so it is reportable.

## Reporting: who, how and how soon

Reporting goes to the **Australian Transport Safety Bureau (ATSB)**, **not CASA**, under the Transport Safety Investigation Regulations. The reason is separation of jobs: CASA is the regulator, which enforces the rules; the ATSB is the investigator, whose only job is to find out what happened so it does not happen again. A no-blame investigator gets more honest reports.

The rules sort occurrences into two groups by urgency.

**Immediately reportable matters** are the serious ones: **accidents, serious incidents**, and the **listed serious events** such as a death or serious injury, serious damage, a missing aircraft, a near collision, or fuel exhaustion. For these the **responsible person** (the **pilot in command**, or the **operator** if the pilot is unable) must:

1. report to the ATSB **as soon as practicable by the quickest means**, normally by **phone** on the 24-hour number **1800 011 034**; and then
2. submit a **written report within 72 hours**.

The phone call comes first because an investigation may need to start at once: the wreckage preserved, the weather recorded, witnesses found while they remember.

**Routine reportable matters** are the other incidents that affect safety, for example an **engine failure that ended in a safe landing**, **airspace infringements**, or a **bird strike**. For these a **written report within 72 hours** through the ATSB online form is enough. Nothing needs to be preserved urgently, but the report adds to the picture that lets the ATSB see patterns.

Two other reports often follow. **Defects** found as a result also go on the **maintenance release** (see [RFRC 2.2](/notes/RFRC/2.2)), and **major defects** are reported to **CASA** through the **defect reporting system**. Your **operator or flying school** will also want its own report.

**Preserve the evidence.** After an accident, **do not move the wreckage except to rescue people or prevent further damage**, unless the **ATSB or the police agree**. The position of a switch or the angle of a control surface can tell an investigator what happened; moving it erases the clue.

![Accident or incident, and how do you report it?](diagram:accident-incident-reporting "Accidents and serious incidents: phone the ATSB straight away, then write within 72 hours; other incidents: write within 72 hours.")

!!! example "Worked example: what must be reported, and how?"
    Work out the category of each occurrence first, then the reporting follows.

    1. *On a solo flight from Jandakot you inadvertently climb into the Class C step above you, notice, and descend out of it.* Nobody hurt, nothing damaged, no near collision: an **incident**, and an airspace infringement is a **routine reportable matter**. A **written report to the ATSB within 72 hours**.
    2. *A student and instructor on a training flight near York have a hard landing in a paddock after the engine stops; the instructor suffers a serious back injury and the wing spar is cracked.* A serious injury and structural damage: an **accident**, **immediately reportable**. The instructor is injured, so the student or the operator **phones the ATSB on 1800 011 034 as soon as practicable**, then a **written report within 72 hours**. Nothing is moved except to help the injured.
    3. *On final at a country strip you see a ute cross the runway in front of you and go around with metres to spare.* No damage, no injury, but an accident nearly occurred: a **serious incident** (a runway incursion). **Immediately reportable**: phone first, then the written report within 72 hours.

    The method: decide accident, serious incident or incident by the outcome, then the reporting rule follows from the category.

Now try one. On climb-out from Northam a large bird hits the windscreen. It cracks but holds, and you return and land safely. Nobody is hurt. What category is it, and what must you report?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    No death or injury, and damage limited to a cracked windscreen with no structural failure: not an accident. No accident nearly occurred in the sense of a near collision, so it is an incident, and a bird strike is a routine reportable matter. A written report to the ATSB within 72 hours, the damage endorsed on the maintenance release, and your operator told.

??? check "Check yourself: why does an immediately reportable matter need a phone call as well as the written report?"
    Because an investigation may need to start at once, to preserve wreckage and evidence and contact witnesses. The phone call reaches the ATSB in minutes; the written report, within 72 hours, follows with the detail.

## SARTIME: making sure someone comes looking

Here is the uncomfortable thought behind search and rescue. You make a forced landing in a remote area, the radio is damaged, and you are uninjured but stuck. Who knows you are missing? If you told nobody when to expect you, the honest answer may be: nobody, until a family member wonders where you are at dinner time, and even then they do not know where you were going.

A **SARTIME** (search and rescue time) fixes that. It is a **time you nominate, in UTC, by which you will report your arrival or the completion of a flight**. You lodge it with **Airservices** through a **flight plan** or the **NAIPS SARTIME form**, or by **phone or radio to Flight Information**. From then on the system is waiting for you.

If you have **not cancelled or amended the SARTIME by that time**, the search and rescue system **assumes you are in trouble** and begins the **alerting phases**: first **attempts to contact you**, then a **communications search** (calling aerodromes and contacts along your route), then a **physical search** by the **Australian Maritime Safety Authority's Joint Rescue Coordination Centre**. The beauty of the system is that it needs nothing from you once you are down: the clock does the alerting.

![What happens if you do not cancel your SARTIME?](diagram:sartime-timeline "Nothing happens until the SARTIME passes; then the alerting phases start, ending in a physical search, so cancel on landing.")

### How to use it

**When to use one:** flights over **remote or inhospitable country**, **long cross-country flights**, and any flight where **nobody else would know you were missing**. In WA that covers almost any flight east of the wheatbelt or along the coast north of Geraldton.

**Choosing the time:** pick a SARTIME that gives a **sensible margin after your planned arrival**: enough to absorb a headwind or a short hold without triggering a search, but not so long that the search starts hours after you went down. The time is in UTC, so convert carefully (WST is UTC + 8; see [BAKC 2.3](/notes/BAKC/2.3)).

**If you are delayed:** **extend it by radio or phone before it expires**.

**On arrival:** **cancel it as soon as you land**. **Failing to cancel wastes search resources** and is embarrassing at best: the alerting phases start for an aircraft that is safely tied down, and people and aircraft may be committed to a search that was never needed.

**The alternative** is to leave a **flight note** with a **responsible person**, who contacts SAR if you do not arrive. The choice between the two is **yours as pilot in command for a private VFR flight**. A flight note relies on that person remembering, understanding the plan and acting; a SARTIME is held by a system that does not forget.

!!! example "Worked example: setting and managing a SARTIME"
    You plan a private flight from Jandakot to Hyden, departing 0930 WST, with a planned flight time of 1 hour 50 minutes. Nobody will be waiting for you at Hyden.

    1. Planned arrival: $0930 + 1{:}50$ gives **1120 WST**.
    2. Add a margin you choose, say 30 minutes, for headwinds and a possible hold: **1150 WST**.
    3. Convert to UTC: $1150 - 8{:}00$ gives **0350 UTC**. You lodge a SARTIME of 0350 UTC through NAIPS.
    4. En route a stronger headwind puts your arrival at 1145 WST, too close to 1150. You **call Flight Information on the radio and extend** the SARTIME to, say, 0420 UTC, before the original expires.
    5. On landing at Hyden you **cancel the SARTIME** by phone at once.

    The margin is your choice as pilot in command, not a regulatory figure; what the rules require is that you extend before expiry and cancel on arrival.

### If the worst happens

If you do go down, the SARTIME means help is coming to the route you notified. That is why the standard survival advice is to **stay with the aircraft**: it is far easier to see from the air than a person, it gives shade, and the search follows your notified route.

![After a forced landing, what are your survival priorities?](diagram:survival-priorities "Protection first, then location, water and food; stay with the aircraft, because the search follows your notified route.")

??? check "Check yourself: you are running late towards a remote strip, and your SARTIME expires in ten minutes. What do you do, and why now?"
    Extend it by radio or phone immediately, before it expires. If it passes uncancelled, the alerting phases begin and resources are committed to looking for an aircraft that is not missing.

## Where the emergency procedures are

In an emergency you do not have time to search the AIP. The **ERSA EMERG section** (the **pink pages**) is the **in-flight reference for emergency procedures**, deliberately printed on coloured paper so you can find it by feel and sight. It contains:

- **distress and urgency calls**: **MAYDAY** (grave and imminent danger, immediate assistance needed) and **PAN PAN** (urgency, a problem without immediate danger);
- the **transponder emergency codes**: **7700 emergency**, **7600 radio failure**, **7500 unlawful interference**;
- **radio failure procedures**;
- **interception procedures and signals**;
- **light signals**;
- **ditching**, **survival** and the **SAR system**.

**AIP GEN 3.6 (search and rescue) and ENR 1.12 (interception)** contain the fuller text. For flight, **carry a current ERSA**, because the EMERG section is the version you can use with one hand while flying the aeroplane.

![What is in ERSA EMERG?](diagram:ersa-emerg-contents "What to notice: the pink pages are grouped by purpose: tell someone, deal with the problem, then survive and be found.")

The transponder codes follow a logic worth seeing. Each one tells ATC something they need to know without a word: 7700 says "I am in trouble", 7600 says "I cannot hear you, but I am following the radio failure procedure", and 7500 says something a pilot may not be able to say aloud with a hijacker in the cabin.

![Which transponder code means what?](diagram:transponder-emergency-codes "7700 emergency, 7600 radio failure, 7500 unlawful interference: all in ERSA EMERG.")

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The AIP holds the full text, but the exam asks for the document that **contains the emergency procedures** for use in flight: **ERSA EMERG**. Do not pick the VFRG or the AIP GEN section when ERSA EMERG is offered.

## Bringing it together

A Saturday flight from Jandakot to Merredin. Before departure you lodge a SARTIME in UTC with a margin after your planned arrival, and you know the ERSA EMERG pages are in the seat pocket. Halfway there the radio goes silent. You open ERSA EMERG, set 7600, and follow the radio failure procedure. Arriving at Merredin you see another aeroplane in the circuit, so on a wide final you switch your landing lights on and off repeatedly to show that you are compelled to land; it extends downwind and gives you the runway. On landing your nosewheel drops into a soft patch and the propeller tips touch the ground. Nobody is hurt and the damage is limited to the propeller, so this is not an accident by the definition, but it affected safety, so it is an incident: a written report to the ATSB within 72 hours, a defect endorsement on the maintenance release, and a call to the operator. And before any of that, because your radio is dead, you borrow a phone and cancel the SARTIME. Every element of the lesson appeared in one bad afternoon that ended well because each system was in place before it started.

??? check "Check yourself: in that scenario, which single action prevents a search for an aircraft that is safely on the ground?"
    Cancelling the SARTIME on landing, by phone if the radio is unserviceable. Until it is cancelled, the system will start the alerting phases at the nominated time.

## Key points

- **2.7.1** Landing or navigation lights switched on and off repeatedly: in difficulty and compelled to land (often a radio failure); give way. A single flash at night can be an acknowledgement, and interception signals use lights too.
- **2.7.2** Accident: death or serious injury, serious damage or structural failure, or the aircraft missing, between boarding for flight and disembarking. Damage limited to engine, propeller, wheels, tyres, brakes, fairings or small dents is not by itself an accident. Incident: anything else that affects or could affect safety; a serious incident is one where an accident nearly occurred.
- **2.7.3** To the ATSB, not CASA. Immediately reportable (accidents, serious incidents, listed events): phone 1800 011 034 as soon as practicable, then written within 72 hours. Routine reportable: written within 72 hours. Defects on the maintenance release and to CASA; do not disturb wreckage.
- **2.7.4** A SARTIME is a UTC time by which you report arrival; lodge it with Airservices, extend it before it expires, cancel it on landing; uncancelled, it starts the alerting phases and a search by the JRCC. The alternative is a flight note with a responsible person.
- **2.7.5** Emergency procedures are in ERSA EMERG, the pink pages: MAYDAY and PAN PAN, 7700, 7600, 7500, radio failure, interception, light signals, ditching, survival and SAR.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Accidents and serious incidents are reported to the ATSB immediately and in writing within 72 hours; routine incidents in writing within 72 hours. A SARTIME must be cancelled on arrival. The emergency procedures are in ERSA EMERG. In accident-or-incident questions, test the occurrence against the three accident outcomes (person, airframe, whereabouts) and the list of damage that does not count; the distractor is usually an occurrence that sounds dramatic but meets none of them. Verify the reporting rules and the ATSB number against the current Transport Safety Investigation Regulations and ERSA.
