## What the exam expects (RFRC 2.2)

- **2.2.1** State what the RPL does not let you do: the aircraft, conditions, distance, airspace and height limits.
- **2.2.2** Describe what you must do to keep using the licence: flight review, recent experience for passengers, a current medical, and the documents.
- **2.2.3** State which medical certificates an RPL holder may use, and the limits each one brings.
- **2.2.4** State what a licence holder may do about the daily inspection, the maintenance release and defects.

## Why this matters

The recreational pilot licence is the first licence most WA students hold, and many fly on it for years: weekend flights from Serpentine or Murrayfield, a friend in the right seat, a coffee at a country strip. It is a real licence with real privileges, but every privilege comes with a fence, and the fences are where pilots get into legal trouble without meaning to: a passenger carried with no recent landings, a flight review that lapsed last month, a second passenger on a medical that allows one. The exam asks this subtopic as straight recall of a handful of figures and as "may this pilot do this?" scenarios. This lesson explains why each fence is where it is, so that the figures make sense and the scenarios become easy to reason through.

## What the RPL lets you do, and where it stops

Start with what the RPL is for. It was designed for flying close to home in simple aeroplanes in good weather, with the option to grow it into something bigger one endorsement at a time. Every limit follows from that design: the licence assumes the least demanding conditions, and you earn your way out of each limit by training for it.

The holder of a recreational pilot licence with an aeroplane category rating may fly as pilot in command of a **single-engine aeroplane certified for single-pilot operation with a maximum take-off weight (MTOW) of not more than 1,500 kg**, and may carry passengers (CASR 61.425 to 61.450; verify current text). The limits sit around that core:

- **Day VFR only.** No night flying and no flight in IMC. The RPL training contains no instrument flying beyond the basics and no night flying, so the licence stops at the conditions you were trained for.
- **Private operations or flight training only.** No aerial work and no commercial operations. Nobody pays you to fly, and you do not fly anyone for hire.
- **Within 25 nm of the departure aerodrome** (or in a designated flight training area) unless you hold a **recreational navigation endorsement**. The basic licence teaches you to fly the aeroplane, not to navigate it; 25 nm keeps you in country you can find your way back from.
- **Non-controlled aerodromes and Class G airspace** unless you hold the **controlled aerodrome endorsement** and the **controlled airspace endorsement**. Talking to a tower and following clearances are separate skills, trained and tested separately.
- **Below 10,000 ft** unless you hold the **flight above 10,000 ft endorsement**, and the aircraft and oxygen rules allow it.
- Only aircraft within your **class rating** (single-engine aeroplane) and with any **design feature endorsements** the aeroplane needs: **tailwheel, retractable undercarriage, manual propeller pitch control, pressurisation, floats**. A tailwheel aeroplane handles differently on the ground; a retractable undercarriage can be forgotten. Each feature has its own training.
- **No aerobatics** unless you hold the aerobatic endorsement, and no formation, spinning or other activity without the relevant endorsement.

![What does the bare RPL allow, and what does each endorsement add?](diagram:rpl-privileges-map "Each endorsement lifts one limit; day VFR, private operations, single-engine and 1,500 kg stay whatever you add.")

The diagram shows the pattern clearly. Some limits can be lifted by an endorsement on the RPL itself: distance (navigation endorsement), controlled aerodromes and airspace, height (above 10,000 ft), design features and aerobatics. Others belong to the licence and no endorsement removes them: **single-engine**, **not more than 1,500 kg**, **day VFR**, **private operations**. To get past those you need a higher licence: the **PPL** removes the weight, distance and airspace limits, and the **CPL** allows commercial operations.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The 1,500 kg is the aeroplane's **maximum take-off weight**, the certified figure in the flight manual, not what the aeroplane weighs today. A 1,600 kg MTOW single flown light with one person on board is still outside the RPL.

!!! example "Worked example: may this RPL holder fly this flight?"
    Sam holds an RPL(A) with the recreational navigation endorsement and the controlled aerodrome endorsement, but not the controlled airspace endorsement. Sam wants to take a friend from Murrayfield to York in a four-seat single-engine aeroplane of 1,100 kg MTOW on a Saturday morning.

    1. Aircraft: single-engine, single-pilot, **1,100 kg is not more than 1,500 kg**. Within the licence.
    2. Conditions: day VFR, a private flight with a friend. **Within the licence.**
    3. Distance: York is well beyond 25 nm, but Sam holds the **recreational navigation endorsement**. Allowed.
    4. Airspace: the route must stay in **Class G** and remain clear of controlled airspace, because Sam has no controlled airspace endorsement. The controlled aerodrome endorsement alone does not cover the airspace en route.
    5. Passenger: allowed by the licence, subject to the recency and medical rules in the next sections.

    The flight is legal if Sam plans a route that stays outside controlled airspace and meets the passenger rules.

??? check "Check yourself: an RPL holder adds every endorsement available. Can they now fly a light twin at night?"
    No. Single-engine, day VFR and the 1,500 kg MTOW limit belong to the RPL itself; no endorsement lifts them. A twin needs a multi-engine class rating and a higher licence, and night flying needs a night VFR rating on a PPL or higher.

## Keeping the privileges current

A licence, once issued, does not expire. But skills fade, and the regulations recognise two different kinds of fade. Your **general handling** fades slowly, over years; your **take-off and landing feel** fades quickly, over weeks. So there are two currency rules with two different time windows, matched to what they protect.

### The flight review: can you still fly at all?

You must complete an **aeroplane flight review** with an instructor at least every **24 months**; the review is valid to the end of the 24th month after it was done (CASR 61.400; verify current text). The review is a flight, and a briefing, in which an instructor checks that your handling, your knowledge of the rules and your decision making are still at licence standard. It is not a pass-or-fail flight test in the same sense as the licence test, but if the instructor is not satisfied you do not get the sign-off until you are.

Without a current flight review **you cannot fly as pilot in command at all**, with or without passengers. You may still fly dual with an instructor, which is exactly how you would get back to standard.

### Recent experience: can you carry someone else?

To **carry a passenger**, you must have done at least **3 take-offs and 3 landings in the previous 90 days** in the same category of aircraft (CASR 61.395; verify current text). The logic is that take-offs and landings are where most accidents happen and where your skill decays fastest. Ninety days is short enough to catch a pilot who has not flown for a season; three of each is enough to shake off the rust. If you are not current, the fix is simple: **fly solo circuits first**, then take your passenger.

The rule is about passengers. Alone in the aeroplane you may fly with a current flight review even if your last landing was four months ago; the regulations accept that risk to yourself, but not to someone who is trusting you.

![How far back do the RPL currency rules look?](diagram:rpl-recency-timeline "Passengers need 3 take-offs and 3 landings in the last 90 days; acting as pilot in command at all needs a flight review within 24 months.")

### The other things you must keep in order

- A **medical certificate** that is current and appropriate to the licence (the next section).
- Your **logbook** kept up to date, because it is the evidence of the flight review and the recent experience (see [RFRC 2.1](/notes/RFRC/2.1)).
- The **licence document carried** with the **medical certificate** and **photo identification** when flying (CASR 61.335; check the current requirement for an electronic licence).
- **English language proficiency**, assessed as part of the licence.
- The **endorsements and class ratings** for the aircraft and the operation.

!!! example "Worked example: is this pilot current to take a passenger?"
    Today is 3 October. Priya's logbook shows:

    - flight review completed 18 August two years ago;
    - last three flights: 2 June (two take-offs, two landings), 24 August (one take-off, one landing), 20 September (one take-off, one landing).

    1. Flight review: completed in August two years ago, so valid to the end of the 24th month after it, the end of August this year. **It has expired.** Priya may not fly as pilot in command at all.
    2. Recent experience (for interest): the 90 days before 3 October reach back to early July. Only the August and September flights count: **2 take-offs and 2 landings**, short of 3.

    Two problems, in the order that matters: Priya needs a flight review with an instructor first, which will also count towards recent experience; then, if the review does not bring the total to 3 take-offs and 3 landings in 90 days, some solo circuits before the passenger flight.

??? check "Check yourself: why is the passenger rule 90 days but the flight review 24 months?"
    They protect against different kinds of skill fade. Landing and take-off feel decays within weeks, so recency for passengers looks back 90 days; general handling and knowledge decay slowly, so the full review is needed only every 24 months.

## The medical certificates

Every licence rests on a medical certificate, because no amount of skill helps a pilot who loses consciousness. The RPL is unusual in giving you a choice of three, and the choice follows one principle: **the easier the medical is to get, the tighter the limits that come with it.** A GP who knows you can assess you to a driving standard, but a driving standard was written for a car, so the flying it allows is restricted to aircraft and operations close to driving in risk.

| Certificate | Who issues it | Limits that come with it |
|---|---|---|
| Class 2 medical certificate | A DAME (Designated Aviation Medical Examiner) with CASA | The standard private pilot medical; no extra operational limits; valid 4 years under 40, 2 years over 40 (check current validity periods) |
| Basic Class 2 | Any GP, to the commercial driver medical standard | Private, day VFR, below 10,000 ft, piston aircraft up to 8,618 kg, no more than 5 passengers |
| RAMPC (recreational aviation medical practitioner's certificate) | Any GP, to the private driver standard | Single-engine piston aircraft up to 1,500 kg, day VFR, below 10,000 ft, **no more than 1 passenger**, no aerobatics, private operations |

Read the table from the top down. The **Class 2** is issued by a **DAME**, a doctor trained in aviation medicine and approved by CASA, who examines you to an aviation standard; in return it carries no operational limits beyond the licence's own. The **Basic Class 2** is issued by **any GP** to the **commercial driver** standard, a stricter standard than for a private car, and allows up to **5 passengers**. The **RAMPC** is issued by any GP to the **private driver** standard, the easiest to meet, and allows only **1 passenger**, a single-engine piston aeroplane up to 1,500 kg, and no aerobatics (CASR Part 67 and CASA's RAMPC and Basic Class 2 pages; verify current limits).

![Which medical can an RPL holder use, and what does each one limit?](diagram:rpl-medical-certificates "The easier the medical is to get, the tighter the limits: a RAMPC allows only 1 passenger, a Basic Class 2 up to 5.")

Notice that the licence limit and the medical limit stack: you are bound by whichever is tighter. An RPL holder on a Basic Class 2 is still limited to 1,500 kg by the licence, even though the medical would allow 8,618 kg.

![When the licence, the medical and the aeroplane each set a limit, which one applies?](diagram:tighter-limit-applies "What to notice: the tightest limit wins: 1,500 kg from the licence even on a Basic Class 2, and 1 passenger on a RAMPC however many seats the aeroplane has.")

### Fitness on the day

A certificate says you were fit when you were examined. It says nothing about today. Whatever the certificate, you **must not fly if you know or suspect you are temporarily unfit**: illness, injury, medication, fatigue. And you must **tell CASA or a DAME of a medically significant condition** that could affect your ability to fly safely (CASR 67.265). The rules on alcohol, drugs and temporary unfitness are taught in [RFRC 2.3](/notes/RFRC/2.3).

!!! warning "Common trap"
    "No more than 1 passenger" on a RAMPC is a limit of the **medical**, not the licence or the aeroplane. A four-seat aeroplane and a valid RPL do not let a RAMPC holder take two friends.

Try this one before opening the working. Jo holds an RPL with the navigation endorsement and a RAMPC. Jo plans a day VFR flight to Northam in a 1,150 kg single-engine piston aeroplane with a partner and their teenage son. May Jo take both?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    No. The aircraft, the conditions and the distance are all within the licence and the RAMPC, but the RAMPC allows **no more than 1 passenger**, and Jo plans two. Jo can take one of them, or obtain a Basic Class 2 (up to 5 passengers) or a Class 2 before taking both.

??? check "Check yourself: why does a medical issued to a private driving standard come with more limits than one issued by a DAME?"
    A driving standard assesses fitness for a car, not for flight, so it is accepted only for flying close to driving in risk: one passenger, a light single-engine piston aeroplane, day VFR. The DAME's aviation examination supports the full privileges of the licence.

## Daily inspections, the maintenance release and defects

In [RFRC 2.1](/notes/RFRC/2.1) you met the maintenance release as the document that tells you whether the aeroplane may fly. This section is about what *you*, as a licence holder, may write on it, and what you may not. The dividing line is simple: **engineers certify airworthiness; pilots certify what they have personally checked and observed.**

### The daily inspection

Before the first flight of each day the aeroplane needs a **daily inspection**: a thorough check to the manufacturer's or operator's daily inspection schedule. It is more than the walk-around before each flight. A pilot licence holder **may carry out and certify the daily inspection** on a **Class B (private or aerial work) aircraft** that they are about to fly, **if trained and authorised** to do so by the operator or school (CAR 1988 42ZC; verify current text). The certification goes in **Part 3** of the maintenance release (the daily inspection and time-in-service record) and covers that day.

The reason you may do it is practical: aeroplanes fly from places where no engineer is present at 0700. The reason you must be trained and authorised is that the inspection is only worth what the inspector knows to look for.

![How does the daily inspection differ from the pre-flight inspection?](diagram:daily-vs-preflight-inspection "The daily is once a day, to a schedule, and certified on the maintenance release; the pre-flight is before every flight and not certified.")

### Signing the maintenance release

A **pilot cannot issue a maintenance release**; only a **licensed engineer or an approved organisation** can, because issuing it certifies the scheduled maintenance has been done properly. What a pilot does sign on the maintenance release is:

- the **daily inspection** certification (if authorised);
- the **flight time** after each flight, which keeps the hours remaining accurate;
- **defects** found.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    An exam option that says the pilot may "issue" or "renew" the maintenance release is wrong. The pilot **signs on** the release (daily inspection, flight time, defects) but never **issues** it.

### Reporting defects

A pilot who finds a defect **must endorse it in Part 2** of the maintenance release. The endorsement is how the defect reaches the engineer and every later pilot; a defect you mention only to a friend in the clubhouse does not exist as far as the next pilot is concerned.

![What happens after a defect is found?](diagram:defect-endorsement-flow "The defect goes in Part 2 with a description, date, time in service and signature; the aircraft stays on the ground until an engineer clears it or endorses a permissible unserviceability.")

Beyond the maintenance release, the pilot in command must also report defects that affect safety to the **operator**, and major defects and reportable matters through the transport safety reporting system: the **ATSB** for accidents and incidents (see [RFRC 2.7](/notes/RFRC/2.7)), and **CASA's defect reporting** for airworthiness defects.

??? check "Check yourself: you are authorised to do daily inspections on the club aeroplane. During one you find a cracked exhaust. What may you certify, and what must you do?"
    You must not certify the aircraft as serviceable. Endorse the defect in Part 2 of the maintenance release and tell the operator; the aircraft stays on the ground until a licensed engineer rectifies it or endorses it as a permissible unserviceability.

## Bringing it together

Alex holds an RPL with the navigation endorsement and a Basic Class 2, flight review done fourteen months ago, and last flew six weeks ago: two landings at Serpentine. Alex wants to take two friends to Narrogin in the club's 1,050 kg four-seater on Sunday. Licence: single-engine, under 1,500 kg, day VFR, private, beyond 25 nm with the endorsement; a route planned to stay in Class G: fine. Flight review: well within 24 months. Medical: Basic Class 2 allows up to 5 passengers, so two are fine. Recent experience: only 2 take-offs and 2 landings in the last 90 days, so Alex flies a couple of solo circuits on Saturday. Sunday morning Alex, trained and authorised by the club, does the daily inspection and signs it, checks Part 2, and after the flight writes the time in the maintenance release and the flight in the logbook. Every rule in the lesson came up, and each one had a reason Alex could see.

??? check "Check yourself: which two currency checks must an RPL holder pass before taking a passenger, and which one also stops a solo flight?"
    A flight review within 24 months, and 3 take-offs and 3 landings in the previous 90 days. Only the flight review also stops a solo flight; recent experience applies to carrying passengers.

## Key points

- **2.2.1** Single-engine, single-pilot, MTOW not more than 1,500 kg, day VFR, private or training. Within 25 nm without the navigation endorsement; Class G and non-controlled aerodromes without the controlled airspace and aerodrome endorsements; below 10,000 ft; design features and aerobatics need their endorsements. Endorsements lift some limits; single-engine, 1,500 kg, day VFR and private stay.
- **2.2.2** Flight review every 24 months to act as pilot in command at all; 3 take-offs and 3 landings in the previous 90 days to carry a passenger; current medical, logbook, licence, medical and photo ID carried.
- **2.2.3** Class 2 (DAME, no extra limits), Basic Class 2 (GP, commercial driver standard, up to 5 passengers, piston up to 8,618 kg), RAMPC (GP, private driver standard, 1 passenger, single-engine piston up to 1,500 kg, no aerobatics). Do not fly when unfit; report significant conditions.
- **2.2.4** A trained and authorised pilot may certify the daily inspection; a pilot signs for the daily, the flight time and defects but never issues the release; defects go in Part 2 and to the operator, with serious matters to the ATSB and CASA.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    The numbers the exam likes: 1,500 kg, 25 nm, 3 take-offs and landings in 90 days, a flight review every 24 months, 1 passenger with a RAMPC. In scenario questions, check each limit in turn (aircraft, conditions, distance, airspace, recency, medical) rather than looking for the one that jumps out; the distractor is usually the limit you skipped. Check the current CASR Part 61 wording before your exam, because the Part 61 licensing rules are amended from time to time.
