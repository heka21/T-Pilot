## What the exam expects (RFRC 2.1)

- **2.1.1** Explain why you record flight time in a logbook, and list the other information each entry must carry.
- **2.1.2** Name the documents that hold the aviation law, the aeronautical information and the general operating rules, and know which one answers which question.
- **2.1.3** Explain what the maintenance release is for and how the pilot uses it before and after flight.

## Why this matters

Three pieces of paper decide whether you may fly today: your logbook, which proves you are qualified and current; the rule books, which say what you may and may not do; and the aeroplane's maintenance release, which says whether the aeroplane itself is fit to go. At Jandakot or Murrayfield you will open the maintenance release before every flight, write in your logbook after every one, and reach for ERSA or the VFRG whenever a question comes up that you cannot answer from memory. The exam tests this subtopic in a practical way: it describes a question a pilot has and asks which document answers it, or describes what you find in a maintenance release and asks whether you can fly. This lesson shows you why each document exists, so the "which document" questions answer themselves.

## The logbook: your evidence

Imagine you apply for your PPL flight test. The examiner needs to know that you have flown the hours the regulations require, that your cross-country time is real, and that your flight review is current. They were not there for any of it. The only thing they can look at is your **logbook**, the personal record of every flight you have made. That is the whole reason it exists: it is the **legal evidence of your aeronautical experience**.

Every pilot must keep a logbook and record each flight in it (CASR Part 61 and the Part 61 MOS logbook requirements; verify the current text). The record proves several different things, and each one is used by someone:

- that you have the **hours and experience** for a licence, rating or endorsement you are applying for;
- that you have the **recent experience** to carry passengers (the 3 take-offs and 3 landings in 90 days you will meet in [RFRC 2.2](/notes/RFRC/2.2));
- that your **flight review** is current, and that endorsements and flight tests have been passed and signed off;
- the history of your flying, which **CASA, a flight examiner or a future employer** can ask to see.

Because it is evidence, the logbook is kept for the rest of your flying life. A pilot who gets a commercial licence ten years from now will still be counting hours recorded in this year's training.

### What each entry must show

A useful way to remember the list is to ask what someone checking your experience would need to know about each flight: *when*, *in what*, *who was in charge*, *where*, *how long and of what kind*, and *was anything signed off*. As a minimum the entry includes:

- the **date**;
- the **aircraft type and registration mark**;
- the **name of the pilot in command** and **your own role**: pilot in command, dual (receiving instruction), or in command under supervision;
- the **places of departure and arrival**, or the route;
- the **flight time**, split into **day and night**, and **single or multi-engine**, with **dual, solo and in-command time** recorded separately;
- **instrument time** and **simulator time** where applicable;
- the **nature of the flight**, and any **flight test, flight review or endorsement**, signed by the instructor or examiner where required.

Each column answers a later question. Splitting dual from solo matters because licences require a minimum of each. Splitting day from night matters because a night rating needs night hours. Recording the type and registration matters because endorsements and class ratings depend on what you actually flew.

Make the entry **as soon as practicable**, normally the same day, and keep the running totals accurate. **Falsifying a logbook is an offence**, because a false entry is a false claim of qualification that someone else will rely on.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The logbook is about the **pilot**, not the aircraft. The aeroplane's hours in service are recorded on the maintenance release and in the aircraft's own records. An exam option that says the main purpose of your logbook is to track the aircraft's maintenance hours is wrong: your logbook proves *your* experience.

??? check "Check yourself: why does a logbook entry separate dual time from solo time?"
    Because licences, ratings and endorsements set minimums for each kind of experience, and the logbook is the evidence that you met them. A total alone would not show how much of it you flew without an instructor.

## Where the rules live

When you start flying, the rules seem to come from everywhere: your instructor, a CASA booklet, a page of ERSA, a NOTAM. They are in fact arranged in a clear order, from law made by Parliament down to guidance written to help you. Once you see the order, you can tell what any document is for by asking two questions: **is it law?** and **does it change often?**

![How do the aviation documents stack up, from law to guidance?](diagram:aviation-law-hierarchy "The Act, regulations, MOS and CAOs are law; the AIP family is operational information; the VFRG is guidance that summarises the law.")

### The law

At the top is the **Civil Aviation Act 1988**, the Act of Parliament. It creates CASA and gives it the power to make the detailed rules. It says almost nothing about how to fly an aeroplane; it sets up the system.

Under the Act sit the **regulations**, made by the government and binding on you. There are two sets, because Australia has been moving from an older set to a newer one for many years:

- The **Civil Aviation Safety Regulations 1998 (CASR)** are the main modern regulations, divided into Parts by topic. The ones you will meet most are **Part 61** (flight crew licensing), **Part 67** (medicals), **Part 91** (general operating and flight rules), **Parts 141 and 142** (flight training organisations) and **Part 101** (drones).
- The **Civil Aviation Regulations 1988 (CAR)** are the older regulations still in force where the CASR has not yet replaced them. The one you need to know is **Part 4A**, which holds the **maintenance release** rules.

Regulations state the rule in general terms. The detail, the actual numbers and tables, often sits one level down in a **Manual of Standards (MOS)**, made under a particular CASR Part. The **Part 61 MOS** contains this syllabus and the flight test standards. The **Part 91 MOS** holds the operating detail you fly by: **VMC minima, fuel rules, altimetry and non-controlled aerodrome procedures**. A MOS is law just as the regulation is; the regulation simply points to it for detail that would be clumsy in a regulation.

The **Civil Aviation Orders (CAO)** are the remaining orders made under the CAR. Most have now been replaced by the CASR and its MOS, so you will meet few of them.

### The operational information

Law tells you what you must do. To actually fly, you also need facts about the world: where the runways are, which frequency to call, where the restricted areas sit, and what has changed this week. That is **aeronautical information**, and in Australia it is published by **Airservices Australia**, while the legislation lives on the **Federal Register of Legislation**.

- The **AIP (Aeronautical Information Publication)** is the operational handbook, in three parts: **GEN** (general), **ENR** (en route) and **AD** (aerodromes). It is kept up to date by **AIP Supplements (SUP)** for temporary changes of some length, and **Aeronautical Information Circulars (AIC)** for explanatory and advance information.
- **ERSA**, the En Route Supplement Australia, is the book you carry in the aeroplane: **aerodrome data**, **PRD areas** (prohibited, restricted and danger), **emergency procedures** and **navigation aids**.
- The **DAP** (departure and approach procedures) holds the instrument procedures; as a VFR pilot you will rarely need it.
- The **charts**: WAC, VNC, VTC, ERC and PCA, which show terrain, airspace and routes in picture form (see [RFRC 2.6](/notes/RFRC/2.6) for which ones show airspace).
- **NOTAM** (notices to airmen) carry **temporary or urgent changes to any of the above**: a runway closed for works, a restricted area activated, a navigation aid out of service.

The order matters when two sources seem to disagree. A NOTAM issued this morning overrides the ERSA entry printed months ago, because the NOTAM is how the change is published.

### The guidance

Finally there is material that explains the law but is not law itself. **Advisory Circulars (AC)** explain how to comply with a regulation. The **Visual Flight Rules Guide (VFRG)** is CASA's plain-English summary of the rules for VFR pilots, gathered in one book. It is **guidance, not law**, but it is accurate, it is the book most VFR pilots actually read, and it is **permitted in the RPL and PPL exams**. If the VFRG and the regulation ever differed, the regulation would win.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The VFRG is not the source of the rules. Exam options often offer "the VFRG" for a question whose correct answer is the regulation or the MOS. If the question asks where a rule is **set out** or **contained**, look for the CASR Part or the MOS; if it asks where a VFR pilot can find a **summary**, the VFRG is right.

### Which document answers which question

The practical skill is matching a question to a document. Ask what *kind* of fact you need:

- a rule about **you** (licence, privileges, endorsements): **CASR Part 61**;
- a rule about your **fitness** (medical certificates): **CASR Part 67**;
- a rule about **how you fly** (weather minima, fuel, altimetry, circuits): **CASR Part 91 and the Part 91 MOS**, summarised in the VFRG;
- a fact about a **place** (an aerodrome, a PRD area, a frequency) or an **emergency procedure**: **ERSA**, with the AIP for the full text;
- something that has **changed recently**: **NOTAM**;
- where the **airspace boundaries** are: the **VTC, VNC or ERC**;
- whether the **aeroplane** is fit to fly: the **maintenance release**.

![Which document answers which question?](diagram:where-to-find-it "Rules and standards live in the CASR and its MOS; aerodrome data, PRD areas and emergency procedures in ERSA; today's changes in NOTAM; the aircraft's status in the maintenance release.")

!!! example "Worked example: three questions before a flight to Pinjarra"
    You are planning a solo flight from Murrayfield and three questions come up.

    1. *Am I, as an RPL holder without a navigation endorsement, allowed to go that far?* This is about your licence privileges, so the answer is in **CASR Part 61** (summarised in the VFRG).
    2. *A friend mentioned the strip has works on the runway this month. Is that so?* This is a temporary change, so you check the **NOTAMs** for the aerodrome; the ERSA entry would not yet show it.
    3. *Is the aeroplane I have booked due for its 100-hourly before I get back?* This is about the aircraft, so the answer is on its **maintenance release**.

    Three questions, three different documents, chosen by asking what kind of fact each one needs.

??? check "Check yourself: an ERSA entry shows a runway as available, but a NOTAM issued yesterday says it is closed. Which do you follow, and why?"
    The NOTAM. ERSA is updated on a fixed cycle; the NOTAM is how temporary or urgent changes are published in between, so it is the more recent and authoritative information.

## The maintenance release

You have checked that *you* may fly. The other half of the question is whether the *aeroplane* may. You cannot inspect its engine overhaul history, its airworthiness directives or its last 100-hourly from the walk-around. Someone qualified has done that work, and the **maintenance release** is how they tell you about it.

The maintenance release is the document that **certifies the aircraft is airworthy and may be flown for a stated period**, usually **100 hours or 12 months**, and it **travels with the aircraft** (CAR 1988 Part 4A; verify current text). It is issued by a **licensed aircraft maintenance engineer** after the required inspection. Think of it as the aeroplane's equivalent of your logbook and medical combined: it says what has been done, when the next work is due, and whether anything is wrong with it today.

![How do you know the aeroplane is serviceable today?](diagram:airworthiness-documents-flow "The maintenance release is the key document: current in hours and date, daily inspection certified today, no grounding defect, nothing due before you land.")

### What it shows

The maintenance release has three kinds of content, and each one answers a different pre-flight question:

- **The period and the hours** for which it is valid, and the **maintenance that falls due** within that period with the time or date it is due. This answers: *is the release still current, and is anything due before I land?*
- **The daily inspection certifications and time-in-service record (Part 3)**, one line for each day the aircraft flies. This answers: *has today's daily inspection been done and signed?*
- **The defect endorsements (Part 2)**: defects found, who recorded them, and the engineer's clearance or the **permissible unserviceability** endorsement. This answers: *is there anything wrong with the aircraft, and has it been fixed or approved to fly as it is?*

![What does a maintenance release show, and where?](diagram:maintenance-release-anatomy "What to notice: the validity period and maintenance due, the Part 2 defect endorsements and the Part 3 daily inspection record each answer one pre-flight question.")

A **permissible unserviceability** is a defect that an authorised person has assessed and endorsed as acceptable for flight, often with conditions (for example, a failed cabin light, with no night flight). It is not the same as a defect that has simply been written up and ignored.

### How the pilot uses it

**Before every flight** the pilot in command reads the maintenance release and confirms four things:

1. the aircraft is **within its hours and dates**: the release has not expired on either count;
2. **no maintenance is overdue**, and none will fall due before the end of the planned flight;
3. the **daily inspection is signed for today**;
4. any **endorsed defect has been cleared**, or is a **permitted unserviceability** whose conditions you can meet.

**After flight** the pilot **records the flight time** and **endorses any defect found**. Recording the time is what keeps the hours on the release accurate for the next pilot; endorsing the defect is what stops the next pilot taking off in an aeroplane with a problem you already know about.

The consequence is blunt: an aircraft with an **expired maintenance release**, **overdue maintenance**, or an **uncleared defect that affects safety** is **not airworthy and must not fly**. No amount of urgency, and no instructor's assurance, changes that. You will see what a licence holder may sign on the release, and how defects are endorsed, in [RFRC 2.2](/notes/RFRC/2.2).

!!! example "Worked example: can this aeroplane fly today?"
    You arrive for a 1.5 hour flight. The maintenance release shows:

    - issued at 3,812.4 hours, valid to 3,912.4 hours or to 14 March next year, whichever comes first;
    - today's date is in October, and the hour meter reads 3,909.6;
    - the daily inspection for today is signed;
    - Part 2 has one defect, "left landing light inoperative", endorsed by the engineer as a permissible unserviceability, day VFR only.

    1. Hours remaining: $3912.4 - 3909.6 = \mathbf{2.8\ hours}$. The planned 1.5 hours fits.
    2. Date: October is before 14 March, so the release is **current by date**.
    3. Daily inspection: **signed for today**.
    4. Defect: **endorsed as a permissible unserviceability**, and its condition (day VFR) is one you meet as a day VFR pilot.

    The aeroplane may fly. But note step 1: after your flight it will have about 1.3 hours left, so the next pilot booked for a two-hour navigation exercise cannot take it until it has had its inspection.

Now try one yourself. Same aeroplane, but the hour meter reads 3,911.1 and you are booked for a 1.5 hour flight. May you go?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    Hours remaining: $3912.4 - 3911.1 = 1.3$ hours, which is less than the 1.5 hours planned. The maintenance would fall due before the end of the flight, so you may not fly the planned exercise. You could fly a shorter flight that ends with time to spare, or wait until the inspection is done.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    "Valid for 100 hours or 12 months" means **whichever comes first**. A release issued eleven months ago with only 40 hours used is about to expire on the date, however few hours have been flown.

??? check "Check yourself: the release is current and the daily inspection is signed, but Part 2 has a defect with no engineer's clearance or permissible unserviceability endorsement. Can you fly?"
    Not if the defect affects safety. An endorsed defect that has been neither cleared nor approved as a permissible unserviceability leaves the aircraft unserviceable until a licensed engineer rectifies it or endorses it.

## Bringing it together

A Sunday morning at Jandakot. Before you leave home you check the NOTAMs and see that a restricted area near your training area is active, and you look up its hours in ERSA to plan around it; the CASR and the MOS already told you that you need a clearance to enter it. At the aeroplane you open the maintenance release: current in date, 6.2 hours left before the 100-hourly, today's daily signed, and Part 2 empty. You fly your hour of circuits and steep turns. After shutdown you write 1.0 hour in the maintenance release so the next pilot sees 5.2 hours remaining, then record the flight in your logbook: date, type and registration, yourself as pilot in command, Jandakot to Jandakot, 1.0 hour day single-engine in command. Three documents, each answering a question nobody else could answer for you.

??? check "Check yourself: after a flight you notice a cracked fairing. Which two documents do you write in, and what does each entry do?"
    The maintenance release, where you endorse the defect so the next pilot and the engineer know about it; and your logbook, where you record the flight time as evidence of your experience. The maintenance release tracks the aircraft, the logbook tracks you.

## Key points

- **2.1.1** The logbook is the legal evidence of your experience for licences, ratings, recency and flight reviews. Each entry: date, type and registration, pilot in command and your role, departure and arrival or route, flight time split day and night, single or multi-engine, dual, solo and in command, instrument and simulator time, the nature of the flight and any test or endorsement signed. Record it as soon as practicable; falsifying it is an offence.
- **2.1.2** Law: Act, then CASR (Part 61 licences, 67 medicals, 91 operations) and CAR (Part 4A maintenance release), then the MOS (Part 91 MOS: VMC, fuel, altimetry), then the CAOs. Information: AIP (GEN, ENR, AD, with SUP and AIC), ERSA, DAP, charts and NOTAM, from Airservices. Guidance, not law: ACs and the VFRG, which is permitted in the exam.
- **2.1.3** The maintenance release certifies the aircraft airworthy for a period, usually 100 hours or 12 months, whichever first. Before flight: current in hours and date, nothing overdue, daily inspection signed for today, defects cleared or permissible. After flight: record the time, endorse any defect. Expired, overdue or an uncleared safety defect: do not fly.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Know which document answers which question: VMC minima and fuel rules are in the Part 91 MOS and summarised in the VFRG; aerodrome data and PRD areas are in ERSA; licence privileges are in CASR Part 61; the aircraft's serviceability is in the maintenance release. When a question offers both a law and a summary of it, read whether it asks where the rule is set out (the law) or where to find it explained (the VFRG). Confirm the current regulation numbers before your exam, because CASA moves rules between the CAR and the CASR as the transition continues.
