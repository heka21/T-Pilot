## What the exam expects (RFRC 2.6)

- **2.6.1** Explain how controlled airspace differs from non-controlled airspace, and what each class means for a VFR aeroplane.
- **2.6.2** Name the documents that show where controlled airspace is, and explain how you tell whether a prescribed area is active or not.

## Why this matters

Fly south from Jandakot and you are under Perth's Class C steps; fly north-east and you pass near RAAF Pearce's military zone and restricted areas; head inland and you are soon in Class G with nobody controlling anything. The sky over the Perth basin is divided into volumes with different rules, and none of the boundaries are visible out of the window. As an RPL holder without the controlled airspace endorsement you must stay out of controlled airspace altogether, and every pilot must stay out of an active restricted area without a clearance. An **airspace infringement**, flying into controlled or restricted airspace without a clearance, is among the occurrences most often reported in Australia, and it usually comes from a pilot who did not know where the boundary was or did not check whether an area was active. The exam asks two things: what the difference between the classes means for you, and which document tells you where and when. This lesson answers both from first principles.

## Why airspace is divided at all

Picture the sky near a major airport on a weekday morning: airliners descending in cloud on instrument approaches, others climbing out at 250 knots, light aeroplanes on training flights, a helicopter on a news job. If every one of them relied on seeing the others, an airliner coming out of cloud at 2,000 ft would have seconds to spot a Cessna it had no idea was there. Near busy airports, **see and avoid** alone is not enough, so a controller takes on the job of keeping aircraft apart.

Away from the busy airports, the traffic is light, most of it is VFR, and the cost of controlling every flight would buy little safety. There, pilots keep themselves apart by looking out and talking on the radio.

That is the whole idea of airspace classification. **Controlled airspace** is where an **air traffic controller** issues **clearances** and **instructions**, and you may **not enter or operate without a clearance**. **Non-controlled airspace** is where there is **no clearance**: you fly by the rules of the air, broadcast on the appropriate frequency, and **avoid other traffic yourself**.

Two terms you will see on charts and in the AIP: a **CTA** (control area) is controlled airspace that starts at some height above the ground; a **CTR** (control zone) is controlled airspace that starts at the surface around a controlled aerodrome, so that aircraft taking off and landing there are inside it.

## The classes, one by one

Australia uses the ICAO classes, labelled by letter (AIP ENR 1.4; verify current text). The letters are not a ranking of importance; each one is a different package of what ATC does and what it requires from you. Think of each class as answering three questions for a VFR aeroplane: **do I need a clearance? who keeps me apart from other aircraft? what must I carry?**

| Class | Type | What it means for a VFR aeroplane |
|---|---|---|
| A | Controlled, high level (the upper airspace, generally from FL180 along the east coast and FL245 elsewhere; verify on the current charts) | IFR only; VFR not permitted |
| C | Controlled: around the major airports (Perth) and the en route airspace above Class E or G | A **clearance is required** before entry; ATC separates IFR from VFR and gives VFR traffic information about other VFR; a transponder and radio are required |
| D | Controlled: around regional towered aerodromes such as Jandakot | A **clearance is required**; ATC separates IFR from IFR and gives traffic information to VFR; the tower controls the circuit |
| E | Controlled for IFR, not for VFR | VFR flights do not need a clearance but must meet the VMC minima and must carry a serviceable transponder (AIP ENR 1.6; verify current text); IFR flights are controlled |
| G | **Non-controlled** | No clearance required; pilots are responsible for separation using lookout and radio; a flight information service and traffic information are available on request; CTAF procedures apply at non-controlled aerodromes |

Now the reasoning behind each row.

**Class A** is the high-level airspace where the airliners cruise. Everything there is IFR and fully separated, and a VFR aircraft relying on see and avoid at jet speeds has no place in it. VFR flight is not permitted.

**Class C** surrounds the major airports, such as Perth, and covers much of the en route airspace above Class E or G. It is the busiest airspace a light aeroplane is likely to enter, with fast IFR traffic mixing with VFR. So ATC takes on more: a **clearance is required**, ATC **separates IFR from VFR**, and gives VFR flights **traffic information about other VFR**. Because ATC needs to see you and talk to you to do this, a **transponder and radio are required**.

**Class D** is the airspace around **regional towered aerodromes such as Jandakot**. Traffic is mostly light aircraft at lower speeds, many of them in the circuit. A **clearance is still required**, and **the tower controls the circuit**: it tells you which runway, when to turn, and when you are cleared to land. ATC **separates IFR from IFR** and gives **traffic information to VFR**, but it does not separate VFR from VFR or from IFR; the tower tells you who is around and you keep your own spacing with what you can see.

**Class E** is the in-between class. It is **controlled for IFR, not for VFR**: IFR flights receive clearances and separation, but **VFR flights do not need a clearance**. They must meet the VMC minima (the larger cloud clearances you met in [RFRC 2.3](/notes/RFRC/2.3)) and should carry a transponder, so that ATC and the IFR traffic it is separating can see them.

**Class G** is **non-controlled**: **no clearance required**, and **pilots are responsible for their own separation** using lookout and radio. A **flight information service** and traffic information are **available on request**, but nobody is controlling you. At non-controlled aerodromes in Class G you use **CTAF procedures**, broadcasting your position and intentions on the common frequency. Most of WA's airspace below the en route steps is Class G, and most of your early flying will be in it.

![How do controlled and non-controlled airspace differ?](diagram:controlled-vs-non-controlled-at-a-glance "In controlled airspace ATC clears you and keeps traffic apart; in Class G there is no clearance and separation is up to you.")

!!! warning "Common trap"
    **Class E is controlled airspace**, even though a VFR flight needs no clearance to enter it. The exam likes to offer "Class E is non-controlled" as a distractor. **Class G is the only non-controlled class.**

??? check "Check yourself: in Class D, the tower clears you to land behind a Piper on final. Who is responsible for keeping you apart from the Piper?"
    You are. In Class D, ATC separates IFR from IFR and gives traffic information to VFR; it tells you about the Piper and sequences you, but keeping your spacing behind it is your job by sight.

## How the classes fit together in three dimensions

The classes are volumes, stacked and nested. Around a major airport like Perth, **Class C** starts at the **surface** in the control zone, then extends outwards in **steps**: rings of controlled airspace whose **lower limit rises with distance** from the airport, like an upside-down wedding cake. Under each step, between the ground and the step's lower limit, the airspace is usually **Class G**. You may fly there without a clearance, as long as you stay below the step.

Above the steps and away from the airports sits **Class E** in some areas, with **Class A** at high level. Around a regional tower such as Jandakot, a **Class D** control zone sits from the surface up to its published upper limit, often tucked beneath the Class C steps.

![How do the airspace classes fit together over a region?](diagram:airspace-cross-section "Controlled airspace sits above lower limits that step up with distance from the airport; below the steps you are in Class G and need no clearance.")

This is why the boundary you must watch is often **vertical**, not horizontal. Flying south from the Perth area, you can be in Class G at 1,500 ft with Class C starting at 2,500 ft above you. Climb through that lower limit without a clearance and you have infringed controlled airspace, even though you never crossed a line on the ground.

### Who may fly where

An **RPL holder needs the controlled airspace and controlled aerodrome endorsements before flying in Class C or D or at a towered aerodrome** (see [RFRC 2.2](/notes/RFRC/2.2)). Without them, your airspace is Class G and non-controlled aerodromes.

### Prohibited, restricted and danger areas

Alongside the classes are areas set aside for particular activities: the **prohibited, restricted and danger areas**, together called **PRD areas**, and other special-use airspace such as military operating areas.

- A **prohibited area** may not be entered at all.
- A **restricted area** is treated like controlled airspace: **you need the controlling authority's clearance to enter it when it is active**. Military flying, weapons ranges and rocket launches use them.
- A **danger area** warns of an activity that could be hazardous (for example parachuting or gunnery); you may enter, but it is your responsibility to know the risk.

![May you fly into a prohibited, restricted or danger area?](diagram:prd-areas-entry "What to notice: prohibited means never, restricted means only with a clearance when active, danger means you may enter but the risk is yours to know.")

??? check "Check yourself: you are below a Class C step whose lower limit is 3,500 ft, in Class G. Do you need a clearance, and what limits your climb?"
    No clearance is needed while you stay in Class G below the step. The step's lower limit caps your climb: you must stay below 3,500 ft unless you obtain a clearance to enter the Class C (and hold the endorsement to do so).

## Finding the boundaries: the charts

Since the boundaries cannot be seen, you find them on charts. The **documents that show controlled airspace** are:

- the **Visual Terminal Chart (VTC)**, at the largest scale, around the major airports;
- the **Visual Navigation Chart (VNC)**, for wider areas;
- the **En Route Chart Low (ERC-L)**;
- the **Terminal Area Chart**.

They show the **lateral boundaries** and **vertical limits** of each CTA step and the control zones. A label such as **"C LL 4500"** means **Class C with a lower limit of 4,500 ft**: the step begins at 4,500 ft and you are in Class G below it (unless another class sits there).

The **WAC shows no airspace**. It is a topographic chart for finding your way, and using it alone near Perth is how infringements happen. The **AIP ENR 2 and 3** sections and the **Designated Airspace Handbook** describe the airspace in words, which you rarely need as a VFR pilot but which are the legal definition behind the charts.

![What does each visual chart show?](diagram:chart-features-panel "The WAC shows terrain but no airspace; the VNC and VTC add controlled airspace, PRD areas and frequencies; the ERC-L shows airspace and routes with no terrain.")

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The WAC is a good navigation chart and it **shows no airspace at all**. An exam option offering the WAC for airspace boundaries is wrong every time.

## Is it active? Hours, ERSA and NOTAM

A chart shows **where** an airspace is. It does not tell you whether it is **in force today**. Many controlled zones and restricted areas operate only at certain times, and outside those times the rules change. So the second half of the job is checking **when**.

### Control zones at regional towers

**Control zones and Class D airspace at regional towers may only operate during the tower's published hours.** A regional tower is staffed for the busy part of the day, not all night. **Outside those hours the airspace reverts to Class G** (or Class E above), and the aerodrome becomes a **CTAF aerodrome**: you fly non-controlled procedures, broadcasting on the CTAF. The hours are in **ERSA** for the aerodrome, and they **may be changed by NOTAM**, for example for a staff shortage or a public holiday.

![What happens to a Class D zone when the tower closes?](diagram:class-d-tower-hours "Outside the tower's ERSA hours the zone becomes Class G (or E above) and the aerodrome a CTAF aerodrome.")

### Restricted and danger areas

**Restricted and danger areas** show their **hours of activity** in the **ERSA PRD section** and **AIP ENR 5.1**, in one of several forms: **H24** (always), **HJ** (sunrise to sunset), **specified UTC times**, or **by NOTAM** (active only when a NOTAM says so). **A NOTAM can activate, deactivate or change them.** If an area is active and you do not have a clearance, **you must stay out**.

**Temporary restricted areas**, set up for bushfires, air shows or VIP movements, are promulgated by **NOTAM** and sometimes by **AIP Supplement**. A bushfire in the Perth Hills in summer can create one overnight.

**Military control zones and restricted areas** around bases such as **RAAF Pearce** are active at the published hours and otherwise as notified.

### The pre-flight check

Put together, the check before every flight near controlled or restricted airspace is a sequence, each step answered by a different source:

1. **Chart** for the boundaries and limits: *where* is it?
2. **ERSA** for the hours and the controlling authority: *when* is it normally active, and *who* controls it?
3. **NOTAMs** for changes: is *today* different?
4. **If in doubt, call** the controlling authority or ATC on the published frequency.

![How do you find out whether airspace is active?](diagram:airspace-active-check "Chart for where, ERSA for when and who, NOTAM for today's changes, and a call to ATC if still unsure.")

![Which document answers which question about a restricted area?](diagram:flight-documents-flow "The chart shows where and how high; ERSA PRD and ENR 5.1 give the hours and authority; NOTAMs activate or change it; when in doubt, call.")

!!! example "Worked example: can you route through that restricted area on Saturday?"
    You plan a Saturday flight north-east of Perth. Your VNC shows a restricted area across your direct track.

    1. **Chart:** the VNC gives its boundary and its vertical limits, and its identifier. Your planned altitude is inside those limits, so it matters whether the area is active.
    2. **ERSA PRD section:** look up the identifier. Suppose the entry says the area is active **"by NOTAM"**, with the controlling authority named. That means it is inactive unless a NOTAM says otherwise.
    3. **NOTAMs:** check the area NOTAMs for Saturday. Suppose there is one activating it from 0100 to 0800 UTC. Converting with WA's UTC + 8 (see [BAKC 2.3](/notes/BAKC/2.3)), that is **0900 to 1600 WST**: active for your whole flight.
    4. **Decision:** without a clearance you **must stay out**. Plan a route around it, or a time outside the activation, or contact the controlling authority before the flight.

    Had there been no NOTAM, the "by NOTAM" entry would have meant the area was inactive and you could route through.

Now try one. On a Sunday you plan to land at a regional towered aerodrome at 1830 local, after its ERSA tower hours have ended for the day. No NOTAM changes the hours. What airspace will you be in, and which procedures do you use?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    Outside the tower's hours the Class D zone reverts to Class G (or Class E above), and the aerodrome becomes a CTAF aerodrome. You use non-controlled procedures: broadcast your calls on the CTAF and arrange your own separation. No clearance is needed, and an RPL holder without the controlled aerodrome endorsement could land there at that time.

??? check "Check yourself: a restricted area's ERSA entry says H24. A NOTAM says it is deactivated this weekend. Is it active on Saturday?"
    No. ERSA gives the standing hours, but a NOTAM can activate, deactivate or change a PRD area, and it is the more recent information. On Saturday the area is inactive.

## Bringing it together

You hold an RPL with the navigation endorsement but no controlled airspace endorsement, and you want to fly from Murrayfield to Northam and back. Out of the window there is nothing to tell you where you may fly; on the VTC and VNC there is everything. You trace your route; suppose it passes under Class C steps near the coast, so you plan an altitude comfortably below each step's lower limit, reading each "C LL" label in turn. North-east of Perth the chart shows the military zone and restricted areas around RAAF Pearce; you look up those near your track in the ERSA PRD section for their hours and controlling authority, then check the NOTAMs to see whether anything is active on the day. One is active by NOTAM during your planned times, so you route around it. Northam is a non-controlled aerodrome in Class G, so you plan CTAF calls there. The flight stays in Class G throughout, as your licence requires, and every boundary was found on paper before engine start.

??? check "Check yourself: which three sources together tell you where a restricted area is, when it is normally active, and whether today is different?"
    The chart (VTC, VNC or ERC-L) for where; ERSA's PRD section or AIP ENR 5.1 for the normal hours and controlling authority; and the NOTAMs for any change today.

## Key points

- **2.6.1** Controlled airspace (A, C, D, E): ATC issues clearances and separates some or all traffic; you need a clearance to enter (except VFR in Class E). Class C: clearance, IFR separated from VFR, transponder and radio. Class D: clearance, tower controls the circuit, traffic information to VFR. Class E: controlled for IFR, no clearance for VFR. Class G, the only non-controlled class: no clearance, separate yourself, information on request, CTAF at aerodromes. PRD areas sit alongside; an active restricted area needs a clearance.
- **2.6.2** VTC, VNC, ERC-L and Terminal Area Chart show the boundaries and limits ("C LL 4500" = Class C from 4,500 ft); the WAC shows none. ERSA gives tower hours and PRD hours (with AIP ENR 5.1); NOTAM changes them. Outside tower hours a Class D zone becomes Class G (or E above) and a CTAF aerodrome. Chart for where, ERSA for when and who, NOTAM for today, call if in doubt.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Class G is the only non-controlled class. Class E is controlled airspace, but VFR flights do not need a clearance in it. The WAC never shows airspace, and ERSA plus NOTAMs tell you whether an area is active. When a question asks "which document", decide whether it is asking where (a chart), when (ERSA) or what has changed (NOTAM). Confirm class boundaries and requirements against the current AIP ENR 1.4 and charts, because airspace around Perth is redesigned from time to time.
