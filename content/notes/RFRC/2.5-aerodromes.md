## What the exam expects (RFRC 2.5)

- **2.5.1** On a diagram of your training aerodrome, point out the movement area and its parts, and say what the taxiway, runway and helipad markings tell you.
- **2.5.2** On a circuit diagram, name the upwind, crosswind, downwind and base legs and locate the dead side.
- **2.5.3** Say what a white cross on the movement area means.
- **2.5.4** Recognise and explain runway, threshold and runway end markings, cone and gable markers, taxiway markings, holding points and bays, and two white crosses beside the primary wind indicator.

## Why this matters

An aerodrome talks to you in paint and shapes. At a busy field like Jandakot the markings tell you where you may taxi, where you must stop and where the landing area begins; at a country strip with no radio traffic and nobody in the clubhouse, the cones, gables and crosses may be the only information you get about which parts of a gravel runway are safe. Get them wrong and you taxi onto an active runway, land on a closed one, or touch down among gliders. The circuit, meanwhile, is the shared pattern that lets a dozen aircraft use one runway without a controller. The exam shows you diagrams and asks you to name what you see: a leg of the circuit, a marker, a cross. This lesson builds each one from the reason it exists, so the names follow.

## The parts of an aerodrome

Picture the aerodrome from above. There are places where aeroplanes park, places where they move about on the ground, and places where they take off and land. The vocabulary simply names those parts.

The **movement area** is the part of the aerodrome used for **take-off, landing and taxiing**. It has two parts:

- the **manoeuvring area**: the **runways and taxiways**, where aircraft move under their own power to and from take-off and landing;
- the **apron**: where aircraft **park, load and refuel**.

The distinction matters because the rules differ. On the apron, aircraft are parked, people walk about and fuel trucks move. On the manoeuvring area, everything is moving with a purpose, and at a controlled aerodrome you need a clearance to be there at all. The grass, buildings and roads around them are not part of the movement area.

When you look at your training aerodrome's diagram in ERSA, trace the movement area: find the apron, follow the taxiways out to each runway, and notice that the runways and taxiways together make up the manoeuvring area.

![Which parts are the movement area, the manoeuvring area and the apron?](diagram:movement-area-plan "What to notice: runways and taxiways make the manoeuvring area; add the apron and you have the movement area; grass, buildings and roads are neither.")

### Colour tells you what you are on

The single most useful fact about aerodrome paint is its colour. **Runway markings are white. Taxiway markings are yellow.** If the line under your nosewheel is yellow you are on a taxiway; if it is white, you are on a runway, and you should have a reason to be there. This colour rule is how pilots who taxi onto the wrong surface realise their mistake.

**Runway markings** describe the runway to an approaching aircraft:

- The **runway designator**: the magnetic direction of the runway to the nearest 10 degrees, with the last zero dropped, for example **24** for 240 degrees. Where there are parallel runways, a letter: **L, C or R** for left, centre or right, as seen by the approaching pilot. Jandakot's parallel runways carry L and R.
- The **centreline**: a dashed white line down the middle.
- The **threshold**, shown by the "**piano keys**": white stripes running along the runway at its start.
- The **aiming point** (two broad bars) and **touchdown zone** bars on larger runways.
- **Side stripes** marking the edges of the paved runway.

**Taxiway markings** guide you on the ground:

- a **continuous yellow centreline** to follow;
- **holding position markings**: **two solid and two dashed lines across the taxiway**. **Hold on the solid side.** The solid lines face you as you approach the runway, saying "stop here"; from the runway side the dashed lines let you cross freely when vacating;
- **edge markings** where needed, and painted guidance to parking.

A **helipad** is marked by a white or yellow letter **H** inside a circle or square. It is the touchdown area for helicopters: keep aeroplanes clear of it, because a helicopter may arrive vertically from above you.

Signs follow a colour code too: **red signs with white letters are mandatory** (runway holding positions, "NO ENTRY"); **yellow and black signs give information**: a **location sign** (yellow letters on black) names the taxiway you are on, and a **direction sign** (black letters on yellow, with an arrow) points to other taxiways and runways. Red means you must not pass without permission; yellow tells you where you are and where to go.

![What does a red sign mean compared with a yellow sign?](diagram:taxiway-signs "What to notice: the red sign stands at the runway holding position and you must not pass it without permission; yellow signs only tell you where you are and which way to go.")

!!! example "Worked example: reading the paint on the way to the runway"
    You taxi from the apron at your training aerodrome for runway 24R.

    1. Leaving the apron you pick up a **continuous yellow line**: you are on a **taxiway**, part of the manoeuvring area.
    2. A **direction sign** (black letters on yellow) shows an arrow towards "24R": **information**, telling you which way to go.
    3. Ahead, **two solid yellow lines followed by two dashed lines** cross the taxiway, beside a **red sign with white letters** reading "24R": a **runway holding position**. The solid lines face you, so you **stop before them**.
    4. Beyond the lines the paint turns **white**: the runway. You do not cross the solid lines until you are ready (and, at a controlled aerodrome, cleared).

    Each colour and shape told you something without a word on the radio.

??? check "Check yourself: you approach a holding position marking from the runway side after landing and see two dashed lines first. May you cross?"
    Yes. The dashed side faces the runway so that vacating aircraft can cross freely; you only stop when the solid lines face you, approaching the runway.

## The circuit and its legs

A runway can only be used safely by several aircraft at once if they all fly the same pattern, in the same direction, at the same height. That pattern is the **circuit**, and the rules that set it were covered in [RFRC 2.3](/notes/RFRC/2.3). Here the task is naming its parts.

**Standard circuits are left-hand**: every turn is to the left. The pattern is built from the take-off and landing direction, which is into wind. Taking off on runway 24 at a circuit height of 1,000 ft above aerodrome level, the legs are:

1. **Upwind (departure) leg**: straight ahead after take-off, climbing on runway heading. **No turn below 500 ft.**
2. **Crosswind leg**: the first turn, **90 degrees left**, still climbing, at or above 500 ft and usually reaching circuit height before the next turn. It runs across the wind, which gives it its name.
3. **Downwind leg**: **parallel to the runway in the opposite direction to landing**, at circuit height. The wind is behind you, hence the name. The downwind radio call is made here, and the **pre-landing checks** are done on this leg.
4. **Base leg**: **90 degrees left**, descending, flap set. The turn onto base is made when the **touchdown point is about 45 degrees behind the wing**.
5. **Final**: aligned with the runway, descending to land.

![Which leg is which in a left-hand circuit, and where is the dead side?](diagram:circuit-pattern "Follow the aeroplane: upwind, crosswind, downwind, base, final, every turn to the left. The dead side is the other side of the runway.")

The names describe the wind relative to each leg: you take off and land **into** wind, cross it on **crosswind**, have it behind you **downwind**. That is the whole logic, and it means you can rebuild the circuit for any runway: draw the runway, draw the arrow for the landing direction, and put every turn to the left of it.

![Fly the circuit in 3D and change the runway in use](widget:circuit-3d "The circuit is built from the landing direction: swap runways and the whole pattern flips so the turns stay to the left.")

### The dead side

The circuit lies on one side of the runway. The other side, where nobody is flying the pattern, is the **dead side**: the side of the runway **opposite the circuit direction**. For a **left-hand circuit** it is **to the right of the runway when looking along the landing direction**.

The dead side earns its name by being kept clear of circuit traffic, and that makes it the safe place to lose height when joining. Aircraft **joining from overhead** cross the aerodrome above circuit height, **descend on the dead side to circuit height**, and **cross over the upwind end to join crosswind**. That way the joining aircraft never descends through aircraft already flying the pattern.

![How do you join the circuit from overhead?](diagram:overhead-join "Cross overhead above circuit height, descend on the dead side, then cross the upwind end to join crosswind at circuit height.")

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The dead side depends on the circuit direction, not on a fixed side of the aerodrome. If a runway has published **right-hand** circuits, the dead side is on the **left** of the runway looking in the landing direction. Work it out from the pattern, never from memory of "the right side".

!!! example "Worked example: building the circuit for a different runway"
    The wind has swung and the runway in use is now 06, with standard left-hand circuits.

    1. Landing direction: 060 degrees, roughly north-east. **Final** points 060.
    2. Turns are left, so the circuit lies to the **left** of the runway looking along 060: on the **north-west** side.
    3. **Upwind** continues 060 past the far end; **crosswind** turns left to about 330; **downwind** runs 240, parallel and opposite to landing; **base** turns left to about 150; **final** left again onto 060.
    4. The **dead side** is the other side of the runway: the **south-east**.

    Compare runway 24 in the diagram: the whole pattern has swapped to the other side of the runway, which is exactly what the 3D widget shows when you change runways.

??? check "Check yourself: on which leg are the pre-landing checks normally done, and why there?"
    On the downwind leg. It is the longest straight, level part of the circuit before the descent, so there is time to complete the checks before workload rises on base and final.

## The white cross: do not use

A large **white cross** on a runway, taxiway or any part of the movement area means that area is **unserviceable** and **must not be used**. It is the aerodrome's way of saying "closed" in a language visible from 1,000 ft. Crosses at each end of a runway, or along it, close the runway; a cross on a taxiway closes that taxiway. A runway closed for a long period may have its markings **obliterated** as well, so it no longer looks like a runway from the air.

Why a cross, and why white? It can be read from the air in any direction, and it contrasts with both bitumen and grass. The meaning is absolute: there is no "use with caution" version.

??? check "Check yourself: a white cross is painted at the entrance of one taxiway leading to the runway. May you use that taxiway?"
    No. A white cross on any part of the movement area marks it unserviceable. Use another taxiway; the runway itself may still be open if it carries no cross.

## Markings and markers in detail

Sealed runways at towns and cities carry painted markings. Many country strips in WA are gravel or grass, where paint will not last, so they use **markers**: objects placed on the ground. Both systems answer the same questions: where does the usable runway begin and end, where are its edges, and where is the clear area around it?

### Threshold and runway end markings

The **threshold** is where the landing area begins. On a sealed runway it is marked by the **piano keys**, the white longitudinal stripes across the runway end. Sometimes the threshold is **displaced**: moved along the runway away from its physical end, usually because of an obstacle on the approach or weak pavement. A displaced threshold is shown by **arrows pointing to the displaced threshold line**. The paved area before it may be used for **take-off and roll-out but not for landing**: you must not touch down before the line, because the approach to it has been designed to clear the obstacle.

The **runway end** is where the usable runway finishes: shown by a **transverse stripe** or the **end of the side stripes** on a sealed runway, and on an unsealed strip by the **gable or cone markers at the ends**.

### Cones and gables

On an unsealed or unrated runway two kinds of white marker do two different jobs, and the exam tests whether you can tell them apart.

- **Cone markers**: white cones along the **edges of the runway (landing area)**, marking where the usable surface is. Land between the cones.
- **Gable markers**: white gable-shaped (roof-shaped) markers marking the **boundary of the runway strip**. The **runway strip** is the graded area around and beyond the runway that must be kept clear of obstacles, so that an aeroplane running off the side has a reasonable surface to stop on. **A gable marker is not a runway edge.**

So from the inside out: runway surface, then cones at its edges, then the strip, then gables at the strip's boundary.

![What do the runway markings and markers show?](diagram:runway-markings-and-markers "Runway markings are white, taxiway markings yellow; cones mark the runway edge, gables the strip boundary; a white cross means do not use.")

### Taxiway markings, holding points and holding bays

You have met the **yellow centreline and edge lines** and the **runway holding position** markings. There are also **intermediate holding positions**, marked by a **single dashed line**, where you may be asked to wait on a taxiway away from any runway, for example to give way to another aircraft at an intersection.

The **runway holding position** is where you **stop and wait before entering or crossing a runway**. It is placed far enough back that an aircraft waiting there is clear of aircraft using the runway. A **holding bay** is a wider area beside the taxiway where an aircraft can **wait or run up without blocking the taxiway**. At a busy aerodrome, the holding bay is where you do your engine run-up while others taxi past you to the runway.

| Marking | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| Runway markings | White: designator, centreline, side stripes, aiming point and touchdown zone bars where provided |
| Threshold markings | White piano keys at the start of the landing area; arrows to a displaced threshold line, before which you may take off and roll out but not land |
| Runway end markings | Transverse stripe or end of the side stripes; on an unsealed strip, gables or cones at the ends |
| Cone markers | Runway (landing area) edges on an unsealed or unrated runway |
| Gable markers | Boundary of the runway strip, not the runway edge |
| Taxiway markings | Yellow centreline and edges; runway holding positions (two solid, two dashed); intermediate holding positions (a single dashed line) |
| Holding points and bays | Stop before entering or crossing a runway; wait or run up clear of the taxiway |

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Gables are further out than cones. An exam option that says gable markers show the runway edge is the classic distractor: they show the edge of the **strip**, the cleared area around the runway, and landing out at the gables puts you off the prepared surface.

### The double white cross: gliding

One more cross, with a completely different meaning. **Two white crosses displayed beside the primary wind indicator** (the windsock) mean **gliding operations in progress**. Expect **gliders and tug aircraft in the circuit**, **winch cables** across the aerodrome, and glider operations on the ground. A glider cannot go around; a winch cable is nearly invisible from the air. The signal tells you to look for both before you join. Gliding operates at several WA aerodromes, so you may well meet it on a weekend navigation exercise.

The position is part of the meaning: a single white cross on the runway closes it, but crosses in the **signal area beside the windsock** tell you about **activity**, not about a closure.

### Other ground signals

The **primary wind indicator** (windsock) shows **wind direction and roughly its strength**, and is placed where it can be seen from the circuit. A **white dumb-bell** in the signal area means **use the sealed runways and taxiways only**. A **horizontal white landing T** shows the **landing direction** where no windsock applies. **Right-hand circuits** are shown in **ERSA** and, at some aerodromes, by a ground signal: a **right-hand turn arrow** in the signal area.

![What do the ground signals and the windsock tell you?](diagram:ground-signals "A single white cross closes an area; two white crosses by the windsock mean gliding; a dumb-bell means sealed surfaces only.")

??? check "Check yourself: overflying a country aerodrome you see a white dumb-bell in the signal area. What may you use?"
    Only the sealed runways and taxiways. The unsealed surfaces are not to be used, typically because they are soft or under works.

## Bringing it together

A navigation exercise to a gravel strip you have never visited. You arrive overhead above circuit height and look down before doing anything else. The windsock shows the wind favouring runway 32, and beside it in the signal area are two white crosses: gliding in progress. You scan for gliders and a winch line and spot a tug on the ground near the far end. No white dumb-bell, and no white cross on the runway, so the strip is open. The ERSA entry showed no right-hand circuit for 32, so the circuit is left-hand and the dead side is to the right of the runway looking along 320. You descend on the dead side to circuit height, cross the upwind end and join crosswind, then fly downwind, do your checks, and turn base with the threshold 45 degrees behind the wing. On final you aim between the white cones, not out at the gables marking the strip boundary. After landing you taxi to the parking area, keeping clear of the glider launch point. Every marker you saw answered a question before you needed to ask it.

??? check "Check yourself: why does an overhead join descend on the dead side rather than straight down onto downwind?"
    The dead side has no circuit traffic, so you can lose height to circuit height without descending through aircraft already flying the pattern. You then join crosswind level at circuit height, where the other traffic can see you.

## Key points

- **2.5.1** Movement area = manoeuvring area (runways and taxiways) + apron. Runway paint is white (designator to the nearest 10 degrees with L, C, R; centreline; piano keys; aiming point; side stripes); taxiway paint is yellow (centreline; holding positions, hold on the solid side). H in a circle or square is a helipad. Red signs are mandatory, yellow signs inform.
- **2.5.2** Left-hand unless published: upwind straight ahead to at least 500 ft, crosswind, downwind parallel and opposite to landing, base with the threshold 45 degrees behind the wing, final. The dead side is opposite the circuit: descend there when joining from overhead.
- **2.5.3** A white cross on any part of the movement area: unserviceable, do not use.
- **2.5.4** Piano keys start the landing area; arrows to a displaced threshold mean take-off and roll-out but no landing before the line; end stripe or end markers finish it. Cones mark the runway edge, gables the strip boundary. Holding position: stop before a runway; holding bay: wait or run up clear of the taxiway. Two white crosses by the windsock: gliding.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Cones mark the runway edge; gables mark the strip boundary. A white cross means unserviceable; a double white cross by the windsock means gliding. The dead side is opposite the circuit side. Most questions show a diagram: name what you see by asking what job it does (an edge, a boundary, a closure, a warning) and the label follows. Confirm marker conventions against the current AIP AD 1.1 and the workbook figures, which the exam's diagrams are drawn from.
