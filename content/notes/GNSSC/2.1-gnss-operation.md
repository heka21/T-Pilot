## What the exam expects (GNSSC 2.1)

- **2.1.1** Explain how GNSS works, what limits its performance and what errors it suffers, covering: (a) how it fixes position; (b) the operating habits that guard against navigational error and loss of situational awareness; (c) how to do typical navigation tasks on a particular receiver; (d) how the receiver shows that you have passed a waypoint; (e) the operational and serviceability checks; (f) the human factors limitations of using it; (g) the rules that apply to the pilot and the equipment.

## Why this matters

Almost every aeroplane you will fly from Jandakot has a GNSS receiver in the panel or a tablet on your knee, and on a cross-country to Albany or Geraldton it will show you a moving map, your groundspeed and an ETA more accurately than you could ever work them out. That is exactly the danger. The receiver is only as good as the satellites it can see, the database it carries and the waypoints you typed in, and it gives no warning when you have typed the wrong one. Pilots who let the screen do the navigating lose track of where they are, and when the unit fails, or quietly leads them towards a restricted area or a wall of cloud, they have no plan to fall back on. The exam tests the principles (how many satellites, what RAIM does, what DOP means), the procedures (checks, waypoint passage, cross-checking) and the human factors, and it expects the VFR answer: GNSS supplements visual navigation, it does not replace it.

## How a receiver finds its position

GNSS, the **global navigation satellite system**, is the generic name. The American **GPS** is the system most aviation receivers use; Galileo (Europe), GLONASS (Russia) and BeiDou (China) are other constellations, and many modern receivers use more than one. GPS has at least **24 satellites** in six orbital planes about **20,200 km** up, arranged so that a receiver anywhere on Earth normally sees at least five or six of them.

### Measuring a range with a clock

Each satellite carries an extremely accurate atomic clock and broadcasts, continuously, two things: **where it is** (its orbit data, the **ephemeris**) and **what time it is**. The receiver notes when each message arrives and compares that with the time stamped on it. The difference is the signal's travel time. Radio waves travel at the speed of light, about 300,000 km per second, so travel time multiplied by that speed gives the distance to the satellite.

!!! example "Worked example: how fine the timing has to be"
    Given: a satellite 20,200 km away; speed of light about 300,000 km per second.

    1. Travel time: $20{,}200 \div 300{,}000 = \mathbf{0.067\ s}$, about 67 thousandths of a second.
    2. Range error from a timing error of one millionth of a second: $300{,}000 \times 0.000001 = 0.3\ \text{km} = \mathbf{300\ m}$.

    A clock that is out by one millionth of a second puts you 300 m out. The satellites' atomic clocks can manage that; the cheap quartz clock in your receiver cannot. That one fact explains why a receiver needs a fourth satellite.

### Trilateration: spheres that meet at a point

One range tells the receiver it is somewhere on a sphere of that radius centred on the satellite. A second range from another satellite gives a second sphere, and the two intersect in a circle. A third sphere cuts that circle at two points, one of which is far out in space or deep inside the Earth and can be thrown away. So **three ranges** would fix a three-dimensional position: latitude, longitude and height. Finding a position from distances like this is called **trilateration** (from ranges, not angles, which would be triangulation).

### The fourth satellite: the receiver's clock

Three would be enough only if the receiver's clock were perfect, and it is not. The receiver's clock error is the same for every satellite it listens to, so every range is wrong by the same amount. Because each range contains this error, it is called a **pseudorange**. With an unknown clock error there are four unknowns, latitude, longitude, height and receiver clock error, and four unknowns need four measurements. The receiver adjusts its own clock until the four spheres meet at a single point, and in doing so becomes as accurate as the atomic clocks in the sky.

![How do satellite ranges fix a position?](diagram:gnss-trilateration "What to notice: the receiver clock error makes every range wrong by the same amount, so the ranges miss; one extra satellite lets the receiver solve for its clock and make them meet: four for a 3D fix.")

**Four satellites give a 3D fix**: three for position, one for the receiver clock. With only three in view, many receivers fall back to a **2D fix**, assuming a height and solving for latitude and longitude only, which is less reliable.

### RAIM: a fifth satellite to check the others

Four satellites give a position but no way of knowing whether one of them is lying, for example a satellite with a clock fault broadcasting a wrong time. **RAIM**, **receiver autonomous integrity monitoring**, uses extra satellites to check. With **five** in view the receiver can work out several different four-satellite solutions and compare them; if they disagree, one satellite is faulty, and the receiver warns you. That is **fault detection**. With **six**, it can work out which satellite is the culprit and leave it out, carrying on with a good position. That is **fault detection and exclusion** (**FDE**).

When RAIM cannot do its job, because too few satellites are in view or their geometry is poor, the receiver gives a **RAIM not available** or **loss of integrity** message. The position may still be good, but nothing is checking it.

![How many satellites does each job need?](diagram:gnss-satellite-count "What to notice: 3 gives only a 2D fix, 4 a 3D fix, 5 lets RAIM detect a fault and warn you, 6 lets it exclude the faulty satellite (FDE).")

??? check "Check yourself: why does a GNSS receiver need four satellites for a 3D fix when three spheres meet at a point?"
    Because the receiver's clock is not accurate enough, so every range contains the same unknown clock error. That makes four unknowns (latitude, longitude, height and clock error), and solving for four unknowns needs four ranges.

??? check "Check yourself: what is the difference between what five satellites and six satellites give a RAIM receiver?"
    Five let it detect that one satellite is faulty, because the solutions disagree, so it can warn you. Six let it identify the faulty satellite and exclude it, so it can keep navigating with a trustworthy position (fault detection and exclusion).

## Errors and limitations

GNSS is very accurate, but the accuracy is not automatic. Every error source in the table below enters the same way: it makes a measured range a little wrong, so the spheres no longer meet exactly where the receiver is.

| Error | Cause |
|---|---|
| Ionospheric and tropospheric delay | The signal slows passing through the atmosphere; the largest natural error |
| Satellite clock and ephemeris | Small errors in the satellite's time or reported position |
| Multipath | Signals reflected off terrain, buildings or the airframe arrive late |
| Receiver noise | Electronic limits of the receiver |
| **Dilution of precision (DOP)** | Poor satellite geometry (satellites bunched together) magnifies all the other errors |
| Masking | Wings, terrain or the cockpit block satellites, especially in turns |
| Interference and jamming | GNSS signals are very weak; jamming or interference can cause loss of navigation |

**Atmospheric delay** is the biggest natural error because the receiver assumes the signal travelled at the speed of light all the way, but in the charged upper layer of the atmosphere (the ionosphere) and in the moist lower layer (the troposphere) it travels slightly slower. A slower signal takes longer, and longer looks like further away. **Multipath** works the same way: a reflected signal has taken a longer route.

**Dilution of precision** is the one that most needs a picture. Imagine fixing your position from two landmarks. If they are 90 degrees apart, a small error in either bearing moves your fix a little. If they are almost in line, the two position lines cross at a shallow angle, and the same small error slides the fix a long way along them. Satellites behave the same way. Spread across the sky, their range spheres cross cleanly and the errors stay small. Bunched together in one part of the sky, the spheres cross at shallow angles and every range error is magnified. A **high DOP** means poor geometry and a less accurate fix, **even when plenty of satellites are in view**.

![Why does satellite geometry change accuracy?](diagram:gnss-dop-geometry "What to notice: the same range error gives a small fix area when the satellites are spread out (low DOP) and a long thin one when they are bunched (high DOP).")

**Masking** is GNSS's version of line of sight. The antenna on top of the cabin cannot see a satellite low on the side of the lowered wing in a steep turn, nor one behind a ridge in a valley. The receiver may briefly drop below the number of satellites it needs, and RAIM may drop out first because it needs more.

**Interference and jamming** follow from the signal's weakness: it has travelled about 20,200 km from a transmitter of modest power and arrives far below the background radio noise. A faulty electronic device in the cabin, or deliberate jamming (NOTAMs warn of military GNSS testing), can drown it out and stop navigation altogether.

### Augmentation: SBAS and SouthPAN

An **SBAS** (satellite-based augmentation system, such as WAAS in the USA) uses ground stations at known positions to measure the errors, then broadcasts corrections and integrity information from geostationary satellites. Australia and New Zealand are rolling out **SouthPAN**; check the current status and whether its aviation (safety-of-life) service is approved for use before relying on it. Without it, Australian aviation receivers rely on RAIM for integrity.

### Height from GNSS is not altitude

The receiver solves for a height, but it is **geometric height above the ellipsoid**, a mathematical model of the Earth's shape, not pressure altitude. Your altimeter, the airspace steps and every other aeroplane's altitude are pressure-based. **Never use GNSS altitude in place of the altimeter** for vertical separation or terrain clearance.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    "More satellites in view means a more accurate fix" is only half true. Accuracy depends on geometry as well as number. Six satellites bunched in one quarter of the sky can give a worse fix (high DOP) than five spread evenly across it.

??? check "Check yourself: in a steep turn the receiver briefly shows a RAIM warning, then it clears when you roll level. What happened?"
    Masking. The wing and cabin blocked one or more satellites on the low side, so for a moment there were not enough in view, or the geometry was too poor, for RAIM to check the position. Rolling level restored the view.

## Using GNSS without losing the picture

The receiver will draw a magenta line and a little aeroplane on it with total confidence whether the waypoints are right or wrong. The safeguards in this section all aim at one thing: you should always know where you are without the receiver, so that a wrong answer from it stands out at once.

**Plan the flight on a chart first.** Draw the tracks, measure the distances, choose the visual fixes and work out the headings and times. GNSS confirms the plan; it does not replace it. A route you have planned yourself is one you can recognise when the receiver disagrees with it.

**Check each waypoint against the plan.** Check each waypoint's coordinates, and the track and distance between waypoints, against the flight plan. A typing error does not show on the screen as an error; it shows as a confident line to the wrong place.

!!! example "Worked example: what one wrong digit does"
    Given: you intend to enter a waypoint at S31°40.0′ and type S32°40.0′, with the longitude correct. One degree of latitude is 60 nm; one minute is 1 nm.

    1. Latitude error: $32°40' - 31°40' = \mathbf{1°}$.
    2. Position error: $1 \times 60 = \mathbf{60\ nm}$, due south of where you meant.

    The leg on the screen now has the wrong track and the wrong distance. Compared with the 30 nm leg on your plan, it would stand out at once if you checked; if you did not, the receiver will guide you accurately towards a point 60 nm from your destination.

Now try one before opening the solution: your plan shows a leg of 245 degrees magnetic and 34 nm. The receiver shows the same leg as 065 degrees and 34 nm. What is the most likely mistake?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    The two waypoints are in the reverse order. The distance is identical and the track is the reciprocal: $245 - 180 = 065$. Re-order the flight plan (or check that you activated the right leg) before departure.

**Cross-check against the ground and other aids.** At every planned fix, compare what the receiver says with what you see: the town, the railway, the lake. A gross error almost always shows as a mismatch with the ground long before it becomes dangerous. Where the aeroplane has them, VOR and NDB give an independent check.

**Keep the chart open and follow your progress on it.** Mark the time at each fix. If the receiver fails or warns you, you are not starting from nothing: you know where you were ten minutes ago and the heading you have flown since, which is all dead reckoning needs.

**Act on integrity messages.** Monitor RAIM and integrity messages, and act on any "loss of integrity" or "dead reckoning" alert by reverting to other navigation. A receiver in dead reckoning mode has lost its fix and is extrapolating from the last known position, track and groundspeed, and its display may look almost normal.

??? check "Check yourself: the receiver shows you on track, but the lake that should be 3 nm to your left is nowhere to be seen. What do you do?"
    Believe the ground and the chart until the conflict is resolved. Fix your position visually, then check the active waypoint's coordinates and the leg on the receiver. A confident display is not a correct one: a wrong waypoint or a lost fix gives an on-track picture of the wrong route.

## Typical navigation tasks on the receiver

Every receiver is different, and the exam element says "a specific type of aircraft equipment" because you are expected to learn the one you fly from its pilot guide, on the ground, before you fly with it. The functions, though, are common to almost all units.

- **Direct-To** (the D→ key): select a waypoint and the receiver gives you a direct track to it from where you are now. Useful for diversions. On many units it replaces or suspends the active flight plan, so know what yours does.
- **Flight plan**: enter the route waypoints in order, check the track and distance of each leg against your plan, and activate the leg you are flying.
- **OBS or hold mode**: suspends **auto-sequencing**, the receiver's habit of switching to the next leg when you reach a waypoint, so that you can fly a selected course to or from a waypoint, as you would with a VOR.
- **Nearest**: lists the nearest aerodromes with bearing and distance, the most valuable page in an emergency or when weather closes in.
- **The data fields**: groundspeed, ETA and **cross-track error** (XTK), the distance you are left or right of the desired track, let you compare planned and actual progress.

The receiver measures where you have been, not where the nose is pointing, so the track and groundspeed it shows are over the ground. That is useful: the difference between your heading and the GNSS track is your drift, and the difference between your TAS and the GNSS groundspeed is your wind component, both read directly.

![Why can the GNSS track differ from your heading?](diagram:heading-track-drift "The receiver shows the track over the ground; the nose points along the heading, and the angle between them is the drift caused by the wind.")

## Knowing you have passed a waypoint

On a visual navigation leg you know you have reached a fix because you see it beneath you. The receiver has its own set of signs, and you should recognise each one:

- a **waypoint alert** message as you approach (arriving);
- the **TO/FROM** indicator changing from TO to **FROM**;
- the **distance** to the waypoint counting down to zero and then starting to count up;
- the receiver automatically **sequencing** to the next leg, so the desired track changes.

Some units use **turn anticipation**: they start the turn before the waypoint so that you roll out on the next leg rather than overshooting it, and the sequencing happens slightly early. In OBS mode there is no automatic sequencing, so the TO/FROM flag and the distance are your indications, and the receiver will keep you on the old course until you change it.

![What does the receiver show as you pass a waypoint?](diagram:gnss-waypoint-passage "What to notice: alert on approach, distance down to zero, TO flips to FROM; auto-sequencing switches to the next leg (early with turn anticipation), OBS mode never does.")

## Checks before and during flight

**Before flight**, on the ground with the engine running and the avionics on:

- the **self-test** has passed;
- the **database is current**: an expired database may contain wrong or missing waypoints and airspace;
- the satellite status page shows a **3D fix**;
- the displayed position agrees with your known position on the apron;
- the settings are correct: units, CDI scaling, and the map datum, **WGS-84**.

The database check matters because airspace, frequencies and aerodromes change every few months, and the receiver will present an out-of-date boundary with the same confidence as a current one. The datum check matters because coordinates on a different datum describe a different point on the ground.

**In flight**: watch for integrity alerts, check the fix quality, and cross-check with visual fixes.

IFR operations require a **RAIM prediction** (the AIP sets the requirement), a forecast of whether enough satellites in good geometry will be available along the route. VFR pilots should still check **NOTAMs for GNSS outages** and interference testing before relying on the receiver.

## The human factors trap

GNSS is accurate and easy, and those are exactly the qualities that make it hazardous to people.

**Automation complacency** comes first. A display that is right a thousand times in a row trains you to stop checking it. You stop looking out for the fixes, stop timing the legs, and become a passenger following a line.

**Head-down time** is the physical cost. Programming a flight plan or a diversion in flight takes your eyes inside for many seconds at a time, in the very airspace where a lookout matters. Programme on the ground; in flight, keep entries short and break them up with a lookout scan.

**Data entry errors** are inevitable. One wrong letter in an identifier or one wrong digit in a coordinate can give a waypoint hundreds of miles away, as the worked example showed.

**Mode confusion** is not knowing what the receiver is doing: OBS mode left on so it never sequences, or a Direct-To that has dropped the flight plan you thought was active.

**Loss of situational awareness** follows when the unit fails and you have no independent idea of where you are, and over time **skills fade** if GNSS is always used: map reading and dead reckoning are skills that need practice.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The exam's "best practice" answers are never "trust the receiver" or "programme it in flight to stay current". They are: plan on the chart, programme and check on the ground, cross-check visually at every fix, and be ready to continue without it.

## The rules for pilots and equipment

For most private VFR flying, GNSS (including a non-approved handheld) is an **aid** to visual navigation, not a sole means. You must still be able to fix your position by visual reference to the ground or water, and be ready to continue if the receiver fails. When operating at or below **2,000 ft** above ground or water, Australian VFR rules expect navigation by visual reference rather than reliance on GNSS.

The exact conditions under which GNSS may be the primary means of navigation for VFR (an approved receiver, pilot training, position checks) are in the VFRG navigation section, the Part 91 MOS and AIP ENR 1.1; check the current text rather than relying on memory. GNSS used as a primary means, and for IFR, requires an **approved (TSO-certified) installation** and **pilot training and recency** as Part 61 requires. Check AIP GEN 1.5 for the equipment standards.

The logic behind the rules is the logic of this whole lesson. A handheld unit has no certified integrity monitoring, its antenna may be masked inside the cabin, and its battery and cable can fail. An approved installation has been tested to a standard and monitors its own integrity, and a trained pilot knows its modes and warnings. The more you rely on GNSS, the more of both the rules require.

## Bringing it together

You plan a VFR flight from Jandakot to Narrogin with a tablet and a panel GNSS. At home you draw the route on the VNC, pick the fixes, and work out headings and times. You check NOTAMs and find a GNSS interference test in the north of the state, nowhere near your route. At the aeroplane you confirm the database is current, the receiver has a 3D fix and shows you on the right apron, and you enter the flight plan, checking each leg's track and distance against your log. In flight, at each fix you compare the receiver with the ground and the clock. Over the hills east of Perth a RAIM warning appears in a turn and clears when you roll level: masking. Twenty minutes later the panel unit shows "loss of integrity" and does not clear. You note the time, confirm your position against a town and a railway on the chart, and continue on heading and time as planned, using the receiver only as a cross-check. At Narrogin you arrive on time, because the plan you made on the chart never depended on the receiver.

??? check "Check yourself: a VFR pilot with a handheld receiver loses the signal at 1,500 ft above ground. What should already be in place, and why?"
    A position fixed on the chart and the planned heading and time to the next fix, because the handheld is only an aid and the pilot must be able to navigate by visual reference, especially low down. With those, the loss is an inconvenience rather than an emergency.

## Key points

- **2.1.1 (a)** Position by trilateration from pseudoranges: four satellites for a 3D fix (three for position, one for the receiver clock), five for RAIM fault detection, six to exclude a faulty satellite. Errors: atmospheric delay (largest natural), clock and ephemeris, multipath, receiver noise, masking, interference; poor geometry (high DOP) magnifies them all. GNSS height is not pressure altitude.
- **2.1.1 (b)** Plan on the chart first, check every waypoint's coordinates and leg against the plan, cross-check visually at each fix, keep the chart open, and revert on any integrity or dead reckoning alert.
- **2.1.1 (c)** Know your receiver: Direct-To, flight plan, OBS or hold, Nearest, and the groundspeed, ETA and XTK fields.
- **2.1.1 (d)** Waypoint passage: waypoint alert, TO flips to FROM, distance counts down then up, the receiver sequences to the next leg (earlier with turn anticipation, not at all in OBS).
- **2.1.1 (e)** Before flight: self-test, current database, 3D fix, correct position on the apron, correct settings and WGS-84 datum. In flight: integrity, fix quality, visual cross-checks. Check NOTAMs for outages.
- **2.1.1 (f)** Complacency, head-down time, data entry errors, mode confusion, loss of situational awareness and fading skills.
- **2.1.1 (g)** For private VFR, GNSS is an aid to visual navigation; primary-means and IFR use need an approved installation and pilot training and recency (VFRG, Part 91 MOS, AIP ENR 1.1 and GEN 1.5; verify the current text).

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Four satellites for a 3D fix, five for RAIM fault detection, six to exclude a faulty satellite. Poor geometry (high DOP) increases error even when plenty of satellites are in view. For the procedure and human factors questions, choose the answer that keeps you navigating visually with GNSS as the cross-check, and remember that a current database and a position check on the ground come before every flight.
