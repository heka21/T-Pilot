## What the exam expects (BAKC 2.4)

- **2.4.1** State the unit for vertical distance (feet), and explain the difference between height, altitude and elevation.
- **2.4.2** State the units used in Australia for runway dimensions, temperature, atmospheric pressure, weight, volume of liquids and visibility.

## Why this matters

Australian aviation measures in a mixture of units that grew up in different places: feet from British and American practice, metres and kilograms from the metric system, nautical miles and knots from the sea. In one pre-flight you will read a runway length in metres from ERSA, a cloud base in feet from the TAF, a QNH in hectopascals, a temperature in degrees Celsius, a fuel load in litres and a maximum take-off weight in kilograms. Each one sits in its own unit for a reason, and mixing them up has real consequences: fuel entered in litres where the weight and balance wants kilograms makes the fuel look almost 40% heavier on paper than it is (and kilograms entered as litres hide real weight the other way); a cloud base above the aerodrome read as above sea level puts you in cloud over Northam. The exam tests the units directly, and it tests the three vertical words, height, altitude and elevation, which are not interchangeable. This lesson gives each unit its job and shows the two conversions you will make on every flight.

## Vertical distance is measured in feet

Everything vertical in aviation is in **feet (ft)**: circuit height, cruising levels, cloud base, aerodrome elevation, obstacle heights on the chart, the altimeter itself. Feet were the unit of the countries that built the early aviation system, and the world has kept them for vertical distance so that a pilot and a controller anywhere share the same numbers. Australia follows that convention even though it is otherwise metric.

A useful size to have in your head: 1,000 ft, a typical circuit height, is about 300 m. Thousands are written with a comma, 1,000 ft, and in radio calls read as "one thousand".

## Height, altitude and elevation

Three words describe vertical distance, and they differ in **what you measure from** and **what you are measuring**. Picture an aeroplane flying the circuit at an aerodrome that sits on a low rise, with a ridge a few miles away.

**Altitude** is the vertical distance of the aeroplane above **mean sea level** (MSL), written **ft AMSL** or just "ft" on its own. It is what the altimeter shows when you have the local **QNH** set on its subscale, which is why QNH is the setting you use for flying below 10,000 ft in Australia. "Cruising at 4,500 ft" means an altitude.

**Elevation** is also measured above mean sea level, but it is the word for a point **on, or fixed to, the Earth's surface**: an aerodrome, a runway threshold, the top of a hill. An aerodrome's elevation is surveyed and printed in ERSA, and it never changes. "Aerodrome elevation 99 ft" is Jandakot's.

**Height** is the vertical distance above a **specified datum**, usually the ground or the aerodrome directly below. It is written **ft AGL** (above ground level), or "above aerodrome level". "Circuit height 1,000 ft" is a height: 1,000 ft above the aerodrome, wherever the aerodrome happens to be.

| Term | Measured from | Usually written | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Height** | A specified datum, usually the ground or the aerodrome directly below | ft AGL (above ground level) | "Circuit height 1000 ft AGL" |
| **Altitude** | Mean sea level (MSL) | ft AMSL, read on the altimeter with **QNH** set | "Cruising at 4500 ft" |
| **Elevation** | Mean sea level, for a point **on or fixed to the Earth's surface** | ft AMSL | "Aerodrome elevation 99 ft" |

So height and altitude describe where the **aircraft** is; elevation describes where the **ground** is. The three are linked by a single sum, which you will use on every circuit away from the coast:

$$\text{altitude} = \text{elevation of the ground below} + \text{height above that ground}$$

![What is the difference between height, altitude and elevation?](diagram:height-altitude-elevation "Altitude is measured from sea level, elevation is the ground's altitude, and height is what is left between them: 1300 ft altitude is 1000 ft above a 300 ft aerodrome but only 400 ft above a 900 ft ridge.")

!!! example "Worked example: height from altitude and elevation"
    Given: your altimeter, set to the local QNH, reads 1,300 ft while you fly the circuit at an aerodrome with an elevation of 300 ft. A ridge nearby has an elevation of 900 ft.

    1. Height above the aerodrome: $1300 - 300 = \mathbf{1{,}000}$ ft AGL, circuit height.
    2. Height above the ridge at the same altitude: $1300 - 900 = \mathbf{400}$ ft.

    The altimeter has not changed, but the ground under you has risen 600 ft. Altitude is fixed to sea level; height goes up and down with the terrain.

!!! example "Worked example: over an inland aerodrome"
    Given: you overfly an aerodrome of elevation 1,200 ft with the altimeter reading 2,500 ft on QNH.

    1. Height above the aerodrome: $2500 - 1200 = \mathbf{1{,}300}$ ft AGL.

    An altitude that would be a comfortable 2,500 ft above the beach at Rockingham is only 1,300 ft above this aerodrome: barely above circuit height, so you are in the way of circuit traffic.

Now turn it round, with the altitude as the unknown, before opening the solution. You arrive at a country aerodrome of elevation 650 ft and want to fly the circuit at 1,000 ft above it. What should the altimeter read?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    Altitude $=$ elevation $+$ height $= 650 + 1000 =$ **1,650 ft** on QNH. The circuit height is fixed above the aerodrome, so at every aerodrome the altimeter figure is the elevation plus 1,000.

### The altimeter and the datum

The altimeter is a barometer with a scale in feet. Whatever pressure you set on its subscale becomes its zero. Set the local QNH and it reads altitude above mean sea level; set the standard pressure, 1013.2 hPa, and it reads **pressure height**, used for performance calculations in [BAKC 6.2](/notes/BAKC/6.2). The instrument has no idea what is under it, which is why height above terrain always needs the elevation from the chart.

![Explore what the subscale setting does to the reading](widget:altimeter-subscale "Each hectopascal of difference between the subscale and the real QNH moves the reading about 30 ft.")

For completeness: a **flight level** is a pressure altitude, the altimeter reading with **1013.2 hPa** set, expressed in hundreds of feet. So FL125 is 12,500 ft on the standard setting. Flight levels are used above the transition altitude, which you will meet in the altimetry and flight rules units.

### Cloud heights: which datum?

The difference between height and altitude matters most when you read cloud bases. In a TAF or METAR, cloud heights are **above the aerodrome**: heights. In the GAF (area forecast), they are **above mean sea level**: altitudes. At Jandakot, 99 ft above the sea, the two are nearly the same. At an aerodrome 1,000 ft up, a cloud base of 2,500 ft in the TAF is at 3,500 ft AMSL, and the same base in the GAF would read 3,500.

![Is the cloud base above sea level or above the aerodrome?](diagram:cloud-height-datum "The same cloud base reads lower in a TAF or METAR, which measure from the aerodrome, than in the GAF, which measures from sea level, by the aerodrome elevation.")

!!! warning "Common trap"
    "Elevation" is always for something attached to the ground. If the question is about where an aircraft is above sea level, the word is "altitude"; above the ground, "height". A distractor will offer "elevation" for the aircraft, or "height" for an altimeter reading on QNH.

??? check "Check yourself: you fly at 3,000 ft on QNH from the coast to an aerodrome of elevation 1,000 ft. What happens to your altitude and your height?"
    Your altitude stays at 3,000 ft, because it is measured from sea level and the altimeter reading has not changed. Your height falls from about 3,000 ft over the beach to 2,000 ft over the aerodrome, because the ground has risen 1,000 ft.

## The other units, one at a time

The second element is a list of six quantities, each with its unit. Rather than memorise the list, attach each one to where you meet it.

### Runway dimensions: metres

Runway lengths and widths are in **metres (m)**. ERSA lists each runway in the form "Runway 12/30, 1400 m × 30 m". Runways were surveyed and built under the metric system, and the take-off and landing charts in your POH and the CASA workbook compare the distance you need, in metres, with the distance available. A useful size: 1,000 m is about 3,300 ft, a little over half a nautical mile.

### Temperature: degrees Celsius

Air temperature, dew point and engine temperatures in Australian reports are in **degrees Celsius (°C)**. The METAR shows something like "28/12": temperature 28, dew point 12. The International Standard Atmosphere, which every performance calculation is built on, starts at 15 degrees Celsius at sea level. Some imported aeroplanes have engine gauges in Fahrenheit; the weather is always Celsius. The two temperature numbers in a METAR earn their place: the air temperature drives density and therefore take-off performance, and the gap between temperature and dew point tells you how close the air is to forming fog or cloud, and how likely carburettor ice is.

### Atmospheric pressure: hectopascals

Pressure is in **hectopascals (hPa)**: "QNH 1018". A pascal is a tiny unit of pressure, and a hectopascal is a hundred of them. One hectopascal equals one **millibar**, so older books that talk about millibars use exactly the same numbers. Standard sea-level pressure is **1013.2 hPa**, often rounded to 1013. Some imported aircraft have altimeters with a second subscale in inches of mercury; in Australia you set hPa.

### Weight: kilograms

Aircraft weights, the maximum take-off weight, passenger and baggage weights, and the weight and balance are all in **kilograms (kg)**: "maximum take-off weight 1111 kg". Strictly, a kilogram measures mass, not weight, but aviation uses "weight" for both and the exam does too. Weight is one of the numbers that moves everything else: the take-off distance, the climb rate and the stall speed all rise with it, which is why every kilogram in the cabin and the tanks is accounted for.

### Volume of liquids: litres

Fuel and oil are loaded and measured by volume, in **litres (L)**: "fuel on board 140 L". The bowser meter reads litres, the fuel gauges are calibrated in litres, and fuel consumption is quoted in litres per hour.

### Visibility: metres and kilometres

Visibility is reported in **metres** for lower values and **kilometres** for higher ones: "visibility 3000 m", "visibility 10 km". This matches [BAKC 2.2](/notes/BAKC/2.2). It is the odd one out from navigation distance, which is in nautical miles, so when you compare a visibility with a distance on the chart, convert: 1 km is 0.54 NM.

!!! example "Worked example: visibility in nautical miles"
    Given: one report says visibility 3,500 m; another says 5 km.

    1. 3,500 m is 3.5 km: $3.5 \times 0.54 = \mathbf{1.9}$ NM.
    2. 5 km: $5 \times 0.54 = \mathbf{2.7}$ NM.

    In 5 km visibility you will see a town on your track only once you are less than 3 NM from it: a minute and a half at 120 kt.

??? check "Check yourself: why is 1 hPa the same as 1 mb, and what does that mean for an old textbook that gives a QNH of 1015 mb?"
    The hectopascal and the millibar are the same size of unit under different names, so the number does not change: 1015 mb is 1015 hPa, and you set 1015 on the subscale.

## Litres and kilograms: converting fuel

Here two of the units have to talk to each other. The weight and balance is done in kilograms, but fuel goes in by the litre. To put fuel into a weight calculation you need the weight of one litre, which is the fuel's **specific gravity (SG)**: how heavy the liquid is compared with the same volume of water, which weighs 1 kg per litre. For avgas the SG is about **0.72**, so a litre of avgas weighs about 0.72 kg, and 100 L of avgas weighs about **72 kg**. Check the figure your flight manual uses, because the SG changes a little with temperature and fuel grade.

Getting this conversion wrong is not a paper error. In 1983 an airliner in Canada ran out of fuel in flight because the fuel load had been worked out in pounds where the aircraft's systems expected kilograms; it carried less than half the fuel the crew believed. The same kind of slip in a light aeroplane, litres treated as kilograms, either overloads the aeroplane on paper or leaves the tanks short.

The conversion is one multiplication or one division:

$$\text{kg} = \text{L} \times \text{SG} \qquad \text{L} = \text{kg} \div \text{SG}$$

![How do the units you will meet convert?](diagram:units-conversion-ladder "Read straight across each scale: 1 NM is 1.852 km, 110 kt is 204 km/h, and 100 L of avgas weighs about 72 kg.")

!!! example "Worked example: fuel from litres to kilograms"
    Given: 120 L of avgas on board, SG 0.72; later, full tanks of 180 L.

    1. 120 L: $120 \times 0.72 = \mathbf{86.4}$ kg.
    2. 180 L: $180 \times 0.72 = \mathbf{129.6}$ kg.

    That is the number that goes into the weight and balance. Entering "180" in the kilograms column would overstate the fuel by about 50 kg.

!!! example "Worked example: kilograms back to litres"
    Given: the weight and balance shows you can carry only 90 kg of fuel with your passengers on board. SG 0.72.

    1. Litres: $90 \div 0.72 = \mathbf{125}$ L.

    You tell the refueller 125 litres. Litres are always the bigger number for avgas, because a litre weighs less than a kilogram.

![Convert fuel between litres and kilograms](widget:fuel-litres-to-kg "Kilograms of avgas are always about three-quarters of the litres: multiply litres by 0.72, divide kilograms by 0.72.")

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Multiply litres by the SG to get kilograms; divide kilograms by the SG to get litres. If your answer in kilograms is bigger than the litres you started with, you divided when you should have multiplied.

??? check "Check yourself: why does fuel need converting at all before it goes into the weight and balance?"
    Because the weight and balance is in kilograms but fuel is loaded and measured by volume in litres. The SG, about 0.72 kg per litre for avgas, turns one into the other.

## Bringing it together

You are planning a solo flight from Jandakot to an inland strip of elevation 1,100 ft. ERSA lists its runway as 1,200 m long, metres. The METAR at the nearest reporting station gives 32/08: 32 degrees Celsius, a hot day, which will lengthen your take-off run. The QNH is 1009 hPa, which you will set so your altimeter reads altitude, and the TAF gives broken cloud at 3,000 ft, which is above the aerodrome, so the base is at about 4,100 ft AMSL; a cruise at 3,000 ft keeps you more than 1,000 ft below it. You will fly the circuit at the strip at 1,100 + 1,000 = 2,100 ft on QNH. You ask for 120 L of avgas; at an SG of 0.72 that is 86.4 kg in the weight and balance, comfortably under the maximum take-off weight in kilograms. The forecast visibility is 8 km, about 4.3 NM. Eight quantities, eight units, each in its own place.

## Key points

- **2.4.1** Vertical distance is in feet. Altitude is the aircraft above mean sea level (altimeter on QNH); elevation is a point on the ground above mean sea level; height is the aircraft above a datum, usually the ground (AGL). Altitude = elevation + height. A flight level is altitude on 1013.2 hPa in hundreds of feet.
- **2.4.2** Runway dimensions in metres; temperature in degrees Celsius; pressure in hectopascals (1 hPa = 1 mb, standard 1013.2); weight in kilograms; fuel and oil in litres; visibility in metres and kilometres. Avgas weighs about 0.72 kg per litre: multiply litres by SG for kilograms, divide kilograms by SG for litres.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    The split to remember: **feet** for anything vertical, **metres** for runways and visibility, **nautical miles** for navigation distance, **knots** for speed, **hPa** for pressure, **kg** for weight, **litres** for fuel and oil, **°C** for temperature. Expect one question on the three vertical words (the aircraft has altitude or height, the ground has elevation), a subtraction of elevation from altitude, and a fuel conversion with SG 0.72.
