## What the exam expects (BAKC 6.3)

- **6.3.1** Explain the speed and structural limits: the normal operating speed V<sub>NO</sub>, the never exceed speed V<sub>NE</sub>, the manoeuvring speed V<sub>A</sub>, the turbulence penetration speed V<sub>B</sub>, and the limit and design load factors.
- **6.3.2** Describe the situations in which an aeroplane can be pushed past its speed or load factor limits.

## Why this matters

On a hot afternoon descending from the hills towards Jandakot, the air over the Darling Scarp is often rough, and the aeroplane, nose down with cruise power still on, accelerates into the yellow arc almost without you noticing. Nothing breaks. But you are now flying in a region where a sharp gust could load the structure past what it was designed for, and the protection the designers built in depends on you knowing where that region starts. Speed limits are the line between flight the aeroplane was built for and flight it was merely not tested beyond. The exam asks you to define each V-speed, to know the load factors for the normal category, to explain why V<sub>A</sub> falls with weight, and to recognise the situations that break limits. All of those follow from one idea, that lift and gust loads grow with speed, so this lesson starts there.

## What actually breaks an aeroplane: load factor

The wing has to carry the weight of the aeroplane. In straight and level flight lift equals weight, so the wing carries exactly one weight's worth of load. Whenever the flight path curves upward, in a pull-up, a level turn or a sharp upward gust, the wing has to make more lift than the weight, and the spar, the wing attachments and everything bolted to them carry that extra load.

The **load factor**, written $n$, is the ratio of lift to weight:

$$n = \frac{\text{lift}}{\text{weight}}$$

It is measured in **g**. Level flight is 1 g. At 2 g the wing is making twice the weight in lift, and everything in the aeroplane, including you, feels twice as heavy. A load factor can also be negative, when the wing pushes downward, as in a sharp push-over.

The most common way to raise the load factor is a level turn. Banking tilts the lift; to keep the vertical part of it equal to the weight, total lift must grow (see [RBKA 3.5](/notes/RBKA/3.5)). The result depends only on the bank angle $\theta$:

$$n = \frac{1}{\cos\theta}$$

![Explore how bank angle sets the load factor and the stall speed](widget:bank-angle "Load factor creeps to 1.41 at 45 degrees, doubles by 60 and reaches the normal category limit near 75; stall speed rises with the square root.")

!!! example "Worked example: load factor in a level turn"
    Given: level turns at 45 degrees and at 60 degrees of bank.

    1. At 45 degrees: $\cos 45° = 0.707$, so $n = 1 \div 0.707 = \mathbf{1.41\ g}$.
    2. At 60 degrees: $\cos 60° = 0.5$, so $n = 1 \div 0.5 = \mathbf{2.0\ g}$.
    3. Going from 45 to 60 degrees adds 15 degrees of bank and **0.59 g**; the curve steepens fast beyond 60.

    A 60 degree steep turn, which you will fly in training, already puts twice the aeroplane's weight on the wing.

## Limit and design load factors

The designers cannot make an aeroplane infinitely strong, because strength costs weight. So they choose the load the structure must carry in service, and build in a margin above it.

The **limit load factor** is the **maximum the aeroplane is designed to take in service without permanent deformation**. Up to the limit load the structure flexes and springs back. For a light aeroplane it depends on the **category** it is certified in, which in turn depends on what it is built to do:

| Category | Positive limit | Negative limit | Design (ultimate) load, positive |
|---|---|---|---|
| Normal | +3.8 g | −1.52 g | 5.7 g |
| Utility | +4.4 g | −1.76 g | 6.6 g |
| Aerobatic | +6.0 g | −3.0 g | 9.0 g |

The **design (ultimate) load factor** is the **limit load multiplied by a safety factor of 1.5**: the load at which the structure **may fail**. Between the limit and the ultimate load the structure is not expected to break, but it **bends permanently**: a wing spar can take a set, rivets can pull, and the aeroplane is damaged even though it landed. Beyond the ultimate load it **breaks**.

![How far is a steep turn from the structural limits?](diagram:load-factor-limits "A 60° turn already uses 2 of the normal category's 3.8 g; between 3.8 and 5.7 g the structure bends permanently, beyond 5.7 g it breaks.")

The safety factor is not spare strength for you to use. It covers the things the design cannot control exactly: variations in materials and manufacture, ageing, corrosion and fatigue. An aeroplane that has been taken past its limit load once may be weaker the next time, which is why an over-stress must be reported and inspected (see [RBKA 3.8](/notes/RBKA/3.8)).

!!! example "Worked example: the ultimate load for a utility category aeroplane"
    Given: a utility category aeroplane, limit load factor +4.4 g, safety factor 1.5.

    1. Ultimate load factor: $4.4 \times 1.5 = \mathbf{6.6\ g}$.
    2. Margin between limit and ultimate: $6.6 - 4.4 = \mathbf{2.2\ g}$, the band in which the structure bends permanently.

    Many training aeroplanes are certified in both categories, utility at reduced weight. Loaded heavily into the normal category, the same aeroplane's limit drops to 3.8 g.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The **limit** load is the one you must not exceed in flight, not the ultimate load. Reaching 4 g in a normal category aeroplane does not break it, but it has gone past the 3.8 g limit and may now be permanently deformed. "It didn't break" is not the same as "it is undamaged".

??? check "Check yourself: in a normal category aeroplane, what happens to the structure at 3 g, at 5 g and at 6 g?"
    At 3 g it is within the 3.8 g limit and flexes elastically. At 5 g it is past the limit but below the 5.7 g ultimate load: it may be permanently bent. At 6 g it is past the ultimate load and may fail.

## The manoeuvring speed, V<sub>A</sub>

Here is the most useful idea in the subtopic. A wing can only make so much lift at a given speed: pull back hard and the angle of attack rises until the wing reaches its critical angle and **stalls**, after which the lift stops rising (see [RBKA 3.6](/notes/RBKA/3.6)). The maximum lift available grows with the square of the airspeed, so the maximum load factor the wing can produce before it stalls also grows with the square of the speed:

$$n_\text{max} = \left(\frac{V}{V_S}\right)^2$$

where $V_S$ is the 1 g stall speed. At the stall speed the wing can make just 1 g. At twice the stall speed it can make 4 g before it stalls.

So at low speed the stall protects the structure. However hard you pull, the wing stalls before it can make enough lift to overload anything. At high speed it does not: the wing can make more lift than the structure can carry, and a hard pull reaches the limit load before the stall.

The crossover is the **manoeuvring speed**, V<sub>A</sub>: **the highest speed at which full, abrupt control deflection will stall the wing before the limit load factor is reached**. Below V<sub>A</sub> a full control input stalls the wing first; above V<sub>A</sub> a full control input or a sharp gust can overstress the airframe. Setting $n_\text{max}$ equal to the limit load gives it directly:

$$V_A = V_S \times \sqrt{n_\text{limit}}$$

![Where does the stall protect the structure, and where does it not?](diagram:v-n-diagram "Left of VA the stall curve is below +3.8 g, so a full pull stalls first; right of VA a pull or gust can reach the limit load before the stall.")

The **V-n diagram** draws this. The curved left boundary is the stall: the most load factor the wing can make at each speed. The flat top is the +3.8 g limit. They meet at V<sub>A</sub>. Inside the envelope the aeroplane is safe; above the curve it is stalled; above the flat top it is overstressed. For an aeroplane that stalls at 52 kt, $V_A = 52 \times \sqrt{3.8} = 52 \times 1.95 \approx 101$ kt.

**V<sub>A</sub> is lower at lower weight.** This surprises people, so take it slowly. A lighter aeroplane stalls at a lower speed, because it needs less lift to support it. Its stall curve on the V-n diagram therefore sits further left, and it meets the 3.8 g line at a lower speed. Put another way: at any given speed, a lighter aeroplane can be pulled to a higher load factor before the wing stalls, because the same maximum lift is shared over less weight. So it reaches the limit load factor at a lower speed. Because stall speed varies with the square root of weight, so does V<sub>A</sub>:

$$V_{A,\text{new}} = V_{A,\text{max weight}} \times \sqrt{\frac{W_\text{actual}}{W_\text{max}}}$$

![Why is VA lower when the aeroplane is lighter?](diagram:va-vs-weight "What to notice: the lighter aeroplane stalls at a lower speed, so its stall curve sits to the left and meets +3.8 g at about 90 kt instead of 101 kt.")

!!! example "Worked example: manoeuvring speed at a lighter weight"
    Given: the POH gives V<sub>A</sub> = 100 kt at the maximum weight of 1,050 kg. Today you are flying solo with part fuel at 840 kg.

    1. Weight ratio: $840 \div 1050 = \mathbf{0.80}$.
    2. Square root: $\sqrt{0.80} = \mathbf{0.894}$.
    3. V<sub>A</sub> at 840 kg: $100 \times 0.894 = \mathbf{89\ kt}$.

    Solo and light, your protection from a full control input runs out 11 kt earlier than the placard says. Many POHs give V<sub>A</sub> at more than one weight for exactly this reason; use the one for the weight you are at, or the lower one.

Now try one before opening the solution: a normal category aeroplane stalls at 48 kt at its present weight. What is its manoeuvring speed?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    $V_A = V_S \times \sqrt{3.8} = 48 \times 1.949 = 93.6$, so **about 94 kt**. At or below about 94 kt a full, abrupt elevator input will stall the wing before it reaches 3.8 g; above it, the same input could overstress the structure.

V<sub>A</sub> is **not marked on the airspeed indicator**. It is in the POH limitations section and often on a placard. One more caution: V<sub>A</sub> protects you against a single full deflection in one axis. Full deflections in more than one axis at once, or rapid reversals of a control, can overload parts of the structure even below V<sub>A</sub>.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    V<sub>A</sub> **decreases** as weight **decreases**. The tempting answer is the opposite, because a lighter aeroplane feels as though it has strength to spare. It has the same structure, but its wing can make the limit load at a lower speed, because it needs less lift for each g.

??? check "Check yourself: why does flying below the manoeuvring speed protect the structure from a full, abrupt pull on the control column?"
    Because below V<sub>A</sub> the wing reaches its critical angle and stalls before it can make enough lift to reach the limit load factor. The stall caps the load. Above V<sub>A</sub> the wing can generate the limit load before it stalls.

## Gusts, V<sub>NO</sub> and V<sub>B</sub>

You do not have to pull the control column to load the structure. The air can do it for you.

Picture the aeroplane cruising level when it flies into a rising current of air. For an instant the air is coming at the wing from slightly below as well as from ahead, so the **angle of attack** jumps, and so does the lift. How much? The extra angle depends on the gust speed compared with your airspeed, and the lift that angle produces depends on the square of your airspeed. Put the two together and the **extra load from a given gust grows in proportion to your airspeed**. Fly through the same bump twice as fast and it hits the structure twice as hard.

![Why does the same gust hit harder at higher speed?](diagram:gust-load-vs-airspeed "What to notice: each gust line fans up from 1 g in proportion to airspeed; the design gust stays inside +3.8 g up to VNO and crosses it beyond. The speeds on the chart are schematic.")

That is why the aeroplane has a speed above which it should only be flown in smooth air. The **normal operating speed**, V<sub>NO</sub>, also called the **maximum structural cruising speed**, is the **top of the green arc**. Up to V<sub>NO</sub> the structure can take the **design gust loads**, the strongest gusts the designers assumed you would meet in turbulence. **Do not exceed V<sub>NO</sub> except in smooth air, and then only with caution.** Between V<sub>NO</sub> and V<sub>NE</sub> is the **yellow arc**, the caution range for smooth air only.

The **turbulence penetration speed**, V<sub>B</sub> (strictly the **design speed for maximum gust intensity**), is the **recommended speed in rough air**. It is low enough that a strong gust stalls the wing momentarily rather than overloading the structure, and high enough to leave a margin above the stall so the aeroplane remains controllable in the bumps. V<sub>B</sub> is **often not published for light aeroplanes**; in that case the POH gives a **turbulence penetration speed near V<sub>A</sub>**, and that is the speed you slow to.

## The never exceed speed, V<sub>NE</sub>

At the top of the scale is the **red line**, the **never exceed speed**, V<sub>NE</sub>. **Beyond it structural failure, flutter or control problems are possible.** **Flutter** is a vibration in which a control surface or wing begins to oscillate, fed by the airflow itself, and grows until something breaks, sometimes within seconds. The designers have shown that the aeroplane is free of it up to a speed above V<sub>NE</sub>, with a margin; above the red line that margin is gone. V<sub>NE</sub> is **a limit, not a target**: the yellow arc below it is for smooth air, and the red line is never crossed, in any air.

![What do the colour arcs on the airspeed indicator mean?](diagram:asi-colour-arcs "Green tops out at VNO, yellow runs from VNO to the VNE red line and is for smooth air only; VA is not marked on the dial.")

| Speed | Name | Marked on the ASI | What it protects |
|---|---|---|---|
| V<sub>A</sub> | Manoeuvring speed | No | Full, abrupt control deflection stalls the wing before the limit load; lower at lower weight |
| V<sub>B</sub> | Turbulence penetration speed | No | Recommended rough-air speed; near V<sub>A</sub> if not published |
| V<sub>NO</sub> | Normal operating (maximum structural cruising) speed | Top of the green arc | Design gust loads; above it, smooth air only |
| V<sub>NE</sub> | Never exceed speed | Red line | Structural failure, flutter and control problems beyond it |

??? check "Check yourself: you are cruising in the yellow arc when the air becomes bumpy. What do you do, and why does it help?"
    Reduce power and slow to the turbulence penetration speed (V<sub>B</sub>, or the POH speed near V<sub>A</sub>), well below V<sub>NO</sub>. The load a gust puts on the structure is proportional to airspeed, so slowing reduces every bump's load, and at V<sub>A</sub> or below a strong gust will stall the wing momentarily rather than overstress it.

## How the limits get exceeded

Every way of breaking these limits is a version of one of two things: going too fast, or pulling too hard at speed. Knowing the situations lets you see them coming.

**Spiral dives and other nose-low upsets.** In a spiral dive the wing is not stalled; the aeroplane is in a steepening, descending turn with the nose low. The speed rises quickly through V<sub>NO</sub> towards V<sub>NE</sub>, and the bank makes the natural instinct, pulling back, tighten the spiral and add load factor on top of the excess speed. The recovery order matters for exactly that reason: **close the throttle, roll the wings level, then ease out**. Rolling level first points the lift upward, so the pull to recover is not adding to a turn.

![How do you tell a spin from a spiral dive?](diagram:spin-vs-spiral-dive "In the spiral the airspeed is high and rising; recover by closing the throttle, rolling level, then easing out gently.")

**Steep turns at high speed and rapid pull-ups**, including a **harsh recovery from a dive or an accelerated stall**. Each adds load factor by curving the flight path sharply; above V<sub>A</sub> nothing stops that reaching the limit.

**Turbulence above V<sub>A</sub>.** A vertical gust adds angle of attack and load factor in proportion to the speed, so the faster you are, the harder each bump. Above V<sub>NO</sub> the design gust loads are no longer covered.

**Flying in the yellow arc in anything but smooth air.** The yellow arc is legal, but only for smooth air; a descent into turbulent air at a yellow-arc speed is the classic way to meet a gust bigger than the structure was designed for at that speed.

**Flap extended above V<sub>FE</sub> or operated above V<sub>FO</sub>, and retractable gear above its limits.** Flaps and undercarriage are built for lower speeds than the airframe; their limits are separate speed limits in the POH.

![Where are the flap speeds on the speed scale?](diagram:flap-speeds-vfo-vfe "Slow to VFO before selecting flap; VFE is then the limit for flying with it out.")

**Descending with high power and not watching the airspeed.** Lowering the nose with cruise power set turns height into speed on top of the engine's thrust. It is the easiest way to drift through V<sub>NO</sub> without noticing, especially on a long descent into Jandakot from the hills.

**Over-controlling above V<sub>A</sub>**, for example a full aileron and rudder input in a wake turbulence encounter. The instinct to fight an unexpected roll with everything you have is exactly the full deflection that V<sub>A</sub> protects against below it, and that can overstress the structure above it.

The defences follow from the causes. **Slow to V<sub>A</sub> or the turbulence penetration speed in rough air.** **Recover from any nose-low attitude by rolling level and reducing power before easing out.** **Keep the speed in the green arc unless the air is smooth.** Plan descents with reduced power, and use the controls smoothly at speed.

??? check "Check yourself: in a spiral dive, why do you roll the wings level before easing out of the dive?"
    Because while the aeroplane is banked, back pressure tightens the turn and adds load factor without raising the nose much, on top of a speed that is already high. Rolling level first points the lift upward, so the pull-out does the job with the least load factor, and closing the throttle stops the speed building further.

## Bringing it together

You are returning from Northam solo, light, at 840 kg. Over the scarp the air is rough, and you are descending towards Jandakot with cruise power still set. The speed creeps up through the top of the green arc, V<sub>NO</sub>, into the yellow, a range built for smooth air only; each bump now loads the structure in proportion to your speed. Your POH placard gives V<sub>A</sub> at maximum weight, but at your light weight it is about 11 kt lower, so you are further above it than the placard suggests, and a sharp gust or a reflex full control input could reach the 3.8 g limit before the wing stalls. Past 3.8 g the structure may bend permanently; past 5.7 g it may fail. The fix is the one the lesson leads to: reduce power, raise the nose gently, and slow to the turbulence penetration speed for your weight, then continue the descent at a lower rate. The bumps feel the same; the loads they put on the wing are much smaller.

## Key points

- **6.3.1** Load factor is lift divided by weight, in g; a level turn gives $1/\cos\theta$ (2 g at 60 degrees). The **limit** load (normal +3.8 / −1.52 g, utility +4.4 / −1.76, aerobatic +6.0 / −3.0) is the most without permanent deformation; the **design (ultimate)** load is 1.5 times the limit (5.7 g normal), where the structure may fail. **V<sub>A</sub>**: full abrupt deflection stalls the wing before the limit load; not on the ASI; **lower at lower weight**. **V<sub>B</sub>**: rough-air penetration speed, near V<sub>A</sub> if not published. **V<sub>NO</sub>**: top of the green arc, design gust loads; above it smooth air only. **V<sub>NE</sub>**: the red line; flutter, structural failure, control problems beyond it.
- **6.3.2** Limits are broken by spiral dives and nose-low upsets, steep turns and harsh pull-ups at speed, turbulence above V<sub>A</sub>, the yellow arc in rough air, flap or gear above their limits, power-on descents without watching the speed, and over-controlling above V<sub>A</sub>. Slow down in turbulence; roll level and reduce power before easing out.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    V<sub>A</sub> decreases as weight decreases. Candidates often guess the opposite. The reason is that a lighter aeroplane generates the same limit load at a lower speed because it needs less lift to reach the same g. Have by heart: normal category +3.8 g and −1.52 g, ultimate 1.5 times the limit, 2 g in a 60 degree turn, V<sub>NO</sub> at the top of the green arc, V<sub>NE</sub> the red line, and V<sub>A</sub> not marked on the dial.
