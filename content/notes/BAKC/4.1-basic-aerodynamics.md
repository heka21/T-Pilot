## What the exam expects (BAKC 4.1)

- **4.1.1** Describe the aeroplane's energy state in terms of kinetic energy, potential energy and inertia.
- **4.1.2** Explain what is meant by aerofoil, angle of attack and relative airflow; centre of pressure and centre of gravity; and the four forces, lift, weight, thrust and drag.
- **4.1.3** Describe the chord, span and camber of an aerofoil, and what an aerodynamic stall is.

## Why this matters

Every lesson on flight from here on is written in the words this one defines. When your instructor at Jandakot says "you're slow and low, you've got no energy", or "the angle of attack is too high on that turn", or "watch the CG with three up", each phrase is a precise idea, and the exam uses the same words with the same precision. Most wrong answers in this subtopic come from one confusion: treating the angle of attack as if it were the nose attitude, or lift as if it always pointed straight up. This lesson builds each term from a picture you already have, so that the lift formula in [BAKC 4.2](/notes/BAKC/4.2), the climb in [BAKC 4.3](/notes/BAKC/4.3) and the stall in [RBKA 3.6](/notes/RBKA/3.6) all rest on solid ground. The exam asks for definitions here, usually as "the angle between which two lines", "which way does lift act" and "what does inertia do in wind shear".

## Energy: speed and height are stored work

Picture a training aeroplane on a long final at 70 kt and 500 ft. It has two things that took fuel to get: speed and height. Both are **energy**, the capacity to do work, and the pilot spends them, stores them and swaps one for the other on every flight. The physics is in [BAKC 2.5](/notes/BAKC/2.5); here is what it means for an aeroplane.

**Kinetic energy** is the energy of motion:

$$KE = \tfrac{1}{2} m v^2$$

where $m$ is the mass and $v$ the speed. Because speed is squared, a little extra speed is a lot of extra energy: 10 percent fast carries 21 percent more kinetic energy, and that energy has to go somewhere when you land.

![Why does a little extra speed mean a lot more energy?](diagram:kinetic-energy-speed-squared "Kinetic energy goes with speed squared: 10 percent fast is 21 percent more energy, double the speed is four times.")

**Potential energy** is energy stored by height in the Earth's gravity:

$$PE = m g h$$

where $g$ is the acceleration due to gravity and $h$ the height. Double the height and you double the potential energy. Height is energy in the bank: in a glide with the engine stopped, it is the only energy you have left to buy distance with.

The **energy state** of the aeroplane is the sum of the two at any moment. A high energy state is fast, high or both; a low energy state is slow and low, close to the stall with no height to recover in, which is where most loss-of-control accidents begin. The pilot manages the energy state with two controls. The **throttle** adds energy to the system (or, closed, lets drag take it away). The **elevator** does not add any energy at all; it only shares the existing energy between speed and height. Pull back without adding power and you trade speed for height; push forward and you trade height for speed.

![What happens to the energy in a zoom climb?](diagram:energy-trade-zoom "With the throttle fixed the total stays the same: the kinetic energy lost between 100 kt and 60 kt reappears as about 280 ft of height.")

!!! example "Worked example: how much height does a zoom buy?"
    Given: an aeroplane at 90 kt raises the nose without adding power and slows to 60 kt. Ignore drag.

    1. Convert the speeds: $90 \times 0.514 = \mathbf{46.3\ m/s}$ and $60 \times 0.514 = \mathbf{30.9\ m/s}$.
    2. Kinetic energy lost per kilogram: $\tfrac{1}{2}(46.3^2 - 30.9^2) = \tfrac{1}{2}(2144 - 955) = \mathbf{594\ J/kg}$.
    3. Height that energy buys, $h = \Delta KE / g$: $594 / 9.81 = \mathbf{60.6\ m}$.
    4. In feet: $60.6 \times 3.28 = \mathbf{about\ 200\ ft}$.

    Mass cancels out, so the answer is the same for a Jabiru or a Boeing. In real air drag takes its share and you get a little less, but this is why a go-around begun with speed in hand starts climbing at once, and why an aeroplane that is low and slow has nothing to trade.

Compare it with the diagram's zoom from 100 kt to 60 kt, which buys about 280 ft. Starting 10 kt faster bought 80 ft more, because the energy goes with the square of the speed.

??? check "Check yourself: you raise the nose with the throttle fixed and the airspeed falls. Has the total energy changed?"
    Ignoring drag, no. The elevator only moves energy between kinetic (speed) and potential (height). Only the throttle adds energy, and drag removes it, so a climb that holds its speed needs more power.

## Inertia: the aeroplane keeps doing what it was doing

Stand in a bus that brakes hard and you keep moving forward. That reluctance to change motion is **inertia**, Newton's first law: a body stays at rest, or keeps moving at the same speed in the same direction, unless a force acts on it. Inertia depends only on **mass**: the heavier the body, the more force it takes, or the longer a given force takes, to change its motion.

For an aeroplane, inertia shows up in three places.

**Take-off and landing.** A heavy aeroplane takes longer to accelerate to lift-off speed with the same thrust and longer to stop with the same brakes. That is one reason the take-off and landing distances in [BAKC 6.2](/notes/BAKC/6.2) grow with weight.

**Changes of flight path.** When you change the attitude, the flight path does not change at once. The aeroplane carries on along its old path for a moment until the changed lift has had time to bend it. On a fast, steep approach this catches pilots out: you begin the round-out at the usual height, but the descent has momentum, the path is slow to flatten and the aeroplane arrives on the runway harder than you meant.

**Wind shear.** This is the one the exam likes. Imagine you are on final with a 20 kt headwind and you descend through a layer where the headwind drops to 5 kt. The air around you has changed speed; the aeroplane, because of its inertia, has not. For a moment it keeps its old **groundspeed**, and since groundspeed equals airspeed minus headwind, losing 15 kt of headwind means losing about 15 kt of **airspeed**. Lift falls with the airspeed, the aeroplane sinks, and only thrust and time (or a lower nose) can rebuild the speed. A heavier aeroplane, with more inertia, is slower to regain its airspeed after the shear.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Inertia holds the aeroplane's speed *over the ground*, not its airspeed. That is why a sudden change in the wind shows up as a sudden change in indicated airspeed. A decreasing headwind (or increasing tailwind) drops the IAS; an increasing headwind raises it for a moment.

??? check "Check yourself: on climb-out the headwind suddenly increases by 10 kt. What does the airspeed do at first, and why?"
    It rises by about 10 kt. Inertia keeps the aeroplane's groundspeed for a moment, and more headwind at the same groundspeed means more air flowing past the wing. The aeroplane balloons upward until it settles at its trimmed speed again.

## The aerofoil and the air it meets

Hold your hand out of a car window, flat and level, and you feel only a push backwards. Tilt the front edge up a little and your hand is pushed up as well. Curve your hand slightly and the upward push grows for the same tilt. That is a wing in miniature.

An **aerofoil** is a shape designed to produce a useful aerodynamic force, mostly at right angles to the airflow, for as little drag as possible as it moves through the air. The wing is the obvious one, but the tailplane, the fin and each propeller blade are aerofoils too. A typical light-aeroplane wing section is rounded at the front edge (the **leading edge**), curved on top, flatter underneath and tapered to a sharp **trailing edge**.

The **relative airflow** is the air as the aerofoil sees it. If the aeroplane moves forward through still air at 90 kt, the wing meets air coming at it at 90 kt from exactly the opposite direction. So the relative airflow is equal in speed and **parallel and opposite to the flight path**. Notice that this has nothing to do with the horizon. In a climb the aeroplane is going up the slope, so the relative airflow comes from ahead and slightly above; in a descent it comes from ahead and slightly below.

![Where does the relative airflow come from in a climb and a descent?](diagram:relative-airflow-climb-descent "The relative airflow always comes straight back down the flight path: from ahead and slightly above in a climb, slightly below in a descent.")

The **angle of attack** is the angle between the wing's **chord line** (the straight line from leading edge to trailing edge, described below) and the relative airflow. It is the single most important angle in flying, because it decides how hard the wing is working: more angle of attack makes more lift at the same speed, up to the stall.

![What are the parts of an aerofoil, and where is the angle of attack measured?](diagram:aerofoil-terminology "The angle of attack is between the chord line and the relative airflow, not the horizon; lift acts at right angles to the relative airflow.")

The angle of attack is not the **pitch attitude**, which is the angle between the aeroplane's longitudinal axis and the horizon, the thing you see out of the windscreen. The two are the same only in level flight (and then only roughly, because the wing is bolted on at a small angle to the fuselage). Climb, descend or turn and they come apart, because the relative airflow follows the flight path and the flight path is no longer level.

!!! example "Worked example: angle of attack in a climb"
    Given: nose attitude 10 degrees above the horizon; flight path climbing at 6 degrees. Ignore the small rigging angle between the chord and the fuselage.

    1. The relative airflow comes from directly along the flight path, so it is inclined **6 degrees** above the horizontal ahead.
    2. Angle of attack = attitude minus flight path angle: $10° - 6° = \mathbf{4°}$.

    The nose looks high, but the wing is working gently, well below the stall.

![Why is the angle of attack only 4° when the nose is 10° up?](diagram:angle-of-attack-vs-attitude "Measure the angle of attack from the flight path, not the horizon: 10° attitude minus a 6° climb path leaves about 4°.")

Now try one the other way round before opening the solution. On a powered approach the nose is 2 degrees below the horizon and the aeroplane is descending along a 5 degree path. What is the angle of attack?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    The relative airflow comes up the descent path, from 5 degrees below the horizontal. The chord is 2 degrees below the horizontal, so it sits $5° - 2° = $ **3 degrees** above the relative airflow. The nose is below the horizon, yet the wing still has a positive angle of attack, because the air is coming at it from further below.

The same reasoning tells you that a wing can have a large angle of attack with the nose on or below the horizon: if the aeroplane is sinking fast, the relative airflow comes steeply from below. Try it.

![Can a wing have a high angle of attack with the nose below the horizon?](widget:attitude-and-angle-of-attack "Exceed about 16 degrees between the chord line and the relative airflow and the streamers break away, whatever the attitude or the airspeed.")

??? check "Check yourself: the angle of attack is the angle between which two lines?"
    The chord line of the aerofoil and the relative airflow. Not the horizon and not the longitudinal axis: the relative airflow comes from wherever the aeroplane is going, so the angle of attack follows the flight path, not the view out of the windscreen.

## The four forces

In flight four forces act on the aeroplane. Two of them come from the air, one from the engine and one from the Earth.

**Lift** is the part of the total aerodynamic force on the wing that acts **at right angles to the relative airflow**. It comes from the difference in pressure between the lower and upper surfaces: the curved upper surface and the angle of attack speed the air up over the top and lower its pressure, while the air beneath is slowed and its pressure raised. In level flight the relative airflow is horizontal, so lift points straight up. In a climb, a descent or a turn it does not.

**Drag** is the part of the aerodynamic force that acts **parallel to the relative airflow, in the same direction as the airflow**, which means it opposes the aeroplane's motion through the air. It is the price of moving and of making lift, and [BAKC 4.2](/notes/BAKC/4.2) takes it apart.

**Thrust** is the forward force from the propeller, acting along the **thrust line**. The propeller blades are aerofoils, and the "lift" they make points forward.

**Weight** is the force of gravity on the aeroplane's mass, always acting **vertically downward** towards the centre of the Earth, whatever the aeroplane's attitude.

In **steady straight and level flight** (constant speed, constant height) nothing is accelerating, so the forces balance: **lift equals weight and thrust equals drag**. If thrust exceeds drag the aeroplane accelerates or climbs; if lift exceeds weight it starts to climb or turn.

![Which way does each of the four forces act in steady level flight?](diagram:four-forces-level-flight "Lift equals weight and thrust equals drag; the CP behind the CG makes a nose-down couple that the tailplane balances.")

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Lift is defined as perpendicular to the **relative airflow**, not perpendicular to the ground or to the wing's chord. Drag is parallel to the relative airflow. Weight is the only one of the four that always points the same way, straight down.

## Centre of pressure and centre of gravity

The forces in the diagram are drawn as single arrows, but they are really spread out. Every square centimetre of wing has its own small pressure difference, and every kilogram of fuel, seat and pilot has its own small weight. To work with them we replace each spread-out force with one arrow acting at one point.

The **centre of pressure** (CP) is the point on the chord line through which the total aerodynamic force, and so the lift, can be taken to act. On a conventional cambered wing it is not fixed: as the angle of attack increases through the normal flight range, the pressure peak over the top surface moves forward and the **CP moves forward**. At the stall the flow over the rear of the wing breaks away and the CP moves **rapidly rearward**, which is part of why the nose drops at the stall.

![Where does the lift act as the angle of attack changes?](diagram:cp-movement-with-aoa "The centre of pressure moves forward as the angle of attack rises, then jumps rearward when the wing stalls.")

The **centre of gravity** (CG) is the point through which the whole weight of the aeroplane acts: its balance point. It depends on how you load the aeroplane. Two heavy people in the back seats and full baggage move it aft; burning fuel from tanks ahead of or behind it moves it as the flight goes on. Weight and balance calculations exist to keep it between the limits ([BAKC 6.4](/notes/BAKC/6.4)).

The CP and CG are almost never in the same place. In a conventional design the CP is a little **behind** the CG. Lift pushes up behind the balance point while weight pulls down at it, so the pair forms a **couple**, two equal and opposite forces that are not in line, which tends to pitch the nose **down**. The tailplane balances that couple with a small downward force at the tail. The arrangement is deliberate: an aeroplane that is nose-heavy with a tail holding it up tends to lower its nose and gain speed if it slows down, which is the start of the pitch stability you will meet in [RBKA 3.2](/notes/RBKA/3.2).

??? check "Check yourself: what is the difference between the centre of pressure and the centre of gravity?"
    The centre of pressure is where the lift (the total aerodynamic force) acts, and it moves forward as the angle of attack increases. The centre of gravity is where the weight acts, and it moves only with loading and fuel burn. With the CP behind the CG, lift and weight form a nose-down couple balanced by the tailplane.

## The shape of a wing: chord, span and camber

Look at a wing from the end and you see the aerofoil section; look at it from above and you see its plan. Three measurements describe the shape.

The **chord line** is the straight line joining the leading edge to the trailing edge of the section. Its length is the **chord**. On a tapered wing the chord is longer at the root than at the tip, so we speak of the average or mean chord. The chord line is the reference from which the angle of attack is measured.

The **span** is the distance from wing tip to wing tip. Span divided by the average chord is the **aspect ratio**. A glider's long, slender wing has a high aspect ratio; a short, broad wing has a low one. High aspect ratio wings make less induced drag for the same lift, because a smaller share of the wing is near the tips where the air spills round (the reason is in [BAKC 4.2](/notes/BAKC/4.2)).

**Camber** is the curvature of the aerofoil section. Draw a line halfway between the upper and lower surfaces from leading edge to trailing edge: that is the **mean camber line**. Camber is how far the mean camber line bows away from the chord line. A symmetrical aerofoil, the same shape top and bottom, has none. More camber gives more lift at the same angle of attack, and that is exactly how a plain or slotted **flap** works: lowering it increases the camber of the inner wing so that it makes the lift it needs at a lower speed.

??? check "Check yourself: what does lowering flap change about the aerofoil, and what does that do?"
    It increases the camber (and usually the chord). More camber makes more lift at the same angle of attack, so the wing can support the aeroplane at a lower speed, which is what you want for approach and landing.

## The aerodynamic stall

Go back to the hand out of the car window. Tilt it further and further and at some point the upward push suddenly fades and your hand starts to buffet. The air can no longer follow the curve of the back of your hand; it breaks away and tumbles.

A wing does the same. As the angle of attack increases, lift increases, until the airflow over the upper surface can no longer follow its curve. It **separates**, first near the trailing edge and then further forward, leaving a turbulent wake over the top of the wing. Lift falls sharply and drag rises sharply. The angle at which this happens is the **critical angle of attack**, about **15 to 18 degrees** for a typical light-aeroplane wing, and a wing beyond it is in an **aerodynamic stall**.

![What happens to the airflow as the wing approaches the stall?](diagram:stall-sequence "The flow breaks away from the rear of the wing first and spreads forward; the warnings arrive in order: low speed, sloppy controls, stall warning, buffet.")

Because the stall is caused by angle of attack, a wing can stall at **any airspeed and any attitude**. It stalls at a higher speed in a steep turn or a hard pull-out, because the wing needs a larger angle of attack to make the extra lift; it can stall with the nose below the horizon if the aeroplane is sinking fast. The stall speed in the flight manual is only the speed at which the critical angle is reached in one particular condition. Everything about recognising and recovering from it is in [RBKA 3.6](/notes/RBKA/3.6); for this subtopic you need the definition and its cause.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    A stall is not "flying too slowly" and it is not "the engine stopping". It is the wing exceeding its critical angle of attack. Low speed is the most common way to get there in level flight, but an exam answer that defines the stall by speed or attitude is wrong.

## Bringing it together

You are on base at Jandakot, 80 kt and 800 ft, and the tower asks you to extend downwind for traffic. The terms in this lesson describe everything that happens next. Your energy state is moderate: some speed, some height. As you turn final the sea breeze, a 15 kt headwind at circuit height, weakens to 5 kt near the ground: inertia keeps your groundspeed for a moment and the IAS sags by about 10 kt. To hold the glide path you raise the nose, which raises the angle of attack, which moves the centre of pressure forward and asks the tailplane for a little more down force. The aeroplane is now slower, with the wing working harder and nearer its critical angle, at a nose attitude that looks quite ordinary because the descent path brings the relative airflow from below. The right response comes from the energy picture: add power to put energy back into the system, rather than using the elevator to borrow height you do not have.

## Key points

- **4.1.1** Kinetic energy ($\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$, speed squared) plus potential energy ($mgh$) is the energy state; the throttle adds energy, the elevator only trades speed for height. Inertia keeps the aeroplane's groundspeed and path for a moment, so wind shear shows up as a sudden change of airspeed.
- **4.1.2** The angle of attack is between the chord line and the relative airflow, which is parallel and opposite to the flight path, not the horizon. Lift acts at right angles to the relative airflow, drag parallel to it, thrust along the thrust line, weight straight down. Lift equals weight and thrust equals drag in steady level flight. The CP (where lift acts) moves forward as the angle of attack rises; the CG (where weight acts) moves with loading; CP behind CG makes a nose-down couple the tailplane balances.
- **4.1.3** Chord: leading edge to trailing edge. Span: tip to tip; span over chord is aspect ratio. Camber: the curvature of the section, more camber more lift. A stall is the angle of attack exceeding the critical angle, about 15 to 18 degrees, at any speed and any attitude.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Expect definitions phrased as "the angle between…" (chord line and relative airflow), "lift acts…" (perpendicular to the relative airflow) and "the centre of pressure…" (moves forward with increasing angle of attack until the stall). For inertia, the exam's scenario is a decreasing headwind on approach: the airspeed drops because the groundspeed is held. Any option that ties the stall to a speed or an attitude rather than an angle of attack is the distractor.
