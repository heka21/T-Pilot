## What the exam expects (BAKC 6.4)

- **6.4.1** Explain the terms used in weight and balance: datum, arm, moment, station, centre of gravity and its limits, empty weight, operating weight, MTOW, zero fuel weight and MZFW, and MLW.
- **6.4.2** Find and use the loading information for an aircraft: its MTOW, the capacity and arm of each baggage locker, the fuel's capacity, arm, grade and specific gravity, and the position and arm of each seat.
- **6.4.3** Work out whether the aircraft is loaded within its CG limits.
- **6.4.4** State what is likely to happen if the weight limits are exceeded.

## Why this matters

Three friends, their overnight bags and full tanks for a weekend at Rottnest or Busselton: it is the flight every new pilot wants to make, and it is the one most likely to put a four-seat trainer over its maximum weight or behind its aft CG limit. Nothing about the aeroplane looks different when it is overloaded or tail-heavy on the apron. The difference shows up on the take-off roll, in the climb over the trees, or in a stall that will not recover. A weight and balance calculation is the only way to know before you start. The exam always includes one, and CASA lists the loading systems among the areas candidates get wrong most often, usually from reading the wrong line on a graph or converting fuel the wrong way. This lesson builds the method from the physics of a lever, so that every step makes sense and every check has a reason.

## The lever: why position matters as much as weight

You already know the physics from a playground seesaw. A light child far from the pivot can balance a heavy adult close to it. What matters is not the weight alone but the weight multiplied by its distance from the pivot: the **turning effect**. A 20 kg child 2 m out balances a 40 kg adult 1 m in, because $20 \times 2 = 40 \times 1$.

An aeroplane is a seesaw lying along its length. Every item in it, the engine, the pilot, the passengers, the fuel, the bags in the back, pushes down at its own position, and the aeroplane balances at one point: the **centre of gravity** (CG), the point at which its whole weight can be thought of as acting. The wing's lift has to be balanced around that point by the tailplane, and the aeroplane is only controllable and stable when the CG lies within a narrow range the designers have tested. Weight and balance is the arithmetic of finding that point.

The analogy breaks down in one place. A seesaw pivots at a fixed point; an aeroplane does not pivot about any point you choose. So instead of measuring from a pivot we measure from an agreed reference, and work out where the balance point is relative to it.

## The language of weight and balance

**Datum.** The **datum** is a reference plane, chosen by the manufacturer, from which all horizontal distances are measured. It is often the firewall or a point ahead of the nose. It is arbitrary: the CG comes out at the same physical place whichever datum you use, but every figure in a loading system must be measured from the same one. Putting it ahead of the nose has a convenience: every distance is then positive.

**Arm.** The **arm** is the horizontal distance from the datum to the centre of gravity of an item, in millimetres or inches; **positive aft of the datum**. A pilot's seat with an arm of 2,000 mm means the pilot's weight acts 2,000 mm behind the datum.

**Moment.** The **moment** is **weight multiplied by arm**: the turning effect of that item about the datum, the seesaw's "weight times distance". In metric loading systems its unit is the kilogram millimetre (kg mm). Because the numbers get big, some loading systems divide moments by a fixed amount and call the result **index units**; the logic is unchanged (see [POPC 2.1](/notes/POPC/2.1)).

**Station.** A **station** is a location in the aircraft identified by its arm, for example "station 2,100" for the rear seats. The loading system lists the stations: the front seats, rear seats, each baggage locker and the fuel tanks.

**Centre of gravity and limits.** The **CG** is the point at which the total weight is considered to act: **total moment divided by total weight**. The **forward and aft CG limits** are the range within which the aircraft is controllable and stable. On many aeroplanes the **forward limit moves aft at higher weights**, so the limits form an **envelope** of weight against CG rather than two fixed numbers.

**Empty weight** (basic empty weight). The weight of the aircraft with its fixed equipment, **unusable fuel and full oil**, as weighed, with its own arm. It is different for each individual aeroplane, which is why the weight and balance data in the POH must be for this airframe.

**Operating weight.** Empty weight plus the **crew and crew items**, and on some systems the oil. It is the starting point when the crew is the same on every flight.

**MTOW**, the **maximum take-off weight**, is the **structural limit for take-off** (see [BAKC 6.2](/notes/BAKC/6.2) for why the weight you may actually use on a hot day can be lower).

**Zero fuel weight and MZFW.** **Zero fuel weight** is the weight with **everything on board except usable fuel**. The **maximum zero fuel weight** exists because of where the load sits. Fuel in the wings hangs where the lift is made, so it bends the wing very little; people and bags in the fuselage hang from the wing roots and bend the wing upward. MZFW **limits the wing-bending load from the fuselage contents**: beyond it, any additional load must be fuel.

**MLW**, the **maximum landing weight**, is the **structural limit for landing**, where the undercarriage and its attachments take the impact. On light trainers it often equals MTOW.

??? check "Check yourself: why does the maximum zero fuel weight limit the load in the cabin but not the fuel in the wings?"
    Because fuel in the wing tanks sits where the lift is generated, so it adds little bending load at the wing roots. Weight in the fuselage hangs from the roots and bends the wings upward. MZFW caps that fuselage load; any weight above it must be fuel.

## Finding the loading information

The figures you calculate with are not things you remember; they belong to the aircraft and are in its POH section 6 or the workbook's loading system. Knowing where each one lives, and what each one means, is half of element 6.4.2.

**MTOW** (and MLW and MZFW where they exist) is in the limitations. Every calculation ends by comparing your total with it.

**Seats.** Each seat row has an **arm**. Front seats are usually close to the CG range; rear seats are well aft of it, which is why rear passengers move the CG aft so strongly. Some aeroplanes give an adjustable seat a range of arms; use the one the loading system specifies.

**Baggage lockers.** Each locker has an **arm** and a **capacity**: a **maximum weight**, set by the strength of its floor and tie-downs. A locker's limit is a limit in its own right: a load can be inside the CG envelope and under MTOW and still be illegal because one locker is overloaded. Lockers are usually the furthest aft station in a light aeroplane, so a heavy bag there has a large moment.

**Fuel.** The loading system gives the **usable capacity** in litres, the **arm** of the tanks, the **grade** of fuel the engine needs, and the **specific gravity** (SG) to use. The grade matters for the engine, not the arithmetic: AVGAS 100LL is dyed blue and AVGAS 100/130 green, and a fuel sample of the wrong colour, or clear, means the wrong fuel or water.

![What should the fuel sample look like?](diagram:fuel-sample-colours "AVGAS 100LL is blue, 100/130 green, Jet A-1 clear to straw; a clear sample from an AVGAS aeroplane means water or jet fuel.")

The **specific gravity** is the fuel's density compared with water. A litre of water has a mass of 1 kg; a litre of AVGAS has a mass of about **0.71 to 0.72 kg**. Fuel is measured in litres and loaded in kilograms, so:

$$\text{kilograms} = \text{litres} \times \text{SG} \qquad \text{litres} = \frac{\text{kilograms}}{\text{SG}}$$

Litres to kilograms is **multiply** by the SG; kilograms to litres is **divide**. A quick sense check: fuel is lighter than water, so the number of kilograms must be **smaller** than the number of litres. Use the SG given in the question or the loading system.

!!! example "Worked example: converting fuel both ways"
    Given: SG 0.72.

    1. Full tanks of 150 L: $150 \times 0.72 = \mathbf{108\ kg}$. Fewer kilograms than litres: correct direction.
    2. The load sheet shows you can carry 95 kg of fuel before reaching MTOW. In litres: $95 \div 0.72 = 131.9$, so **131 L** (round down: rounding up would put you over).

    The second direction is the one people get wrong under exam pressure, because multiplying feels natural. Check the answer against the rule: litres must come out larger than kilograms.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Multiplying when you should divide. If converting kilograms to litres gives you fewer litres than kilograms, you have multiplied by the SG instead of dividing. The same slip in the other direction makes 150 L of fuel weigh 208 kg.

## Finding the CG: why moment divided by weight works

Now the method. Imagine replacing everything in the aeroplane, every item at its own arm, with a single weight equal to the total, placed at one point. For that single weight to have the same turning effect about the datum as all the items together, it must satisfy:

$$\text{total weight} \times \text{CG arm} = \text{sum of all the moments}$$

That point is the CG. Rearranging:

$$\text{CG} = \frac{\text{total moment}}{\text{total weight}}$$

The method is the same for every loading system:

1. List each item with its weight and arm: empty aircraft, pilot and front passenger, rear passengers, baggage by locker, fuel.
2. Convert fuel in litres to kilograms using the SG.
3. Multiply each weight by its arm to get its moment. Add the weights and add the moments.
4. Divide the total moment by the total weight to get the CG.
5. Check the total weight against MTOW (and MZFW and MLW where applicable), and the CG against the limits at that weight.

!!! example "Worked example: four people, bags and full fuel"
    Given: a trainer with an empty weight of 680 kg at an arm of 2,050 mm, MTOW 1,050 kg, CG limits 2,000 to 2,120 mm aft of datum. Front seats at 2,000 mm, rear seat at 2,900 mm, baggage at 3,500 mm, fuel at 2,300 mm, SG 0.72.

    | Item | Weight (kg) | Arm (mm) | Moment (kg mm) |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | Empty aircraft | 680 | 2,050 | 1,394,000 |
    | Pilot and passenger | 160 | 2,000 | 320,000 |
    | Rear passenger | 70 | 2,900 | 203,000 |
    | Baggage | 20 | 3,500 | 70,000 |
    | Fuel 150 L × 0.72 | 108 | 2,300 | 248,400 |
    | **Total** | **1,038** | | **2,235,400** |

    1. Total weight: **1,038 kg**, under the 1,050 kg MTOW.
    2. CG: $2{,}235{,}400 \div 1{,}038 = \mathbf{2{,}154\ mm}$.
    3. Against the limits: 2,154 mm is **aft of the 2,120 mm limit** by 34 mm.

    The weight is legal, but the aeroplane is tail-heavy and must not fly loaded like this.

![Where does the worked-example aeroplane balance?](diagram:moment-arm-seesaw "Each load pushes down at its arm; the total moment over the total weight puts the balance point at 2,154 mm, aft of the 2,120 mm limit.")

The diagram shows the seesaw. The bag at 3,500 mm and the rear passenger at 2,900 mm sit far behind the limits; their moments pull the balance point aft out of all proportion to their weight.

So the load must change, and each change has to be worked through. **Moving the 20 kg bag** from the 3,500 mm locker to the front at 2,000 mm removes $20 \times (3500 - 2000) = 30{,}000$ kg mm, giving $2{,}205{,}400 \div 1{,}038 = 2{,}125$ mm: still just aft. **Removing the baggage altogether** gives $2{,}165{,}400 \div 1{,}018 = 2{,}127$ mm: also aft, and, notice, *further* aft than moving it, because taking the bag away also made the divisor smaller. The rear passenger's 70 kg at 2,900 mm is the load that has to change. With the front seats full there is nowhere further forward to put them; leaving them behind (bag still in the locker) gives $(2{,}235{,}400 - 203{,}000) \div (1{,}038 - 70) = 2{,}032{,}400 \div 968 = 2{,}100$ mm, inside the limits.

That is the lesson of the example: **re-check the CG after every change**, because changing a weight changes the divisor as well as the moment, and the result is not always what intuition says.

!!! example "Worked example: the same aeroplane, checking the weight with heavier front-seat occupants"
    Given: the same load, but the pilot and front passenger together weigh 170 kg instead of 160 kg.

    1. Total weight: $680 + 170 + 70 + 20 + 108 = \mathbf{1{,}048\ kg}$.
    2. Against MTOW: $1{,}050 - 1{,}048 = \mathbf{2\ kg}$ under. Legal on weight, with almost no margin; a slightly heavier bag or a top-up of fuel would put it over.
    3. Moment: $2{,}235{,}400 + 10 \times 2{,}000 = 2{,}255{,}400$ kg mm, so CG $= 2{,}255{,}400 \div 1{,}048 = \mathbf{2{,}152\ mm}$: still aft of the limit.

    A weight check and a CG check are separate tests, and a load has to pass both.

Now try one before opening the solution. The same aeroplane, flown solo: the pilot weighs 85 kg in the front seat, no rear passenger, a 30 kg bag in the 3,500 mm locker, and 120 L of fuel at SG 0.72 in the tanks at 2,300 mm. Is it within limits at take-off, and again after you have burned 60 L?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    Fuel: $120 \times 0.72 = 86.4$ kg.

    | Item | Weight (kg) | Arm (mm) | Moment (kg mm) |
    |---|---|---|---|
    | Empty aircraft | 680 | 2,050 | 1,394,000 |
    | Pilot | 85 | 2,000 | 170,000 |
    | Baggage | 30 | 3,500 | 105,000 |
    | Fuel 120 L | 86.4 | 2,300 | 198,720 |
    | **Total** | **881.4** | | **1,867,720** |

    Take-off CG $= 1{,}867{,}720 \div 881.4 = $ **2,119 mm**. Inside the 2,000 to 2,120 mm limits, but only **1 mm from the aft limit**. A solo pilot with a heavy bag is a tail-heavy case: there is no front passenger to balance the bag.

    After burning 60 L ($60 \times 0.72 = 43.2$ kg): weight $881.4 - 43.2 = 838.2$ kg; moment $1{,}867{,}720 - 43.2 \times 2{,}300 = 1{,}867{,}720 - 99{,}360 = 1{,}768{,}360$ kg mm; CG $= 1{,}768{,}360 \div 838.2 = $ **2,110 mm**. The fuel tanks at 2,300 mm are **aft of the CG**, so burning fuel moves the CG **forward**. Still inside. Legal, but moving the bag forward would give a much more comfortable margin.

![Load an example aeroplane and watch the CG move on the envelope](widget:weight-and-balance "The point must stay inside the envelope at take-off and after the fuel has burned off; the forward limit tightens as weight rises.")

The widget shows why you check at more than one weight. As fuel burns, the loading point moves along a line on the envelope, and both ends of that line, the take-off point and the landing point, must be inside it. Whether burning fuel moves the CG forward or aft depends on where the tanks are relative to the CG: tanks aft of the CG, as here, move it forward as they empty; tanks forward of it move it aft. Where the loading system gives an MZFW, the zero fuel point must also be within limits, since that is where the CG ends up if you burn everything.

The CASA workbook's loading systems ALPHA, BRAVO and CHARLIE use index units and graphs instead of raw moments, but the logic is identical. They are covered in detail in the PPL operations notes ([POPC 2.1](/notes/POPC/2.1)) and the exam will give you the workbook.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    A load under MTOW is not necessarily a legal load. The weight check and the CG check are separate, and the CG must be inside the limits at the actual weight, both at take-off and as the fuel burns. Re-calculate after every change rather than adjusting the old answer by eye.

??? check "Check yourself: you remove a bag from the rear locker and the CG moves forward less than you expected. Why?"
    Because removing the bag reduces both the total moment and the total weight. The CG is moment divided by weight, so the smaller divisor partly cancels the smaller moment. That is why you re-calculate after every change.

??? check "Check yourself: the fuel tanks are aft of the CG. Does burning fuel move the CG forward or aft, and why?"
    Forward. Taking weight away from a station aft of the CG removes a large moment relative to its weight, so the balance point moves away from it, forward. If the tanks were forward of the CG, burning fuel would move the CG aft.

## What happens when the limits are exceeded

The limits are not paperwork. Each one protects something physical, and exceeding it has predictable results.

**Over MTOW.** Everything that depends on weight gets worse at once. The wing must make more lift at every speed, so the stall speed rises (stall speed goes with the square root of weight). The aeroplane needs a higher speed to lift off and more distance to reach it: a **longer take-off run and distance**. The surplus of power over that needed to fly level shrinks, so the **rate and angle of climb fall**. Cruise speed and range drop, the aeroplane is less manoeuvrable, it lands faster and needs a **longer landing distance**. And the **structural loads go beyond those the airframe and undercarriage were certified for**, especially in turbulence or a hard landing: the limit load factor is a multiple of the certified weight, so an aeroplane 10 percent over MTOW puts its 3.8 g design load on the structure at about $3.8 \div 1.1 \approx 3.5$ g. It is also **illegal**.

**Over MLW.** The undercarriage and the structure around it take the impact of touchdown, and above MLW they can be **damaged on landing**. In a trainer with MLW equal to MTOW this is rarely the issue, but on larger aeroplanes fuel may have to be burned off before landing.

The CG limits protect controllability and stability. To see why, remember how the aeroplane balances in pitch. The CG normally sits ahead of the wing's centre of lift, so the nose wants to drop, and the tailplane holds it up by pushing **down**. That tail download is what makes the aeroplane stable: if the nose pitches up, the tail's angle of attack changes and the tail pushes it back. How hard the tail has to push, and how much leverage it has, both depend on where the CG is.

![Why does CG position change stall speed and stability?](diagram:tail-download-cg "What to notice: a forward CG needs a big tail download, so the wing carries more and stalls at a higher speed, but the long tail arm makes it very stable; aft, the download, the stall speed and the tail's leverage all shrink.")

**CG forward of the limit.** The nose is heavy, so the tail must push down harder. The wing has to carry that extra download as well as the weight, so it flies at a higher angle of attack: **higher stall speed** and **more drag from the trim**, so less range. The aeroplane is **very stable**, which sounds good but means it resists your pitch inputs. The serious problem comes at the flare: at low speed the elevator is less effective, and with a nose-heavy aeroplane you may need **more elevator to flare than you have**, landing nosewheel first.

**CG aft of the limit.** The opposite. The tail needs to push down less, so the stall speed is **lower**. But the CG is now close to the centre of lift, so the tail's leverage over the CG is short and the restoring moment is weak: **reduced longitudinal stability**. The controls feel **light and over-sensitive** in pitch, and it is easy to over-rotate or overstress the aeroplane. Most dangerous of all, the elevator may not have the leverage to unstall the wing: **stall and spin recovery may be impossible**, and an aft-CG aeroplane can enter a flatter spin from which the tail cannot recover it.

![What goes wrong with the CG outside each limit?](diagram:cg-position-effects "Forward: stable but heavy, higher stall speed, maybe not enough elevator to flare. Aft: twitchy and unstable, with stall and spin recovery possibly impossible.")

!!! warning "Common trap"
    An aft CG gives a **lower** stall speed, and the exam may offer that as if it were a benefit. It is not: the price is reduced stability and possibly no stall or spin recovery. A forward CG is the safer of the two errors, but it still raises the stall speed and can leave you without enough elevator to flare.

??? check "Check yourself: why does a forward CG raise the stall speed?"
    Because the tailplane must push down harder to hold the nose up, and the wing has to make enough lift to support that download as well as the aeroplane's weight. More lift needed at the same speed means a higher angle of attack, so the wing reaches its critical angle at a higher speed.

## Bringing it together

Friday afternoon at Jandakot, planning Saturday's trip to Busselton with two friends and weekend bags. You take the empty weight and arm from this aircraft's POH, not the club's other one of the same type. Front seats, rear seat and the rear locker each have their arm, the locker has its own weight limit, and the fuel goes in at its arm after converting litres to kilograms by multiplying by 0.72. The first sum comes out under MTOW but aft of the limit: the bags and the rear passenger are all well behind the CG range. You move the heaviest person to the front, re-calculate (moment *and* weight), and find it inside. Then you check it again with the fuel you expect to have at Busselton: the tanks are aft of the CG, so the CG moves forward as you burn fuel and the landing point is inside too. You also notice that with the full tanks you planned, you are 15 kg under MTOW on a summer afternoon, which the climb weight limit in [BAKC 6.2](/notes/BAKC/6.2) may not allow. The calculation took ten minutes; the alternative is finding out on the take-off roll.

## Key points

- **6.4.1** Datum: the reference for all distances. Arm: distance from the datum, positive aft. Moment: weight × arm. Station: a location identified by its arm. CG: total moment ÷ total weight, within forward and aft limits (an envelope). Empty weight includes unusable fuel and full oil; operating weight adds the crew. MTOW and MLW are structural limits; MZFW caps the fuselage load that bends the wing.
- **6.4.2** Seats, lockers and tanks each have an arm; lockers also have a maximum weight; fuel has a usable capacity, a grade and an SG. Litres × SG = kilograms; kilograms ÷ SG = litres.
- **6.4.3** List weights and arms, find the moments, add, divide. Check the weight against MTOW and the CG against the limits, at take-off and after fuel burn. Re-calculate after every change, because the divisor changes too.
- **6.4.4** Over MTOW: higher stall speed, longer take-off and landing, poorer climb, structural overload, illegal. Over MLW: landing damage. Forward CG: nose heavy, higher stall speed, possibly not enough elevator to flare. Aft CG: unstable and over-sensitive, stall and spin recovery possibly impossible.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Work in the units of the loading system and do not round until the end. CASA lists the loading systems among the areas candidates get wrong most often, usually from reading the wrong line on a graph or converting fuel the wrong way. Litres to kilograms is multiply by the SG; kilograms to litres is divide. Expect a question with a load under MTOW but a CG outside the limits; the answer is to change the distribution of the load, not to take off because the weight is fine.
