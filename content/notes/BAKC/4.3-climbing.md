## What the exam expects (BAKC 4.3)

- **4.3.1** Explain how rate of climb differs from angle of climb: what each measures, what sets it, the speed that gives the best of each, and what wind does to each.

## Why this matters

"How well does it climb?" has two answers, and they are not the same. Departing a farm strip near York with a line of gums at the far fence, you care about how much height you gain before you reach the trees: that is **angle**. Climbing out of Jandakot to get on top of the haze before the hills, you care about how many feet you gain each minute: that is **rate**. The aeroplane gives its best of each at a different speed, the POH lists both, and choosing the wrong one at the wrong moment has put aeroplanes into trees with the engine working perfectly. The exam checks that you can tell the two apart, pick Vx or Vy for a situation, convert between feet per minute and feet per nautical mile, and say what a headwind or tailwind does to each. This lesson builds both from the forces in a climb, then does the arithmetic.

## Two questions about one climb

Picture two aeroplanes leaving the same runway at the same moment. After one minute the first is 700 ft up and 1.5 NM down the track; the second is 650 ft up but only 1 NM down the track. Which climbs better?

The first has gained more height **per minute**: it has the better **rate of climb**, measured in feet per minute (fpm) and shown on the vertical speed indicator. The second has gained more height **per mile**: it has the better **angle of climb**, the steepness of its path, measured as a gradient in feet per nautical mile, as a percentage, or in degrees. If there is a 600 ft hill 1.2 NM from the runway, only the second aeroplane is above it when it gets there, even though the first is climbing faster.

Hold onto that picture: **rate goes with time, angle goes with distance**. Everything else in this lesson follows from it.

A gradient can be given three ways, and the exam uses all of them. **Feet per nautical mile** is the most practical for pilots, because charts and obstacles are in nautical miles. A **percentage** is height gained per 100 units of distance; since a nautical mile is about 6,076 ft, 1 percent is about 61 ft per NM. **Degrees** is the angle of the path itself; for the shallow climbs of a light aeroplane, 1 degree is about 106 ft per NM. So a climb of 450 ft per NM is about 7.4 percent and a little over 4 degrees: much flatter than it feels from the cockpit.

![How do feet per NM, percent and degrees compare?](diagram:climb-gradient-units "450 ft per NM is about 7.4 percent and a little over 4 degrees: 1 percent is about 61 ft per NM, 1 degree about 106.")

??? check "Check yourself: which instrument shows the rate of climb, and in what units? Which shows the angle?"
    The vertical speed indicator shows the rate, in feet per minute. No instrument shows the angle over the ground directly; you work it out from the rate and the groundspeed, or judge it from the view of obstacles ahead.

## What makes an aeroplane climb

In level flight thrust balances drag and lift balances weight ([BAKC 4.1](/notes/BAKC/4.1)). Now tilt the flight path upwards by a climb angle, written $\gamma$ (gamma). Lift still acts at right angles to the flight path and drag still acts back along it, but weight still points straight down, so it is no longer at right angles to the path.

Split the weight into two parts. One part, $W\cos\gamma$, acts across the flight path, and lift balances it; in a shallow climb $\cos\gamma$ is very nearly 1, so lift is only a little less than weight. The other part, $W\sin\gamma$, acts **backwards along the flight path**, exactly like extra drag. To hold a steady climb, thrust must overcome both:

$$T = D + W\sin\gamma$$

![How do the four forces balance in a steady climb?](diagram:forces-in-a-climb "Part of the weight acts back along the flight path, so thrust must equal drag plus that part; the excess thrust sets the climb angle.")

Rearrange it and you have the angle of climb:

$$\sin\gamma = \frac{T - D}{W}$$

The steepness of the climb depends on the **excess thrust**, thrust minus drag, as a fraction of the weight. More thrust (full power, dense air) or less drag (clean configuration, the right speed) steepens the climb; more weight flattens it. This is the key result: **the angle of climb is set by excess thrust**.

The rate of climb is how fast the aeroplane gains height. It moves along the path at speed $V$, and the vertical part of that is $V\sin\gamma$:

$$\text{rate of climb} = V \sin\gamma = \frac{V\,(T - D)}{W}$$

Thrust times speed is **power** (the work done each second). So $V \times (T - D)$ is the power available minus the power needed to fly level, the **excess power**. **The rate of climb is set by excess power**, and it is the same idea in different clothes: excess thrust tells you how steep, excess thrust times speed tells you how fast upwards.

??? check "Check yourself: the angle of climb depends on excess thrust; the rate depends on excess power. What is the difference between the two?"
    Excess power is excess thrust multiplied by the speed. The same surplus thrust lifts the aeroplane up the slope faster if it is moving along the slope faster, so a slightly faster climb can give a better rate even though its angle is a little flatter.

## Vx and Vy: two best speeds

Both excess thrust and excess power change with airspeed, and they peak at different speeds.

For a propeller aeroplane, thrust is greatest at low speed and falls as speed rises, while total drag is the U-shaped curve of [BAKC 4.2](/notes/BAKC/4.2). The gap between them, excess thrust, is largest at a fairly low speed, a little above the stall. That speed gives the steepest climb, the most height per unit of distance: it is **Vx, the best angle of climb speed**.

Fly a few knots faster than Vx and the excess thrust shrinks a little, so the path flattens. But the aeroplane is now covering more ground each minute, and the product of the two, the excess power, keeps rising for a while. It peaks at a higher speed: **Vy, the best rate of climb speed**, the most height per minute.

![Where on the climb curve are Vx and Vy?](diagram:climb-rate-vs-airspeed "Vy is the top of the rate curve; Vx is where the steepest line from the origin touches it, always at a lower speed.")

You can find both on one graph of rate of climb against airspeed. Vy is simply the top of the curve. Vx is where a straight line drawn from the origin just touches the curve: the slope of that line is rate divided by speed, height per distance, and the touching point has the steepest possible slope. The touching point is always to the left of the top, so **Vx is always lower than Vy** for a propeller aeroplane.

Typical figures for a two-seat trainer are Vx about 60 kt and Vy about 75 kt, but these are only an example: use the figures in your own POH. The POH also tells you which flap setting it means, because flap usually costs climb performance, and how the speeds change with weight.

![When do you climb at Vx and when at Vy?](diagram:vx-vs-vy "Vx gives the most height per distance (to clear an obstacle); Vy the most height per minute. At each minute mark the Vy aeroplane is higher.")

So the choice is about what you are trying to beat:

- **Vx** when the problem is **distance**: an obstacle close to the end of the runway, a short strip with trees, rising ground ahead. You accept a lower rate to get the steepest path. Vx is close to the stall and the engine is working hard at a low cooling airspeed, so you use it only until the obstacle is cleared, then lower the nose to Vy.
- **Vy** when the problem is **time**: getting to cruise height, getting above the weather or terrain in the least time, or a normal climb-out. Many pilots climb a little faster than Vy en route (a cruise climb) for engine cooling and a better view over the nose.

| | Rate of climb | Angle of climb |
|---|---|---|
| Measures | Height gained **per unit time** | Height gained **per unit horizontal distance** |
| Units | Feet per minute, on the VSI | Feet per NM, percent or degrees |
| Set by | **Excess power** | **Excess thrust** |
| Best speed | **Vy** (higher) | **Vx** (lower) |
| Used for | Reaching height in the least time; normal climbs | Clearing obstacles close to the runway |
| Changed by wind? | No: rate is relative to the air | Yes, over the ground: headwind steepens, tailwind flattens |

Everything that cuts thrust or power, or adds weight or drag, worsens both. A hot day at Northam, a heavy load or a dirty wing all reduce excess thrust and excess power, so both angle and rate fall ([BAKC 6.2](/notes/BAKC/6.2) covers density height).

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The exam offers Vy for clearing an obstacle because it is "the best climb speed". It gives the best *rate*, which is height per minute, not per mile. For obstacles, the question is how high you are when you reach them, and that is the best *angle*: Vx.

??? check "Check yourself: why is Vx always slower than Vy in a propeller aeroplane?"
    Vx is where excess thrust is greatest, and for a propeller aeroplane that occurs at low speed. Going faster loses a little excess thrust but covers ground faster, so excess power (thrust times speed) keeps rising until Vy. The top of the rate curve is therefore always at a higher speed than the steepest-angle point.

## Converting rate into angle

The rate of climb comes from the VSI or the POH. The angle you need comes from the chart: how high is the obstacle, and how far away? To compare them, convert the rate into a gradient over the ground using the **groundspeed**.

At a groundspeed of $GS$ knots you cover $GS/60$ nautical miles each minute. In that minute you gain the rate of climb in feet. So:

$$\text{gradient (ft per NM)} = \frac{\text{rate of climb (fpm)} \times 60}{\text{groundspeed (kt)}}$$

!!! example "Worked example: still-air gradient"
    Given: rate of climb 600 fpm; groundspeed 80 kt (no wind).

    1. Distance per minute: $80 / 60 = \mathbf{1.33\ NM}$.
    2. Gradient: $600 \times 60 / 80 = \mathbf{450\ ft\ per\ NM}$.

    Every nautical mile you fly, you gain 450 ft. A 300 ft obstacle 1 NM from the start of the climb is cleared by about 150 ft.

The same steps with the brief's own numbers, and with wind, show how the arithmetic is used in practice.

!!! example "Worked example: one climb, three winds, one obstacle"
    Given: Vy climb at 700 fpm and 90 kt true airspeed. Obstacle 400 ft high, 1.5 NM from the point where the climb starts.

    1. Gradient needed: $400 / 1.5 = \mathbf{267\ ft\ per\ NM}$, plus a margin.
    2. Still air (groundspeed 90 kt): $700 \times 60 / 90 = \mathbf{467\ ft\ per\ NM}$. Height at 1.5 NM: $467 \times 1.5 = \mathbf{700\ ft}$, clearing by **300 ft**.
    3. 15 kt headwind (groundspeed 75 kt): $700 \times 60 / 75 = \mathbf{560\ ft\ per\ NM}$. Height at 1.5 NM: **840 ft**, clearing by **440 ft**.
    4. 15 kt tailwind (groundspeed 105 kt): $700 \times 60 / 105 = \mathbf{400\ ft\ per\ NM}$. Height at 1.5 NM: **600 ft**, clearing by **200 ft**.

    All three clear the obstacle, the tailwind case by least. The VSI read 700 fpm every time: the wind changed only how much ground you covered while gaining that height.

Sometimes the unknown is the rate you need. Try this before opening the solution. A departure from a short strip needs at least 300 ft per NM to clear the ridge, and your groundspeed in the climb will be 70 kt. What rate of climb must the aeroplane achieve?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    Rearrange the formula: rate $=$ gradient $\times$ groundspeed $/ 60 = 300 \times 70 / 60 =$ **350 fpm**. In one minute at 70 kt you fly 1.17 NM, and 1.17 NM at 300 ft per NM is 350 ft. Check the POH climb chart for the day's density height: if the aeroplane cannot make 350 fpm with a margin, you do not go.

## What wind does, and what it does not

The rate of climb is measured relative to the air. The aeroplane climbs through the air mass at the same feet per minute whether that air mass is still or moving across the ground at 20 kt. So **wind does not change the rate of climb**, and the POH's Vx and Vy, which are airspeeds, remain the speeds to fly.

What wind changes is the path **over the ground**. A headwind cuts the groundspeed, so in each minute of climbing you cover less ground and the path over the ground is steeper. A tailwind raises the groundspeed and flattens the path. Since obstacles are fixed to the ground, the angle that matters for clearing them is the angle over the ground, and that is why you take off into wind.

![How does wind change the climb over the ground?](diagram:climb-gradient-wind "The VSI shows 600 fpm in all three cases, but a 20 kt tailwind flattens the path from 450 to 360 ft per NM and cuts the obstacle margin to 60 ft.")

!!! example "Worked example: the 20 kt wind on the 600 fpm climb"
    Given: rate of climb 600 fpm, true airspeed 80 kt, obstacle 300 ft high at 1 NM.

    1. 20 kt headwind, groundspeed 60 kt: $600 \times 60 / 60 = \mathbf{600\ ft\ per\ NM}$; obstacle cleared by **300 ft**.
    2. 20 kt tailwind, groundspeed 100 kt: $600 \times 60 / 100 = \mathbf{360\ ft\ per\ NM}$; obstacle cleared by only **60 ft**.

    The still-air margin of 150 ft has doubled with the headwind and shrunk to less than half with the tailwind, with exactly the same aeroplane, power and VSI reading.

Near the ground the wind usually strengthens with height, because friction slows it at the surface. Climbing into a headwind that grows as you climb helps a little: the airspeed tends to rise and the aeroplane can be held in a slightly steeper attitude. Climbing out with a tailwind that grows does the opposite, and a sudden loss of headwind on climb-out is wind shear, which drops the airspeed ([BAKC 4.1](/notes/BAKC/4.1)).

!!! warning "Common trap"
    "A headwind improves the climb" is half right. It improves the **angle over the ground**, not the **rate**. If an option says a headwind increases the rate of climb, it is wrong; if it says the rate is unchanged and the angle over the ground increases, it is right.

??? check "Check yourself: climbing at a steady 500 fpm, you turn from into wind to downwind. What happens to the VSI reading, and to your clearance over the hills ahead?"
    The VSI still reads about 500 fpm, because the rate is measured relative to the air and the wind carries the whole air mass. The clearance shrinks: the groundspeed is now higher, so you cover more ground for each foot gained and the path over the ground is flatter.

## Bringing it together

You are leaving a 900 m strip near Toodyay on a warm morning, two on board. The trees at the far fence are about 60 ft tall and the land rises beyond them towards a ridge. The windsock shows a light breeze straight down the strip, so you take off into it: the lower groundspeed shortens the run and steepens your path over the ground. After lift-off you hold Vx, accepting a modest rate on the VSI, because the trees are a distance problem. Once they are well below, you lower the nose to Vy: now the problem is time, getting high enough above the ridge and the turbulence over it as quickly as possible. Had the wind been a tailwind, the VSI would have shown the same rate at each speed, but the path over the trees would have been flatter, and the right decision would have been to use the other end of the strip or to wait.

## Key points

- **4.3.1** Rate of climb is height per unit time (fpm, on the VSI), set by excess power, best at **Vy**; angle of climb is height per unit distance (ft per NM, percent, degrees), set by excess thrust, best at **Vx**, which is always the lower speed. Use Vx to clear obstacles close ahead and Vy to gain height in the least time. Gradient (ft per NM) $=$ rate (fpm) $\times 60 \div$ groundspeed (kt). Wind changes the angle over the ground, not the rate: a headwind steepens the path, a tailwind flattens it.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Remember "rate goes with time, angle goes with distance": Vy and feet per minute for time, Vx and feet per nautical mile for obstacles. Expect one conversion from fpm and groundspeed to feet per NM (multiply by 60, divide by groundspeed), one choice between Vx and Vy, and one wind question where the right answer is "rate unchanged, angle over the ground changed".
