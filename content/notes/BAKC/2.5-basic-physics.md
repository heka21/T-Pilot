## What the exam expects (BAKC 2.5)

- **2.5.1** Explain kinetic and potential energy and how each shows up in ordinary flying.
- **2.5.2** Explain what "aircraft energy state" means in terms of kinetic and potential energy.
- **2.5.3** Explain what acceleration, deceleration, climbing and descending each do to the aircraft energy state.

## Why this matters

Every approach you fly is an exercise in energy. Turn final at Jandakot too high and too fast and you have more energy than the runway can absorb: the aeroplane floats past the touchdown markers and you go around. Turn final low and slow on a gusty afternoon and you have too little: a sink you cannot stop with power, close to the stall. The engine failure you will practise over the training area is the same problem without an engine: all you have is the height and the speed you had when it stopped, and the field you can reach depends on how you spend them. The exam asks about this subtopic in words rather than numbers: what doubling the speed does to kinetic energy, where the energy in a glide comes from, and what happens to the energy state in a zoom or a level acceleration. This lesson starts with the forces that move an aeroplane, because energy is what those forces do over a distance.

## Forces and Newton's three laws

Before energy, forces. A **force** is a push or a pull, measured in newtons (N). Four of them act on an aeroplane in flight: **lift** from the wings, **weight** from gravity, **thrust** from the propeller and **drag** from the air resisting the aeroplane's motion. How those four forces change the aeroplane's motion was set out by Isaac Newton in three laws. Each has a flying example you already know.

**First law: an object keeps doing what it is doing unless a net force acts on it.** A body at rest stays at rest, and a body moving in a straight line at a steady speed keeps doing exactly that, unless the forces on it are out of balance. This tendency to resist a change of motion is **inertia**. In steady straight and level cruise the four forces balance: lift equals weight, thrust equals drag. There is no net force, so the aeroplane neither speeds up, slows down, climbs nor descends. Balance does not mean "no motion"; it means "no change of motion". The pen that floats off the glareshield in turbulence shows the same law: it carries on in a straight line while the aeroplane drops away under it.

![Which forces balance in steady level flight?](diagram:four-forces-level-flight "Lift equals weight and thrust equals drag: with no net force the aeroplane holds its speed and height.")

**Second law: a net force makes a mass accelerate.** The acceleration is the net force divided by the mass, $a = F/m$. Open the throttle at the start of the take-off roll and thrust exceeds drag and rolling friction; the difference accelerates the aeroplane down the runway. The same law explains why a heavier aeroplane accelerates more slowly and needs more runway: the same excess thrust is pushing more mass. A turn is an acceleration too, a change of direction rather than speed, so it also needs a net force: you bank so that part of the lift points towards the centre of the turn.

**Third law: every force has an equal and opposite reaction.** You cannot push on something without it pushing back on you. The propeller drives a mass of air backwards; the air drives the propeller, and the aeroplane, forwards. The wing deflects air downwards behind it; the air pushes the wing upwards. That is why a wing in flight leaves a **downwash** behind it, and why a large aeroplane leaves wake turbulence: the air it pushed down is still moving.

![Where do Newton's three laws show up in flight?](diagram:newtons-laws-in-flight "Balanced forces mean no change of motion; a net 1,500 N on 1,000 kg gives 1.5 m/s²; push air back or down and it pushes you forward or up.")

!!! example "Worked example: the second law on the take-off roll"
    Given: an aeroplane of mass 1,000 kg early in the take-off roll, with a thrust of 2,500 N and drag plus rolling friction of 1,000 N.

    1. Net force: $2500 - 1000 = \mathbf{1{,}500}$ N forward.
    2. Acceleration: $a = F/m = 1500 / 1000 = \mathbf{1.5}$ m/s².

    Carry 200 kg more and the same 1,500 N gives $1500 / 1200 = 1.25$ m/s²: slower acceleration, a longer run to lift-off speed.

??? check "Check yourself: in a steady climb at constant airspeed, is there a net force on the aeroplane?"
    No. The speed and direction are constant, so by the first law the forces are in balance. The climb is not an acceleration; thrust is simply larger than drag by the amount needed to balance the part of the weight acting back along the climbing path.

## Forces have direction: adding them tip to tail

A force has a size and a direction, and both matter. Quantities like that are **vectors**, the same idea as the wind velocity in [BAKC 2.2](/notes/BAKC/2.2), which is a speed with a direction. You draw a vector as an arrow: its length is the size, its direction is the direction.

To add two forces you cannot just add the numbers, because they may not point the same way. Instead, draw the first arrow, then draw the second starting from the **tip** of the first. The arrow from the tail of the first to the tip of the second is the **resultant**: the single force that has the same effect as the two together. This is **tip-to-tail** addition. Two forces in the same direction add as ordinary numbers; two in opposite directions subtract; two at right angles form the sides of a right-angled triangle, and the resultant is its long side.

![How do you add two forces that point different ways?](diagram:vector-addition-tip-to-tail "Start the second arrow at the tip of the first; the resultant runs from the first tail to the last tip, so equal and opposite forces add to zero.")

A steady glide shows why this matters. With the engine at idle there is no thrust, just lift, drag and weight. Lift acts at right angles to the flight path and drag acts back along it. Added tip to tail, lift and drag make a resultant that points straight up and exactly balances the weight. That is how the aeroplane can be in equilibrium, with no net force, while it descends: the path is tilted so that the lift and drag together can hold the weight.

![How can lift and drag together hold up the weight in a glide?](diagram:forces-in-a-glide "Lift and drag add to a resultant that is equal and opposite to the weight; the glide angle is set by the ratio of drag to lift.")

!!! example "Worked example: adding lift and drag in a glide"
    Given: a 1,000 kg aeroplane (weight about 9,810 N) in a steady glide with a lift to drag ratio of 10. Lift is about 9,760 N and drag about 976 N, at right angles to each other.

    1. Add tip to tail; the forces are at right angles, so use Pythagoras: $R = \sqrt{9760^2 + 976^2}$.
    2. $R = \sqrt{95{,}257{,}600 + 952{,}576} = \sqrt{96{,}210{,}176} = \mathbf{9{,}809}$ N.

    The resultant equals the weight to within rounding, and it points straight up. That is the condition for a steady glide.

In a climb the same idea runs the other way. Split the weight into two parts, one along the path and one across it. The part along the path acts backwards like extra drag, so the engine must supply thrust to overcome drag plus that part of the weight. You will meet this again in the climbing and descending lessons.

![What extra job does thrust do in a climb?](diagram:forces-in-a-climb "Part of the weight acts back along the climb path, so thrust must equal drag plus that part of the weight.")

??? check "Check yourself: two forces of 300 N act on a body, one pointing forward and one pointing back. What is the resultant, and what happens to the body's motion?"
    Zero. Added tip to tail, the second arrow returns to where the first began. With no net force the body keeps its present motion, whether that is at rest or moving steadily.

## Kinetic and potential energy

**Energy** is the capacity to do **work**, and work is done when a force moves something through a distance. Push a stalled car 10 m along a road and you have done work; the car now has the energy of motion it did not have before. An aeroplane in flight carries two forms of mechanical energy, and the whole of energy management is about the balance between them.

### Kinetic energy: the energy of speed

**Kinetic energy (KE)** is the energy a body has because it is moving:

$$KE = \tfrac{1}{2} m v^2$$

where $m$ is the mass and $v$ the speed. Two things follow. Kinetic energy is proportional to **mass**: a heavier aeroplane at the same speed has more of it. And it goes with the **square** of the speed: **doubling the speed gives four times the kinetic energy**, and even a small increase in speed adds a disproportionate amount.

For an aeroplane the speed that matters for flying is its speed through the air, so kinetic energy is tied to **airspeed**. On the ground, during the landing roll, what the brakes have to absorb depends on the speed over the ground, which is why a tailwind landing is so much longer.

![Why does a little extra speed mean a lot more energy?](diagram:kinetic-energy-speed-squared "Kinetic energy goes with speed squared: 10 percent fast is 21 percent more energy, double the speed is four times.")

!!! example "Worked example: what a fast approach costs"
    Given: the POH approach speed is 65 kt. Case 1: you touch down 10 percent fast. Case 2: you touch down at 75 kt.

    1. Case 1 energy ratio: $1.1^2 = \mathbf{1.21}$: **21 percent** more energy to dissipate.
    2. Case 2 speed ratio: $75 / 65 = 1.154$.
    3. Case 2 energy ratio: $1.154^2 = \mathbf{1.33}$: **33 percent** more energy.

    Ten knots fast does not sound much, but the brakes, tyres and drag must absorb a third more energy. That is why excess approach speed lengthens the landing roll so much, and why it is a common cause of long landings.

### Potential energy: the energy of height

**Potential energy (PE)** is energy stored by **height** in the Earth's gravity:

$$PE = m g h$$

where $g$ is the acceleration due to gravity, 9.81 m/s², and $h$ is the height. It took work to lift the aeroplane there, and it is all available to be given back as the aeroplane comes down. Potential energy goes up in a straight line with height: double the height above the ground and you double the potential energy. Like kinetic energy, it is proportional to mass.

The two kinds of energy are interchangeable. Lower the nose and gravity accelerates the aeroplane: height becomes speed. Raise the nose and the aeroplane slows as it climbs: speed becomes height. In the absence of drag and thrust, the total stays the same and only the share changes. This is the **conservation of energy**, and it is the most useful single idea in this subtopic.

### Speed as stored height

Put the two formulas side by side and ask: if all the kinetic energy at a given speed were turned into height, how much height would it buy? Set $\tfrac{1}{2} m v^2 = m g h$; the mass cancels, which means the answer is the same for a Jabiru and a jumbo jet:

$$h = \frac{v^2}{2g}$$

At 100 kt, which is 51.4 m/s, that height is $2646 / 19.6 = 135$ m, about **440 ft**. Every aeroplane flying at 100 kt carries, in its speed, the equivalent of about 440 ft of height. This is why pilots call airspeed **"stored height"**: it can be cashed in for altitude by raising the nose, and height can be cashed in for speed by lowering it.

How aeroplanes use the two stores, in ordinary flying:

- **Take-off.** The engine turns the chemical energy in the fuel into kinetic energy along the runway, accelerating to lift-off speed, then into potential energy in the climb.
- **Pull-up or zoom.** Raising the nose without adding power trades kinetic energy for potential energy: you gain height and lose airspeed.
- **Glide.** With the engine at idle, there is no thrust doing work. The aeroplane steadily gives up potential energy to do the work against drag, which is why a glide must descend.
- **Landing.** All the kinetic energy at touchdown must be absorbed by the brakes, tyres and drag.

??? check "Check yourself: in a glide with the engine at idle, why does the aeroplane have to descend?"
    Because drag is always taking energy away and, with no thrust, the only store left to pay for it is potential energy. Holding a steady airspeed, the aeroplane converts height into the work done against drag, so it must lose height continuously.

## Aircraft energy state

Add the two stores together and you have the **aircraft energy state**: the **total mechanical energy** the aeroplane has at a moment, kinetic energy (airspeed) plus potential energy (height). It describes how much margin the pilot has to work with. Because the mass cancels in "speed as stored height", it is often easiest to think of the energy state as a single height: the actual height plus the extra height the speed would buy. Two aeroplanes at 1,000 ft, one at 60 kt and one at 100 kt, are in very different energy states: the faster one has the equivalent of roughly 280 ft more to spend.

A **high energy state** is fast, high, or both. It is useful when you need range or options, for reaching a forced-landing field or clearing terrain, but on approach it means too fast or too high: floating, long landings and overruns.

A **low energy state** is slow, low, or both: close to the stall with little height to recover. Many loss-of-control accidents begin here, typically on the base turn or final, where there is neither speed nor height to spare.

The pilot manages the energy state with three tools, and it pays to keep them separate in your mind:

- The **throttle** adds energy to the system or stops adding it. More power is the only way to raise the total.
- The **elevator** shares the existing energy between speed and height. It cannot create energy; it only moves it from one store to the other.
- **Drag**, always present and increased by flap, removes energy. It is how you get rid of an excess.

![How do power and flap change a descent at the same airspeed?](diagram:powered-descent-vs-glide "Power adds energy and flattens the path; flap adds drag, removes energy and steepens it, all at the same indicated airspeed.")

??? check "Check yourself: you are on final, on the correct approach path but 10 kt fast. Which tool removes the excess energy, and which only moves it?"
    More drag (flap, if within its limiting speed) or less power removes the excess. Raising the nose with the elevator only moves the energy from speed into height, which would put you above the approach path instead.

## Accelerating, decelerating, climbing and descending

The third element asks what four simple manoeuvres do to the energy state. Each one is a change in one store, the other or both, and the question is always whether the total changed, and if so where the energy came from or went.

**Accelerating at constant height.** Kinetic energy rises; potential energy stays the same. The total has gone up, so the energy came from outside: the engine has done more work than drag has removed. You opened the throttle.

**Decelerating at constant height.** Kinetic energy falls; potential energy stays the same. The total has gone down, so drag has taken more than the engine provided. You reduced power, or extended flap.

**Climbing at constant speed.** Kinetic energy stays the same; potential energy rises. The total has gone up, so the engine supplied it: thrust exceeds drag by enough to lift the weight. This is a normal climb, and it is why climb performance depends on spare power.

**Descending at constant speed.** Kinetic energy stays the same; potential energy falls. The total has gone down: you reduced power, and the energy lost from height has gone into overcoming drag.

**Zoom climb or dive with power unchanged.** Here one store rises while the other falls, and, apart from what drag takes, the total is roughly constant. That is the trade, and it is the case the exam likes most.

| Change | Kinetic energy | Potential energy | Total energy state |
|---|---|---|---|
| Accelerate at constant height | Increases | No change | Increases (engine work exceeds drag) |
| Decelerate at constant height | Decreases | No change | Decreases (drag exceeds thrust) |
| Climb at constant speed | No change | Increases | Increases (engine supplies the extra) |
| Descend at constant speed | No change | Decreases | Decreases (energy lost to drag; power reduced) |
| Zoom climb, power unchanged | Decreases | Increases | Roughly constant (traded) |
| Dive, power unchanged | Increases | Decreases | Roughly constant (traded) |

The rearranged energy formula tells you how much height a zoom can buy. Ignoring drag, the kinetic energy you give up between two speeds becomes height:

$$h = \frac{v_1^2 - v_2^2}{2g}$$

with the speeds in metres per second (1 kt = 0.514 m/s).

![What happens to the energy in a zoom climb?](diagram:energy-trade-zoom "With the throttle fixed the total stays the same: the kinetic energy lost between 100 kt and 60 kt reappears as about 280 ft of height.")

!!! example "Worked example: a zoom from 100 kt to 60 kt"
    Given: 100 kt = 51.4 m/s, 60 kt = 30.9 m/s, $g = 9.81$ m/s², drag ignored.

    1. Squares: $51.4^2 = 2642$; $30.9^2 = 955$.
    2. Height: $h = \dfrac{2642 - 955}{2 \times 9.81} = \dfrac{1687}{19.6} \approx \mathbf{86}$ m.
    3. In feet: $86 \times 3.28 \approx \mathbf{280}$ ft.

    In practice drag makes the real gain smaller, but the example shows that airspeed is stored height.

!!! example "Worked example: a zoom from 120 kt to 80 kt"
    Given: 120 kt = 61.7 m/s, 80 kt = 41.2 m/s, drag ignored.

    1. Squares: $61.7^2 = 3807$; $41.2^2 = 1697$.
    2. Height: $h = \dfrac{3807 - 1697}{19.6} = \dfrac{2110}{19.6} \approx \mathbf{108}$ m.
    3. In feet: $108 \times 3.28 \approx \mathbf{350}$ ft.

    The same 40 kt loss buys more height starting from a higher speed, because the energy goes with the square: the top 40 kt of a fast aeroplane's speed holds more energy than the bottom 40 kt.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    "Climbing" does not by itself mean the energy state has increased. If the airspeed fell while you climbed with the power unchanged, you only traded speed for height and the total is roughly the same. The total rises only if the engine supplied more work than drag removed: the speed held or rose while you climbed, or rose while you held height.

??? check "Check yourself: an aeroplane climbs at a constant airspeed with the throttle fixed. Has its total energy changed, and where did any change come from?"
    It has increased: kinetic energy is unchanged and potential energy has risen. Since nothing was traded from speed, the extra energy must have come from the engine, through thrust exceeding drag.

## Bringing it together

You are cruising at 110 kt and 2,500 ft over farmland east of the Scarp when the engine stops. Your energy state at that moment is everything you have: 2,500 ft of height plus the height stored in 110 kt. The POH best glide speed is 70 kt, the speed at which the aeroplane loses the least energy to drag for each mile covered. Holding 110 kt would waste energy on high drag, so you raise the nose and let the speed decay to 70 kt, trading the excess kinetic energy for height. Ignoring drag, 110 kt (56.6 m/s) down to 70 kt (36.0 m/s) is worth $\left(3204 - 1297\right) / 19.6 \approx 97$ m, about 320 ft; drag takes some of it, so the altimeter will show less, but it is height you would otherwise have thrown away. From there the glide spends potential energy at a steady rate to pay for drag. The field you pick has to be within reach of that energy. Over the field you find you are high: rather than diving, which would only turn the excess height into excess speed and a float, you extend flap to add drag and remove energy, and you touch down at the right speed. Every element of this lesson was in that one forced landing: speed as stored height, the total energy state, and what a climb, a descent and a deceleration each do to it. The POH procedure for your type governs the details.

## Key points

- **2.5.1** Kinetic energy is the energy of speed, $\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$: double the speed, four times the energy; 10 percent fast, 21 percent more. Potential energy is the energy of height, $mgh$. Both grow with mass, and each can be traded for the other: airspeed is stored height ($h = v^2/2g$, about 440 ft at 100 kt).
- **2.5.2** The aircraft energy state is the total of kinetic and potential energy: speed plus height. High is fast or high (floats on approach); low is slow or low (near the stall, little to recover with). The throttle adds energy, the elevator shares it, drag removes it.
- **2.5.3** Accelerating level and climbing at constant speed raise the total (the engine pays); decelerating level and descending at constant speed lower it (drag takes it). A zoom or dive with fixed power only trades one store for the other. Underlying all of it: forces in balance mean no change of motion (Newton's first law), a net force accelerates a mass (second law), and forces add as vectors, tip to tail.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    The questions here are about the trade. If the speed falls while the aeroplane climbs with the throttle fixed, energy has been traded, not gained. If it climbs and the speed holds, the engine supplied the extra. In a glide the energy to overcome drag comes from height. Have by heart that kinetic energy goes with the square of the speed and potential energy goes in a straight line with height, and read every option for whether the total changed or only the share.
