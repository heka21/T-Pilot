## Why this matters

Engines rarely fail without warning: a needle creeps, a light flickers, the RPM sags, and the pilot who reads those signs early can fix the problem or pick a field while the engine still runs. This lesson takes each system from [BAKC 3.1](/notes/BAKC/3.1) and asks: what do you see, what do you do, and what happens if it cannot be fixed? Its second half is about **carburettor ice**, which can form on a mild, humid morning over Rottnest and is where the exam's questions cluster.

## When a component fails

### Alternator

When the alternator stops, the battery quietly takes over. The **ammeter** shows a **discharge**, the **voltmeter** sags, many aeroplanes show a **warning light**, and later radios fade. Check the **alternator switch** and **field breaker**, cycle the alternator once if the POH allows, and if it stays off **reduce the load to essentials**. The battery may last perhaps **30 to 60 minutes**; then you lose radios, transponder, electric flaps, fuel pump and lights, so land before that. The engine runs on regardless.

### Magneto

A failed magneto shows as **rough running and lost RPM**, or an **excessive drop on the magneto check** ([BAKC 3.3](/notes/BAKC/3.3)). Select each magneto in turn and stay on the one that runs smoothly. You keep an engine, with less power and **no redundancy**: land as soon as practicable.

![Why does a light aeroplane have two magnetos and two spark plugs per cylinder?](diagram:dual-ignition-system "Each magneto feeds one plug in every cylinder through its own leads, so one failure costs a little power, not the engine. The switch stops a magneto by earthing it.")

### Battery

A failing battery shows as an **engine that will not crank**, weak electrical services, or a **discharge even with the alternator working**. Charge or replace it, and **do not hand-swing the propeller** unless trained and approved. A flat battery is no reserve and cannot absorb the alternator's surges, so electrical reliability suffers.

### Ignition switch

A faulty switch or P-lead shows as the **engine still running at OFF** on the dead cut, or a magneto that **cannot be selected**. Report it, and treat the **propeller as live**: a live magneto fires the engine if the propeller is moved by hand, and that kills people.

### Fuel vent blockage, fuel or booster pump

A blocked vent or failed pump shows as **falling fuel pressure**, then **rough running**, then a **stopped engine**; a blocked vent can buckle the tank. Follow the fuel's path: **booster pump on** (it takes over from a failed engine pump), **change tanks** (the other vent is probably clear), **mixture rich**. Otherwise fuel starvation means a forced landing.

![Where are the vents, pumps and drains in the fuel system?](diagram:fuel-system-schematic "Fuel flows from a vented tank through the selector and strainer to the booster and engine-driven pumps; a blocked vent or failed pump starves the engine with fuel in the tank.")

### Oil cooler and cowl flaps

A blocked oil cooler, or **cowl flaps** (doors that let more cooling air out of the cowling) stuck shut, traps heat: **rising oil temperature and CHT**, then **falling oil pressure** as the oil thins. **Open the cowl flaps, enrich, reduce power, lower the nose.** Unresolved, overheating leads to detonation and failure.

### Vacuum pump

A failing vacuum pump shows as **low suction**, perhaps a **flag**, and attitude and directional indicators that **slow, drift and topple**. Cross-check with the **turn coordinator**, **compass** and **pressure instruments** and stay visual: with no reliable attitude reference, do not enter cloud ([BAKC 3.5](/notes/BAKC/3.5)).

### Hydraulic brakes

A brake leak shows as a **spongy or sinking pedal**, a **pull to one side**, or **fluid** on the wheel. On the ground, get it fixed. Found on landing: pump the pedal, use the good brake with rudder and aerodynamic braking, choose a long runway; otherwise you may run off the end.

??? check "Check yourself: why is a magneto failure a reason to land soon even though the engine is still running smoothly on the other one?"
    Because the redundancy is gone. Dual ignition exists so that one failure does not stop the engine; with one magneto out, a single further failure in the remaining system (a lead, the magneto itself) stops it.

## When a gauge looks wrong

A needle is out of place: what could cause it, and what do you do?

**Oil pressure low with oil temperature rising.** Together these mean the engine is losing oil or failing to circulate it: low oil quantity, a leak, a pump failure or a blocked cooler. Less oil carries the same heat, so temperature rises as pressure falls. Reduce power, enrich, and **land as soon as possible**. If the pressure falls but the temperature stays steady, the gauge itself may be at fault, but treat it as real until you are on the ground. Unresolved, the engine seizes.

**Oil temperature high on its own**, with normal pressure, is usually the operation rather than a fault: a hot day, a long slow climb, cowl flaps closed. Lower the nose, reduce power, open the cowl flaps, or the oil breaks down.

**CHT high** means the cylinders are too hot: a lean mixture, high power at low airspeed, cowl flaps closed, or detonation already happening. Enrich, reduce power, increase airspeed, open the cowl flaps. Otherwise detonation and engine damage follow.

**Fuel pressure low** means fuel is not reaching the engine properly: a failed pump, a blocked vent, an empty tank or vapour lock. Booster pump on, change tanks, mixture rich. Otherwise the engine stops.

**The tachometer** can be wrong in two directions. **RPM falling with the throttle unchanged** in a fixed-pitch aeroplane points to carburettor ice first, then a magneto problem or a propeller fault: apply carburettor heat, then check the magnetos. **RPM over the red line** is usually a fixed-pitch propeller in a dive: throttle back and raise the nose. Unresolved, these lead to engine failure or over-stress.

**Ammeter or loadmeter.** A discharge, or zero load, means the alternator has stopped. A **very high charge** that does not settle after start means a battery or voltage regulator fault, and a runaway charge can overheat the battery. Reduce load, check breakers, cycle the alternator.

**Voltmeter.** Low voltage means the alternator is not charging. **High voltage** means the regulator has failed and the alternator is overcharging: switch the alternator off, as the POH directs, before it damages the battery and avionics.

**Engine icing** shows as a **gradual loss of RPM** (fixed pitch) or of **manifold pressure** (constant speed), often with rough running. Apply **full carburettor heat and leave it on** until the engine recovers. Unresolved, the engine stops. The rest of this lesson is about this row.

| Gauge | Abnormality and likely cause | Actions | Consequence if unresolved |
|---|---|---|---|
| Oil pressure low, temperature rising | Low oil quantity, oil leak, pump failure, cooler blocked | Reduce power, enrich, land as soon as possible; if pressure falls with a steady temperature suspect the gauge, but treat it as real | Engine seizure |
| Oil temperature high alone | Hot day, long climb at low speed, cowl flaps closed | Lower the nose, reduce power, open cowl flaps | Oil breakdown and overheating |
| CHT high | Lean mixture, high power at low airspeed, cowl flaps closed, detonation | Enrich, reduce power, increase airspeed, open cowl flaps | Detonation and engine damage |
| Fuel pressure low | Pump failure, blocked vent, empty tank, vapour lock | Booster pump on, change tanks, mixture rich | Engine stops |
| Tachometer | RPM falling with the throttle unchanged: carburettor ice, magneto problem, propeller fault. RPM over the red line: fixed-pitch propeller in a dive | Carburettor heat, magneto check, throttle back or raise the nose for an overspeed | Engine failure or over-stress |
| Ammeter or load meter | Discharge or zero load: alternator failed. Very high charge: battery fault or regulator fault | Reduce load, check breakers, cycle the alternator | Battery flat; a runaway charge can overheat the battery |
| Voltmeter | Low: alternator not charging. High: regulator failed | As for the ammeter; switch off the alternator if voltage is high | Electrical loss or damage to avionics |
| Engine icing | Gradual loss of RPM (fixed pitch) or manifold pressure (constant speed), rough running | Full carburettor heat and leave it on until the engine recovers | Engine stops |

??? check "Check yourself: oil pressure is falling and oil temperature is rising together. Why is that combination more serious than a high oil temperature alone?"
    Together they point to the engine losing oil or its circulation: less oil is carrying the heat, so it gets hotter, and less is reaching the bearings, so pressure falls. A high temperature alone usually just means a hot day or a slow climb. The combination means seizure is coming: land as soon as possible.

## How carburettor ice forms

Recall the carburettor from [BAKC 3.1](/notes/BAKC/3.1): air speeds up through the venturi and its pressure drops, fuel sprays into it, and the throttle butterfly above the venturi sets how much mixture reaches the cylinders. Two things happen to that air that make it cold.

**Expansion.** When air's pressure drops, its temperature drops with it. The air in the venturi and squeezing past a partly closed butterfly is at lower pressure, so it is colder.

**Evaporation.** Liquid fuel turning into vapour takes heat from its surroundings, the way sweat cools your skin. The fuel evaporating into the airstream takes that heat from the air.

Together these can lower the air temperature by **20 to 30 degrees**. Air entering at 20 degrees can be below freezing by the time it passes the butterfly. If that air is humid, its water vapour condenses and freezes onto the cold metal, narrowing the passage and choking the engine of mixture. That is why **carburettor ice does not need freezing weather**: it needs **moisture** and a **temperature drop**, and the carburettor supplies the temperature drop itself.

## Three kinds of engine ice

The ice forms in three places, under different conditions, with different severity.

**Throttle ice** forms on and just behind the **partly closed throttle butterfly**, where the pressure drop, and so the cooling, is greatest. It is most likely at **low power** (descent, taxi, idle, the glide approach), when the butterfly is nearly closed, in **high humidity** and at outside temperatures of roughly **minus 5 to plus 20 degrees**. It is the most **common and most dangerous** kind: around a nearly closed butterfly the gap is tiny, so a small amount of ice blocks it, and an engine at low power has little heat to resist.

**Fuel evaporation ice** (refrigeration ice) forms in the **venturi and the induction passage downstream of the fuel jet**, where the fuel evaporates. Because evaporation happens at any throttle setting, it can form at **any power**, including cruise, in **high humidity** at outside temperatures of roughly **minus 10 to plus 30 degrees**. It is **serious**, and the one that catches you in cruise when you are not expecting ice.

**Impact ice** forms on the **air intake, filter and intake ducting** when **visible moisture** (cloud, rain, sleet) at or **below freezing** strikes them and freezes on contact, just as ice forms on a wing. It can **block the air supply** completely. It affects fuel-injected engines as well as carburettors, and the alternate air source exists to bypass it.

![Where does each kind of engine ice form?](diagram:carburettor-ice-locations "Throttle ice at the nearly closed butterfly is the most dangerous; fuel evaporation ice forms in the venturi at any power; impact ice blocks the intake.")

The CASA carburettor icing chart plots the risk against outside air temperature and humidity (dew point). It shows **serious icing at any power** possible from well below freezing to about **30 degrees** when humidity is high, and **serious icing at descent power** over an even wider range. High humidity is the common factor, which makes coastal Western Australia in the cooler months, and any day with showers about, a carburettor icing environment.

![At what temperatures can engine ice form?](diagram:carburettor-ice-temperature-ranges "In humid air every kind except impact ice is possible well above freezing, roughly up to 20 or 30 degrees.")

| Type | Where and when | Conditions | Severity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Throttle ice | On and behind the partly closed throttle butterfly, where the pressure drop is largest | Most likely at low power (descent, taxi, idle) with the butterfly nearly closed, high humidity, temperatures roughly minus 5 to plus 20 degrees | The most common and most dangerous: a small amount of ice at a nearly closed butterfly can stop the engine |
| Fuel evaporation (refrigeration) ice | In the venturi and manifold downstream of the fuel jet, where fuel evaporates | Any power setting, worst with high humidity and outside temperatures roughly minus 10 to plus 30 degrees | Serious; builds up at cruise power where throttle ice is less likely |
| Impact ice | On the air intake, filter and intake ducting | Visible moisture (cloud, rain, sleet) at temperatures at or below freezing | Can block the air supply; the alternate air source bypasses it |

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The exam's distractor is "carburettor ice is most likely on cold, dry days near freezing." It is the opposite: it needs moisture, and the most dangerous kind is most likely at **low power** in **humid air** at **mild temperatures**. A clear, frosty, bone-dry winter morning at Merredin is a lower risk than a grey, drizzly 15 degree morning at Jandakot.

??? check "Check yourself: why is throttle ice most likely during a glide approach rather than in cruise?"
    In the glide the throttle butterfly is nearly closed, so the air squeezing past it has its largest pressure drop and cooling, and the gap is small enough for a little ice to block. The engine at idle also has little heat to melt it.

## The danger of chasing the RPM

You are cruising in a fixed-pitch trainer and notice the RPM is a little lower than you set. Nothing else seems wrong, so you nudge the throttle forward and the RPM comes back. Ten minutes later it has crept down again, and you nudge it again.

Behind the panel, ice is growing in the carburettor, narrowing the passage, so less mixture reaches the cylinders and the RPM falls. Opening the throttle wider lets more mixture past the ice, so the RPM recovers, but the ice keeps growing. Each throttle increment **hides the symptom** while the cause gets worse. Eventually the throttle is fully open, there is nothing left to give, the ice keeps building, and the engine stops. By then the engine has little heat left to melt so much ice.

![Why does opening the throttle hide carburettor ice?](diagram:chasing-the-rpm "Each extra bit of throttle buys the RPM back while the ice keeps growing, until full throttle leaves nothing and the engine stops.")

So the rule is: any **unexplained loss of RPM** (or manifold pressure) is **carburettor ice until proven otherwise**. Apply **full carburettor heat and leave it on**, and only then think about the throttle.

## Checking the carburettor heat before take-off

The check uses the fact that hot air is less dense, so an engine breathing it makes less power.

During the run-up, at the POH check RPM (often 1,700 to 1,800 RPM), apply **full carburettor heat** and watch the tachometer.

- The RPM should **drop**, typically by about 100 to 150 RPM in a trainer (your POH may give its own figure), because the heated air is less dense. A drop proves hot air is reaching the carburettor.
- Leave the heat on for several seconds. If the RPM then **rises while the heat is still on**, there **was ice** in the carburettor: the hot air has melted it and the engine is breathing more freely.
- Return the control to **cold**. The RPM should return to its original value, or a little higher if ice was cleared.
- **No drop at all** means the heat is **not working**: a disconnected control, a damaged hot-air duct or flap. Do not take off into conditions where carburettor ice is likely.

![What should the RPM do during the carburettor heat check?](diagram:carb-heat-check-rpm "Heat on: the RPM drops. A rise while the heat is still on means there was ice. Back to cold: the RPM returns.")

!!! example "Worked example: reading the carburettor heat check"
    Given: run-up at 1,700 RPM on a mild, humid morning. Full heat applied.

    1. Heat on: the RPM falls to 1,580. Drop: $1700 - 1580 = \mathbf{120\ RPM}$, within the normal 100 to 150: hot air is reaching the carburettor.
    2. Heat held on for ten seconds: the RPM stays at 1,580. **No rise**, so there was no ice.
    3. Heat off (cold): the RPM returns to **1,700**, the starting value.

    The system works and the carburettor was clear. Select cold before take-off so full power is available.

Now try two variations before opening the solution. (a) Same run-up: heat on, the RPM falls to 1,560, then climbs to 1,610 with the heat still on; back on cold it settles at 1,740. (b) Heat on, the RPM stays at 1,700.

??? solution "Show the solution"
    (a) Drop $1700 - 1560 = 140$ RPM, a working system. The rise of $1610 - 1560 = 50$ RPM with the heat still on means **ice was present** and has melted. Back on cold the RPM is **40 higher** than when you started, because the starting figure was already reduced by the ice. Taxiing at low power in humid air iced the carburettor; expect more and use heat as the POH requires.

    (b) **No drop** means hot air is not reaching the carburettor: the carburettor heat is **not working**. The aeroplane is unserviceable for any flight where carburettor ice is possible, which on a humid morning is every flight.

## Using carburettor heat

### Anti-icing: preventing ice

Use carburettor heat **before** ice has a chance to form: before reducing power for a descent or a prolonged low-power approach, and when flying in high humidity, as the POH directs. Use **full heat or none**. Partial heat without a carburettor air temperature gauge can warm air that was well below freezing up into the icing range, making ice more likely, not less.

### De-icing: removing ice that has formed

When you suspect ice, apply **full heat and keep it on**. Expect the engine to run **rougher** and the RPM to **fall further** at first. The hot air is less dense (the normal drop), and the melting ice is going through the engine as water. Do not turn the heat off because of the roughness; hold it on. As the ice clears, the RPM rises, the engine smooths out, and when you return to cold the RPM settles **higher than before** you applied the heat.

![How does the RPM tell you whether there was carburettor ice?](diagram:carburettor-heat-decision "Ice: a drop, roughness, a rise while the heat is on, and higher RPM than before on cold. No ice: a small drop that stays, then back to the original.")

### Ground operation

Carburettor heat air is taken from around the exhaust and is normally **unfiltered**. On the ground, especially on a dusty strip, it draws **dust and grit** straight into the engine, where it wears the cylinders and rings. So use it only for the pre-take-off check unless the POH says otherwise, and select **cold before take-off** so full power is available.

??? check "Check yourself: you apply full carburettor heat for suspected ice and the engine immediately runs rougher. Do you leave the heat on, and why?"
    Yes. The roughness is the melted ice passing through the engine plus the expected power loss from less dense hot air. Turning the heat off leaves the ice to keep building. Hold full heat until the engine smooths and the RPM recovers.

## Alternate air and carburettor heat

The two controls look alike and both bring in a different air source, but they solve different problems.

**Carburettor heat** is fitted to **carburettor engines**. It brings **hot air** from a muff around the exhaust into the carburettor, to **prevent or melt carburettor ice** inside it.

**Alternate air** is fitted to **fuel-injected engines**, which cannot form carburettor ice because they have no venturi ([BAKC 3.1](/notes/BAKC/3.1)). Their risk is a **blocked air intake**: impact ice on the filter, dust, or a collapsed filter. Alternate air opens a second, **unfiltered** air source from inside the cowling that **bypasses** the blocked intake. On many engines the alternate air door opens **automatically** when the intake blocks and the suction behind it rises.

## What carburettor heat costs

Hot air is **less dense**, so the engine draws in less mass of air on each stroke and makes **less power**: the **RPM drops** with a fixed-pitch propeller, the **manifold pressure drops** with a constant-speed one. The carburettor delivers about the same fuel for the same volume of air, so the less dense air makes the **mixture richer**. Expect a slight loss of performance, **higher fuel consumption** for the power produced, and slightly rougher running; lean as the POH permits if heat must stay on for long.

That is why carburettor heat is **not used for take-off** unless the POH requires it: you lose take-off power, and hot intake air on an engine already working hard at full power raises the risk of **detonation**. Select cold before take-off and before a go-around.

??? check "Check yourself: why does carburettor heat make the mixture richer?"
    The hot air is less dense, so a given volume of it contains less mass of air (and oxygen), while the carburettor meters about the same fuel for that volume. Same fuel, less air: a richer mixture.

## Fuel vaporisation

Fuel is a liquid only as long as it is cool enough for its pressure. Heat it, or lower the pressure on it, and it boils into **vapour**. That happens most on **hot days**, and especially after a **hot engine has been shut down and restarted**: with no cooling air flowing, heat soaks from the engine into the fuel lines and pump, and the fuel in them boils. A pump designed to move liquid cannot move bubbles, so the supply to the engine becomes intermittent. This is **vapour lock**.

The symptoms are what an intermittent fuel supply looks like: **fluctuating fuel pressure and fuel flow**, **rough running**, **surging**, and **difficulty starting**.

![Why does a hot restart give fluctuating fuel pressure?](diagram:vapour-lock "What to notice: heat soak boils the fuel in the lines and a pump cannot move vapour; the booster pump raises the pressure, pushing the vapour through or collapsing it back to liquid.")

To rectify it, switch on the **electric booster pump**: it raises the pressure in the lines, which pushes the vapour through or collapses it back into liquid, and restores the flow of liquid fuel. Enrich the mixture if needed, and follow the **POH hot-start procedure**. On fuel-injected engines that procedure usually includes keeping the **mixture at idle cut-off until the engine fires**, so that the vapour is cleared without flooding the engine. The POH procedure for your type takes precedence over any generic one.

## Key points

- **3.4.1** Each component failure has a cockpit sign, an action and a consequence: alternator (discharge; shed load; land before the battery dies), magneto (rough, stay on the good one, no redundancy), battery (no crank; never hand-swing untrained), ignition switch (live propeller), vent or pump (falling fuel pressure; booster pump, change tanks), cooler or cowl flaps (overheating), vacuum pump (gyros topple; stay visual), brakes (spongy pedal; long runway).
- **3.4.2** Low oil pressure with rising temperature means losing oil: land. High CHT or oil temperature: enrich, reduce power, more airflow. Low fuel pressure: booster pump and tanks. Falling RPM: carburettor heat first. Discharge or low volts: alternator failed. High volts: regulator failed, alternator off.
- **3.4.3** Carburettor heat check at the POH RPM: a drop (typically about 100 to 150 RPM) proves heat; a rise with heat on means ice was present; no drop means the heat is not working.
- **3.4.4** Throttle ice: low power, humid, roughly minus 5 to plus 20 degrees, the most dangerous. Fuel evaporation ice: any power, humid, roughly minus 10 to plus 30. Impact ice: visible moisture at or below freezing, blocks the intake.
- **3.4.5** Opening the throttle to recover lost RPM hides the ice while it grows, until full throttle leaves nothing and the engine stops.
- **3.4.6** Anti-ice before low power, full heat or none; de-ice with full heat held on through the roughness; minimise use on the ground (unfiltered air) and select cold for take-off.
- **3.4.7** Carburettor heat melts or prevents ice in a carburettor; alternate air gives a fuel-injected engine an unfiltered path around a blocked intake.
- **3.4.8** Carburettor heat reduces power (RPM or manifold pressure drops) and richens the mixture.
- **3.4.9** Vapour lock: fluctuating fuel pressure and flow, rough running, surging, hard starting; booster pump on, enrich, POH hot-start procedure.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Two reliable exam facts: carburettor ice is most likely at low power in humid air at moderate temperatures, not in freezing weather; and the correct response to an unexplained RPM loss is full carburettor heat, left on, before touching the throttle. Know the three results of the carburettor heat check (drop, drop then rise, no drop) and what each means, and that alternate air is for fuel-injected engines and a blocked intake.
