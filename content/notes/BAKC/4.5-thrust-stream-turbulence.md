## What the exam expects (BAKC 4.5)

- **4.5.1** Describe how the danger from thrust stream turbulence (jet blast, propeller wash, rotor downwash) changes as the engine power changes and as you move closer to or further from the source.

## Why this matters

Wake turbulence ([BAKC 4.4](/notes/BAKC/4.4)) is the hazard you meet in the air. Thrust stream turbulence is its cousin on the ground: the fast, turbulent air blown backwards by an engine or downwards by a rotor. A light aeroplane weighs very little and presents large, light surfaces to the air, so a blast that a parked airliner hardly notices can lift a wing, swing the tail or flip a two-seat trainer onto its back. You will meet it taxiing at a regional airport such as Karratha, Kalgoorlie or Geraldton behind a jet with its beacon on, at the holding point behind a turboprop about to line up, in the run-up bay at Jandakot with another aircraft's propwash aimed at you, and near the helicopter pads where rescue and police helicopters hover-taxi. The exam asks one thing about it in several ways: how the hazard changes with the power the other engine is using and with your distance from it. This lesson builds that answer from how thrust is made.

## What thrust stream turbulence is

An engine makes thrust by throwing air backwards. That is Newton's third law: to push the aeroplane forward, the propeller or the jet engine must push a mass of air backwards, and the faster and the more air it accelerates each second, the more thrust it gets. The air it has thrown back does not stop when it leaves the engine. It carries on as a fast, turbulent stream behind the aircraft until it has mixed with enough surrounding air to slow down. That stream is **thrust stream turbulence**, and it comes in three forms.

**Jet blast** is the exhaust behind a jet engine. A jet accelerates a relatively small mass of air to a very high speed, so close behind the engine the blast is extremely fast, and near the exhaust it is also hot.

**Propeller wash** (prop wash, or slipstream) is the air behind a propeller. A propeller accelerates a larger mass of air to a lower speed, so the stream is slower than jet blast but still strong close behind, especially when the aircraft is running up or applying power to start moving.

**Rotor downwash** is the air blown down by a helicopter's rotor. A hovering helicopter's rotor must push down enough air every second to hold the whole weight of the helicopter up. That air hits the ground and spreads **outward along the surface in all directions**, so unlike jet blast it is not confined to the area behind the aircraft. It is strongest when the helicopter is hovering close to the ground or hover-taxiing.

Unlike wing-tip vortices, which come from **lift**, thrust stream turbulence comes from **thrust**. It is mainly a hazard **on the ground and close to it**: on aprons, taxiways, at holding points and in the run-up bay, and at low level near a hovering helicopter.

??? check "Check yourself: what is the difference in origin between wake turbulence and thrust stream turbulence?"
    Wake turbulence is the pair of wing-tip vortices left behind by a wing making lift. Thrust stream turbulence is the air accelerated backwards (or downwards, for a rotor) by an engine or rotor making thrust. One comes from lift, the other from thrust, which is why thrust stream turbulence matters most on the ground, where engines run at high power while the wing makes little lift.

## Why a fast stream pushes so hard

To see why the hazard changes as it does, go back to the lift formula in [BAKC 4.2](/notes/BAKC/4.2). The force that moving air exerts on a surface, whether it is a wing making lift or a tailplane being shoved sideways, goes with the dynamic pressure, $\tfrac{1}{2}\rho V^2$. The speed is squared. So a blast twice as fast pushes four times as hard, and a blast at half the speed pushes only a quarter as hard.

That single fact explains both halves of the exam answer. Anything that makes the stream faster where you are (more power at the source, or being closer to it) increases the force on your aeroplane steeply; anything that makes it slower (less power, more distance) reduces it steeply.

!!! example "Worked example: why a little faster is a lot worse"
    Given (illustrative speeds, not measured figures): at your position behind a turboprop, the propwash reaches you at 20 kt while it is taxiing. It then applies more power and the stream reaching you rises to 30 kt; on another occasion you are closer and the stream reaches you at 40 kt.

    1. Force ratio, 30 kt against 20 kt: $(30/20)^2 = 1.5^2 = \mathbf{2.25}$.
    2. Force ratio, 40 kt against 20 kt: $(40/20)^2 = 2^2 = \mathbf{4}$.

    A stream only 50 percent faster pushes more than twice as hard, and doubling its speed quadruples the push. Your trainer's tail and wings feel the force, not the speed, which is why the hazard grows so quickly with power and with closeness.

## How the hazard grows with engine power

The more power an engine is using, the more air it moves and the faster it throws it, so the stronger and longer the blast. **The hazard increases with power.** For the exam, and in practice, it helps to think of three settings.

**Idle, stationary.** The smallest hazard, but not zero. A large jet at idle still produces a strong blast close behind its engines, strong enough to blow a light aeroplane or a person over.

**Breakaway thrust.** An aircraft standing still has inertia ([BAKC 4.1](/notes/BAKC/4.1)) and its tyres have settled into the surface; to start it moving takes considerably more power than to keep it rolling. Turning on the ground needs extra power too, often on one engine, which swings the blast sideways across the apron as the aircraft turns. This **breakaway thrust** is **considerably greater than idle**, and it is used exactly where light aircraft are likely to be: on aprons, leaving parking bays, at the holding point as the aircraft starts to line up. A stationary jet with its beacon on that is about to move is therefore more dangerous to be behind than the same jet rolling steadily along a taxiway at idle.

![What does breakaway thrust do to the blast behind a turning jet?](diagram:breakaway-thrust-swing "Starting to move and turning takes considerably more than idle power, and the turn sweeps that blast sideways across the apron.")

**Take-off power, or an engine ground run.** The **greatest** hazard, with the blast extending a long way behind the aircraft. An aircraft lined up for take-off, or one doing a full-power engine run on the ground for maintenance, is producing as much thrust stream as it ever will.

| Power setting of the other aircraft | Hazard behind it |
|---|---|
| Idle, stationary | Smallest, but not zero; a large jet at idle still produces a strong blast close behind |
| **Breakaway thrust** (the power needed to start moving from rest or turn on the ground) | Considerably greater than idle, and often used close to other aircraft on aprons |
| Take-off power, or engine ground run | **Greatest**, extending a long way behind the aircraft |

The red flashing or rotating **anti-collision beacon** is your clue. It shows that engines are running or about to start, and it is switched on before the first engine turns. A beacon on a stationary aircraft means: assume breakaway thrust is coming.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    "A stationary aircraft is less dangerous than a moving one" sounds sensible and is wrong for thrust stream. The aircraft that has stopped and is about to move will apply breakaway thrust, considerably more than the idle thrust of one already taxiing steadily. The exam's trap answer is that the taxiing aircraft is worse.

??? check "Check yourself: why does breakaway thrust exceed the thrust needed to keep taxiing?"
    Because the aircraft at rest must be accelerated from a standstill against its inertia and the extra resistance of tyres settled on the surface, and turning on the ground needs extra power as well. Once it is rolling, much less thrust keeps it moving.

## How the hazard fades with distance

Once the stream leaves the engine it starts to mix with the still air around it. Its edges drag surrounding air along, so the stream widens and the same momentum is shared among more and more air. The result is a stream that is **wider but slower** the further it goes. **The hazard decreases with distance from the source.**

But it decreases **gradually**, not suddenly. A stream that is very fast close behind a large jet at high power still has plenty of speed after it has spread a long way. Behind a large jet at high power the blast can still be strong enough to flip a light aircraft, or to blow debris and people over, well over a hundred metres back (there is no single safe distance to memorise here; check the current AIP for any specified distances).

| Distance from source | Hazard |
|---|---|
| Close behind (or close to a hovering helicopter) | Highest: very high speed, possibly hot exhaust, flying stones and debris |
| Further away | Lower, but still significant for large aircraft at high power |

![How does the danger area behind a jet change with power and distance?](diagram:jet-blast-hazard-zones "Idle is smallest, breakaway thrust considerably bigger, take-off power biggest; the blast fades only gradually with distance.")

Power and distance work **together**. The hazard at your position depends on both: how hard the stream started out, and how much it has slowed by the time it reaches you. So a large jet at take-off power can be dangerous much further away than a light twin at idle close by. When you judge where to stop, ask both questions: what is that aircraft about to do with its power, and how far am I from it?

For a helicopter, the downwash spreads outward around the helicopter rather than streaming back, so the hazard is a ring around it rather than a cone behind it. A larger, heavier helicopter must push more air down to hold itself up, so it produces a **stronger and wider** area of disturbed air. Distance helps in the same way: the outward flow weakens as it spreads, but it reaches further than you might expect across a grass apron or a taxiway.

![Where does a hovering helicopter's downwash go?](diagram:rotor-downwash-outflow "Downwash hits the ground and spreads outward on every side: a ring round the helicopter, not a cone behind it.")

??? check "Check yourself: on a busy apron, a light twin is idling 20 m ahead of you and a large jet is starting its take-off run 150 m ahead. Which stream is the bigger worry, and why?"
    Probably the jet's. The hazard at your position depends on power and distance together: the twin's idle wash has little speed to start with, while the jet's take-off blast starts so fast that it is still strong after spreading and slowing over 150 m. Distance helps only gradually.

## What a blast does to a light aeroplane

A light aeroplane is a large, light structure built to be pushed around by air. On the ground, with its engine at idle and its wings not carrying it, it has very little to resist a strong blast. The harms come in five kinds.

**Being lifted, rolled or turned over.** A blast from behind or from the side lifts a wing or the tail like a strong gust. The aeroplane can be weathercocked, tipped onto a wingtip or turned over while taxiing or parked, especially if a **tail or wing is presented** broadside to the blast. A high-wing trainer is particularly easy to lift.

**Loss of directional control.** Taxiing, a sudden side blast swings the aeroplane off the taxiway faster than rudder and brake can hold it. On the take-off roll behind an aircraft that has just departed, a blast or wash across the runway acts like a sharp crosswind gust at the worst moment.

**Control surface damage.** A blast from behind drives the elevator, rudder and ailerons hard against their stops, which can damage hinges, cables and stops. **Hold the controls firmly** whenever you are exposed, as you would taxiing in a strong wind from the same direction.

**Foreign object damage (FOD).** The stream picks up stones, sand, gravel and loose items and throws them. They chip propellers, crack windscreens, dent leading edges and injure people. Unsealed aprons and the edges of taxiways at country aerodromes are the worst for it.

**Damage to doors and hangars.** Open canopies and doors can be slammed or torn off their hinges, and hangar doors and loose equipment can be blown about.

??? check "Check yourself: name three ways a jet blast can harm a taxiing light aeroplane."
    It can lift a wing or the tail and roll or turn the aeroplane over; it can swing the aeroplane off the taxiway faster than rudder and brakes can hold it; it can drive the control surfaces against their stops and damage them; and it can throw stones and debris into the propeller, windscreen and structure. Any three will do.

## Avoiding it, and not causing it

Everything here follows from power and distance.

- Do not taxi or hold **directly behind** a large aircraft with engines running. Look for the anti-collision beacon, which shows engines are running or about to start.
- Allow extra distance behind a jet that is about to move (it will use breakaway thrust) and behind aircraft at the holding point that will apply take-off power.
- Position your aircraft at the holding point and in the run-up bay so your own **prop wash** is not directed at other aircraft, open hangars or people.
- Keep well clear of hovering and hover-taxiing helicopters, and avoid taxiing downwind of them. The AIP contains guidance on clearances from helicopters and jet aircraft on the ground; check the current AIP for any specified distances.

Your own propeller is a thrust stream source too. In the run-up bay at full or high power your propwash will throw gravel at the aeroplane behind you, blow dust into an open hangar and topple a person walking past. Turn the aeroplane so the wash points at clear ground before running up, and use only the power the check needs.

![Where should you wait behind a jet at the holding point?](diagram:holding-point-position "Not directly behind its tail: to the side and as far back as the taxiway allows, angled so the blast does not hit your tail or a wing broadside.")

!!! example "Worked example: waiting behind a regional jet"
    Given: at the holding point you are behind a regional jet that has been cleared to line up.

    1. As it moves off it will apply **breakaway thrust**: a considerably larger blast than its idle.
    2. On the runway it will apply **take-off power**: the largest blast of all, extending a long way back.
    3. So the worst place to be is **directly behind its tail**. The best is to the side of its tail and as far back as the taxiway allows.
    4. If you cannot get out of line, angle your aircraft so the blast does not hit the tail or a wing broadside, and hold the controls firmly.

    The two highest-hazard power settings are both about to happen, and both point straight down the taxiway at you.

## Bringing it together

You land at a regional airport and are cleared to taxi to the general aviation apron. Ahead, a jet has just pushed back with its beacon on, and a rescue helicopter is hover-taxiing towards its pad on the far side. The jet is stationary now, but it is about to apply breakaway thrust, probably while turning, which will swing the blast across your path; you stop well short and to the side and wait until it is rolling steadily away. The helicopter's downwash spreads in every direction from under it, so you hold your position rather than taxi downwind of it until it has settled. When you reach the run-up bay, you turn so that your own propwash points at open grass, not at the aeroplane parked behind. Three sources, one rule: more power means more hazard, more distance means less, and you manage both.

## Key points

- **4.5.1** Thrust stream turbulence is the fast, turbulent air thrown back by a jet or propeller, or down and outward by a rotor. The hazard **increases with power**: least at idle (but not zero), considerably greater at breakaway thrust, greatest at take-off power or an engine ground run. The hazard **decreases with distance**, gradually, as the stream widens and slows; behind a large jet at high power it remains dangerous a long way back (check the current AIP for any specified distances). The force goes with the square of the stream's speed, so small increases in power or small reductions in distance make a large difference. Helicopter downwash spreads outward in all directions, stronger and wider from a heavier helicopter.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    The answer is nearly always one of two lines: more power means more hazard, more distance means less hazard (gradually). Breakaway thrust is higher than idle, so a stationary jet with its beacon on that is about to move is more dangerous than one taxiing steadily at idle. For rotor downwash, remember it spreads outward in all directions, not just behind.
