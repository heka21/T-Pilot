## What the exam expects (BAKC 5.1)

- **5.1.1** Pick out and name the main features printed on the visual charts: relief, water, towns and transport, obstacles, aeronautical information and the graticule.
- **5.1.2** Say which charts show controlled airspace (CTA) and prohibited, restricted and danger (PRD) areas, and which do not.

## Why this matters

On your first navigation exercise out of Jandakot you will have a chart folded on your knee, and everything you see through the windscreen has to be matched against something printed on it: the Avon River at Northam, the railway running east to Merredin, the power line that crosses your track, the hill with a spot height of 1,800-odd feet. Before that, at the planning table, the same charts tell you where the Perth Class C steps start, where the restricted areas sit, and which frequency to be on. Choose the wrong chart and you can navigate perfectly straight into controlled airspace without knowing it was there, because one of the most-used charts shows no airspace at all. The exam tests two things here: whether you recognise the symbols, and whether you know which chart carries airspace. This lesson explains what each chart is for, why it shows what it shows, and how to read its features.

## A family of charts, each drawn for a job

Every chart is a compromise between **coverage** and **detail**. A sheet of paper is only so big, so a chart that covers half of Western Australia cannot also show every farm track, and a chart that shows every farm track around Perth cannot reach Kalgoorlie. The compromise is fixed by the **scale**: the ratio between a distance on the chart and the same distance on the ground.

At **1:1,000,000**, one centimetre on the chart is a million centimetres on the ground, which is 10 km. At **1:500,000** one centimetre is 5 km, and at **1:250,000** one centimetre is 2.5 km. A larger second number means a smaller-scale chart: more country on each sheet, less detail on each square centimetre. Keep that inverse relationship clear, because "large scale" and "small scale" are easy to swap. A 1:250,000 chart is the large-scale one; it shows a small area in a lot of detail.

![How much detail does each chart scale give?](diagram:chart-scale-comparison "What to notice: 10 km is 1 cm on the WAC, 2 cm on the VNC and 4 cm on the VTC; the smaller the number after the colon, the more detail and the less country on each sheet.")

Airservices Australia publishes the charts you will use, and each one exists for a job:

**WAC, World Aeronautical Chart**, at 1:1,000,000. It is a topographic chart: terrain, towns, roads, railways, rivers, aerodromes, obstacles, the magnetic variation and the latitude and longitude grid. It shows **no airspace**. Its job is visual navigation over long distances, and it is the chart the RPLA and PPLA exams are built around, because it covers the whole country in a consistent way and it is the one you measure tracks and distances on.

**VNC, Visual Navigation Chart**, at 1:500,000. Twice the detail of the WAC, and it adds what the WAC leaves out: **controlled airspace with its lower and upper limits, PRD areas, frequencies and reporting points**. VNCs cover the capital cities and the busy regions around them, where airspace is complicated enough that a VFR pilot needs both the ground and the airspace on one sheet.

**VTC, Visual Terminal Chart**, at 1:250,000. The most detailed of the visual charts, drawn around major aerodromes such as Perth and Jandakot. It shows detailed topography together with **CTA steps, control zones, PRD areas, VFR routes and lanes of entry, reporting points and frequencies**. This is the chart you fly with in and out of the Perth basin, because the airspace changes every few miles.

**ERC-L, En Route Chart Low**, at various scales. Built for IFR pilots: air routes, navigation aids, frequencies, airspace and PRD areas, with **no topography**. A VFR pilot uses it as the authoritative picture of airspace boundaries en route, outside the areas the VNC and VTC cover.

**PCA, Planning Chart Australia**, a small-scale chart of the whole country. It shows which WAC covers which area, the forecast area boundaries, time zones and summary PRD information. You plan with it, choose your other charts from it, and do not navigate with it.

**TAC, Terminal Area Chart**, at various scales, shows the airspace detail in the terminal areas. It is mostly an IFR chart but it does show CTA boundaries.

![What does each visual chart show?](diagram:chart-features-panel "The WAC has topography but no airspace; the VNC and VTC add airspace and frequencies; the ERC-L has airspace but no topography.")

The pattern is worth seeing as one idea. The visual charts that are drawn for the **busy places** (VNC, VTC) carry both the ground and the airspace. The chart drawn for **long distances** (WAC) carries only the ground. The chart drawn for **IFR** (ERC-L) carries only the airspace. **ERSA**, the En Route Supplement Australia, is a book rather than a chart, but it goes with them: it carries the aerodrome entries and the PRD listings that explain what the charts show (see [BAKC 5.2](/notes/BAKC/5.2)).

| Chart | Scale | Topography | Airspace and PRD areas | Main use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| WAC | 1:1,000,000 | Yes | **No** | Long-distance visual navigation; the exam chart |
| VNC | 1:500,000 | Yes | Yes, with frequencies and reporting points | Visual navigation near controlled airspace |
| VTC | 1:250,000 | Yes, detailed | Yes, with VFR routes and lanes | Terminal areas such as Perth and Jandakot |
| ERC-L | Various | **No** | Yes, with air routes and navaids | IFR; VFR airspace reference en route |
| PCA | Small | Outline only | Summary | Planning and choosing charts |
| TAC | Various | Limited | CTA boundaries | Mostly IFR terminal areas |

!!! warning "Common trap"
    "Large scale" means a large ratio of chart to ground, which is the *small* second number. The 1:250,000 VTC is the large-scale chart with the most detail; the 1:1,000,000 WAC is the small-scale chart with the least. If a question asks which chart shows the most detail, look for the smallest number after the colon.

??? check "Check yourself: on a 1:500,000 VNC, how far on the ground is 4 cm on the chart, and is a VNC larger or smaller in scale than a WAC?"
    At 1:500,000 one centimetre is 5 km, so 4 cm is 20 km. The VNC is the larger scale of the two: half the ratio of the WAC, so twice the detail over a quarter of the area per sheet.

## Reading the ground: relief and water

Most of what you navigate by is the shape of the land and the water on it, so the chart spends most of its ink there.

**Relief** is how the chart shows height. Three devices work together. **Contour lines** join points of equal elevation; where they crowd together the ground is steep, where they spread apart it is gentle. **Hypsometric tints** are the colour bands between contours, darkening or changing hue with height, so that you can see at a glance where the high ground is without reading a single number. **Spot heights** are a dot with a number beside it: the surveyed elevation of a hilltop or other point, in feet above mean sea level. On the VNC and VTC a large **maximum elevation figure** is printed in each grid square: the height of the highest terrain or obstacle in that square, rounded up with a margin, so that you can pick a safe altitude for a whole area without hunting for every spot height.

The reason the chart gives you three ways of showing the same thing is that each answers a different question. Tints answer "where is it high?", contours answer "how steep and which way?", and spot heights and maximum elevation figures answer "how high exactly?". When you plan a route along the Darling Scarp, you use all three.

**Hydrography** is the water. Coastlines, rivers and lakes are blue. A river that does not always run is an **intermittent** river and is drawn with a dashed line; the same convention applies to intermittent and salt lakes, which are drawn differently from permanent ones. This matters in Western Australia more than almost anywhere: in February most of the "lakes" in the Wheatbelt are white salt pans and most of the "rivers" are dry beds, and the chart is telling you that by the way it draws them. A dry salt lake is still an excellent landmark, often better than a permanent one, because its shape is distinctive and its colour stands out.

??? check "Check yourself: a blue line on the WAC is drawn dashed rather than solid. What is it telling you, and does it still make a useful landmark?"
    It is an intermittent watercourse, one that does not always flow. It may be a dry bed on the day, so look for the line of vegetation or the shape of the bed rather than water. It is still a usable feature, but less conspicuous than a permanent river.

## Reading the ground: what people have built

**Culture** is the chart's word for human-made features. **Towns and cities** are yellow areas or circles, with the symbol growing with the size of the place; a built-up area like Perth is drawn to its real shape, a small town is a circle. **Roads** are red or brown depending on their class, and **railways** are black lines with cross ticks along them, a pattern that is easy to recognise and good to know because a railway and a road often run side by side out of a Wheatbelt town. **Power lines** are drawn as lines with their own symbol, and **pipelines**, prominent buildings, mines, towers and masts each have a symbol of their own.

Linear features are the visual navigator's best friend. A road, a railway, a power line or a pipeline gives you a line to cross at a time you can predict, which checks your groundspeed, or a line to follow, which checks your track. Before you fly, find the features that cross your track at right angles and the ones that run along it.

**Obstacles** are tall structures that could hit you: masts, towers, chimneys, wind turbines. The obstacle symbol carries two figures: the **height above ground** and the **elevation of the top above sea level**. You need both. The elevation is what you compare with your altimeter, set to QNH, because the altimeter reads height above sea level. The height above ground tells you how much it stands out from the land around it. A **lit obstacle** has a flash symbol beside it.

![Which symbols should you be able to pick out on a visual chart?](diagram:chart-legend-symbols "Railways have cross ticks, intermittent rivers are dashed, isogonals are dashed lines labelled with the variation, and airspace never appears on the WAC.")

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The figure to compare with your altimeter is the obstacle's **elevation above mean sea level**, not its height above the ground. A 150 ft mast on a 1,200 ft ridge reaches about 1,350 ft on your QNH altimeter. Read the legend of the chart you are using to see which of the two figures is which.

## Reading the aeronautical information

Over the ground picture, the chart prints what a pilot needs that a road map does not.

**Aerodromes** have symbols that tell you whether they are civil or military and whether their runways are sealed. **Navigation aids** have their own symbols with identifier and frequency. **Reporting points** are named places where you report your position, and on the VTC **VFR routes** and lanes of entry show the paths you are expected to follow in and out of the terminal area.

**Isogonals** are dashed lines joining places of equal **magnetic variation**, the angle between true north and magnetic north, each labelled with the variation in degrees east or west. They exist because tracks are measured on the chart against true north, from the graticule, but you fly them by the magnetic compass. The isogonal nearest your route gives the correction (see [BAKC 2.1](/notes/BAKC/2.1)). In Western Australia the variation is small, roughly 1 to 2 degrees west around Perth, which is why it is easy to forget and why the exam likes to test it.

The **graticule** is the grid of latitude and longitude lines, on the WAC usually every 30 minutes, with tick marks for each minute along the lines. You use it to plot a position from coordinates (an ERSA entry gives each aerodrome's position), to measure true track against a meridian, and to measure distance: one minute of latitude is one nautical mile, so the ticks along a meridian are a ready-made distance scale.

Finally, on the charts that show it, **airspace**: the boundaries of control zones and CTA steps, each labelled with its lower and upper limits, and the boundaries of **PRD areas**, each labelled with its identifier and limits. That brings us to the second element.

??? check "Check yourself: why does the chart print isogonals at all, when you could just draw tracks magnetic?"
    Because the chart's grid is the latitude and longitude graticule, which is aligned with true north, so tracks are measured true. The compass points to magnetic north, and the variation changes from place to place. The isogonal tells you the variation along your route so you can convert the true track to the magnetic one you fly.

## Which charts show controlled airspace and PRD areas

Now the question the exam asks most directly. Controlled airspace is airspace in which air traffic control provides a service and a clearance is needed to enter; **PRD areas** are prohibited, restricted and danger areas, each with its own rules (see [BAKC 5.2](/notes/BAKC/5.2) for what the letters mean). You need to know where both are before you fly.

**Controlled airspace** is shown on the **VTC, VNC and ERC-L**, and the TAC shows CTA boundaries too. It is **not** shown on the WAC. A WAC alone therefore cannot keep you out of controlled airspace. Plan any flight near a controlled aerodrome or under CTA steps with the VNC or VTC for that area, and elsewhere with the ERC-L.

![Where does each class of airspace sit?](diagram:airspace-cross-section "Control zones start at the surface, the CTA steps above them rise with distance, and Class G lies underneath; the VNC and VTC draw these boundaries with their limits, the WAC does not.")

The reason the chart has to show vertical limits as well as boundaries is that controlled airspace is layered. Around Perth the Class C steps have lower limits that rise as you move away from the airport, so you can fly under a step in Class G without a clearance as long as you stay below its base. The VTC prints that base on each step. On a WAC you would see only the ground beneath it.

**PRD areas** are shown on the **VTC, VNC and ERC-L** with their identifier (for example R155) and their vertical limits. The chart shows *where* the area is and how high it goes. It does not tell you *when* it is active, who controls it, or whether you are likely to get a clearance through it. For that the chart sends you to **ERSA's PRD section** and **AIP ENR 5.1**, which give the full description, the controlling authority, the hours of activity and any conditions. The **PCA** carries a summary.

!!! example "Worked example: choosing the charts for a flight from Jandakot to Northam"
    Given: a VFR flight from Jandakot east over the Darling Scarp to Northam, departing through the Jandakot control zone and passing under the Perth Class C steps.

    1. Departure and the first part of the track lie in the Perth terminal area, where the airspace changes every few miles: **the Perth VTC**, for the Jandakot control zone, the step bases, the VFR route out of the basin and the reporting points.
    2. The rest of the route lies under and beyond the outer steps: **the VNC** covering Perth, which shows the outer step bases and any PRD areas along the way.
    3. For the long-distance navigation picture and the exam-style track and distance measurement: **the WAC**, knowing that it shows none of the airspace you have just checked.
    4. For anything the charts flag but do not explain, such as a restricted area beside the track: **ERSA** (PRD section) and the NOTAMs.

    The WAC alone would let you navigate to Northam accurately and give you no warning of the controlled airspace you cross on the way.

Now try one before opening the solution: you are planning a flight across the Wheatbelt, far from any controlled aerodrome, and you have only the WAC. A friend says you need nothing else because "there is no airspace out there". What do you check, and on what?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    The WAC cannot tell you whether there is controlled airspace or a PRD area on the route, because it shows none. Check the route on the **ERC-L** (which shows airspace and PRD areas everywhere) or the **VNC** if one covers the area, then look up any PRD area you find in **ERSA's PRD section** for its hours and conditions, and check the **NOTAMs** for temporary restricted areas. "There is no airspace out there" is a conclusion you reach from those, not from the WAC.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The WAC is the navigation chart the exam is built around, so it is tempting to answer "WAC" to every chart question. For controlled airspace and PRD areas the answer is the **VTC, VNC or ERC-L**, never the WAC. And a chart tells you only where a restricted area is; when it is active comes from ERSA and NOTAM.

??? check "Check yourself: a VNC shows a restricted area beside your track with its identifier and limits. What does the chart not tell you, and where do you find it?"
    The chart does not give the hours of activity, the controlling authority or the conditional status (whether a clearance is likely). Those are in ERSA's PRD section and AIP ENR 5.1, and the NOTAMs may change them on the day.

## Bringing it together

You are planning a Saturday flight from Jandakot to Cunderdin and back. On the PCA you confirm which WAC covers the route and that the whole flight is in one time zone. On the WAC you draw the track, measure the true track against a meridian and the distance against the latitude ticks, and apply the 1 to 2 degrees west variation from the nearest isogonal. Along the line you mark the features you will cross: the scarp, the Avon valley and its river (solid near the coast, dashed further inland where it does not always flow), the Great Eastern Highway and the railway beside it, a power line, and the maximum elevation figures that set your lowest safe altitude. Then you put the WAC aside and open the Perth VTC and VNC, because the WAC has shown you none of the airspace: the Jandakot control zone, the Perth step bases you must stay under on the way out, and a restricted area whose identifier you note. ERSA's PRD section tells you when that area is active, and the NOTAMs tell you whether anything has changed. Four documents, each answering the question it was drawn for.

## Key points

- **5.1.1** Visual charts show relief (contours, hypsometric tints, spot heights, and maximum elevation figures on the VNC and VTC), water (intermittent rivers and lakes drawn differently), culture (towns yellow, railways with cross ticks, roads, power lines), obstacles (height above ground and elevation above sea level, lit ones with a flash), aeronautical information (aerodromes, navaids, reporting points, isogonals) and the graticule. Scale: 1:1,000,000 WAC, 1:500,000 VNC, 1:250,000 VTC; the smaller the number after the colon, the more detail.
- **5.1.2** Controlled airspace and PRD areas are shown on the **VTC, VNC and ERC-L** (and CTA on the TAC), never on the WAC. The chart shows where an area is and its limits; ERSA PRD and AIP ENR 5.1 give its hours, controlling authority and status.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    The trap is the WAC. It is the navigation chart for the exam, but it shows no airspace at all. If a question asks which chart identifies controlled airspace or restricted areas, the answer is the VTC, VNC or ERC-L, never the WAC. Know the three scales, and be able to recognise a railway (cross ticks), an intermittent river (dashed), an isogonal (dashed and labelled with the variation) and a maximum elevation figure (the large number in a VNC or VTC grid square, the highest terrain or obstacle in that square with a margin).
