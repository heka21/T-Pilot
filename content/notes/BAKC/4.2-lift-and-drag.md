## What the exam expects (BAKC 4.2)

- **4.2.1** Say whether an aerofoil's lift and drag go up or down when the airspeed changes and when the angle of attack changes.
- **4.2.2** Explain the drag families on a subsonic aeroplane: parasite (zero-lift) drag made up of form, interference and skin friction drag; and induced (lift-dependent) drag.
- **4.2.3** State how total drag changes with airspeed.

## Why this matters

On every flight you trade lift against drag without thinking about it. Slow down on downwind at Jandakot and you raise the nose to hold height; fly a long, flat final at Murrayfield and you find you need more power to go slower; trim for the best glide after an engine failure over the wheatbelt and you are choosing the bottom of a drag curve. Each of those is one line of this lesson. The lift formula tells you what you can change to make more or less lift and what the day decides for you. The drag curve tells you why the aeroplane needs the least power to go far at one particular speed, and why slower than that it starts to work against you. The exam asks about this with direct "increase or decrease" questions, with a V² calculation, and with the classic trap of swapping parasite and induced drag.

## The lift formula, term by term

In [BAKC 4.1](/notes/BAKC/4.1) you met lift as the force at right angles to the relative airflow, made by a pressure difference across the wing. How big that force is depends on four things, and the **lift formula** puts them in one line:

$$L = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho V^2 \, S \, C_L$$

Read it from left to right as a story about air hitting a wing.

**$\rho$ (rho) is the air density**, the mass of air in each cubic metre. Denser air means more air molecules arriving at the wing every second, so more lift. The pilot does not choose it: it falls with height, with heat and with low pressure, which is the whole subject of density height in [BAKC 6.2](/notes/BAKC/6.2). **The day controls $\rho$.**

**$V$ is the true airspeed**, the speed of the wing through the air. It appears squared, because faster air brings more molecules each second *and* hits each one harder. Double the speed and both effects double, so lift goes up four times. **The pilot controls $V$**, with power and attitude.

**$S$ is the wing area.** It is fixed by the designer (flap that extends backwards adds a little). For a given aeroplane, treat it as constant.

**$C_L$ is the lift coefficient**, a number that says how good the wing is at turning dynamic pressure into lift at its present angle of attack. It depends on the aerofoil shape, on the flap setting and, above all, on the **angle of attack**. **The pilot controls $C_L$** through the angle of attack, with the elevator.

The first group, $\tfrac{1}{2}\rho V^2$, has a name of its own: **dynamic pressure**, the pressure the air would exert if you brought it to rest against a flat plate. It is exactly what the pitot tube and the airspeed indicator measure. That is why the ASI is so useful: whatever the density and the true airspeed, the same **indicated airspeed** means the same dynamic pressure, which means the wing will make the same lift at the same angle of attack.

![What does each term of the lift formula mean, and which can the pilot change?](diagram:lift-formula-factors "The pilot controls lift through angle of attack (CL) and speed (V²); density comes with the day, and wing area is fixed.")

Drag follows exactly the same pattern, with a drag coefficient $C_D$ in place of $C_L$:

$$D = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho V^2 \, S \, C_D$$

In level flight, lift must equal weight. The weight is fixed for the moment, so if one term in the lift formula goes up, another must come down. That simple balance answers most questions in this subtopic.

## Lift and drag with airspeed

Hold the angle of attack constant, so $C_L$ and $C_D$ are fixed, and change the speed. Both lift and drag change with $V^2$: double the speed and lift and drag are four times as great; halve it and they are a quarter.

But in level flight you cannot simply let lift quadruple; the aeroplane would climb. So in practice, when you speed up you lower the nose, the angle of attack falls and $C_L$ comes down to keep lift equal to weight. When you slow down, you raise the nose and $C_L$ goes up. Every speed in level flight has its own angle of attack: high speed, small angle; low speed, large angle, with the largest at the stall speed.

!!! example "Worked example: what doubling the speed does to lift"
    Given: an aeroplane in level flight at 60 kt accelerates to 120 kt. Air density unchanged.

    1. Speed ratio: $120 / 60 = \mathbf{2}$.
    2. Lift at the *same* angle of attack: $2^2 = \mathbf{4}$ times as much.
    3. To stay level, lift must still equal weight, so $C_L$ must fall to $1/4$ of its value: **a quarter of the lift coefficient**, which means a much smaller angle of attack.

    This is why the nose sits well down in a fast cruise and well up in slow flight. The pilot is holding lift constant by trading angle of attack against $V^2$.

The day can change the balance too. Climb high and the density falls; to hold the same lift with the same $C_L$, $\rho V^2$ must stay the same.

!!! example "Worked example: half the density"
    Given: in ISA the density is about half its sea-level value at around 22,000 ft. You hold the same angle of attack (same $C_L$) and the same weight. At sea level the aeroplane flies at 100 kt.

    1. Lift must not change, so $\rho V^2$ must not change.
    2. $\rho$ has halved, so $V^2$ must **double**.
    3. $V$ must rise by $\sqrt{2} = \mathbf{1.41}$: true airspeed $100 \times 1.41 = \mathbf{141\ kt}$.
    4. Dynamic pressure $\tfrac{1}{2}\rho V^2$ is unchanged, so the **indicated airspeed is still 100 kt**.

    The wing needs the same IAS at any height for the same angle of attack; only the true airspeed rises. That is why the IAS stall speed does not change with altitude ([RBKA 3.6](/notes/RBKA/3.6)) and why a high, hot strip needs a longer run: lift-off happens at the same IAS but a higher TAS and groundspeed.

Now try one before opening the solution. An aeroplane cruising level at 100 kt slows to 70 kt at the same density and weight. By how much must the lift coefficient change?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    At the same angle of attack the lift would be $(70/100)^2 = 0.49$ of what it was. To keep lift equal to weight, $C_L$ must rise by $1/0.49 = 2.04$, so **roughly double**. The pilot does it by raising the nose to a higher angle of attack, which is why slow flight looks nose-high.

??? check "Check yourself: at a constant angle of attack the speed rises by 10 percent. What happens to lift and drag?"
    Both rise by about 21 percent, because both go with the square of the speed ($1.1^2 = 1.21$). To stay level you would lower the nose slightly to bring the lift back to the weight.

## Lift and drag with angle of attack

Now hold the speed constant and change the angle of attack. As the wing is tilted further into the airflow, the air over the top is accelerated more, the pressure difference grows and $C_L$ rises. Over the normal range it rises almost in a straight line: each extra degree adds about the same amount of lift.

That continues up to the **critical angle**, about 16 degrees on a typical light-aeroplane wing, where $C_L$ reaches its maximum, $C_{L\max}$. Beyond it the flow separates from the upper surface ([BAKC 4.1](/notes/BAKC/4.1)) and lift falls away: the stall.

![How does the lift coefficient change with angle of attack?](diagram:cl-vs-angle-of-attack "Each extra degree adds about the same lift until the critical angle (about 16°), where CL peaks and the wing stalls.")

Drag behaves differently. It **increases with angle of attack all the way**: slowly at small angles, then faster as the angle grows, because both the wing's frontal area and the lift-dependent drag (below) grow. Near and beyond the stall drag rises sharply as the separated wake gets bigger. So at the stall you lose lift and gain drag at the same time, which is why the aeroplane sinks so quickly when it stalls.

| Change | Lift | Drag |
|---|---|---|
| Airspeed increased, angle of attack constant | Increases (with $V^2$) | Increases (with $V^2$) |
| Airspeed decreased, angle of attack constant | Decreases | Decreases |
| Angle of attack increased, below the critical angle | Increases | Increases |
| Angle of attack increased beyond the critical angle | Decreases sharply | Increases sharply |

??? check "Check yourself: you increase the angle of attack from 2 degrees to 8 degrees at a constant airspeed. What happens to lift and to drag?"
    Both increase. Below the critical angle, more angle of attack raises the lift coefficient almost in proportion, and drag rises too, slowly at first and faster as the angle grows. Only past the critical angle does lift fall while drag keeps rising.

## Parasite drag: the cost of pushing through the air

Drag on a subsonic aeroplane comes in two families. The first is the drag the aeroplane would have even if it made no lift at all, the cost of pushing an object through air. It is called **parasite drag**, or **zero-lift drag**, and it has three parts.

**Form drag** (pressure drag) comes from the shape of the object. Air flowing around a blunt shape cannot follow it closely at the back; it separates and leaves a low-pressure wake behind. The high pressure on the front and the low pressure behind push the object backwards. Put your hand flat out of the car window and the push is mostly form drag. Streamlining, with rounded fronts and long tapering tails, lets the air close in smoothly behind and shrinks the wake. Wheel spats, cowlings and a tapered fuselage all exist for this.

**Skin friction drag** comes from the air's viscosity, its stickiness. The layer of air touching the skin is dragged to a stop, and the thin **boundary layer** above it is sheared between the stationary skin and the moving air. The rougher the surface, the thicker and more turbulent the boundary layer and the higher the friction. Dirt, squashed insects, rough paint, protruding rivet heads and frost all increase it. A clean, polished wing is faster than a dirty one for the same power.

**Interference drag** comes from junctions. Where the wing meets the fuselage, a strut meets the wing or an undercarriage leg meets the wheel fairing, the airflows around the two parts meet and disturb each other. The total drag of the joined parts is more than the sum of their drags separately. Smooth fillets and fairings at the junctions reduce it.

All three grow with dynamic pressure, so **parasite drag rises with the square of the airspeed**. Double the speed and parasite drag is four times as great.

![What are the three parts of parasite drag?](diagram:parasite-drag-types "Form drag comes from the shape and its wake, skin friction from the sticky boundary layer, interference from junctions; all three rise with V².")

## Induced drag: the price of making lift

The second family exists only because the wing is making lift. It is called **induced drag** or **lift-dependent drag**.

A wing making lift has higher pressure below it than above. Along most of the span the air cannot get from one to the other, but at the tips it can: the high-pressure air underneath spills outward and round the tip into the low-pressure region on top. As the aeroplane moves forward this circulation is left behind as two rotating **wing-tip vortices**, the same vortices that cause the wake turbulence of [BAKC 4.4](/notes/BAKC/4.4).

The vortices push the air behind the wing downward. This **downwash** means the wing is flying in air that is moving slightly down, so the effective relative airflow over the wing is tilted. Lift acts at right angles to that tilted airflow, so the lift vector itself tilts backwards. Its rearward part is a drag force: induced drag.

![How does downwash turn lift into drag?](diagram:induced-drag-downwash "Downwash tilts the airflow at the wing, so the lift, at right angles to it, leans back; its backward part is induced drag.")

The harder the wing works, the bigger the pressure difference, the stronger the vortices and the bigger the tilt. So induced drag is **greatest at a high angle of attack**: at **low speed**, at **high weight** and in a **turn**, where the wing must make more lift. At high speed the wing needs only a small angle of attack, the vortices are weak, and induced drag is small. Roughly, **induced drag falls with the inverse square of the airspeed**: double the speed and it falls to about a quarter.

Wing shape changes it too. A long, slender wing of **high aspect ratio** spreads the lift over a long span, so less of it is lost at the tips; that is why gliders have them. Tapered tips and winglets reduce it a little further.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Parasite drag goes **up** with speed; induced drag goes **down** with speed. Exam options swap them. Induced drag is highest at low speed and high angle of attack, just before the stall, which is also when the wing-tip vortices, and the wake turbulence behind a large aeroplane, are strongest.

??? check "Check yourself: which type of drag is increased by frost on the wing, and which by flying slowly at a high angle of attack?"
    Frost roughens the surface and increases skin friction drag, part of parasite drag (it also disturbs the airflow and lowers the maximum lift). Flying slowly at a high angle of attack increases induced drag, because the wing is working hard and the tip vortices and downwash are strong.

## Total drag and the drag curve

Put the two families on one graph against airspeed. Parasite drag starts small at low speed and climbs steeply as speed rises. Induced drag starts large near the stall and falls away as speed rises. Their sum, **total drag**, is a U-shaped curve: high at low speed, where induced drag dominates; high at high speed, where parasite drag dominates; and lowest in between.

![How does total drag change with airspeed?](diagram:drag-curves-vs-airspeed "Parasite rises and induced falls with speed; total drag is least where they are equal, the best lift/drag speed. Slower than that needs more thrust.")

The bottom of the U is where the two are **equal**. Below that speed, slowing down adds more induced drag than it saves in parasite drag; above it, speeding up adds more parasite drag than it saves in induced drag. The speed for **minimum total drag** is also the speed of the **best lift/drag ratio**, because in level flight lift equals weight and is fixed, so least drag means the most lift for each unit of drag. For a light piston aeroplane that speed is close to the **best glide speed** and the **best range speed** at low level (see [RBKA 3.1](/notes/RBKA/3.1) and [RBKA 3.4](/notes/RBKA/3.4)).

!!! example "Worked example: drag either side of the minimum"
    Given (illustrative round numbers): at 70 kt an aeroplane is at its minimum-drag speed, with parasite and induced drag each 600 N. Parasite drag goes with $V^2$, induced with $1/V^2$.

    1. At 100 kt, parasite: $600 \times (100/70)^2 = 600 \times 2.04 = \mathbf{1{,}224\ N}$.
    2. At 100 kt, induced: $600 \times (70/100)^2 = 600 \times 0.49 = \mathbf{294\ N}$. Total **1,518 N**.
    3. At 55 kt, parasite: $600 \times (55/70)^2 = 600 \times 0.617 = \mathbf{370\ N}$.
    4. At 55 kt, induced: $600 \times (70/55)^2 = 600 \times 1.62 = \mathbf{972\ N}$. Total **1,342 N**.

    Against 1,200 N at 70 kt, flying 15 kt slower costs about 12 percent more drag, so the aeroplane needs more thrust to fly slower. Faster or slower than the bottom of the curve, total drag goes up.

### The back of the drag curve

On the right-hand side of the curve, above the minimum-drag speed, the aeroplane is well behaved. If a gust slows it, drag falls, the surplus thrust accelerates it back, and the speed returns. If it speeds up, drag rises and slows it again. Speed looks after itself.

On the left-hand side, below the minimum-drag speed, the relationship is reversed. Slowing down *increases* the drag, which slows the aeroplane further, which increases the drag again. To fly slower you need **more** thrust, not less. This region is called the **back of the drag curve** (or the back side, or the region of reversed command).

It matters most on a slow, low final approach. Suppose you are a little low and a little slow and you raise the nose to stretch the approach. The higher angle of attack adds induced drag, the speed falls, the drag rises further, and the aeroplane sinks faster rather than going further. The pitch input that works on the front of the curve makes things worse on the back. The answer is **power** to hold the speed, with the attitude controlling the path, and the habit of flying the approach at the POH speed so that you stay near the bottom of the curve rather than sliding up its back.

??? check "Check yourself: why does an aeroplane need more power to fly slower than its minimum-drag speed?"
    Below that speed induced drag is the larger part of total drag, and it grows as the speed falls and the angle of attack rises. Slowing down therefore increases total drag, so more thrust is needed to hold the lower speed.

## Bringing it together

You leave the circuit at Jandakot and climb to 4,500 ft to fly to Northam on a 30 degree afternoon. The air at height is thinner, and the day has made it thinner still: $\rho$ is down. To hold your cruise IAS the aeroplane must fly at a higher true airspeed, and the wing works at the same angle of attack as it does at that IAS anywhere. Over Northam you slow to circuit speed: $V^2$ falls, so you raise the nose and $C_L$ rises; the tip vortices strengthen and induced drag grows while parasite drag shrinks. On final you are a few knots slow, on the back of the drag curve, so a raised nose would cost speed and height together. Power holds the speed; the attitude holds the path.

## Key points

- **4.2.1** $L = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho V^2 S C_L$. At a constant angle of attack, lift and drag both go with $V^2$. Below the critical angle, more angle of attack means more lift and more drag; past it, lift falls sharply and drag rises sharply. The pilot controls $V$ and $C_L$; the day controls $\rho$; the same IAS means the same dynamic pressure.
- **4.2.2** Parasite drag (form, skin friction, interference) is the cost of moving through the air and rises with $V^2$. Induced drag comes from the tip vortices and downwash tilting the lift vector back; it is greatest at low speed, high angle of attack and high weight, and is reduced by high aspect ratio.
- **4.2.3** Total drag is U-shaped against airspeed, least where parasite equals induced: the best lift/drag speed, close to best glide and best range for a piston aeroplane. Slower than that is the back of the drag curve, where flying slower needs more thrust.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Have $V^2$ at your fingertips: double the speed, four times the lift and drag at the same angle of attack; 70 percent of the speed, about half (0.49). Expect a definition of interference drag (junctions), a "when is induced drag greatest" (slow, high angle of attack, heavy) and a "how does total drag vary" (decreases to a minimum, then increases). If an option says parasite drag decreases with speed, it has swapped the two families.
