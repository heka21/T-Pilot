## What the exam expects (BAKC 3.3)

- **3.3.1** State what causes detonation (the wrong use of the mixture control, the wrong combination of manifold pressure and RPM, and fuel of too low an octane) and what it does to the engine.
- **3.3.2** Describe what prolonged idling, and flying with the wrong mixture setting, do to an engine.
- **3.3.3** Give the reasons behind the limits on oil pressure, oil temperature, cylinder head temperature and RPM, and behind the ignition checks before take-off and at shutdown, the limit on using the starter motor, not using pitot heat on the ground, and keeping the engine warm on long descents.
- **3.3.4** Explain what blue or black exhaust smoke tells you.

## Why this matters

The limits in section 2 of your POH and the checks in section 4 can look like a list of numbers to memorise. Every one of them is there because an engine was damaged, or a pilot was hurt, by doing the opposite. The previous lesson, [BAKC 3.1](/notes/BAKC/3.1), showed how the engine works; this one shows how you can hurt it and how the checklist stops you. You will use it every flight: in the run-up bay at Jandakot reading the magneto drop, on a long glide back from the training area keeping a little power on, on a hot climb out of Northam watching the CHT creep up. The exam asks for the cause-and-effect pairs: what leads to detonation, why the dead cut check exists, what blue smoke means.

## Detonation: the engine hitting itself

### What normal combustion looks like

When the spark plugs fire, the mixture does not explode. A **flame front** starts at each plug and spreads across the cylinder in a fraction of a second, burning the mixture progressively. Pressure in the cylinder rises smoothly, peaks just after the piston passes the top, and pushes the piston down in a firm, even shove. That smooth rise is what the engine is designed for.

### What goes wrong

As the flame front advances, the unburnt mixture ahead of it, called the **end gas**, is squeezed and heated by the expanding gas behind it. Normally the flame reaches the end gas before it gets hot enough to ignite on its own. If the end gas does reach its ignition temperature first, it does not burn progressively: it **explodes**, all at once. That is **detonation**. Instead of a firm push, the piston and cylinder head receive a sharp hammer blow, a pressure spike far above normal, many times a second.

![How does detonation differ from normal combustion?](diagram:detonation-vs-normal-combustion "Normal combustion is a smooth flame front and a smooth pressure rise; detonation explodes the end gas and hammers the piston with a pressure spike.")

So anything that makes the end gas **hotter**, puts it under **higher pressure**, or gives it **more time** before the flame arrives makes detonation more likely, and anything that makes the fuel **less resistant** to self-ignition does too. The syllabus names three causes, and each fits that pattern.

**A mixture that is too lean at high power.** A lean mixture burns hotter (it has less spare fuel to soak up heat as it evaporates) and more slowly. The end gas is hotter and the flame takes longer to reach it. This is why you take off and climb at full rich, as [BAKC 3.1](/notes/BAKC/3.1) explained: the extra fuel is coolant.

**High manifold pressure with low RPM.** On an aeroplane with a constant-speed propeller, you set manifold pressure with the throttle and RPM with the propeller lever. High manifold pressure packs a dense charge into the cylinder, so the pressure at the top of the stroke is high. Low RPM means the piston moves slowly, so the end gas sits at that high pressure for longer. High pressure plus time is the recipe. That is why the POH gives approved combinations, and why the standard sequence is to **increase RPM before manifold pressure** when adding power and **reduce manifold pressure before RPM** when taking it off, so the engine is never "over-boosted".

**Fuel of too low an octane.** The **octane rating** measures how well a fuel resists self-ignition under heat and pressure. An engine built for 100 octane has a compression ratio that relies on it. Lower-octane fuel lets the end gas ignite on its own at pressures the engine was designed to tolerate ([BAKC 3.2](/notes/BAKC/3.2)).

On top of these, a **high cylinder head temperature** from any cause makes every one of them worse. The classic set-up is a steep, slow climb on a hot day with the mixture leaned too far: high power, little cooling air, hot end gas.

### What it does, and what you do

The effects follow from the hammer blow: **rough running**, a **sharp rise in CHT** (the shock waves scrub away the thin layer of cooler gas that normally insulates the cylinder walls), **loss of power**, and, if it continues, **burnt valves, holed pistons, cracked cylinder heads and engine failure**. In a light aeroplane engine detonation is often hard to hear over the noise, so the CHT gauge and the feel of the engine are your warnings.

The remedy reverses the causes: **enrich the mixture** (cooler end gas), **reduce power** (lower cylinder pressure), **lower the nose** to increase airspeed and cooling airflow, and **open the cowl flaps** if fitted.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Detonation is not the same as **pre-ignition**. Pre-ignition is the mixture being lit *before* the spark, by a glowing hot spot such as a carbon deposit or an overheated plug. Detonation happens *after* the spark, when the unburnt end gas explodes. Both cause rough running and high temperatures, and one can lead to the other, but the exam's list of causes (lean mixture, high manifold pressure with low RPM, low octane) is the list for detonation.

??? check "Check yourself: why does leaning the mixture too far at climb power make detonation more likely?"
    A lean mixture burns hotter and more slowly, and has less excess fuel to absorb heat. The unburnt end gas is therefore hotter and waits longer for the flame to reach it, so it is more likely to reach its self-ignition temperature and explode.

## Idling too long and the wrong mixture in flight

### Prolonged idling

On a busy Saturday at Jandakot you can sit at the holding point at idle for ten minutes behind a queue of departures. At idle the engine is running slowly, cool and very rich: the carburettor's idle system deliberately supplies extra fuel so the engine will idle smoothly, and the cylinders are too cool to burn it all. The unburnt fuel leaves carbon, and the lead in AVGAS leaves lead deposits, on the spark plugs. This is **plug fouling**. A fouled plug cannot spark properly, so the engine may run rough and, typically, the next **magneto check fails** with an excessive drop on one side. Idling for long periods also keeps the oil cool, which does it no good either.

Many POHs give a remedy: before the run-up, or if a magneto check shows a fouled plug, run the engine at a higher RPM with the mixture leaned for a short period to burn the deposits off, then repeat the check. During long waits on the ground, some POHs also recommend leaning the mixture at idle to reduce fouling. Follow your POH.

### The wrong mixture in flight

[BAKC 3.1](/notes/BAKC/3.1) explained what each mixture does; here is what it does to *you* if you get it wrong for long.

**Too rich in cruise**, typically from forgetting to lean at altitude: the engine loses some power and may run rough, plugs foul, and above all **fuel consumption rises**. A flight planned on the POH's leaned fuel flow will burn noticeably more than planned, and the fuel reserve you planned on is no longer there. Cooler running also means more deposits.

**Too lean**, typically from leaning at high power or forgetting to enrich before a climb: **CHT and EGT rise**, power falls, the engine roughens, and at high power the stage is set for **detonation** and engine damage.

![What happens as you lean the mixture?](diagram:mixture-rich-to-lean "Power falls away on both sides of the right mixture; too rich runs cool and fouls plugs, too lean runs hot and invites detonation.")

??? check "Check yourself: you sat at idle for 15 minutes waiting for a departure slot. The magneto check now shows a large drop on the left magneto. What is the likely cause and what do you do?"
    Plug fouling from prolonged rich idling, on a plug fed by the left magneto. Run the engine at the higher RPM with the mixture leaned, as the POH directs, to burn off the deposits, then repeat the check. If the drop is still outside the limit, the aeroplane does not fly.

## Why the limits exist

### Oil pressure: minimum

Oil pressure is the push that drives oil into the bearings. Below the **minimum oil pressure**, oil is no longer reaching the crankshaft and camshaft bearings and the cylinder walls in the quantity they need. Metal starts to touch metal, the bearings overheat, and the engine seizes. A minimum oil pressure is therefore the edge of engine failure, and a falling oil pressure in flight is a reason to land as soon as possible.

### Oil temperature: minimum and maximum

**Minimum.** Cold oil is thick. It flows slowly through the narrow passages, so some parts are starved of oil even though the pressure gauge may read **high** (the pump is pushing hard against thick oil). Thick oil also flows poorly between the piston rings and cylinder walls. Apply high power to a cold engine and those parts take the load before the oil reaches them. That is why you warm the engine until the oil temperature reaches the POH minimum before take-off or full-power checks.

**Maximum.** Hot oil thins. A thin oil film can break through under load, so lubrication fails, and very hot oil also breaks down chemically and loses its ability to carry heat away. A high oil temperature is also a symptom: it tells you the engine as a whole is running too hot.

### Cylinder head temperature: minimum and maximum

**Minimum.** A cylinder that runs too cold burns its mixture incompletely, so deposits build up and plugs foul. Many engines also have a minimum CHT for applying high power, for the same reason as the oil.

**Maximum.** The cylinder heads are aluminium alloy, which loses strength rapidly as it gets hot. Above the maximum CHT the metal is weakened, valves can burn, and the high temperature itself encourages **detonation**, which raises the CHT further. The maximum CHT is the line beyond which the engine starts to damage itself.

### Maximum RPM

Everything that moves up and down in the engine (pistons, rods, valves) is stopped and restarted twice a revolution, and the force needed to do that rises with the **square of the RPM**: 10 percent more RPM is about 21 percent more load. Exceed the red line and those loads can overstress the crankshaft, connecting rods and valve gear, and the valves may "float" (fail to close in time). The **propeller** is limited too: its tips are moving fastest, and as they approach the speed of sound they lose efficiency, become noisy, and the blades face very high stresses. In a fixed-pitch aeroplane the commonest way to overspeed is a dive with power on, which is why you throttle back when the nose goes down.

### Ignition checks before take-off

The pre-take-off **magneto check** confirms both ignition systems work independently. With the engine at the POH check RPM, you select the left magneto alone, note the RPM, return to both, then select the right alone. Running on one magneto, each cylinder has only one spark, the burn is slower and the RPM drops a little. The check asks two questions:

- Is **each drop within the limit**? An excessive drop, or rough running, on one side means a fault on that side: a fouled plug, a bad lead, a faulty magneto.
- Is the **difference between the two drops within its limit**? A big difference means one side is weaker than the other.

Typical magneto-check numbers for a trainer are a maximum drop of about **125 to 175 RPM** on either magneto and a maximum difference of about **50 RPM** between them, but the only numbers that count are the ones in your POH.

There is a third result to watch for: **no drop at all**. A healthy engine always loses a little RPM on one magneto. If selecting LEFT gives no drop, the right magneto is probably not being earthed (a broken P-lead or a faulty switch), so the engine is still running on both, and that magneto will be live when the switch says OFF.

![What are you looking for in the magneto check?](diagram:magneto-check-rpm-drop "Each magneto alone must drop the RPM by no more than the POH limit, and the two drops must be close to each other.")

!!! example "Worked example: reading a magneto check"
    Given: your POH allows a maximum drop of 125 RPM on either magneto and a maximum difference of 50 RPM. Check RPM on BOTH is 1,800. Left alone: 1,710. Right alone: 1,650.

    1. Left drop: $1800 - 1710 = \mathbf{90\ RPM}$, within 125.
    2. Right drop: $1800 - 1650 = \mathbf{150\ RPM}$, **over** the 125 limit.
    3. Difference: $150 - 90 = \mathbf{60\ RPM}$, also over the 50 limit.

    The right ignition system has a problem, probably a fouled plug. Try the POH clearing procedure (higher RPM, leaned, briefly) and repeat the check. If it still fails, taxi back: the aeroplane is unserviceable.

Try one yourself first. Same POH limits, BOTH 1,800 RPM. Left alone 1,720, right alone 1,690. Does it pass?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    Left drop $1800 - 1720 = 80$ RPM; right drop $1800 - 1690 = 110$ RPM; both are within 125. Difference $110 - 80 = 30$ RPM, within 50. **It passes**, provided the engine ran smoothly on each magneto.

### Ignition check at shutdown: the dead cut

Recall from [BAKC 3.1](/notes/BAKC/3.1) that the ignition switch stops a magneto by connecting it to earth through its P-lead, and that a broken P-lead leaves that magneto **live** with the switch at OFF. You would never know from the cockpit: the engine runs normally on BOTH, and the fault shows itself only when someone moves the propeller.

The **dead cut** check finds it. At idle, just before shutdown, you switch the ignition briefly to OFF. The engine should **falter and begin to stop**; you switch straight back to BOTH before it does, then shut down normally with the mixture. If the engine keeps running with the switch at OFF, a magneto is still live: the aeroplane is dangerous to anyone who touches the propeller, and it must be reported and fixed before anyone goes near it.

![What does the dead-cut check prove?](diagram:dead-cut-check "What to notice: at OFF an intact P-lead earths the magneto and the engine falters; if it keeps running, a P-lead is broken and the propeller is live.")

### Prolonged use of the starter motor

The starter is a small, powerful electric motor built for **short bursts**. Cranking draws a very large current, and the motor heats quickly; it has no cooling to cope with continuous running. Crank too long and it overheats and can burn out, and the battery runs flat too. So the POH gives a **cranking limit** (a number of seconds) and a **cooling period** before the next attempt. If the engine will not start within it, something else is wrong (over-priming, flat battery, fuel off), and more cranking will not fix it.

### Pitot heat on the ground

The **pitot heater** is an electric element inside the pitot tube that keeps ice out of it in flight. It is powerful because in flight a fast, cold airflow is carrying its heat away. On the ground there is no airflow, so the element **overheats and can burn out**, and the probe gets hot enough to **burn your hand** on the walk-round. Check that it works by feeling the probe warm after a brief switch-on, as the POH directs, then switch it off.

### Keeping the engine warm on long descents

A long glide from 4,500 ft back to the circuit at idle power is pleasant for you and hard on the engine, for three reasons.

**Shock cooling.** At idle the cylinders are making little heat while the airflow over them, at a fast descent speed, is removing a lot. The cylinder heads cool rapidly, and different parts cool at different rates, which sets up stresses that can **crack cylinders** and distort them; a rapidly cooled engine can also glaze its cylinder bores.

**Plug fouling.** A long period at idle is a long period rich and cool: exactly the conditions that foul plugs on the ground.

**Carburettor ice.** Low power means a nearly closed throttle butterfly, which is where carburettor ice forms most readily ([BAKC 3.4](/notes/BAKC/3.4)), and an engine at idle has little heat to spare to melt it.

The answer is to **keep some power on**, descending at a moderate rate rather than a glide, to **descend in stages** if you can, and to apply **carburettor heat** as the POH requires before reducing power. Some POHs also recommend briefly opening the throttle every so often during a long glide to warm the engine and clear the plugs.

![Why does a long idle glide hurt the engine?](diagram:shock-cooling-descent "What to notice: at idle the cylinder heads cool fast and end up too cold; some power on, descending in stages, keeps the fall gentle.")

| Limit or action | Reason |
|---|---|
| Minimum oil pressure | Below it the bearings and cylinder walls are not being lubricated or cooled; the engine will seize |
| Minimum oil temperature | Cold oil is thick and does not flow properly, so parts are starved of oil and pressure may read high; warm the engine before applying high power |

??? check "Check yourself: why can the oil pressure read high just after a cold start, and why is that not good news?"
    Cold oil is thick, so the pump has to push hard against it and the gauge shows a high pressure. But thick oil flows slowly through the narrow passages, so the bearings and cylinder walls are actually getting less oil than they need. That is why you wait for the minimum oil temperature before high power.

## Reading the exhaust smoke

The colour of the smoke from the exhaust tells you what is burning that should not be.

**Blue smoke** is **oil** being burnt in the cylinders. Oil is getting past a seal that should keep it out of the combustion chamber: **worn piston rings** (the oil control ring is no longer scraping the cylinder wall clean) or **worn valve guides** (oil runs down the valve stem into the cylinder). An **over-filled sump** can do it too, by throwing too much oil onto the cylinder walls. Expect **high oil consumption** and **oil-fouled plugs**. A puff of blue on start-up after the aeroplane has been sitting can be oil that has seeped past the rings of the lower cylinders overnight; continuous blue smoke in flight is a maintenance problem.

**Black smoke** is **unburnt fuel**, leaving as soot: the mixture is **too rich**. Check the things that make it rich: the **mixture control** (not leaned when it should be), the **primer** (if it is not locked, it can keep feeding raw fuel into the cylinders), and the **carburettor heat** (hot air is less dense, so the same fuel meets less air and the mixture richens, see [BAKC 3.4](/notes/BAKC/3.4)). A rich engine will also show fouled plugs and higher fuel consumption.

??? check "Check yourself: after a flight with continuous blue exhaust smoke, the dipstick shows the engine used far more oil than usual. What is the likely cause?"
    Oil is reaching the combustion chambers and burning, most likely past worn piston rings or valve guides. That explains both the blue smoke and the high consumption. It is a maintenance item before the next flight.

## Bringing it together

A summer afternoon: you have waited 12 minutes at idle behind traffic at the Jandakot holding point. On the run-up the left magneto drops 160 RPM against a POH limit of 125 and the engine is rough on that side: idle has fouled a plug. You lean, run at the clearing RPM for the POH's period, and repeat: the drop is now 95 and the difference within limits. Departing, you climb at full rich and a speed that keeps air over the cylinders, watching the CHT, because a lean mixture at climb power on a 38 degree day is detonation waiting to happen. Coming home from the training area you plan a powered descent rather than an idle glide, with carburettor heat as the POH requires, so the cylinders stay warm, the plugs stay clean and the butterfly is not sitting nearly closed in humid coastal air. At shutdown the dead cut makes the engine falter, as it should. Every one of those actions is a limit or check from this lesson doing its job.

## Key points

- **3.3.1** Detonation is the unburnt end gas exploding after the spark. Causes: too lean at high power, high manifold pressure with low RPM, too low an octane, made worse by high CHT. Effects: rough running, sharp CHT rise, power loss, damage. Remedy: enrich, reduce power, lower the nose, open cowl flaps.
- **3.3.2** Prolonged idling runs cool and rich and fouls the plugs (a failed magneto check); too rich in flight wastes fuel and fouls plugs, too lean runs hot and invites detonation.
- **3.3.3** Minimum oil pressure protects the bearings; oil and CHT limits keep the oil flowing and the metal strong; maximum RPM protects the moving parts and propeller; the magneto check proves both ignition systems; the dead cut proves the switch earths both magnetos; starter and pitot heat limits stop them overheating; a warm engine on descent avoids shock cooling, fouling and carburettor ice.
- **3.3.4** Blue smoke is oil burning (worn rings or guides, over-filled sump); black smoke is too rich a mixture.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    The examined pairs are cause and effect: lean mixture at high power leads to detonation; prolonged idling leads to plug fouling; cold oil reads high pressure but lubricates poorly; a long idle descent risks shock cooling and carburettor ice; the dead cut finds a live magneto. For the magneto check, quote the POH limits; typical trainer values are a drop of about 125 to 175 RPM and a difference of about 50 RPM.
