## What the exam expects (BAKC 5.2)

- **5.2.1** Look up a given aerodrome in ERSA and read its runway data: designators, length, width, surface, slope, strength, lighting and the remarks that go with them.
- **5.2.2** Read the data for a prohibited, restricted or danger area: its type, limits, controlling authority and conditional status.
- **5.2.3** Work out from ERSA when a restricted area is active, converting the times from UTC to local time.

## Why this matters

Before you land at a country strip you have never seen, say Cunderdin or York, everything you know about it comes from one entry in ERSA: how long the runway is, whether it is sealed or gravel, which way it slopes, whether it has lights, which way the circuit goes and what frequency to call on. Before you route past a military or industrial area, ERSA tells you whether that red-hatched shape on the chart is a firm "keep out" or a "call and ask". And because ERSA writes its times in UTC, you have to convert them before they mean anything in Perth. The exam gives you an ERSA extract (or lets you bring your own current ERSA) and asks you to find things in it, so this lesson is about reading: where each item lives, what each abbreviation means, and how to turn the printed times into local time without getting the conversion backwards.

## How ERSA is organised

**ERSA**, the En Route Supplement Australia, is the book of facts that do not fit on a chart. It is published by Airservices Australia and amended on a regular cycle, so the copy you use must be current. Two sections matter here. The **FAC** (facilities) section has an entry for every listed aerodrome, alphabetically by state. The **PRD** section lists the prohibited, restricted and danger areas with their details. The **introduction and legend** at the front explain the abbreviations, and you should read it once, because almost every field in an entry is abbreviated.

ERSA is not the only document you need, and part of reading it well is knowing what it does not tell you. It is the *permanent* picture. Temporary changes, such as a runway closed for works or a restricted area activated for an exercise, come by **NOTAM** and override ERSA for as long as they are in force.

## Reading an aerodrome entry, block by block

Every FAC entry follows the same layout, so once you can read one you can read them all. Picture the invented aerodrome in the diagram below, Example Creek in Western Australia, and walk through it from the top.

![What is in each block of an ERSA aerodrome entry?](diagram:ersa-entry-anatomy "Runway length and width are in metres in the runway table; elevation, variation and the time zone are in the header.")

**The header** identifies the aerodrome and gives the facts about the place as a whole. For Example Creek it reads: the name and state, **UTC +8**, **ELEV 1050**, a position of S31 00.0 E117 00.0, **VAR 1 DEG W**, and the operator with a phone number. The **elevation** is in **feet** above mean sea level; it is the number you set your altimeter to when you have no QNH, and the starting point for the pressure height and density height calculations of [BAKC 6.2](/notes/BAKC/6.2). The **time zone** tells you how local time relates to UTC: Western Australia is UTC plus 8. The **magnetic variation** is the same quantity the isogonals show on the chart ([BAKC 5.1](/notes/BAKC/5.1)). The **operator** is who you phone for permission or for anything the entry does not answer.

**The runway table** is the heart of the entry. Each runway has a line, and each line has the same fields in order:

- The **designators**, such as 06/24. A runway is named by its magnetic direction rounded to the nearest 10 degrees, with the last zero dropped, so runway 06 points roughly 060 degrees magnetic and runway 24, the same strip used the other way, roughly 240. The two ends always differ by 18.
- The **length and width**, in **metres**: 1200 × 30 means 1,200 m long and 30 m wide. This is the one place on the page where the unit changes from feet to metres, and the exam knows it.
- The **surface**: sealed, gravel, grass or natural. Surface changes the take-off and landing distances (gravel and grass lengthen both), so you need it before you open the performance chart.
- The **slope**, as a percentage and a direction, such as **0.5% up E**: the runway rises towards the east. A runway with no slope given is effectively level.
- The **pavement strength**, which tells the operator of a heavy aircraft whether the surface will carry it. For a light trainer it is rarely limiting.
- The **lighting**, if any. A runway without lighting listed is not lit, and you cannot use it at night.

Where they are published, the **runway distances supplement** gives four declared distances for each runway: **TORA** (take-off run available, the length of runway you can roll on), **TODA** (take-off distance available, the run plus any clearway beyond the end over which you can climb), **ASDA** (accelerate-stop distance available, the run plus any stopway) and **LDA** (landing distance available, which can be shorter than the runway if the threshold is displaced). For a small country strip the runway table is usually all there is.

**The aerodrome diagram** shows the layout: runways, taxiways, the apron, and the circuit direction where it is non-standard. Use it to picture the place before you arrive.

**Communications and navigation aids** list the frequencies: the **CTAF** at a non-controlled aerodrome or the tower frequencies at a controlled one, an **AWIS** (automatic weather) frequency or phone number if there is one, and any navigation aids. At Example Creek there is a CTAF, an AWIS and an NDB.

**Remarks and local traffic regulations** are where the rules peculiar to this aerodrome live, and they are the block pilots skip and then regret skipping. At Example Creek: **right-hand circuits for runway 24**, noise abatement, operating hours, **PPR** (prior permission required: you must phone the operator before you arrive), and AVGAS available.

!!! example "Worked example: runway 24 at Example Creek"
    Question: what are the length, width, surface, slope and lighting of runway 24, and is there anything special about using it?

    1. Find the line in the runway table that contains 24: it is the **06/24** line, because 06 and 24 are the two ends of the same runway.
    2. Length and width: **1,200 m × 30 m**, in metres, from the LENGTH × WIDTH column.
    3. Surface: **sealed**.
    4. Slope: **0.5% up E**. Runway 24 points roughly 240 degrees magnetic, towards the west-south-west, so you take off from the eastern, higher end and roll **downhill**. Over 1,200 m a 0.5 percent slope is a rise of $1200 \times 0.005 = \mathbf{6\ m}$, about 20 ft from end to end.
    5. Lighting: the table notes lighting for **RWY 06/24**, so runway 24 is lit.
    6. Remarks: **right-hand circuits for runway 24**. If you join for 24 you fly the circuit to the right, not the standard left.

    Everything the runway table gave you goes straight into the take-off and landing charts; the remark changes how you join.

Now try one before opening the solution: what can you find out about runway 30 at Example Creek, and could you use it for a night landing?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    Runway 30 is the other end of the **12/30** line: **800 m × 18 m**, **gravel**, with no slope given, so treat it as level. The table lists lighting only for 06/24, so **12/30 is unlit and cannot be used at night**. Being gravel and 400 m shorter, it will also give you a longer take-off and landing distance on a shorter strip, so check the performance chart before planning to use it. The right-hand circuit remark applies to runway 24, not to 30, so the circuit on 30 is the standard left-hand one.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Runway lengths and widths in ERSA are in **metres**, while the elevation in the same entry is in **feet**. A 1,200 m runway is about 3,900 ft long. Mixing the two, or reading the length as feet, makes a short strip look generous or a generous one look short.

??? check "Check yourself: an ERSA runway line reads 14/32, 950 × 18, natural, 1.2% up NW. Which way do you roll downhill?"
    The runway rises towards the north-west. Runway 32 points roughly 320 degrees magnetic, towards the north-west, so taking off on 32 is uphill. Runway 14 points south-east, away from the high end, so taking off on 14 is downhill.

## Reading the PRD areas

Some airspace is set aside because something dangerous or sensitive happens in it: a naval base, a weapons range, a parachuting zone, blasting at a mine. Rather than draw the whole story on the chart, the chart shows the shape, the identifier and the limits, and ERSA's PRD section (with AIP ENR 5.1) gives the rest. The letter at the start of the identifier tells you the kind of area and how strictly you are kept out.

**P, prohibited.** Flight is not permitted at any time, for any reason. These are few, over sensitive installations.

**R, restricted.** Flight is not permitted unless the **controlling authority** gives you a clearance, with whatever conditions it attaches. Most restricted areas are not active all the time, which is why the hours matter.

**D, danger.** Flight is permitted, but a hazard exists: gunnery, parachuting, gliding, blasting. Nobody will stop you entering, but you are expected to know what the hazard is and to avoid the area if that is practicable.

Restricted areas carry one more piece of information that tells you how to plan: the **conditional status**.

- **RA1**: a clearance can normally be obtained, and you may plan to fly through the area.
- **RA2**: do not flight plan through the area; ATC may give a clearance tactically on the day, but do not count on it. Plan to avoid it.
- **RA3**: no clearance will be available. Plan around it.

The ERSA PRD listing for a restricted area gives, in order: the **identifier and name**, the **vertical limits** (for example SFC to 5,000 ft, or up to FL125), the **hours of activity**, the **controlling authority** with its contact details, and the **conditional status**. A typical listing reads like: "R155 Garden Island: SFC to 5,000 ft, H24, controlling authority Navy, RA3." (This is an illustration of the format; read the real area's limits, hours and status from the current ERSA.)

![What does each field of an ERSA PRD listing tell you?](diagram:prd-listing-anatomy "What to notice: the letter says how strictly you are kept out, the limits whether you are inside, the hours whether it is active, the authority whom to ask, and the RA status whether you may plan through. The listing is illustrative, not a real area.")

!!! example "Worked example: reading a restricted area listing"
    Given the listing: "R155 Garden Island: SFC to 5,000 ft, H24, controlling authority Navy, RA3."

    1. The letter **R**: a restricted area. Entry needs a clearance from the controlling authority.
    2. Vertical limits: from the **surface to 5,000 ft**. Below 5,000 ft over the island you are inside it.
    3. Hours: **H24**, active continuously. There is no quiet time to wait for.
    4. Controlling authority: **Navy**. That is who would issue a clearance, and who to contact with questions.
    5. Conditional status: **RA3**. No clearance will be available.

    Conclusion: plan your route around this area, laterally or above its upper limit with a margin; do not plan to request a clearance through it.

![Which document answers which question?](diagram:flight-documents-flow "Runway data: ERSA FAC. A restricted area: chart for the identifier and limits, ERSA PRD for hours and status, NOTAM for changes, then the controlling authority.")

??? check "Check yourself: a restricted area beside your track is listed RA1 and another is listed RA2. How does that change your planning?"
    You may plan through the RA1 area, expecting a clearance to be normally available, though you still need one before entering. You should plan to avoid the RA2 area: you may not flight plan through it, and a tactical clearance on the day is not assured. With RA3 there is no clearance at all.

## When is a restricted area active?

A restricted area that is not active is ordinary airspace. So the question that saves you a long detour, or keeps you out of trouble, is whether it is active at the time you will be there. ERSA's **hours of activity** field answers it with a small set of abbreviations:

| Abbreviation | Meaning |
|---|---|
| H24 | Active continuously |
| HJ | Active during daylight (sunrise to sunset) |
| HN | Active during the night |
| HO | Active during the hours of operation of the unit |
| Specific times | Given in UTC, for example 0000-0800 Mon-Fri; convert to local by adding 8 hours in Western Australia |
| By NOTAM | Activated by NOTAM; check NOTAMs before flight |
| Active at times as notified | Also depends on NOTAM or the controlling authority |

The first four are words in disguise: H24 is "hours, 24"; HJ comes from the French *jour*, day; HN is night; HO is "hours of operation". "By NOTAM" means the area sleeps until a NOTAM wakes it: **if an area is shown as NOTAM and no NOTAM has activated it, it is inactive**.

The specific times are the ones you calculate. ERSA writes them in **UTC**, Coordinated Universal Time, so that one book works across every Australian time zone. Western Australia has no daylight saving, so **Western Standard Time (WST) is always UTC plus 8 hours** (the conversion is taught in full in [BAKC 2.3](/notes/BAKC/2.3)). To read an ERSA time in Perth, add 8. If the answer passes 2400, subtract 24 and move to the next day.

![What does an ERSA time of 2200–0600 mean in Western Australia?](diagram:utc-to-wst-activity "Add 8 hours: 2200–0600 UTC is 0600–1400 WST, the same moments read on a different clock.")

The diagram shows why the conversion can look strange and still be right. The UTC clock and the WST clock are two labels for the same moments. An activity period that crosses midnight UTC, such as 2200 to 0600, looks like an overnight period in UTC but sits entirely in the WST morning and early afternoon, because midnight UTC is 0800 in Perth.

!!! example "Worked example: an area active 2200-0200 UTC"
    Given: ERSA lists a restricted area as active 2200-0200 UTC. You plan to pass it at 0930 WST.

    1. Start of activity: $2200 + 8 = 3000$, which is past 2400, so subtract 24: **0600 WST**, on the next calendar day from the UTC date.
    2. End of activity: $0200 + 8 = $ **1000 WST**.
    3. So the area is active **0600 to 1000 WST**, all within one WST morning, even though the UTC period crossed midnight.
    4. 0930 WST is inside that window: the area **is active** when you get there.

    Either plan around it, ask the controlling authority for a clearance (depending on its conditional status), or time your flight to pass after 1000 WST.

!!! example "Worked example: an area active 0000-0800 Mon-Fri"
    Given: ERSA lists an area as active 0000-0800 UTC, Monday to Friday. Is it active on a Wednesday afternoon at 1530 WST?

    1. Start: $0000 + 8 = $ **0800 WST**.
    2. End: $0800 + 8 = $ **1600 WST**.
    3. Because 0000 UTC on a Monday is 0800 WST on the same Monday, the days line up: the area is active **0800 to 1600 WST, Monday to Friday**.
    4. 1530 WST on a Wednesday is inside the window: **active**. Arrive after 1600 and it is not.

    A working-hours area in Perth is a working-hours area in UTC shifted by eight.

Now try one before opening the solution: an area is listed active 1400-2200 UTC. When is it active in WST, and is it active at 0500 WST?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    Start $1400 + 8 = 2200$ WST. End $2200 + 8 = 3000$, minus 24 gives **0600 WST the next day**. So the area is active **2200 WST to 0600 WST**, overnight in Perth. 0500 WST falls inside that window, so the area **is active** at 0500. This time the WST period crosses local midnight even though the UTC one did not.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The conversion goes one way: **UTC to WST, add 8**. Subtracting gives a time sixteen hours out, which in the exam is usually one of the distractors. Watch the day too: 2200 UTC Monday is 0600 WST **Tuesday**, so an area listed for certain UTC days can fall on different WST days. When a listing crosses midnight UTC and the day matters, confirm with the NOTAMs or the controlling authority.

Reading the hours is not the end of the check. A restricted area can be **activated, deactivated or have its limits changed by NOTAM**, so read the NOTAMs for the area before every flight. If you are still in doubt, call the controlling authority listed in ERSA. And remember that an active restricted area needs a clearance; without one, you stay out.

![Is that airspace active? The pre-flight check](diagram:airspace-active-check "Chart for the boundaries and limits, ERSA PRD for the hours and controlling authority, NOTAM for changes, then ask the controlling authority; without a clearance, stay out.")

??? check "Check yourself: an ERSA listing shows a restricted area's hours as NOTAM. You find no NOTAM for it. Is it active?"
    No. An area activated by NOTAM is inactive unless a current NOTAM has activated it. Check the NOTAMs on the day of flight, because the answer can change, and call the controlling authority if anything is unclear.

## Bringing it together

On a weekday you plan Jandakot to Example Creek, arriving about 1100 WST. The FAC entry for Example Creek gives an elevation of 1,050 ft (your QNH reference and the start of your density height sum), runway 06/24 at 1,200 m sealed and lit with a 0.5 percent slope rising to the east, and 12/30 at 800 m gravel and unlit. The remarks say PPR, so you phone the operator, and right-hand circuits on 24, so with a westerly forecast you plan a right-hand circuit on to 24, landing slightly downhill. On the chart a restricted area sits beside your track. ERSA PRD lists it 0000-0800 Mon-Fri UTC with RA2 status: that is 0800 to 1600 WST on weekdays, so it will be active when you pass, a clearance is unlikely at short notice, and you plan to fly around it. The NOTAMs show no change. Two sections of one book, the NOTAMs, and the plan is complete.

## Key points

- **5.2.1** Runway data is in the ERSA FAC entry: elevation (feet) and time zone in the header; runway designators (magnetic to the nearest 10 degrees), length and width in **metres**, surface, slope, strength and lighting in the runway table; circuit direction, PPR and other local rules in the remarks.
- **5.2.2** P = never; R = only with a clearance from the controlling authority; D = permitted, but know the hazard. RA1 plan through, RA2 plan to avoid, RA3 no clearance. The ERSA PRD listing gives identifier, limits, hours, controlling authority and status.
- **5.2.3** H24, HJ, HN, HO, or specific times in **UTC**: add 8 for WST. An area "by NOTAM" is inactive unless a NOTAM activates it. Always check the NOTAMs, and call the controlling authority if in doubt.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    ERSA times are in UTC. A restricted area listed as active 2200-0600 is active from 0600 to 1400 Western Standard Time. Getting the conversion backwards is a common way to lose this mark. In runway questions, the length and width are in metres; read the whole runway line, including the slope direction, and then check the remarks for anything that applies to the runway you were asked about.
