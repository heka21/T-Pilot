## What the exam expects (BAKC 2.2)

- **2.2.1** Name the units for lateral (horizontal) distance: nautical miles for navigation, metres and kilometres for visibility.
- **2.2.2** Define the knot as the unit of aircraft speed.
- **2.2.3** Define wind velocity (W/V) and read it the way it is written.
- **2.2.4** Tell apart indicated airspeed (IAS), calibrated airspeed (CAS), true airspeed (TAS) and groundspeed (GS), and say what turns each into the next.

## Why this matters

Plan your first cross-country from Jandakot to Northam and you will meet every idea in this lesson before you start the engine. The distance on the chart is in nautical miles, the visibility in the forecast is in kilometres, the wind is written as something like 250/15, and the speed you will use to work out the flight time is not the one on the airspeed indicator. Get them muddled and the flight time is wrong, so the fuel is wrong, so the reserve you thought you had is not there. The exam asks short, precise questions here: which unit goes with which job, what a knot is, which way a wind of 090/25 blows, and the order of the four airspeeds. It also expects you to do the one-line arithmetic that links them. This lesson builds the four speeds from the instrument outwards, because once you see what each correction is for, the order looks after itself.

## Distance over the ground: the nautical mile

Look at the edge of any aeronautical chart and you will find the latitude scale: degrees, each divided into 60 minutes. That scale is the reason aviation measures distance in **nautical miles (NM)**. The Earth is close to a sphere, and a full circle around it through the poles is 360 degrees of latitude, each of 60 minutes. A nautical mile was chosen to be **one minute of latitude**. The modern definition fixes it at exactly **1,852 metres**, about 6,076 ft, which is one minute of latitude to within the accuracy you will ever need.

That choice pays off every time you plan. Want to know how far 18 NM is on the chart? Open your dividers to 18 minutes on the latitude scale beside the route and you have it, on any chart, at any scale. Because one degree of latitude is 60 minutes, it is also 60 NM, so Perth to Geraldton, about 4 degrees of latitude further north, is roughly 240 NM in a straight line north-south.

![Why is the latitude scale on a chart also a distance scale?](diagram:nautical-mile-latitude "One minute of latitude is one nautical mile, so 1° = 60' = 60 NM, and dividers set against the latitude scale measure distance on any chart.")

All navigation distances are in nautical miles: distances between places, DME ranges, radius limits around an aerodrome, the lengths of legs on your nav log. "Rottnest is about 18 NM from Jandakot" is the normal way to say it.

| Use | Unit | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Navigation (distances between places, DME ranges, radius limits) | Nautical miles (NM) | "Rottnest is about 18 NM from Jandakot" |
| Visibility | Metres (m) and kilometres (km) | "Visibility 5000 m", "visibility 10 km" |
| Runway length and width | Metres | Covered in [BAKC 2.4](/notes/BAKC/2.4) |

The conversions you need:

- 1 NM = 1,852 m = 1.852 km
- 1 km = 0.54 NM
- 1 statute mile = 0.87 NM (you will rarely meet statute miles in Australia)

![How do the units you will meet convert?](diagram:units-conversion-ladder "Read straight across each scale: 1 NM is 1.852 km, 110 kt is 204 km/h, and 100 L of avgas weighs about 72 kg.")

## Visibility: metres and kilometres

**Visibility**, the distance you can see and identify objects, is the odd one out. It is measured and reported in **metres** for lower values and **kilometres** for higher ones: "visibility 3000 m", "visibility 10 km". It is a weather observation, made by observers and instruments on the ground using the metric system, and the VMC minima you will learn in the flight rules are written in the same units.

The practical consequence is that you will often be holding two different units in your head at once: the forecast says visibility 5 km in showers, and the next town on your route is 8 NM away. When you need to compare them, convert one into the other. A kilometre is a little over half a nautical mile, so the visibility in nautical miles is a little over half the number of kilometres.

!!! example "Worked example: visibility against a navigation distance"
    Given: the forecast visibility is 8 km. The next landmark on track is 5 NM ahead.

    1. Visibility in NM: $8 \times 0.54 = \mathbf{4.3}$ NM.
    2. Compare: $4.3 < 5$, so the landmark is **beyond** the visibility when you start looking.

    You will not see the landmark until you are about 4 NM from it, so you hold heading and time rather than search for it.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The exam offers "kilometres for navigation and nautical miles for visibility" as a distractor, and statute miles appear in American material. In Australia: navigation in nautical miles, visibility in metres and kilometres.

??? check "Check yourself: why is the nautical mile a convenient unit for navigation?"
    Because one nautical mile is one minute of latitude, so the latitude scale on the side of every chart is a ready-made distance scale, and one degree of latitude is 60 NM.

## The knot

Once distance is in nautical miles, speed follows. A **knot (kt)** is a speed of **one nautical mile per hour**. Airspeeds, groundspeeds and wind speeds in Australian aviation are all in knots. The "per hour" is built into the word, so a pilot never says "knots per hour", which would be an acceleration, not a speed. The name comes from ships, which once measured speed by counting knots on a line paid out over the stern in a fixed time.

The knot is to the nautical mile what the kilometre per hour is to the kilometre, so converting between them uses the same 1.852:

$$\text{km/h} = \text{kt} \times 1.852 \qquad \text{kt} = \text{km/h} \div 1.852$$

!!! example "Worked example: knots and kilometres per hour"
    Given: a cruise speed of 110 kt, and a car-park speed limit of 100 km/h you want to compare with your taxi and take-off speeds.

    1. 110 kt in km/h: $110 \times 1.852 = \mathbf{204}$ km/h (203.7).
    2. 100 km/h in knots: $100 \div 1.852 = \mathbf{54}$ kt (54.0).

    A light trainer lifts off at a speed a car on the freeway would recognise, and cruises at about twice it.

### Knots in minutes: the number you will use most

In flight you rarely think in hours. Dividing the speed by 60 gives the nautical miles covered **per minute**, and that is the number that makes mental navigation easy. At 60 kt you cover 1 NM a minute; at 90 kt, 1.5 NM a minute; at **120 kt, 2 NM a minute**. So at 120 kt, a 25-minute leg covers $25 \times 2 = 50$ NM, and a town 10 NM ahead is 5 minutes away.

The general formula for time on a leg is the distance divided by the speed over the ground, turned into minutes:

$$\text{time (min)} = \frac{\text{distance (NM)}}{\text{groundspeed (kt)}} \times 60$$

The speed in that formula is the **groundspeed**, because the distance is over the ground. Why that is not the number on the airspeed indicator is the rest of this lesson.

??? check "Check yourself: what is wrong with saying an aeroplane cruises at '100 knots per hour'?"
    A knot is already one nautical mile per hour, so "knots per hour" would mean a change of speed each hour, an acceleration. The correct phrase is simply "100 knots".

## Wind velocity

A **speed** is how fast something moves. A **velocity** is how fast **and in what direction**. Wind is a velocity: 15 kt of wind blowing straight down the runway and 15 kt blowing straight across it are the same speed but very different winds. So **wind velocity**, written **W/V**, is given as a direction and a speed together.

The convention is that the direction is where the wind blows **from**, as a 3-figure group, followed by the speed in knots. **270/15** means a 15 kt wind blowing **from** the west, towards the east. A wind "from 090" is an easterly. That matches how you feel wind: you face into a westerly wind when you face west, and the sea breeze at Perth is a south-westerly because it comes off the sea from the south-west.

![Which way does a 270/15 wind blow?](diagram:wind-velocity-from "Wind is named for where it comes from: 270/15 blows from the west, a headwind flying west and a tailwind flying east.")

Naming the wind for where it comes from makes the headwind question simple. If the wind direction is the same as your heading, the wind is coming straight at your nose: a pure **headwind**. If it is the reciprocal of your heading, it is a pure **tailwind**. Anything in between has a headwind or tailwind part and a crosswind part, and a runway wind is usually some of each.

![Split any wind into headwind and crosswind components for a runway](widget:crosswind-component "Thirty degrees off the runway already puts half the wind across it; compare the crosswind with the POH's demonstrated figure.")

The direction reference matters, and it connects with [BAKC 2.1](/notes/BAKC/2.1). Winds in forecasts (TAF, GAF or area forecast) and in METAR and SPECI reports are given in degrees **true**, because they are meteorological products drawn on true-north maps. Surface winds passed by ATC or on an ATIS are in degrees **magnetic**, so you can compare them directly with the runway number. Around Perth the difference is only a degree or two; elsewhere it can be ten.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    A wind is named for where it comes **from**, not where it goes. A westerly, 270, blows towards the east: it is a headwind for an aeroplane flying west and a tailwind for one flying east. The exam's distractor is the reverse.

??? check "Check yourself: the ATIS at Jandakot gives a wind of 240/12 and runway 24 is in use. What is the wind doing to the take-off?"
    It is a 12 kt headwind straight down the runway: the wind comes from 240 magnetic and the runway points 240 magnetic. Both figures are magnetic, which is why ATIS winds are given that way.

## Four airspeeds: IAS, CAS, TAS and GS

Now the speed of the aeroplane itself. There are four numbers, and each answers a different question. The way to keep them straight is to start with the instrument and correct it, one error at a time, until you arrive at the speed over the ground.

![How do you get from indicated airspeed to groundspeed?](diagram:airspeed-chain-ictg "Always I, C, T, G: instrument and position error, then density, then wind.")

### Indicated airspeed: what the instrument shows

The airspeed indicator does not measure speed directly. A **pitot tube** facing forward into the airflow feels the air rammed into it, and a **static vent** on the side of the fuselage feels the still pressure of the surrounding air. The airspeed indicator measures the difference, which is the **dynamic pressure**: the extra pressure caused by the aeroplane's motion through the air. Dynamic pressure grows with the speed and with the **density** of the air (formally, it is $\tfrac{1}{2}\rho V^2$). The dial is marked in knots on the assumption that the air has standard sea-level density.

![Where does the airspeed indicator get its pressures?](diagram:pitot-static-system "The pitot tube feeds only the airspeed indicator; the static vent feeds all three pressure instruments, and the ASI measures the difference.")

The reading on the dial is the **indicated airspeed (IAS)**. It is the speed the wing "feels", because the wing's lift depends on the same dynamic pressure. That is why the aeroplane stalls, rotates and reaches its structural limits at the same IAS whatever the altitude, and why every speed in the POH and on the coloured arcs of the ASI is an indicated speed. You fly the aeroplane on IAS.

### Calibrated airspeed: correcting the instrument's errors

No pitot-static installation is perfect. The static vent sits on the side of a fuselage that disturbs the air around it, and as the angle of attack and the flap setting change, the pressure it senses changes slightly. That is **position error** (also called installation error). The instrument itself, with its capsule and gearing, has a small **instrument error** as well. Correct the IAS for these two and you have the **calibrated airspeed (CAS)**: the IAS the instrument would show if it were perfect. The correction comes from the airspeed calibration table in section 5 of the POH, and in a light aeroplane it is usually only a few knots, largest at low speed with flap down.

### True airspeed: correcting for density

Now climb. The air thins, so at the same speed through it the pitot tube receives less dynamic pressure and the needle reads lower. Hold the same IAS and the aeroplane must actually be moving faster through the thinner air to produce the same dynamic pressure. The real speed of the aeroplane through the air mass is the **true airspeed (TAS)**, and it is CAS corrected for **air density**, which means for the pressure altitude and the temperature. In practice you do it with the flight computer.

A rule of thumb gives you the size of the effect: **TAS exceeds IAS by about 2 percent per 1,000 ft** of altitude. At 8,000 ft, an IAS of 100 kt is roughly $100 \times 1.16 = 116$ kt TAS. At sea level on a standard day the two are the same. TAS is the speed you use for navigation: it is how fast the aeroplane is really moving through the air, before the wind is considered.

### Groundspeed: correcting for wind

The air mass itself is moving over the ground. The **groundspeed (GS)** is the aeroplane's actual speed **over the ground**, and it is the TAS combined with the wind velocity. Flying straight into a headwind, the wind is subtracted; straight downwind, it is added. With the wind at an angle only part of it acts along your track, and you will work those cases on the flight computer in the navigation unit. Groundspeed is the speed for time, distance and fuel: how long the leg will take.

![What does a 20 kt headwind or tailwind do to groundspeed?](diagram:groundspeed-headwind-tailwind "Same TAS of 105 kt: a 20 kt headwind leaves 85 kt over the ground, a 20 kt tailwind gives 125 kt.")

| Term | Meaning | How you get it | Used for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **IAS** Indicated airspeed | What the airspeed indicator shows | Read directly | Flying the aeroplane: stall, rotate, limits |
| **CAS** Calibrated airspeed | IAS corrected for instrument error and position (installation) error | IAS plus the correction from the POH table | The input to the TAS calculation |
| **TAS** True airspeed | The actual speed of the aircraft through the air mass | CAS corrected for air density (pressure altitude and temperature) | Navigation and flight planning |
| **GS** Groundspeed | The actual speed of the aircraft over the ground | TAS combined with the wind velocity | Time, distance and fuel |

!!! example "Worked example: headwind and tailwind on the same TAS"
    Given: TAS 105 kt, tracking directly into a 20 kt headwind, then the same aeroplane with a 20 kt tailwind.

    1. Headwind: $GS = 105 - 20 = \mathbf{85}$ kt.
    2. Tailwind: $GS = 105 + 20 = \mathbf{125}$ kt.

    The same aeroplane, at the same power, covers the ground almost 50 percent faster one way than the other. That is why the return leg of a there-and-back flight in a strong wind is never the same length.

!!! example "Worked example: from TAS to flight time"
    Given: TAS 95 kt, a 15 kt headwind straight on the nose, and a leg of 48 NM.

    1. Groundspeed: $95 - 15 = \mathbf{80}$ kt.
    2. Time: $\dfrac{48}{80} \times 60 = 0.6 \times 60 = \mathbf{36}$ min.

    In still air the same leg would take $48 \div 95 \times 60 \approx 30$ minutes. The headwind has added 6 minutes, and 6 minutes of fuel.

Try the whole chain before opening the solution. At 6,000 ft your airspeed indicator shows 100 kt and the POH table says CAS is 2 kt less than IAS at that speed. You have a 20 kt tailwind straight along your track. Using the 2 percent rule of thumb, roughly what is your groundspeed?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    CAS $= 100 - 2 = 98$ kt. TAS: 6,000 ft is 6 lots of 2 percent, 12 percent, so $98 \times 1.12 = 109.8$, about **110 kt**. GS $= 110 + 20 =$ **about 130 kt**. Each step corrected one thing: the instrument, then the density, then the wind.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    TAS is higher than IAS at altitude, not lower. The needle reads low in thin air; the aeroplane is going faster than it shows. And the order never changes: I, C, T, G. A distractor that "corrects IAS for wind to get TAS" has skipped two steps and mixed up the last one.

??? check "Check yourself: why do you fly the approach on IAS but plan the flight on TAS and GS?"
    Because the wing's lift and the stall depend on dynamic pressure, which is exactly what the ASI measures, so the right approach speed is the same IAS at any altitude. Navigation needs the real speed through the air (TAS) and then over the ground (GS) to work out time and fuel.

## Bringing it together

Jandakot to Northam is about 48 NM on the chart: distance in nautical miles. The forecast gives visibility 10 km, a little over 5 NM, so you will see Northam from about 5 NM out. The GAF wind at your planned 4,500 ft is 060/15, in degrees true; your track is about 056 true, so the wind is almost straight on the nose. Your POH gives a cruise of 87 kt indicated at that level. The 2 percent rule adds 4.5 lots of 2 percent, 9 percent, which turns that into $87 \times 1.09 \approx 95$ kt TAS, and the 15 kt headwind takes that to a groundspeed of about 80 kt. Forty-eight nautical miles at 80 kt is 36 minutes, against about 30 in still air. On the ground at Northam the AWIS gives a surface wind in degrees magnetic, ready to compare with the runway number. Every unit and every speed in this lesson has done a job.

## Key points

- **2.2.1** Navigation distance in nautical miles (1 NM = 1,852 m = one minute of latitude); visibility in metres and kilometres (1 km = 0.54 NM).
- **2.2.2** A knot is one nautical mile per hour. kt × 1.852 = km/h. Speed ÷ 60 gives NM per minute: 120 kt is 2 NM a minute.
- **2.2.3** Wind velocity is direction and speed, written direction/speed, with the direction the wind blows **from**: 270/15 is a 15 kt westerly. Forecast and METAR winds are true; ATC and ATIS winds are magnetic.
- **2.2.4** IAS is the dial; CAS corrects instrument and position error; TAS corrects for density (about 2 percent per 1,000 ft); GS corrects for wind. Fly on IAS, navigate on TAS and GS.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Have these by heart: 1 NM = 1,852 m, a knot is 1 NM per hour, wind is named for where it comes from, and the order is I, C, T, G with instrument and position error, then density, then wind. Expect a knots to km/h conversion, a "what does 090/25 mean" question, and a groundspeed question that makes you apply the 2 percent rule before adding or subtracting the wind. Read whether the question gives you IAS or TAS before you start.
