## What the exam expects (BAKC 3.5)

- **3.5.1** Explain what each colour marking on the airspeed indicator means.
- **3.5.2** Describe how the primary flight instruments, and the systems that feed them, work.
- **3.5.3** State what a pitot or static blockage does to each pressure instrument; what using an alternate static source inside the cockpit does to their reliability; what low suction and loss of electrical power do to the gyro instruments; what makes a gyro topple and when it will re-erect; and how, when and why the directional indicator is aligned with the magnetic compass.
- **3.5.4** Describe how you check that the primary flight instruments and the magnetic compass are serviceable.

## Why this matters

Six instruments in front of you answer the questions you ask on every flight: how fast, how high, climbing or descending, which way up, which way pointing, turning or not. On a clear day at Jandakot you could fly the circuit without most of them, but the day the cloud base comes down over the Darling Scarp, or the sun is low over the sea on a hazy evening, they are what keep you oriented. Each one can lie in a particular way, and a pilot who knows how it works can tell when it is lying. The exam tests the colour arcs, the blockage effects (the most commonly examined part), the gyro power sources, and the altimeter subscale arithmetic.

## The pressure instruments and the pitot-static system

Three instruments, the airspeed indicator, the altimeter and the vertical speed indicator, work entirely by measuring air pressure. They need no electrical power and no vacuum, which is why they are the ones you trust when everything else has failed.

They are fed by two sources. The **pitot tube**, usually under the wing, points straight into the airflow. Air rams into it and stops, so the pressure inside is the pressure of the still air around you plus the extra pressure of the air being brought to a halt: **pitot pressure** (total pressure). The **static vent**, a small hole flush with the side of the fuselage where the airflow runs smoothly past, senses only the pressure of the surrounding air: **static pressure**. The difference between them, pitot minus static, is **dynamic pressure**, the pressure due to the aeroplane's motion through the air, $\tfrac{1}{2}\rho V^2$.

![Which instruments use pitot pressure and which use static?](diagram:pitot-static-system "Pitot pressure goes only to the ASI; static pressure feeds all three, so a static fault affects every pressure instrument.")

Notice the plumbing in the diagram, because the whole of the blockage section depends on it. **Pitot pressure goes only to the ASI. Static pressure goes to all three.**

### The airspeed indicator

Inside the ASI is a thin, flexible metal **capsule** (a diaphragm). Pitot pressure is piped into the capsule; static pressure fills the sealed case around it. The static pressure acts on both sides and cancels out, so the capsule expands by an amount that depends only on the difference, **dynamic pressure**. Gears turn that expansion into a needle movement, calibrated in knots. That reading is **indicated airspeed** (IAS).

Dynamic pressure depends on air density as well as speed. At altitude, where the air is thinner, the aeroplane must fly faster through the air (a higher true airspeed) to produce the same dynamic pressure and the same IAS. But dynamic pressure is exactly what the wing feels too, so the wing stalls at the same IAS at any height. That is why stall speeds and limit speeds are given as **indicated** speeds.

### The altimeter

The altimeter is a barometer. Inside its case is a stack of sealed, evacuated **aneroid capsules**, and the case is filled with static pressure. As you climb, static pressure falls, the capsules expand, and gears drive the needles round. The scale is calibrated using the standard atmosphere, in which pressure falls by about **1 hPa for every 30 ft** near sea level.

The **subscale** (the small window where you set a pressure in hectopascals) sets the **datum**, the pressure level the altimeter treats as zero. Set the local **QNH** and the altimeter reads **altitude**, height above mean sea level. Set **1013 hPa** and it reads **pressure height**. Turning the subscale up by 1 hPa moves the reading up by about 30 ft.

![Which height is which?](diagram:height-altitude-elevation "With QNH set the altimeter reads altitude above mean sea level; height above the ground is altitude minus the elevation of what is beneath you.")

That 30 ft rule gives the relationship between the two readings:

$$\text{pressure height} \approx \text{altitude} + (1013 - \text{QNH}) \times 30\ \text{ft}$$

!!! example "Worked example: the wrong subscale setting on the ground"
    Given: an aerodrome elevation of 500 ft; today's QNH 1003 hPa; the previous pilot left 1013 set on the subscale.

    1. Subscale error: $1013 - 1003 = \mathbf{10\ hPa}$ too high.
    2. Reading error: $10 \times 30 = \mathbf{300\ ft}$.
    3. Altimeter reads: $500 + 300 = \mathbf{800\ ft}$.

    The altimeter over-reads by 300 ft: it shows 800 ft while you are sitting on a 500 ft aerodrome. In flight with that setting you would be 300 ft lower than indicated. Setting the QNH before take-off is what makes the reading true.

Any setting that is too high makes the altimeter read too high, by 30 ft per hectopascal: set 1023 when the QNH is 1013 and it over-reads by $(1023 - 1013) \times 30 = 300$ ft, so you are 300 ft lower than it shows. Try the pressure-height version yourself: an aerodrome at 1,200 ft elevation with QNH 1008. What is the pressure height?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    Pressure height $= 1200 + (1013 - 1008) \times 30 = 1200 + 150 =$ **1,350 ft**. A QNH below 1013 means the air here is at a lower pressure than standard, so the aerodrome behaves as if it were higher; with 1013 set the altimeter would read 1,350 ft.

The same error happens in flight without anyone touching the subscale, when the **pressure changes along your route**. The pilot's saying is **"from high to low, look out below"**.

!!! example "Worked example: from high to low"
    Given: you depart with the QNH of 1020 hPa set and fly towards a low-pressure trough where the QNH is 1005 hPa, without resetting.

    1. Subscale now too high by: $1020 - 1005 = \mathbf{15\ hPa}$.
    2. Over-reading: $15 \times 30 = \mathbf{450\ ft}$.

    If you hold an indicated 2,500 ft, you are actually at about 2,050 ft: **450 ft lower than indicated**. Over rising ground or under a lowering cloud base, that is the margin that disappears. Flying from low to high pressure does the opposite: the altimeter under-reads and you are higher than it shows.

![Explore the altimeter subscale](widget:altimeter-subscale "Each hectopascal between the subscale and the real QNH moves the reading about 30 ft; set too high a number, or fly from high pressure into low, and you are lower than the altimeter says.")

### The vertical speed indicator

The VSI shows the **rate** at which static pressure is changing. Static pressure goes straight into a capsule, and also into the instrument case, but only through a tiny **calibrated leak** (a restriction). In level flight the pressures are equal and the needle reads zero. In a climb, static pressure falls; the capsule follows at once but the case lags behind, because air can only escape through the leak slowly. The case is left at a slightly higher pressure than the capsule, the capsule is squeezed, and the needle shows a climb in feet per minute. The faster the climb, the bigger the difference.

Because it works on that lag, the VSI **lags** the real change by a few seconds: it shows the **trend** almost at once but takes a few seconds to settle on the true rate. An abrupt pitch change can also show a brief **reverse indication** before it settles. Use it to confirm a trend, not to chase the needle.

![What is inside each pressure instrument?](diagram:asi-altimeter-vsi-cutaways "What to notice: all three cases hold static pressure; the ASI capsule is fed pitot pressure, the altimeter capsules are sealed, and the VSI case fills only through a calibrated leak.")

??? check "Check yourself: why does the wing stall at the same indicated airspeed at 7,000 ft as at sea level?"
    Both the wing and the ASI respond to dynamic pressure, which depends on density and speed together. At altitude the aeroplane must fly at a higher true airspeed to produce the same dynamic pressure, so the wing reaches its critical angle at the same IAS. The true airspeed at the stall is higher; the indicated one is not.

## The colours on the airspeed indicator

The ASI's face carries coloured arcs that let you see at a glance where the needle is relative to the aeroplane's speed limits. Each arc begins or ends at a **V-speed** from the POH.

The **white arc** is the **flap operating range**. Its bottom is **V<sub>SO</sub>**, the stall speed in the landing configuration (full flap, power off, maximum weight). Its top is **V<sub>FE</sub>**, the maximum speed with flap extended: above it the air loads could damage the flaps.

The **green arc** is the **normal operating range**. Its bottom is **V<sub>S1</sub>**, the stall speed clean (flaps up). Its top is **V<sub>NO</sub>**, the **maximum structural cruising speed**, the fastest you should fly in turbulence.

The **yellow arc**, from V<sub>NO</sub> to V<sub>NE</sub>, is the **caution range**: **smooth air only**, and **no abrupt or large control inputs**. A sharp gust at these speeds can overstress the airframe.

The **red line** is **V<sub>NE</sub>**, the **never-exceed speed**. Beyond it, structural failure or flutter is possible.

Twin-engine aeroplanes also carry a **blue line**, the best single-engine rate-of-climb speed (V<sub>YSE</sub>).

![What do the colours on the airspeed indicator mean?](diagram:asi-colour-markings "White is the flap range, green normal, yellow smooth air only, red never exceed; each arc starts or ends at a V-speed, and VA is not marked.")

| Marking | Meaning | Speeds at the ends |
|---|---|---|
| White arc | Flap operating range | Bottom: V<sub>SO</sub>, stall speed in the landing configuration. Top: V<sub>FE</sub>, maximum speed with flap extended |
| Green arc | Normal operating range | Bottom: V<sub>S1</sub>, stall speed clean (flaps up). Top: V<sub>NO</sub>, maximum structural cruising speed |
| Yellow arc | Caution range: smooth air only, no abrupt control inputs | From V<sub>NO</sub> to V<sub>NE</sub> |
| Red line | V<sub>NE</sub>, never-exceed speed | Structural failure possible beyond it |
| Blue line (twins) | Best single-engine rate of climb speed, V<sub>YSE</sub> | |

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The **manoeuvring speed V<sub>A</sub>** is **not marked** on the ASI. It is placarded or in the POH, and it **reduces as weight reduces**, so a single number on the dial would be wrong most of the time. The exam offers "the top of the green arc" as a distractor: that is V<sub>NO</sub>.

## The gyroscopic instruments

### What a gyro does

A **gyroscope** is a heavy wheel (the **rotor**) spinning fast. Spinning gives it two properties. **Rigidity in space**: it resists any attempt to tilt its spin axis, the way a spinning bicycle wheel resists being turned, so it holds its orientation while the aeroplane moves around it. **Precession**: if you do push on it, it responds not in the direction you pushed but at **90 degrees** to it, in the direction of rotation. Instruments use one property or the other. The rotor is spun either by **air** (drawn through the instrument by the **vacuum system**, [BAKC 3.1](/notes/BAKC/3.1)) or by an **electric motor**.

![What are the two properties of a gyro?](diagram:gyro-rigidity-and-precession "What to notice: the spin axis holds its direction while the aeroplane moves around it; a push on the rim acts 90 degrees further round, in the direction of rotation.")

### The attitude indicator

The **attitude indicator** (artificial horizon) uses a rotor spinning about a **vertical axis**, held rigid in space by its gimbals. The horizon bar is tied to the gyro, so it stays level with the real horizon while the aeroplane, and the little aeroplane symbol fixed to the case, pitch and roll around it. In most trainers it is **vacuum driven**.

### The directional indicator

The **directional indicator** (DI, heading indicator) uses a rotor spinning about a **horizontal axis**, rigid in azimuth: it holds a fixed direction while the aeroplane turns around it, and a compass card attached to it shows the heading. Unlike the compass it does not swing or lag in turns. But it has **no magnetic sense** at all: it shows whatever heading you set on it. In most trainers it is **vacuum driven**.

### The turn coordinator

The **turn coordinator** uses **precession**. Its gyro is mounted so that when the aeroplane yaws or rolls, the gyro precesses and tilts a small aeroplane symbol, showing the **rate of turn**; aligning the wing with the marks gives a **rate one turn** (3 degrees per second, a full circle in two minutes). Below it is the **inclinometer**, a ball in a curved liquid-filled tube, which shows **balance**: in a balanced turn the ball sits in the middle. The turn coordinator is normally **electric**, so that a vacuum failure does not take all three gyros out at once.

![What does each gyro instrument show, and what drives it?](diagram:gyro-instruments-panel "The attitude and heading indicators are vacuum driven; the turn coordinator is electric, so one failure never takes out all three.")

![Slip, balanced or skid: what does the ball tell you?](diagram:slip-and-skid-ball "The ball sits to the inside of a turn when you slip and to the outside when you skid; step on the ball to centre it.")

### The magnetic compass

The **magnetic compass** is a magnet assembly with a card attached, floating in a damping liquid, that aligns itself with the Earth's magnetic field. It needs no power and is the reference everything else is checked against. It has well-known errors: **variation** (magnetic north is not true north), **deviation** (magnetic fields from the aeroplane's own metal and electrics, recorded on a **deviation card** beside it), **dip** (the field lines slope into the Earth, tilting the magnets), and from dip, **turning and acceleration errors**. The important point here is that turning and acceleration errors only disappear in **straight, level, unaccelerated flight**.

![True, magnetic and compass headings: why do they differ?](diagram:true-magnetic-compass "Variation separates true and magnetic north; deviation, from the aeroplane itself, separates magnetic and compass north.")

??? check "Check yourself: why is the turn coordinator usually electric when the attitude and directional indicators are vacuum driven?"
    So that a single failure cannot take out every gyro instrument. If the vacuum pump fails, the turn coordinator still shows rate of turn and balance; if the electrical system fails, the attitude and directional indicators keep running.

## When the instruments lie

### Pitot and static blockages: the mechanism

Every blockage effect follows from two facts: which pressure each instrument uses, and what happens to that pressure once it is **trapped**.

**Pitot tube blocked, drain hole clear.** The pitot tube has a small drain hole to let water out. If ice blocks the front of the tube but the drain is open, the trapped pitot pressure leaks away through the drain until it equals static pressure. Dynamic pressure falls to nothing, and the **ASI falls to zero**. The altimeter and VSI, which use only static, are unaffected.

**Pitot tube and drain both blocked.** Now the pitot pressure is **trapped** at whatever it was when the blockage happened. Static is still live. The ASI reads (trapped pitot) minus (live static). Climb, and static pressure falls, so the difference grows: the **ASI over-reads**, showing an increasing speed even if you are slowing. Descend, and static rises: the **ASI under-reads**. In level flight it stays stuck at the same reading whatever your real speed. The ASI has become an altimeter. That is the dangerous case: a pilot who climbs to "fix" an apparently rising airspeed is slowing towards the stall.

**Static vent blocked.** Now static pressure is **trapped**, and it feeds all three. The **altimeter freezes** at the height where the blockage occurred, because its case pressure no longer changes. The **VSI reads zero**, because the capsule and case pressures are both trapped and equal. The **ASI** reads (live pitot) minus (trapped static): correct only at the height where the vent blocked. Climb, and the real static pressure falls while the trapped one does not, so the ASI subtracts too much: it **under-reads**. Descend and it **over-reads**, which can take you towards V<sub>NE</sub> while the needle looks safe.

**Alternate static source inside the cockpit.** Many aeroplanes have a valve that lets you take static pressure from inside the cabin instead. In an unpressurised aeroplane the air flowing over the cabin makes the cabin pressure slightly **lower** than the outside static pressure. Lower static pressure looks like more height and more speed: the **altimeter reads slightly high**, the **ASI reads slightly high**, and the **VSI shows a brief climb** at the moment you select it, then settles. The instruments are usable but less accurate; the POH gives the corrections.

![What does each blockage do to the three pressure instruments?](diagram:pitot-static-blockages "A static blockage hits all three instruments; a pitot blockage hits only the ASI; the alternate static makes them read slightly high.")

### The blockage table

Now that each row has a reason, here is the recap the exam draws on:

| Fault | Instrument | Climbing | Descending | Level flight |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pitot and drain blocked | ASI | Over-reads | Under-reads | Stuck at the reading when blocked |
| | Altimeter, VSI | Normal | Normal | Normal |
| Pitot blocked, drain clear | ASI | Falls to zero | Falls to zero | Falls to zero |
| | Altimeter, VSI | Normal | Normal | Normal |
| Static vent blocked | ASI | Under-reads | Over-reads | Correct at the blockage height |
| | Altimeter | Frozen | Frozen | Frozen |
| | VSI | Zero | Zero | Zero |
| Alternate static (cockpit) | ASI, altimeter | Slightly high | Slightly high | Slightly high |
| | VSI | Brief climb when selected, then normal | | |

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Mixing up which fault hits which instruments. A **pitot** fault affects **only the ASI**, because only the ASI uses pitot pressure. A **static** fault affects **all three**. If the altimeter has frozen and the VSI reads zero, it is the static vent; selecting the alternate static source is the cure.

??? check "Check yourself: with the static vent blocked, you descend from 4,500 ft. What do the altimeter, VSI and ASI show?"
    The altimeter stays at 4,500 ft and the VSI at zero, because both are fed with the trapped static pressure. The ASI over-reads, because the real (pitot) pressure rises as you descend while the trapped static does not, so the difference it measures is too large.

### Low suction and loss of electrical power

When the **vacuum pump** weakens, less air spins the rotors of the attitude and directional indicators. They **slow down**, lose rigidity, and begin to **drift and eventually topple**, often slowly and without a flag. The instruments do not tell you they are failing; the **suction gauge** does, which is why it is part of your scan.

**Electrical failure** takes out the **turn coordinator** (and an electric attitude indicator, on aeroplanes so equipped) but leaves the vacuum instruments running. Knowing which instrument runs on which power source is what tells you which ones to trust.

### Toppling and re-erection

A gyro's rotor is held in **gimbals**, rings that let the case move around it, and each gimbal has a mechanical stop. Push the aeroplane far enough in pitch or roll and a gimbal hits its stop; the force then acts on the rotor itself, it precesses violently and the display tumbles: the gyro has **toppled**. Older attitude indicators topple beyond about **100 to 110 degrees of bank or 60 to 70 degrees of pitch**, depending on the make, and aerobatics will topple most of them. A rotor that has **slowed** (low suction, or just after start) loses rigidity and can topple at much smaller angles.

A toppled gyro is useless until it **re-erects**. Its **self-erecting mechanism** gradually nudges the rotor back to its proper axis once the aeroplane is in **straight and level flight** and the rotor is **up to speed**, usually within a few minutes. Some instruments have a **caging knob** that locks the gyro upright to reset it quickly.

### Synchronising the directional indicator

The DI has no idea where north is, so it must be **set to the magnetic compass**, and because it **drifts** it must be **reset** regularly. It drifts for two reasons: **friction** in its bearings and gimbals slowly precesses the rotor, and the **Earth rotates** beneath a gyro that is holding a fixed direction in space (**apparent drift**).

So the rule has three parts:

- **How**: read the compass and set the DI to agree, with the setting knob.
- **When**: about **every 10 to 15 minutes**, and before any turn onto a new heading.
- **Why straight and level**: only in **straight, level, unaccelerated flight** is the compass free of its turning and acceleration errors. Set the DI from a compass that is swinging in a turn and you copy the compass's error into an instrument that would otherwise have been more accurate.

![Why must the DI be reset?](diagram:di-drift-and-reset "What to notice: the heading error grows steadily until you reset to the compass; reset in a turn and you copy the compass's turning error across.")

??? check "Check yourself: why must you not reset the DI during a turn?"
    In a turn the magnetic compass suffers turning errors and does not show the correct heading. Setting the DI then copies that error across. Reset it only in straight, level, unaccelerated flight, when the compass is reading correctly.

## Checking the instruments before flight

Each check tests the mechanism you have just learnt, so you can work them out rather than memorise them.

**Before start**:

- **ASI** reads zero (or roughly the wind speed if you are parked facing a strong wind). The **pitot cover is off** and the **static vents are clear**: a blocked vent will not show on the ground.
- **Altimeter**: set the aerodrome **QNH**; it should read the **aerodrome elevation** within the permitted tolerance (within 100 ft for a VFR flight, 60 ft for IFR; check your operator's requirement and the current AIP ENR 1.7, and see [BAKC 6.2](/notes/BAKC/6.2)). The **subscale** should turn smoothly.
- **VSI** reads **zero** on the ground.
- **Magnetic compass**: full of fluid, **no bubbles**, the **deviation card** present, and a sensible heading for the direction you are parked.

**After start and during the taxi**:

- **Attitude indicator** erects within a few minutes and shows level with the aeroplane level; **suction gauge** in the green.
- **Turn coordinator**: in a taxi turn the aeroplane symbol shows a turn in the **direction of turn** and the **ball moves to the outside** (the aeroplane is skidding on the ground).
- **Directional indicator**: set to the compass, and it should **turn the correct way** in taxi turns.
- **Compass**: **swings freely** and turns the correct way, and reads the runway direction when you line up.

**After take-off**: confirm the **ASI rises** on the take-off roll (the "airspeed alive" call), the **altimeter and VSI show the climb**, and the **DI agrees with the compass** once you are established in straight and level flight.

## Bringing it together

You depart Jandakot on a cool morning with QNH 1021 set, heading for Narrogin, where the forecast QNH is lower ahead of a trough. In cloud-free air you notice the ASI creeping up during a cruise climb even though the attitude has not changed, while the altimeter and VSI behave normally. Only the ASI is wrong, so it is a pitot fault, and an ASI that rises in the climb means the pitot and its drain are blocked: you fly attitude and power, switch on pitot heat, and ignore the needle. Later, 15 minutes after you last set the DI, you check it against the compass in level flight and correct a few degrees of drift. Approaching Narrogin you get the area QNH: 1009. Your altimeter has been set 12 hPa too high, so it has been over-reading by about 360 ft over the rising wheatbelt: high to low, look out below. You reset it before descending.

## Key points

- **3.5.1** White arc: V<sub>SO</sub> to V<sub>FE</sub>, flap range. Green: V<sub>S1</sub> to V<sub>NO</sub>, normal. Yellow: V<sub>NO</sub> to V<sub>NE</sub>, smooth air only. Red line: V<sub>NE</sub>. V<sub>A</sub> is not marked.
- **3.5.2** The ASI measures pitot minus static (dynamic pressure, so IAS); the altimeter is an aneroid barometer whose subscale sets the datum (about 30 ft per hPa); the VSI measures the rate of static change through a calibrated leak and lags. The attitude indicator and DI use rigidity (usually vacuum), the turn coordinator uses precession (usually electric); the compass is self-contained.
- **3.5.3** Pitot blocked: ASI only (drain clear, falls to zero; drain blocked, acts as an altimeter). Static blocked: altimeter frozen, VSI zero, ASI under-reads climbing and over-reads descending. Alternate static: all read slightly high, brief climb on the VSI. Low suction topples the vacuum gyros; electrical failure loses the turn coordinator. Gyros topple at gimbal limits or low rotor speed and re-erect in straight and level flight. Reset the DI to the compass every 10 to 15 minutes, in straight, level, unaccelerated flight.
- **3.5.4** On the ground: ASI zero, covers off, altimeter reads elevation with QNH set, VSI zero, compass full with no bubbles; in the taxi, gyros erect and turn the right way, ball to the outside of turns; on take-off, airspeed alive.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    The blockage questions are the most common: a blocked static vent freezes the altimeter and zeroes the VSI; a pitot blocked with its drain acts as an altimeter. Know which gyro is driven by which system (attitude and DI vacuum, turn coordinator electric), why the DI is set only in straight and level flight, and the subscale arithmetic: 30 ft per hectopascal, and "from high to low, look out below".
