## What the exam expects (BAKC 6.2)

- **6.2.1** Differentiate between pressure height and density height.
- **6.2.2** Describe how to use the altimeter on the ground to obtain the local QNH, the pressure height of the aerodrome and its elevation.
- **6.2.3** Calculate density height from pressure height (or elevation and QNH) and temperature, and pressure height from elevation and QNH.
- **6.2.4** State the effect on take-off, landing and take-off climb performance of headwind and tailwind, temperature, QNH, elevation, ground effect and wind shear, and frost.
- **6.2.5** Explain maximum structural take-off and landing weight, and the climb weight limit.

## Why this matters

A training aeroplane that lifts off comfortably at Jandakot on a cool June morning can need half as much runway again at Northam on a 40 degree February afternoon, and climb at a fraction of the rate once it is airborne. Nothing about the aeroplane has changed; the air has. Pilots who do not understand that run off the end of strips every summer, or clear the fence and then cannot out-climb the rising ground. The exam cares a great deal: CASA lists "working out density height and pressure height" among the most common errors in the RPLA exam, and every performance chart in the workbook starts with those two numbers. This lesson gives you the physical picture first, then the arithmetic, then what each factor does to the distances.

## Three heights for one aerodrome

Stand on the apron at an inland aerodrome. There are three different "heights" you could attach to the spot you are standing on, and performance depends on the third one.

**Elevation** is the surveyed height of the aerodrome above mean sea level. It is printed in ERSA and never changes.

**Pressure height** (pressure altitude) answers a different question: in the **International Standard Atmosphere**, the model atmosphere that performance charts are drawn for, at what height would the pressure be the same as the pressure here today? If today's sea-level pressure is standard, 1013.25 hPa, the pressure height equals the elevation. If a trough has pulled the QNH down to 1000 hPa, the air here is thinner than standard and the aerodrome is behaving as if it were higher than its elevation. In the lower atmosphere pressure falls about **1 hPa for every 30 ft** of height, so each hectopascal below 1013 adds about 30 ft to the pressure height.

**Density height** (density altitude) asks the question that the wing, the propeller and the engine actually care about: at what height in the standard atmosphere would the air be as *thin* as it is here today? Density depends on pressure and on temperature. The standard atmosphere has a temperature of **15 degrees at sea level, falling 2 degrees per 1,000 ft**. Warm air is less dense than standard air at the same pressure, so a hot day pushes the density height above the pressure height by about **120 ft for every degree** above standard.

![Which height is which?](diagram:height-altitude-elevation "Elevation is the ground's height above mean sea level and never changes; what the altimeter reads depends on the subscale setting.")

So a 1,300 ft aerodrome on a 35 degree day with a low QNH might have a density height of 4,000 ft. The aeroplane, which has no idea what the survey says, performs exactly as it would at 4,000 ft on a standard day. That single idea is the whole of this subtopic. Pressure height is the stepping stone; density height is the answer.

??? check "Check yourself: an aerodrome's elevation is 1,000 ft and today its pressure height is also 1,000 ft. What does that tell you about the QNH?"
    The QNH is 1013 hPa, standard. Pressure height equals elevation only when the sea-level pressure is standard. A lower QNH would put the pressure height above 1,000 ft, a higher one below.

## Reading the heights off the altimeter

The altimeter is a barometer with a scale in feet and an adjustable datum, the **subscale**. Whatever pressure you set on the subscale, the altimeter shows the height above the level where that pressure exists. On the ground that gives you three readings for the price of one instrument:

- **Local QNH.** Turn the subscale until the altimeter reads the known aerodrome elevation. The number now in the window is the local QNH, the sea-level pressure that makes the altimeter read true height above mean sea level. This is how you get a QNH at a strip with no ATIS or AWIS.
- **Pressure height.** Set **1013 hPa** on the subscale. The altimeter now reads the aerodrome's pressure height directly, because that is what pressure height means.
- **Elevation.** Set the **aerodrome QNH** given by the tower, ATIS or AWIS. The altimeter should read the aerodrome elevation. This doubles as the **pre-flight altimeter check**: with the correct QNH set, a reading within **100 ft** of the elevation is acceptable for VFR (AIP ENR 1.7; verify the current figure). A larger error makes the altimeter unserviceable.

![Explore what the subscale setting does to the reading](widget:altimeter-subscale "Each hectopascal of difference between the subscale and the real QNH moves the reading about 30 ft.")

Each hectopascal on the subscale moves the needle about 30 ft. That is the same 30 ft per hectopascal as in the pressure height formula, and it is why a 10 hPa error in the subscale puts you 300 ft from where you think you are.

??? check "Check yourself: you are parked at a strip with no weather service and you want the local QNH. What do you do with the altimeter?"
    Set the subscale until the altimeter reads the strip's elevation from ERSA. The subscale then shows the local QNH. (Set 1013 instead and the altimeter reads the pressure height.)

## The calculations

Two formulas, used in sequence. First get the pressure height; then correct it for temperature to get the density height.

### Pressure height from elevation and QNH

$$\text{pressure height} = \text{elevation} + (1013 - \text{QNH}) \times 30$$

If the QNH is **below** 1013 the bracket is positive and the pressure height is **above** the elevation (thin air, worse performance). If the QNH is **above** 1013 the bracket is negative and the pressure height is **below** the elevation. Keep the sign.

### Density height from pressure height and temperature

First find what the temperature *should* be at the pressure height on a standard day:

$$T_\text{ISA} = 15 - \frac{2 \times \text{pressure height}}{1000}$$

Then add 120 ft for every degree the actual temperature is above that (or subtract if it is below):

$$\text{density height} = \text{pressure height} + 120 \times (T_\text{actual} - T_\text{ISA})$$

!!! example "Worked example: a hot afternoon inland"
    Given: elevation 1,700 ft, QNH 1009 hPa, temperature 38 degrees.

    1. Pressure height: $1700 + (1013 - 1009) \times 30 = 1700 + 120 = \mathbf{1{,}820\ ft}$.
    2. ISA temperature at 1,820 ft: $15 - 2 \times 1.82 = 15 - 3.6 = \mathbf{11.4}$ degrees.
    3. Deviation from ISA: $38 - 11.4 = \mathbf{26.6}$ degrees above.
    4. Density height: $1820 + 120 \times 26.6 = 1820 + 3192 = \mathbf{5{,}010\ ft}$ (5,012).

    The strip is at 1,700 ft, but the aeroplane will take off and climb as though it were at 5,000 ft on a standard day: roughly a 15 percent loss of engine power and a much longer run.

!!! example "Worked example: a mild morning at Jandakot"
    Given: elevation 99 ft, QNH 1018 hPa, temperature 24 degrees.

    1. Pressure height: $99 + (1013 - 1018) \times 30 = 99 - 150 = \mathbf{-51\ ft}$, call it −50 ft. High pressure has put the aerodrome "below sea level" in standard terms.
    2. ISA temperature at −50 ft: $15 + 0.1 = \mathbf{15.1}$ degrees.
    3. Deviation: $24 - 15.1 = \mathbf{8.9}$ degrees above.
    4. Density height: $-50 + 120 \times 8.9 = -50 + 1068 = \mathbf{1{,}020\ ft}$ (1,018).

    A sea-level aerodrome on a 24 degree day is already performing like a 1,000 ft one. Warmth alone does that.

Now try one before opening the solution: elevation 1,300 ft, QNH 1004 hPa, temperature 33 degrees.

??? solution "Show the solution"
    Pressure height $= 1300 + (1013 - 1004) \times 30 = 1300 + 270 = 1{,}570$ ft. ISA temperature at 1,570 ft $= 15 - 3.1 = 11.9$ degrees. Deviation $= 33 - 11.9 = 21.1$ degrees. Density height $= 1570 + 120 \times 21.1 = 1570 + 2532 =$ **about 4,100 ft** (4,102; rounding the ISA temperature to 12 degrees gives 4,090, which is also acceptable).

![Work out pressure height and density height for any aerodrome and day](widget:density-height "Heat is the big one: every degree above ISA adds about 120 ft of density height.")

Two practical notes. The CASA take-off and landing charts in the workbook are entered with **pressure height and temperature**, and the chart does the density correction internally, so for a chart question you stop after the pressure height and read the temperature on the chart's own scale. And when a question gives you the pressure height directly, skip the first step: the exam often does this.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Three sign errors account for most wrong answers. Low QNH *raises* the pressure height (subtract the QNH from 1013, not the other way round). The ISA temperature is worked out at the *pressure height*, not at the elevation. And a temperature *below* ISA gives a density height *below* the pressure height: the correction can be negative. Keep the signs, work to the nearest 10 ft and round only at the end.

??? check "Check yourself: the QNH is 1025 hPa. Is the pressure height above or below the elevation, and by how much?"
    Below, by $(1013 - 1025) \times 30 = -360$ ft. High pressure means denser air than standard, so the aerodrome behaves as if it were 360 ft lower than it is.

## What the day does to the aeroplane

Now the second half of the subtopic: for each factor the syllabus names, does it make the take-off and landing distances longer or shorter, and the climb better or worse, and why. Almost everything here comes back to **air density** or to **groundspeed**.

### Density: temperature, QNH and elevation

Thin air hurts three times over. The **engine** breathes less oxygen per stroke, so a normally aspirated engine loses roughly 3 percent of its power per 1,000 ft of density height. The **propeller** bites less air, so thrust falls further. And the **wing** needs a higher true airspeed to make the same lift at the same indicated airspeed, so the aeroplane must be accelerated to a higher groundspeed before it can lift off, with less thrust to do it. Take-off distance grows, landing distance grows (the touchdown true airspeed is higher, so there is more energy to stop), and the climb, which depends on the surplus of power over what is needed to fly level, shrinks fastest of all.

![How much power does a normally aspirated engine lose with height?](diagram:power-loss-with-altitude "Roughly 3 percent per 1,000 ft of density height; at 5,000 ft the engine has about 85 percent of its sea-level power.")

Every factor that raises density height therefore lengthens both distances and flattens the climb: **higher temperature**, **lower QNH** and **higher elevation**. The three are one factor wearing three hats.

### Wind: headwind and tailwind

Wind does not change the air the wing flies through; it changes how fast the aeroplane moves over the ground while it reaches flying speed. The aeroplane lifts off at a fixed *airspeed*. Into a headwind, part of that airspeed is supplied by the wind before the aeroplane has moved, so lift-off happens at a lower **groundspeed**, and since the energy that has to be built up (or, on landing, dissipated by the brakes) goes with the *square* of groundspeed, the saving is bigger than the wind looks.

!!! example "Worked example: what 10 kt of wind does to the ground roll"
    Given: lift-off airspeed 60 kt. Compare a 10 kt headwind and a 10 kt tailwind, ground roll only, other effects ignored.

    1. Headwind: groundspeed at lift-off $= 60 - 10 = 50$ kt. Energy ratio $(50/60)^2 = \mathbf{0.69}$: the roll is about **30 percent shorter**.
    2. Tailwind: groundspeed at lift-off $= 60 + 10 = 70$ kt. Energy ratio $(70/60)^2 = \mathbf{1.36}$: the roll is about **36 percent longer**.

    The tailwind costs more than the headwind saves, which is why charts punish tailwinds steeply and why the usual rule of thumb says a tailwind of 10 percent of the lift-off speed adds roughly 20 percent to the whole take-off distance. The same arithmetic applies to the landing roll.

For the climb, wind changes the **angle** over the ground but not the **rate**. The aeroplane climbs at the same feet per minute whatever the wind; into a headwind it covers less ground in each minute, so it is higher when it reaches the fence or the trees.

![Does wind change how fast or how steeply you climb?](diagram:climb-gradient-wind "Same 600 ft per minute in every case; the headwind steepens the path over the ground, the tailwind flattens it.")

A crosswind contributes only its headwind component to performance, and its crosswind component to your control problem.

![Split any wind into headwind and crosswind components for a runway](widget:crosswind-component "Thirty degrees off the runway already puts half the wind across it; compare the crosswind with the POH's demonstrated figure.")

### Ground effect and wind shear

Within about a wingspan of the ground the wingtip vortices are squeezed and **induced drag** falls. The aeroplane will fly in **ground effect** at a speed and angle of attack at which it could not fly clear of it. On take-off that means an aeroplane dragged off early will lift off, climb to 10 or 15 ft, run out of ground effect, and either settle back or hang there unable to climb while the trees get closer. On landing it means an aeroplane that is a little fast will **float**, using up runway. Ground effect shortens the ground run but can wreck the climb-out, which is why you accelerate to the POH speed before rotating.

**Wind shear** is a change in wind speed or direction over a short distance. On climb-out, a sudden loss of headwind, for example climbing through a wind gradient or into a sea-breeze front at Jandakot, drops the indicated airspeed and the lift at once. The aeroplane sinks or stalls with the attitude unchanged. CASA names wind shear as a weak area in the RPLA exam. The protection is speed in hand and an attitude you can lower.

### Frost

Even a thin, barely visible frost on the wing on a winter morning at Northam or Narrogin roughens the surface and distorts the leading edge. The airflow separates earlier, the maximum lift coefficient falls and the stall speed rises (see [RBKA 3.6](/notes/RBKA/3.6)). The aeroplane needs a longer run to reach a speed that will lift it, and may not fly at all at the normal rotation speed. The climb suffers because the wing is making less lift for more drag. All frost comes off before flight, with no exceptions for "a thin layer".

![What lengthens the take-off and landing distances?](diagram:takeoff-landing-factors "Everything that lowers density, adds weight, adds a tailwind or slows the roll lengthens the distances; only a headwind shortens both.")

### The recap table

| Factor | Take-off distance | Landing distance | Take-off climb | Why |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Headwind | Shorter | Shorter | Steeper over the ground | Lift-off and touchdown at a lower groundspeed; more height per unit of ground |
| Tailwind | Longer, markedly | Longer, markedly | Flatter | Higher groundspeed; the energy goes with the square |
| Higher temperature | Longer | Longer | Worse | Lower density: less power, less thrust, higher TAS for the same IAS |
| Lower QNH | Longer | Longer | Worse | Higher pressure height, so lower density |
| Higher elevation | Longer | Longer | Worse | Lower density |
| Ground effect | Can shorten the ground run, but the aeroplane may not climb out | Float | Sags on leaving it | Reduced induced drag within a wingspan of the ground |
| Wind shear | Variable | Variable | Dangerous | A sudden loss of headwind drops the IAS and lift |
| Frost | Longer, may prevent take-off | Longer | Worse | Lower maximum lift, higher stall speed |

The charts also handle factors the syllabus does not list here: runway **slope** (uphill lengthens the take-off, downhill lengthens the landing), **surface** (grass, gravel and wet surfaces lengthen both) and **weight** (heavier lengthens both and reduces the climb).

![Uphill or downhill?](diagram:runway-slope-take-off-and-landing "Taking off uphill, part of the thrust climbs the slope; landing downhill, gravity fights the brakes.")

??? check "Check yourself: a 10 kt tailwind and a 10 kt headwind are not equal and opposite in their effect on the take-off roll. Why?"
    Because the energy the aeroplane must build up goes with the square of its groundspeed at lift-off. The tailwind raises that groundspeed and the square makes the increase larger than the decrease a headwind gives. Charts and the 10 percent rule of thumb reflect this: tailwinds cost more than headwinds save.

??? check "Check yourself: why is ground effect a hazard on take-off if it shortens the ground run?"
    Because the aeroplane can fly in ground effect at a speed at which it cannot climb out of it. Dragged off early, it lifts to a wingspan or so, loses the reduced induced drag, and either settles back or hangs there unable to climb while obstacles approach.

## Weight limits

The syllabus ends with two kinds of weight limit, and the distinction matters because on a hot day the lower one wins.

**Maximum structural take-off weight** (MTOW) is the heaviest weight at which the airframe is certified to begin a take-off; **maximum structural landing weight** (MLW) is the heaviest at which it is certified to land, where the undercarriage and its attachments take the loads. Both are set by the strength of the structure, are printed in the POH limitations section, and do not change with the weather.

The **climb weight limit** is different. It is the heaviest weight at which the aeroplane can still achieve the **required climb gradient after take-off** at the pressure height and temperature of the day; it is sometimes called the performance-limited or **WAT limit**, for weight, altitude and temperature. On a cool day at sea level it is usually above the structural MTOW and never bites. At Northam at 38 degrees it can be well below it, and then the climb weight limit is the limit. The take-off weight chart in the workbook gives this figure alongside the runway-length-limited weight.

The take-off weight you may actually use is the **lowest** of four numbers: the structural MTOW, the climb weight limit for the day, the weight the runway length allows from the take-off chart, and the weight that will have burned down to MLW by the time you land.

??? check "Check yourself: what is the difference between the maximum structural take-off weight and the climb weight limit?"
    The structural limit is fixed by the airframe's strength and never changes. The climb weight limit is the heaviest weight at which the aeroplane can still make the required climb gradient at today's density height, so it falls as the day gets hotter or the aerodrome higher, and when it is lower than the structural limit it is the one that applies.

## Bringing it together

You plan to leave Northam at two in the afternoon in late January with three friends and full fuel. ERSA gives the elevation; the AWIS gives QNH 1006 and 39 degrees. Working as above, the pressure height is a couple of hundred feet above the elevation and the density height comes out more than 3,000 ft above the strip: the engine is down to roughly 87 percent power, the propeller is pulling thin air, and the wing needs a higher true airspeed to lift off. The sea breeze has not arrived, so the wind is a light northerly, a slight tailwind for the runway you would otherwise choose. The take-off chart says the runway is long enough, barely, but the take-off weight chart says the climb weight limit is 60 kg below your planned weight. Nothing about the structure stops you; the climb gradient over the rising ground does. The answers are the ones this lesson leads to: leave fuel behind, leave a passenger behind, wait for the sea breeze and the cooler air, or depart early tomorrow morning, when at 20 degrees the density height will be more than 2,000 ft lower.

## Key points

- **6.2.1** Elevation is surveyed and fixed. Pressure height is the elevation corrected for QNH (30 ft per hectopascal). Density height is pressure height corrected for temperature (120 ft per degree from ISA), and it is what the aeroplane feels.
- **6.2.2** On the ground: subscale set so the altimeter reads the elevation gives the local QNH; 1013 set gives the pressure height; aerodrome QNH set should give the elevation, within 100 ft for VFR (AIP ENR 1.7, verify).
- **6.2.3** Pressure height = elevation + (1013 − QNH) × 30. ISA temperature = 15 − 2 × (pressure height in thousands of feet). Density height = pressure height + 120 × (actual − ISA). Keep the signs.
- **6.2.4** Headwind shortens both distances and steepens the climb; tailwind lengthens both markedly. Higher temperature, lower QNH and higher elevation all mean lower density: longer distances, poorer climb. Ground effect lets you lift off before you can climb. Wind shear drops the IAS. Frost raises the stall speed and lengthens the run.
- **6.2.5** Structural MTOW and MLW are fixed by the airframe. The climb weight limit is the heaviest weight that still gives the required climb gradient today, and the lowest applicable limit governs.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    There is always a pressure height or density height calculation, often with a trap in the sign of the QNH correction or the ISA temperature taken at the elevation rather than the pressure height. Work to the nearest 10 ft and round at the end; answers of 4,090 and 4,100 ft for the same problem are both within the tolerance of a numeric question. For the factors, remember that only a headwind shortens both distances; everything else the syllabus lists lengthens them or leaves the aeroplane unable to climb.
