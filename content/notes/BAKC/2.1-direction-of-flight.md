## What the exam expects (BAKC 2.1)

- **2.1.1** Express a direction three ways: as a 3-figure group, as a 2-figure group (a runway number) and in the clock code.
- **2.1.2** Say what aircraft heading (HDG) means, and how it differs from the direction you actually move.
- **2.1.3** Explain the difference between a direction quoted as true (T), as magnetic (M) and as compass (C), and convert between them.

## Why this matters

Every instruction you will ever get about direction uses one of these forms. Perth Tower clears you to "runway 24", Jandakot Approach calls "traffic two o'clock, two miles, a Cessna at 1,500 ft", and the nav log for your first cross-country to York lists a track you measured off the chart in degrees true and a heading you will fly by the compass. Each of those numbers is measured from a different reference, and a pilot who mixes them up ends up a few degrees off track, looking at the wrong patch of sky for traffic, or lining up on the wrong runway. The exam tests this subtopic with short recall questions and one-step conversions: traffic positions by the clock, runway designators, and the true to magnetic to compass sequence with its memory aid. It all rests on one simple idea, an angle measured clockwise from north, so the lesson starts there.

## Direction is an angle from north

Stand in the middle of a big field facing north and turn slowly to your right. As you turn, the angle between north and the way you are facing grows from zero, through 90 degrees when you face east, 180 degrees when you face south and 270 degrees when you face west, until you are facing north again after a full turn of 360 degrees. That is all an aviation direction is: **the angle, measured clockwise from north, between north and the direction you are describing**. There are no "north-north-east" or "south-west by west" words in the cockpit; there is a number from 001 to 360.

Clockwise matters. A direction of 045 is half-way between north and east, not half-way between north and west. If you ever find yourself unsure, picture the face of a clock laid on a map with 12 pointing north: the hands move the same way the numbers grow.

![How do 3-figure directions and runway numbers work?](diagram:compass-rose-headings "Clockwise from north, always three figures; a runway is its magnetic direction to the nearest 10 degrees with the last zero dropped, and its two ends differ by 18.")

The **reciprocal** of a direction is the direction exactly opposite, 180 degrees away. You need it constantly: the other end of a runway, the way home along the same track, the bearing of you from another aircraft. If the direction is less than 180, add 180; if it is 180 or more, subtract 180. The reciprocal of 060 is 240; the reciprocal of 300 is 120.

## The 3-figure group

Headings, tracks, bearings and wind directions are always written and spoken as **three figures**, with leading zeros where needed. The reason is that a cockpit is full of numbers: speeds, altitudes, frequencies, times. A direction of "five" could be mistaken for anything; "zero zero five" can only be a direction. Three figures also make every direction the same length, so a misheard digit is more likely to be noticed.

| Direction | Written | Spoken |
|---|---|---|
| North | 360 | "three six zero" |
| East | 090 | "zero nine zero" |
| 5 degrees | 005 | "zero zero five" |
| South-west | 225 | "two two five" |

North as a heading is written **360**, not 000. The scale runs from 001 to 360, so a direction of exactly north sits at the end of the scale, not at the start.

??? check "Check yourself: why is a heading of east written 090 and not 90?"
    Because directions are always given as three figures, so that they cannot be confused with a speed, altitude or other number, and so that a missing digit is obvious. Ninety degrees becomes 090, spoken "zero nine zero".

## The 2-figure group: runway numbers

A runway is a long strip pointing in one direction, and pilots need to know that direction at a glance from the air and from the chart. The **runway designator** gives it as a **2-figure group**: take the runway's **magnetic** direction, round it to the nearest 10 degrees, and drop the last zero. A runway aligned 214 degrees magnetic rounds to 210, so it is "runway 21". Its other end points the opposite way, 034 degrees magnetic, which rounds to 030 and becomes "runway 03". Because the two ends are reciprocals, 180 degrees apart, their numbers always differ by **18**.

Perth (YPPH) has runways 03/21 and 06/24. Without looking at a chart you already know that runway 24 points roughly south-west, 240 degrees magnetic, and that a westerly sea breeze will be close to straight down it. The number is painted in large figures at the threshold, so it also tells you which end you are looking at.

The direction behind the designator is magnetic, not true. That is so the number on the runway can be compared directly with the direction indicator and with the magnetic surface wind ATC gives you. Because magnetic north wanders slowly over the years, some runways are renumbered when their magnetic direction drifts across a rounding boundary.

!!! example "Worked example: designating both ends of a runway"
    Given: a country strip aligned 166 degrees magnetic.

    1. Round to the nearest 10 degrees: $166 \to \mathbf{170}$.
    2. Drop the last zero: **runway 17**.
    3. Reciprocal direction: $166 + 180 = \mathbf{346}$ degrees magnetic, which rounds to 350: **runway 35**.
    4. Check: $35 - 17 = \mathbf{18}$.

    The strip is runway 17/35. Landing on 17 you are pointing roughly south; on 35, roughly north.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The runway number is a **magnetic** direction. A question that gives you a runway aligned so many degrees true is asking you to apply variation first. The other trap is the reciprocal: the two ends of a runway always differ by 18, so runway 06 pairs with 24, never with 26.

## The clock code

The 3-figure group is perfect for navigation, but useless for telling someone where to look out of the window right now. For that, aviation uses the **clock code**. Picture a clock face lying flat around your aeroplane with **12 o'clock on the nose**. Three o'clock is off the right wingtip, six o'clock is straight behind and nine o'clock is off the left wingtip. Each "hour" is 360 ÷ 12 = **30 degrees**. The words "high" and "low" are added for traffic above or below your level, and "same level" if it is at your height.

The clock is fixed to the **nose**, not to north and not to your path over the ground. When ATC says "traffic two o'clock", they mean "turn your head 60 degrees right of where your nose points". In a crosswind, where the nose is angled into wind, that is still where you look.

![Where is traffic reported at two o'clock?](diagram:clock-code-traffic "12 o'clock is the nose, not the track: each hour is 30 degrees, so two o'clock on a heading of 090 bears 150.")

Turning a clock position into a direction from north is one sum. Hours to the right of 12 add to your heading; hours to the left subtract. If the answer goes past 360, subtract 360; if it goes below zero (or to zero), add 360.

!!! example "Worked example: traffic by the clock"
    Given: you are heading 090. Traffic is reported at two o'clock low, and later a second aircraft at ten o'clock.

    1. Two o'clock is 2 hours right of the nose: $2 \times 30 = \mathbf{60}$ degrees right.
    2. Bearing of the first aircraft: $090 + 60 = \mathbf{150}$, below your level.
    3. Ten o'clock is 2 hours left of the nose: **60** degrees left.
    4. Bearing of the second aircraft: $090 - 60 = \mathbf{030}$.

    You look 60 degrees right and down for the first, 60 degrees left for the second.

!!! example "Worked example: when the sum wraps past north"
    Given: on heading 240, traffic at ten o'clock. Later, on heading 350, traffic at two o'clock.

    1. Ten o'clock is 60 degrees left: $240 - 60 = \mathbf{180}$. The traffic bears 180, due south of you.
    2. Two o'clock is 60 degrees right: $350 + 60 = 410$.
    3. 410 is past 360, so subtract a full circle: $410 - 360 = \mathbf{050}$.

    The second traffic is north-east of you. Any time the sum passes 360 or drops below zero, add or remove a full circle.

![Where is traffic at two o'clock when you are heading 350?](diagram:relative-bearing-wrap "Ten of the 60 degrees take you to north and the other 50 carry on past it: 350 + 60 = 410, minus a full circle is 050.")

The same arithmetic works for any angle measured from the nose, which is called a **relative bearing**. An object 100 degrees right of the nose is a little behind the right wingtip, between three and four o'clock.

!!! example "Worked example: a relative bearing to a magnetic bearing"
    Given: heading 300°M. A dam is 100 degrees right of the nose.

    1. Add the relative bearing to the heading: $300 + 100 = 400$.
    2. Remove a full circle: $400 - 360 = \mathbf{040}$°M.

    From where you are, the dam bears 040 degrees magnetic.

??? check "Check yourself: in a strong crosswind your nose points 10 degrees left of your track. ATC calls traffic at twelve o'clock. Where do you look?"
    Straight over the nose, not along your track. The clock code is fixed to the aeroplane's longitudinal axis, so twelve o'clock is wherever the nose points, whatever your path over the ground.

## Heading

Sit in the cockpit and look along the top of the engine cowling. The direction that line points, the aeroplane's **longitudinal axis**, measured clockwise from north, is the aeroplane's **heading (HDG)**. Heading is where the aeroplane is *pointing*. It can be quoted against true, magnetic or compass north, so a full heading always carries its letter: 095°M, 093°T.

Pointing is not the same as going. An aeroplane flies through a mass of air, and the air itself is moving over the ground: that is the wind. Picture a ferry crossing a river. It points upstream of the jetty on the far side, because the current carries it sideways while it crosses. An aeroplane is the same. The direction it actually moves over the ground is its **track**, and the angle between the heading and the track is the **drift**. Drift is caused entirely by the wind. With no wind, or with the wind straight on the nose or straight on the tail, heading and track are the same.

![What is the difference between heading and track?](diagram:heading-track-drift "The nose keeps pointing along the heading while the wind carries the aeroplane sideways along the track; the angle between them is drift.")

This is why your nav log has two columns. You draw the **track** on the chart, because that is where you want to go over the ground, and you work out the **heading** you must fly to make good that track in today's wind. A wind from the left drifts you right, so you point the nose a little left of the track to compensate; you will do the arithmetic for that with the flight computer in the navigation unit. For this subtopic, hold on to the two definitions and the fact that only the heading is shown directly by the instruments.

The clock code connects back here: because 12 o'clock is the nose, it is fixed to your heading, not your track.

??? check "Check yourself: in still air, what is the drift, and how do heading and track compare?"
    The drift is zero and heading and track are the same. Drift is caused by the wind carrying the aeroplane sideways, so with no wind there is nothing to separate where the nose points from where the aeroplane goes.

## Three norths: true, magnetic and compass

So far "north" has been a single direction. In fact there are three, and the same physical direction gets a different number against each.

### True north

**True north** is the direction of the geographic North Pole, the point the Earth spins about. The meridians of longitude on every aeronautical chart run true north and south, so when you lay a protractor on the chart and measure a track against a meridian, you get a **true** direction, written °T. True north is the only north that never moves, and it is the one maps are built on.

### Magnetic north and variation

A compass needle does not point at the geographic pole. It lines up with the Earth's magnetic field, which points towards **magnetic north**, a region in the Canadian Arctic a long way from the geographic pole, and slowly wandering. A direction measured against the magnetic field is a **magnetic** direction, written °M.

The angle between true north and magnetic north, at the place you are standing, is the **variation**. It is called **east** when magnetic north lies east of true north, and **west** when it lies west. Variation depends on **where you are**, because your angle to the magnetic pole changes as you move about the Earth, and it changes slowly over the years as the field drifts. It does not depend on what your aeroplane is doing. Charts show it with dashed **isogonal lines**, lines joining places of equal variation, each labelled with its value.

Around Perth the variation is small and westerly, about 1 to 2 degrees west; check the isogonal line on the current chart, because the value changes year by year. Across the eastern states the variation is easterly, and in places much larger. That has a practical consequence for a WA pilot: in the west the true and magnetic numbers are very close, so an error in applying variation here can go unnoticed until you fly somewhere it is 10 degrees or more.

![Why does variation change as you fly east?](diagram:isogonals-wa-to-east "Variation belongs to the place: small and westerly around Perth, easterly and larger across the eastern states. Read the value off the isogonal on the current chart.")

### Compass north and deviation

Your aeroplane's compass is not alone in the cockpit. The engine, the steel parts of the airframe, the radios and the electrical wiring all make magnetic fields of their own, and they pull the compass needle away from magnetic north. The error this causes is the **deviation**, and the direction the compass actually shows is a **compass** direction, written °C.

Deviation depends on **which way the aeroplane is pointing**. Turn the aeroplane and the engine and wiring turn with it, so they sit at a different angle to the Earth's field and disturb the needle by a different amount. Every aeroplane has its own deviation, measured on the ground in a **compass swing** and recorded on the **compass deviation card** mounted near the compass, which lists the correction for each heading.

![How does a true track become a compass heading?](diagram:true-magnetic-compass "Variation east is subtracted, deviation west is added: 120 degrees true becomes 117 magnetic and 119 compass.")

| Reference | North used | Correction to the next | Depends on |
|---|---|---|---|
| True (T) | Geographic North Pole (meridians on the chart) | Variation | Where you are |
| Magnetic (M) | Direction a freely suspended magnet points (magnetic north) | Deviation | Which way the aircraft points |
| Compass (C) | What the aircraft's own compass actually reads | (none) | |

### Converting between them

You work from the chart towards the cockpit: **true, then magnetic, then compass**. The memory aid covers both steps:

**"Variation (or deviation) east, magnetic (or compass) least; west is best."**

"Least" means subtract and "best" means add. If magnetic north lies east of true north, the needle is already turned towards the east, so every direction measured from it comes out smaller: subtract an easterly variation. A westerly variation adds. Deviation works the same way between magnetic and compass. Going the other way, from compass back to true, reverse the signs.

!!! example "Worked example: true to compass with easterly variation"
    Given: a track measured on the chart is 120°T. Local variation is 3°E, and the deviation card shows 2°W on that heading.

    1. Magnetic: east is least, $120 - 3 = \mathbf{117}$°M.
    2. Compass: west is best, $117 + 2 = \mathbf{119}$°C.

    To hold that direction you fly 119 by the compass.

!!! example "Worked example: true to compass with westerly variation"
    Given: a track of 265°T, variation 2°W, deviation 3°E.

    1. Magnetic: west is best, $265 + 2 = \mathbf{267}$°M.
    2. Compass: east is least, $267 - 3 = \mathbf{264}$°C.

    The signs swapped from the first example and so did the arithmetic: add the variation, subtract the deviation.

Now try one in the reverse direction before opening the solution. Your compass reads 095°C. The deviation card shows 1°W for that heading and the variation is 2°W. What is the true direction?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    Going from compass back to true, reverse the rule: west is now subtracted. Magnetic $= 095 - 1 = 094$°M. True $= 094 - 2 =$ **092°T**. Check by going forward: $092 + 2 = 094$°M (west is best), $094 + 1 = 095$°C. It matches.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Variation and deviation are easy to swap. Variation belongs to the **place** (it is on the chart); deviation belongs to the **aeroplane and its heading** (it is on the card in the cockpit). A question that changes the heading but keeps the location changes the deviation only. And the rule works from true towards compass: going backwards with "east is least" gives an answer wrong by twice the correction.

### Which north is used where

In Australia the reference changes with who is giving you the number, and the exam tests it:

- **True:** tracks measured on charts; winds in forecasts (TAF, GAF or area forecast) and in METAR and SPECI reports.
- **Magnetic:** runway directions, headings and tracks given by ATC, and surface winds passed by ATC or on an ATIS, so they can be compared directly with the runway.
- **Compass:** what you actually steer by. In practice you fly the **direction indicator**, a gyro instrument you align with the magnetic compass before take-off and check every 15 minutes or so, because the gyro drifts.

??? check "Check yourself: you fly from Perth to Kalgoorlie. Which changes along the way, variation or deviation, and why?"
    Variation, because it depends on where you are and the isogonal lines change as you move east. Deviation changes only when the heading changes, because it comes from the aeroplane's own magnetic fields turning with it.

## Bringing it together

You are planning Jandakot to Northam. On the chart you rule a line from Jandakot to Northam and measure it against a meridian: about 056°T. The nearest isogonal line says 2°W, so the magnetic track is 058°M; west is best. The deviation card says 1°E on headings near 060, so in still air you would steer 057°C; east is least. Departing runway 06L, the runway number says the runway points about 060 magnetic, so the nose is already close to your track as you lift off. Climbing out towards the hills, the controller calls "traffic ten o'clock, five miles, 2,500 ft". Your heading is 058, so ten o'clock, 60 degrees left of the nose, is $058 - 60 = 358$: almost due north of you. You look out over the left wing towards the front, find it, and report "traffic sighted". Every form of direction in this lesson has turned up in ten minutes of flying.

## Key points

- **2.1.1** Directions are angles clockwise from north. Navigation directions are 3-figure groups (005, 090, 360 for north). Runways are 2-figure groups: the magnetic direction to the nearest 10 degrees with the last zero dropped, ends differing by 18. The clock code puts 12 o'clock on the nose and each hour is 30 degrees: right adds, left subtracts, wrap past 360.
- **2.1.2** Heading is where the nose points; track is where the aeroplane goes over the ground; drift is the angle between them, caused by the wind.
- **2.1.3** True is measured from the meridians, magnetic from the Earth's field, compass from your own compass. Variation (place) turns true into magnetic; deviation (aeroplane and heading) turns magnetic into compass. East is least, west is best, working from true towards compass. Perth variation is small and westerly; check the current chart.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Expect a clock-code question (multiply the hours by 30 and add or subtract from the heading, watching the wrap past 360), a runway-designator question (ends differ by 18), and a true to magnetic conversion using "east is least, west is best". Read which way the question is converting: from true towards compass, or back again. Remember which reference goes with which source: chart tracks and forecast winds are true, runways and ATC or ATIS winds are magnetic, and 12 o'clock is always the nose.
