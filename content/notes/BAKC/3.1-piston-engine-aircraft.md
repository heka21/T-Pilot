## What the exam expects (BAKC 3.1)

- **3.1.1** Describe the four-stroke cycle and what the cylinders, pistons, piston rings, inlet and exhaust valves, crankshaft, camshaft and spark plugs each do.
- **3.1.2** Describe how altitude and temperature change engine performance, and how throttle position and RPM set the power output.
- **3.1.3** State what the carburettor, throttle, magnetos and dual ignition, alternator, battery and its vent, propeller, circuit breakers, fuses and bus bar, impulse start, oil cooler and fuel tank vents are for.
- **3.1.4** State why you monitor the tachometer, CHT and EGT, voltmeter, ammeter and loadmeter, fuel pressure, and oil temperature and pressure.
- **3.1.5** Describe the lubrication system and the part it plays in cooling the engine.
- **3.1.6** State what the mixture control is for and what an excessively rich or lean mixture does to the engine.
- **3.1.7** Compare a simple carburettor with a fuel injection system, advantages and disadvantages.
- **3.1.8** List what the hydraulic, electrical, ignition and vacuum systems provide in a light aeroplane, and how a pilot detects or deals with a malfunction in each.

## Why this matters

Your training aeroplane at Jandakot or Serpentine has one engine, and everything you do before flight, from the fuel drain to the magneto check, exists to keep it running for the next hour. You do not need to be a mechanic, but you need a picture of what is under the cowling, because that picture is what lets you read the gauges and catch a problem early. The exam asks recall questions (which stroke makes power) and cause-and-effect ones (what a blocked fuel vent or a lean mixture leads to). The next three lessons, [fuels and oils](/notes/BAKC/3.2), [engine handling](/notes/BAKC/3.3) and [malfunctions](/notes/BAKC/3.4), build directly on this one.

## How the engine turns fuel into rotation

A mixture of petrol vapour and air is squeezed into a small space and set alight, and the hot gas pushes a piston, which turns a shaft, which turns the propeller. The engine in a typical trainer does this as a **spark-ignition, four-stroke, air-cooled piston engine**, with four or six cylinders lying flat in two opposed rows (**horizontally opposed**).

### The parts and what each does

Each **cylinder** is a strong metal tube, closed at the top by the **cylinder head**, in which the burning happens. Thin **fins** on the outside give the passing air a large surface to carry heat away; that is what "air-cooled" means.

Inside it slides the **piston**, a close-fitting metal plug that draws the mixture in, squeezes it, is driven down by the burning gas and pushes the exhaust out. A **connecting rod** links it to the crankshaft.

The piston cannot fit perfectly, because metal expands as it heats, so the gap is sealed by springy **piston rings**. The top (compression) rings stop the high-pressure gas leaking past into the crankcase, so the force of combustion goes into the piston. The bottom (oil control) ring scrapes oil off the cylinder wall, leaving a thin film but keeping most of it out of the combustion chamber. Worn rings cost both power and oil, and are one cause of the blue exhaust smoke in [BAKC 3.3](/notes/BAKC/3.3).

In the cylinder head, the **inlet valve** lets the fresh mixture in and the **exhaust valve** lets the burnt gas out. Each is held shut by a spring and pushed open at the right moment.

The **crankshaft** turns the piston's up-and-down motion into rotation, as your legs drive a bicycle's cranks. In most light aeroplanes the propeller bolts straight onto it, so propeller RPM and engine RPM are the same.

The **camshaft** times the valves: a lobe for each valve pushes it open and lets the spring close it. It is geared to turn at **half the crankshaft speed**, for a reason the next paragraph makes clear.

The **spark plugs**, **two in every cylinder**, ignite the compressed mixture.

### Four strokes, two turns

A **stroke** is one movement of the piston, up or down. One working cycle takes four:

1. **Induction.** The inlet valve opens and the piston moves down. The space above it grows, the pressure there falls, and fuel-air mixture flows in to fill it.
2. **Compression.** Both valves close and the piston moves up, squeezing the mixture into a fraction of its original volume. Compressed mixture burns faster and pushes harder.
3. **Power.** Just before the piston reaches the top, both spark plugs fire. The burning gas heats up, its pressure rises sharply and it drives the piston down. This is the **only stroke that produces power**.
4. **Exhaust.** The exhaust valve opens and the piston moves up, pushing the burnt gas out through the exhaust pipe.

"Suck, squeeze, bang, blow" is the memory aid instructors use.

![What happens in the cylinder during each of the four strokes?](diagram:four-stroke-cycle "Watch the valves: only one is ever open, and both are shut for compression and power. The crank turns twice for one spark.")

Each stroke is half a turn of the crankshaft, so a cycle takes **two crankshaft revolutions**, and each valve opens once every two turns: hence the half-speed camshaft. The other three strokes are carried through by the crankshaft's momentum and the other cylinders' power strokes.

??? check "Check yourself: why does the camshaft turn at half the speed of the crankshaft?"
    Each valve must open once per four-stroke cycle, and a cycle takes two turns of the crankshaft. Turning the camshaft once for every two crankshaft turns brings each lobe round to its valve exactly once per cycle.

## What sets the power: density, throttle and RPM

Think of the engine as an air pump that burns fuel on the way through. Fuel is easy to supply; the limit on power is how much **oxygen** each cylinder swallows on its induction stroke, and since the cylinder's volume is fixed, that depends on how **dense** the air is.

### Altitude and temperature

As you climb the air thins: the same volume, less mass, less fuel burnt, less power. As a rule of thumb, a normally aspirated engine (one without a supercharger or turbocharger to pack the air in) loses about **3 percent of its sea-level power for every 1,000 ft**. By about 7,000 to 8,000 ft a typical trainer at full throttle can only produce about 75 percent power.

![How much power is left at full throttle as you climb?](diagram:power-loss-with-altitude "Roughly 3 percent less per 1,000 ft: by 7,000 to 8,000 ft full throttle gives only about 75 percent.")

Temperature works the same way. Warm air is less dense than cool air at the same pressure, so a hot day costs power just as height does. Humid air costs a little more again, because water vapour displaces some of the oxygen. What the engine responds to is **density height**, the height in the standard atmosphere that has the same density as the air around you; you will calculate it in [BAKC 6.2](/notes/BAKC/6.2).

### The throttle

The **throttle** moves a flat disc, the **throttle butterfly**, in the passage to the cylinders. Closed, it almost blocks the passage; open, it lets the cylinders fill. The pressure after the butterfly is the **manifold pressure**: opening the throttle raises it, more mixture reaches each cylinder, and each power stroke is stronger.

### RPM

Power is also how *often* the engine does its work: at 2,400 RPM a four-cylinder engine makes 4,800 power strokes a minute, at 1,200 RPM half as many. More RPM, for the same charge per cylinder, is more power.

With a **fixed-pitch propeller**, the kind on most trainers, you cannot set these separately: opening the throttle makes the engine turn faster, and the **tachometer is your power gauge**. With a **constant-speed propeller** the propeller lever sets the RPM and the throttle sets the manifold pressure, and you need both gauges to know the power.

??? check "Check yourself: you take off from Northam on a 40 degree day. Why does the engine give less power at full throttle than it does at Jandakot in winter?"
    The hot air is less dense, so each cylinder draws in less mass of air (and oxygen) on every induction stroke and can burn less fuel. Northam's higher elevation lowers the density further.

## The components around the engine

### The carburettor and throttle

The engine needs fuel and air in the right proportion, with the fuel broken into a fine mist. In most trainers the **carburettor** does both. Air is forced through a narrowing, the **venturi**, where it speeds up and its pressure drops. A **float chamber** beside it holds a steady level of fuel, and a **main jet** connects it to the venturi throat. The lower pressure in the throat draws fuel up and sprays it into the air; the faster the airflow, the more fuel is drawn, so fuel is metered in proportion to the air. A **mixture control** needle adjusts how much fuel reaches the jet, and the **throttle butterfly** sits just above the venturi.

![How does a carburettor meter fuel into the air?](diagram:carburettor-cross-section "Low pressure in the venturi draws fuel from the float chamber through the main jet; the mixture needle meters it, the throttle butterfly sets how much mixture reaches the cylinders.")

Hold on to one fact for [BAKC 3.4](/notes/BAKC/3.4): the pressure drop in the venturi and at the butterfly, and the fuel evaporating, both chill the air, enough to form ice inside the carburettor on a warm, humid day.

### Magnetos and dual ignition

A spark plug needs many thousands of volts. They come from a **magneto**, an engine-driven generator with a spinning permanent magnet, a coil that steps the voltage up and a distributor that sends each spark to the right cylinder. A magneto is **self-contained**: it needs no battery and no aircraft electrical system, so a total electrical failure does not stop the engine.

Every aeroplane engine has **two independent magnetos**, each firing **one of the two plugs in every cylinder** through its own leads. This **dual ignition** gives redundancy (if one magneto, lead or plug fails, every cylinder still has a spark, with a small loss of power) and better combustion (two flame fronts burn the mixture faster and more completely).

![Why does a light aeroplane have two magnetos and two spark plugs per cylinder?](diagram:dual-ignition-system "Each magneto feeds one plug in every cylinder through its own leads, so one failure costs a little power, not the engine. The switch stops a magneto by earthing it.")

The ignition switch works backwards from what you might expect. A magneto sparks whenever it turns; to stop it, the switch connects it to earth through a wire called the **P-lead**. "OFF" means "earthed". If a P-lead breaks, that magneto is **live** with the switch at OFF, and moving the propeller by hand can start the engine. Treat every propeller as live.

### Impulse start

At cranking speed a magneto turns too slowly to make a strong spark, and the normal spark, timed well before the piston reaches the top, can drive the piston backwards and kick the propeller back. The **impulse coupling**, a spring-loaded coupling on one magneto, fixes both: during cranking it holds the magneto back while the spring winds up, then lets it flick round fast, giving a **stronger spark** delivered **later** (retarded). The engine starts easily without kick-back; once it is running the magneto runs at normal timing.

??? check "Check yourself: the battery is flat but someone hand-swings the propeller and the engine starts and runs. How is that possible?"
    The magnetos generate their own high voltage from the engine's rotation and need no battery. The battery only powers the starter and the electrical services. This is also why a propeller must always be treated as live.

### The electrical system: alternator, battery, bus bar and protection

The **alternator** is a belt-driven generator that supplies electrical power in flight (radios, transponder, lights, electric flaps and fuel pump, the turn coordinator, pitot heat) and recharges the battery. In most light aeroplanes it needs battery voltage to excite its field, one reason a completely flat battery is a problem.

The **battery** turns the **starter motor** and is the **emergency reserve** if the alternator fails. While charging, a lead-acid battery gives off **hydrogen** (explosive) and **acid fumes** (corrosive); the **battery compartment vent** carries them overboard.

Power is collected on a **bus bar**, a metal strip each service draws from, like a power board at home. Each circuit is protected by a **circuit breaker** (pops out, can be reset) or a **fuse** (melts, must be replaced), which cuts off an overloaded circuit before its wiring overheats and starts a fire. The usual airmanship rule is to reset a popped breaker once only, after it cools: one that trips again is protecting you from a fault.

![How does power flow from the alternator and battery to the services?](diagram:electrical-system-schematic "What to notice: every service draws from the bus bar through its own breaker; the alternator feeds the bus in flight, the battery through the master switch, and the magnetos are not on it at all.")

### The propeller

The **propeller** converts engine torque into **thrust**: each blade is a small rotating wing whose "lift" points forwards. On most trainers it is **fixed pitch**, a blade angle set at manufacture as a compromise between take-off and cruise; constant-speed propellers come later ([PAKA 2.1](/notes/PAKA/2.1)).

### The oil cooler

The **oil cooler** is a small radiator in the cooling airflow that sheds the heat the oil has carried out of the engine, keeping the oil within its temperature limits. A thermostatic valve usually bypasses it while the oil is cold.

### Fuel tank vents

Every litre of fuel that leaves a tank must be replaced by a litre of air. The **fuel tank vent**, a small tube usually under the wing, lets it in. Block it (a mud-wasp nest, ice) and the tank pressure falls as fuel is drawn off, the flow slows and stops, and the engine starves with fuel still in the tank; the low pressure can even buckle the tank. That is why you check the vents on every pre-flight.

![Where are the vents and drains in the fuel system?](diagram:fuel-system-schematic "Each tank has a vent at the top to let air in as fuel is used; block it and the engine starves with fuel still in the tank.")

## Reading the engine gauges

The engine gauges are how the engine talks to you, and the skill is to notice a **trend** long before a needle reaches the red. What each abnormal reading means is in [BAKC 3.4](/notes/BAKC/3.4); here is what each gauge is for.

The **tachometer** shows RPM. With a fixed-pitch propeller it is the **power indicator**; its red line marks the maximum RPM, and it is the gauge you read for the magneto and carburettor heat checks.

The **cylinder head temperature (CHT)** gauge shows how hot the engine itself is working, usually at the hottest cylinder. A high CHT warns of poor cooling, a too-lean mixture or detonation. The **exhaust gas temperature (EGT)** gauge responds quickly to mixture, rising as you lean towards the chemically correct ratio; its main use is to **lean accurately** in cruise.

A centre-zero **ammeter** shows current into the battery (charge) or out of it (discharge). A **loadmeter** shows the alternator's output, from zero upwards. A **voltmeter** shows system voltage, about 14 volts on a 12-volt system (28 on a 24-volt system) while the alternator charges, sinking towards battery voltage when it stops.

The **fuel pressure** gauge confirms fuel is reaching the engine; a fall warns of a failing pump, a blocked vent, an empty tank or vapour in the lines, often before the engine falters.

**Oil pressure** is the most important gauge on the panel: no pressure means dry bearings and an engine about to seize. **Oil temperature** confirms the oil is warm enough to flow and not so hot that it thins. A slow rise in oil temperature with a slow fall in pressure is the classic warning of an engine losing its oil, and it gives you minutes to choose a field while the engine still runs.

## Oil: lubrication and cooling

Metal sliding on metal at engine speeds would weld itself together within minutes. The **lubrication system** keeps a film of oil between every pair of moving surfaces.

The oil lives in a **sump** under the crankcase (a **wet sump**). An engine-driven **oil pump** pushes it through a **filter** and along drilled passages to the bearings, valve gear and cylinder walls, with a **pressure relief valve** to stop cold, thick oil over-pressurising the system; then it drains back to the sump. On the way it **lubricates**, **seals** the piston rings, **cleans** away carbon and metal particles, and **cools**.

Cooling is the part the exam emphasises. The air over the cylinder fins removes most of the heat, but it cannot reach the pistons, the underside of the heads or the bearings. Oil splashed onto those parts carries their heat to the sump and the **oil cooler**. So **oil temperature is as much a cooling gauge as a lubrication gauge**, and an engine low on oil runs hot: less oil carries the same heat.

![Where does the oil go, and how does it carry heat out?](diagram:engine-lubrication-circuit "What to notice: oil through the bearings and under the pistons picks up heat the fins cannot reach and the cooler sheds it; cold, thick oil bypasses the cooler, and the relief valve spills the excess pressure.")

## The mixture control

The **chemically correct** mixture is about 15 parts of air to 1 of fuel, by weight; engines give their best power a little richer than that and their best economy a little leaner. A **rich** mixture has more fuel than the air can burn; a **lean** one has less.

The carburettor meters fuel by the *volume* of air through it, but what burns is the *mass* of air. As you climb each litre weighs less while the fuel delivered stays about the same, so the mixture becomes **progressively richer with height**. The **mixture control** reduces the fuel flow to restore the ratio, giving smoother running, better economy, cleaner plugs and the correct temperatures.

![What happens as you lean the mixture?](diagram:mixture-rich-to-lean "Power falls away on both sides of the right mixture; too rich runs cool and fouls plugs, too lean runs hot and invites detonation.")

**Too rich.** Unburnt fuel goes out of the exhaust. The engine loses power, may run rough, wastes fuel and runs **cool**, because the excess fuel absorbs heat as it evaporates. Lead and carbon foul the spark plugs, and the exhaust may smoke black.

**Too lean.** Power falls, the engine runs rough and, above all, it runs **hot**: CHT and EGT rise, and at high power a lean mixture invites **detonation**, which can wreck pistons and valves in minutes ([BAKC 3.3](/notes/BAKC/3.3)). Leaned to idle cut-off, the engine stops; that is how you shut it down.

Hence the rule: **full rich** for take-off and climb at low levels (the POH typically says below about 5,000 ft), because the extra fuel cools the cylinders at high power. In cruise you lean by the EGT gauge or, with a fixed-pitch propeller, until the RPM peaks and starts to fall, then slightly richer, as the POH directs.

![What does the tachometer do as you lean the mixture?](diagram:leaning-by-rpm "RPM rises to a peak as the mixture approaches the best-power ratio, then falls and the engine roughens: come back to the peak and a little richer.")

!!! warning "Common trap"
    "Lean the mixture to cool the engine" is backwards. Leaning from full rich towards the correct mixture makes the engine *hotter*, and a lean mixture at high power is a cause of detonation. It is the rich mixture that runs cool, which is why you take off at full rich.

## Carburettor or fuel injection

In a **fuel injection** system a pump delivers fuel under pressure to a metering unit, which sends a measured flow to an **injector nozzle** at each cylinder's inlet port, instead of mixing it in a venturi upstream.

A **simple carburettor** is **simple, cheap and easy to maintain**, with few parts to fail. Its weaknesses come from the venturi and the long path to the cylinders: the cooling in the venturi makes it **prone to carburettor ice**, and the mixture does not reach every cylinder in the same strength (**uneven distribution**).

**Fuel injection** removes both. There is **no carburettor ice**, because there is no venturi and the fuel evaporates at the hot inlet port (the air intake can still collect impact ice, which is what "alternate air" is for, [BAKC 3.4](/notes/BAKC/3.4)). Each cylinder gets an **even, precise mixture**: **better economy, more power, smoother running**. The price: it is **more complex and costly**, **needs a fuel pump running at all times**, and is **hard to start when hot**, because fuel in the hot lines boils (**vapour lock**).

![Why can fuel injection not get carburettor ice?](diagram:carburettor-vs-injection "What to notice: the carburettor chills the induction air in the venturi and at the butterfly; injection sprays the fuel at the hot inlet port, so the air is never chilled. Only the intake can still collect impact ice.")

| | Simple carburettor | Fuel injection |
|---|---|---|
| Advantages | Simple, cheap, easy to maintain, tolerant of poor fuel delivery | No carburettor ice, precise and even mixture to each cylinder, better fuel economy and power, smoother running |
| Disadvantages | Prone to carburettor icing, uneven mixture distribution between cylinders | More complex and costly, harder to start when hot (vapour lock), needs a fuel pump at all times |

??? check "Check yourself: why can't a fuel-injected engine get carburettor ice?"
    Carburettor ice comes from the cooling in the venturi and at the throttle butterfly, and from fuel evaporating upstream of the cylinders. An injection system has no venturi and sprays the fuel at the warm inlet port, so neither cooling effect happens in the induction air. The air intake can still ice up (impact ice).

## The aeroplane's systems and what goes wrong

For each of the four systems, ask: what does it run, how would you notice it failing, and what do you do?

### Hydraulic system

In a trainer the **hydraulic system** is usually just the **wheel brakes**: each toe pedal pushes fluid from a master cylinder to the brake at the wheel (larger types add flaps and retractable gear). A leak or air in the lines shows as a **spongy** or **sinking pedal** on the taxi brake check, or fluid on the wheel. On the ground, stop and get it fixed; found in flight, pump the pedal, choose a long runway and keep straight with rudder and the other brake.

### Electrical system

The **electrical system** runs the starter, radios, transponder, lights, electric flaps and fuel pump, the turn coordinator and pitot heat. Its usual failure, the alternator stopping, shows as a discharge, a low voltage or a warning light: check the switch and breaker, shed load and land before the battery runs flat. The engine keeps running on its magnetos.

### Ignition system

The **ignition system** (two magnetos, their leads, two plugs per cylinder) is tested on the run-up by the **magneto check**, one magneto at a time: a drop beyond the POH limit or rough running means a fault on that side. At shutdown a **dead cut** check confirms the switch earths both magnetos ([BAKC 3.3](/notes/BAKC/3.3)).

### Vacuum system

The **vacuum system** drives the **attitude indicator** and **directional indicator**: an engine-driven pump sucks air out of the instrument cases, and the air drawn in spins each gyro. A low **suction gauge** reading or a flag means the gyros will slow, drift and topple; cross-check with the turn coordinator, compass and pressure instruments, and stay clear of cloud ([BAKC 3.5](/notes/BAKC/3.5)).

![What does each gyro instrument show, and what drives it?](diagram:gyro-instruments-panel "The attitude and heading indicators are vacuum driven; the turn coordinator is electric, so one failure never takes out all three.")

??? check "Check yourself: the alternator and the vacuum pump are both driven by the engine. Which instruments would you lose if each failed?"
    Alternator failure: once the battery is flat, the electric instruments (the turn coordinator), radios and other electrical services. Vacuum pump failure: the attitude and directional indicators. The airspeed indicator, altimeter, VSI and compass need neither, and the engine keeps running on its magnetos in both cases.

## Key points

- **3.1.1** Induction, compression, power, exhaust: two crankshaft turns per cycle and only the power stroke does work. Rings seal and scrape oil, the camshaft opens the valves at half crankshaft speed, two plugs per cylinder ignite the charge.
- **3.1.2** Power depends on the mass of air the cylinders swallow: higher altitude and higher temperature reduce it (roughly 3 percent per 1,000 ft); more throttle and more RPM increase it. With a fixed-pitch propeller the tachometer is the power gauge.
- **3.1.3** The carburettor meters fuel through a venturi; magnetos are self-contained and the switch stops them by earthing; the impulse coupling gives a strong, retarded spark for starting; the battery vent removes hydrogen and acid fumes; breakers and fuses protect circuits fed from the bus bar; tank vents let air in as fuel is used.
- **3.1.4** Gauges show trends early: RPM for power, CHT and EGT for heat and mixture, ammeter and voltmeter for charging, fuel pressure for supply, oil pressure for lubrication and oil temperature for cooling.
- **3.1.5** Oil lubricates, seals, cleans and carries heat from the parts the air cannot reach; the oil cooler sheds it. Low oil means a hot engine.
- **3.1.6** The mixture richens as you climb; the mixture control restores the ratio. Too rich: cool, rough, fouled plugs, black smoke. Too lean: hot, rough, detonation.
- **3.1.7** Carburettor: simple and cheap but ices and distributes unevenly. Injection: no carburettor ice, even mixture, better economy, but complex, needs a pump and is hard to start hot.
- **3.1.8** Hydraulic brakes (spongy pedal), electrical (discharge; shed load and land), ignition (magneto check and dead cut), vacuum (low suction; gyros topple, stay visual).

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Know which system drives what: in most trainers the attitude and directional indicators are vacuum driven, the turn coordinator is electric, the ASI, altimeter and VSI need no power at all, and the engine's ignition is independent of the electrical system. Expect recall questions on the power stroke, the camshaft's half speed, the impulse coupling's retarded spark, the battery vent's purpose and what a blocked fuel vent does.
