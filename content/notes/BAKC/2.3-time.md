## What the exam expects (BAKC 2.3)

- **2.3.1** Write any time of day as a 4-figure group on the 24-hour clock.
- **2.3.2** Convert a local standard time (for you, Western Standard Time) into UTC, including the date.
- **2.3.3** Convert a UTC time into local standard time, including the date.

## Why this matters

Almost every time you read in aviation is in UTC, and almost every time you live by is in Western Standard Time. The TAF for Perth that tells you when the fog will lift, the NOTAM that closes a runway at Jandakot for works, the activity times of a restricted area on the way to Bunbury, the time you put on your flight notification: all in UTC. Your alarm clock, last light at York and the time your passengers turn up are in WST. Every pre-flight briefing involves converting between the two, and the mistake that matters is rarely the hours; it is the date. A NOTAM that closes an aerodrome "from 2300 UTC on the 3rd" closes it at 7 o'clock on the morning of the 4th in Perth. The exam asks exactly these conversions, usually with a midnight in the way, and expects the 4-figure format throughout.

## The 24-hour clock as a 4-figure group

The ordinary clock uses the numbers 1 to 12 twice a day and leans on "am" and "pm" to tell the two halves apart. Spoken over a radio, written in a hurry or read on a printout, those two letters are the easiest thing in the world to lose, and losing them puts you 12 hours out. Aviation removes the problem by counting the hours straight through the day from 0 to 23. There is then only one 7 o'clock: 0700 is the morning, 1900 is the evening.

The time is written as a **4-figure group**: two figures for the hours, then two for the minutes, with **no colon and no "am" or "pm"**. The day starts at **0000** (midnight, the beginning of the day) and its last minute is **2359**. Leading zeros are always written, so that every time has four figures: five past seven in the morning is **0705**, not 705. Spoken, it is "zero seven zero five".

The conversion from the 12-hour clock follows from how the day is counted:

- **Morning** (am) times keep their hours, with a leading zero: 7:05 am is 0705.
- **From 1 pm onward**, add 12 to the hour: 3:45 pm is 15 + 45, **1545**.
- **The two awkward hours** are the ones with "12" in them. Noon is 1200, and 12:30 pm is 1230 (nothing to add, because 12 pm is already the twelfth hour). The hour after midnight is hour zero, so 12:20 am is **0020**.

| 12-hour time | 4-figure group |
|---|---|
| 12:20 am | 0020 |
| 7:05 am | 0705 |
| 12:00 noon | 1200 |
| 3:45 pm | 1545 |
| 11:59 pm | 2359 |

![How does a 12-hour time become a 4-figure group?](diagram:twenty-four-hour-clock "Morning hours keep a leading zero; from 1 pm add 12 to the hour, so 3:45 pm is 1545.")

!!! example "Worked example: both ways on the 24-hour clock"
    Given: a briefing at 9:40 pm; a fuel delivery at 12:15 am; a last light time printed as 1925.

    1. 9:40 pm is after 1 pm, so add 12 to the hour: $9 + 12 = 21$, giving **2140**.
    2. 12:15 am is in the hour after midnight, hour zero: **0015**.
    3. 1925 is more than 1259, so subtract 12 from the hour: $19 - 12 = 7$, giving **7:25 pm**.

    Each answer has exactly four figures in the 24-hour form, and no am or pm.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The hour after midnight is 00, not 12 and not 24. 12:20 am is 0020. And never drop the leading zero: "705" is not a valid time group.

### Adding times: the minutes carry at 60

Flight planning constantly adds a flight time to a clock time: departure at 1545, flight time 1 hour 35 minutes, so when do you land? The catch is that a 4-figure group looks like an ordinary number but is not one. The last two figures are minutes, and they run only to 59. Add hours to hours and minutes to minutes separately, and when the minutes reach 60 or more, take 60 away and carry one hour across. Never add the two groups as if they were whole numbers: 1545 + 135 = 1680 is not a time.

!!! example "Worked example: landing time from a departure time"
    Given: departure 1545, flight time 1 hour 35 minutes.

    1. Minutes: $45 + 35 = 80$ minutes, which is 1 hour 20 minutes: write **20**, carry 1 hour.
    2. Hours: $15 + 1 + 1 = \mathbf{17}$.
    3. Landing time: **1720**.

    The same rule, run backwards, gives the latest departure time for a fixed arrival: borrow 60 minutes from the hours when the minutes would go negative.

??? check "Check yourself: why does aviation write times as 4-figure groups instead of using am and pm?"
    Because "am" and "pm" are easily missed or misheard, and a mistake puts you 12 hours out. Counting the hours from 00 to 23 gives each time of day exactly one number, and the fixed four figures make a missing digit obvious.

## UTC: one clock for the whole world

The sun is overhead at noon somewhere different every moment, because the Earth turns 360 degrees in 24 hours, 15 degrees of longitude every hour. Local clocks follow the sun, roughly, so when it is noon in Perth it is about 2 pm in Sydney, early morning in London and the middle of the night in New York. That is fine on the ground, but aviation is an international network. A forecast, a NOTAM or a flight plan written in local time would mean different things to the forecaster in Melbourne, the controller in Perth and the pilot flying across a time-zone boundary. So aviation uses one clock for everyone.

That clock is **UTC, Coordinated Universal Time**: the time at zero degrees of longitude, through Greenwich in England, kept by atomic clocks and never adjusted for summer. Flight notifications, NOTAM, forecasts (TAF and GAF), METAR, ATC logs and SARTIMEs are all in UTC. It is often marked with a **Z**, for "Zulu", so 0530Z means 0530 UTC.

Every local time zone is defined as an **offset** from UTC. Perth is at about 116 degrees east, and the zone that covers Western Australia is set at 120 degrees east, which is $120 \div 15 = 8$ hours of the Earth's turn ahead of Greenwich. So **Western Standard Time (WST) is UTC + 8**.

| Zone | Offset |
|---|---|
| Western Standard Time (WST, Western Australia) | UTC + 8 |
| Central Standard Time (CST: SA, NT) | UTC + 9½ |
| Eastern Standard Time (EST: Qld, NSW, ACT, Vic, Tas) | UTC + 10 |

"Plus" means **ahead**. The sun rises over Australia long before it rises over Greenwich, so Australian clocks are ahead of UTC: when it is 0800 in Perth, it is still midnight UTC. Hold on to that picture and the direction of every conversion follows from it.

Western Australia does **not** use daylight saving, so WST is UTC + 8 all year round. Some eastern and southern states move their clocks an hour forward in summer, which changes their offset from UTC and their difference from Perth for part of the year. You need to remember that if you read times from those states or fly there; for the exam, the conversions are to **standard** time.

![What time and date is it in UTC when it is a given time in Perth?](diagram:utc-and-wst-strip "The same moment sits in the same column: WST is always 8 hours ahead, so the WST date changes at 1600 UTC and early-morning WST is still yesterday in UTC.")

??? check "Check yourself: is Perth ahead of or behind UTC, and how can you remember which?"
    Ahead, by 8 hours. The sun rises in Australia first; when Perth is already at 0800 on its working day, the UTC day is only just beginning at 0000.

## Local standard time to UTC

Perth is 8 hours ahead, so to go from WST to UTC you go **back** 8 hours: **subtract 8**. If the subtraction drops below 0000, you have gone back past midnight. Add 24 to the hours to bring the time back onto the clock, and move the date **back** one day.

!!! example "Worked example: WST to UTC on the same date"
    Given: a planned departure from Jandakot at 1530 WST, and a briefing at 1115 WST.

    1. $1530 - 0800 = \mathbf{0730}$ UTC, same date.
    2. $1115 - 0800 = \mathbf{0315}$ UTC, same date.

    Both WST times are after 0800, so subtracting 8 hours leaves a positive time and the date does not change.

!!! example "Worked example: WST to UTC across midnight"
    Given: a dawn departure at 0545 WST on Tuesday 7 October.

    1. $0545 - 0800$ is negative: you have gone back past midnight.
    2. Add 24 hours to the starting time and subtract again: $2945 - 0800 = \mathbf{2145}$.
    3. Move the date back one day: **2145 UTC on Monday 6 October**.

    You take off on Tuesday morning; the flight notification shows Monday night.

Now try one before opening the solution. A night VFR instructor plans to depart at 0300 WST on Saturday. What is that in UTC, with the day?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    $0300 - 0800$ is negative, so add 24: $2700 - 0800 = 1900$. You crossed midnight going backwards, so the day moves back: **1900 UTC on Friday**. Sanity check: any WST time before 0800 is the previous day in UTC.

## UTC to local standard time

Going the other way, you go **forward** 8 hours: **add 8**. If the result is 2400 or more, you have gone forward past midnight. Subtract 24 to bring the time back onto the clock, and move the date **forward** one day.

![When is an area listed in UTC active by your watch?](diagram:utc-to-wst-activity "Add 8 hours: an area listed active 2200 to 0600 UTC is active 0600 to 1400 WST, and the start falls on the next WST date.")

!!! example "Worked example: UTC to WST on the same date"
    Given: a TAF for Perth that becomes valid at 0000 UTC, and a SIGMET that expires at 1430 UTC.

    1. $0000 + 0800 = \mathbf{0800}$ WST, same date.
    2. $1430 + 0800 = \mathbf{2230}$ WST, same date.

    Both results are below 2400, so the date stays the same.

!!! example "Worked example: UTC to WST across midnight"
    Given: a NOTAM effective from 2300 UTC on 3 October.

    1. $2300 + 0800 = 3100$, which is past 2400.
    2. Subtract 24 hours: $3100 - 2400 = \mathbf{0700}$.
    3. Move the date forward one day: **0700 WST on 4 October**.

    If you read only the date on the NOTAM, you would think the restriction started on the 3rd, a day early.

Try one yourself first. A NOTAM closes a runway from 1700 UTC on Thursday. When does it start in Perth?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    $1700 + 0800 = 2500$; subtract 24 to get 0100, and move the day forward: **0100 WST on Friday**. Sanity check: any UTC time from 1600 onward is the next day in WST.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The hours are rarely wrong; the **date** is. Any WST time **before 0800** is the **previous day** in UTC, and any UTC time **from 1600 onward** is the **next day** in WST. Every time you add or subtract 8, ask whether you crossed midnight, and if you did, change the date in the same direction.

### Checking your answer

Two habits catch nearly every conversion error. The first is to **convert back**: if 1700 UTC Thursday became 0100 WST Friday, then 0100 WST Friday minus 8 hours must return 1700 UTC Thursday. It does, so the answer stands. The second is to picture the **two boundaries** where the date changes. Perth's midnight is 1600 UTC, and the UTC midnight is 0800 in Perth. Between 0800 and 2359 WST, the two clocks share a date. Between 0000 and 0759 WST, Perth has already moved on to tomorrow while UTC is still on today, so the WST date is one ahead. Put any time on that picture before you calculate, and you know whether the date will change before the arithmetic tells you.

??? check "Check yourself: a TAF is valid from 2000 UTC on the 21st. Without working it out fully, is the WST start time on the 21st or the 22nd, and why?"
    The 22nd. Any UTC time from 1600 onward plus 8 hours passes 2400, so it falls on the next WST date.

## Other Australian time zones

The same method works for any zone: add the offset to go from UTC to local, subtract it to go from local to UTC, and watch midnight. You will meet eastern times in national NOTAM, in area forecasts covering the Nullarbor, and whenever you plan a flight across the border.

!!! example "Worked example: UTC to Eastern Standard Time"
    Given: a NOTAM for Brisbane says 2200 UTC; a Sydney briefing says 2330 UTC. Both are on EST, UTC + 10.

    1. Brisbane: $2200 + 1000 = 3200$; $3200 - 2400 = \mathbf{0800}$ EST **the next day**.
    2. Sydney: $2330 + 1000 = 3330$; $3330 - 2400 = \mathbf{0930}$ EST **the next day**.

    The eastern states are 10 hours ahead of UTC, so their date changes at 1400 UTC, two hours before Perth's.

Central Standard Time, in South Australia and the Northern Territory, is **UTC + 9½**, so conversions there carry half an hour: 0100 UTC is 1030 CST. A NOTAM for Alice Springs starting at 2200 UTC begins at $2200 + 0930 = 3130$, which is past 2400, so it is 0730 CST the next day. The half hour changes nothing about the method; it only makes the arithmetic less round, which is where slips happen. The difference between Perth and the eastern states is 2 hours on standard time, and more when daylight saving is in force there.

![What time is it across Australia at the same moment?](diagram:time-zones-australia "WST is UTC + 8, CST UTC + 9½, EST UTC + 10: 0200 UTC is 1000 in Perth and 1200 in Brisbane, and the eastern date changes first, at 1400 UTC.")

??? check "Check yourself: it is 1000 WST in Perth. What standard time is it in Brisbane, and why?"
    1200 EST. Both are measured from UTC: Perth is UTC + 8 and Brisbane UTC + 10, so Brisbane is 2 hours ahead of Perth. (Through UTC: 1000 WST is 0200 UTC, plus 10 is 1200.)

## Bringing it together

It is a Friday evening in December and you are planning a Saturday morning flight from Jandakot to Kalbarri, about 3 hours away. The TAF for Kalbarri is valid from 1800 UTC on Friday; in Perth that is $1800 + 0800 = 2600$, so 0200 WST on Saturday, and it covers your whole flight. A NOTAM closes the Kalbarri runway for works from 0100 to 0300 UTC on Saturday. Adding 8 hours, that is 0900 to 1100 WST on Saturday, so if you take off at 0630 and land at 0930 you will arrive in the middle of the closure. You bring the departure forward to 0540 WST, first light having long passed at that time of year, and land at 0840, twenty minutes before the works start. On the flight notification the take-off time is before 0800 WST, so in UTC it is $2940 - 0800 = 2140$ on **Friday**; the landing, $0840 - 0800 = 0040$ UTC, is on Saturday. You set a SARTIME of 0910 WST, which is 0110 UTC on Saturday. One flight on one Perth morning, and the paperwork spans two UTC dates. That is normal, and it is exactly where a careless conversion would have put you on a closed runway.

## Key points

- **2.3.1** Times are 4-figure groups on the 24-hour clock, from 0000 to 2359: hours then minutes, no colon, no am or pm, leading zeros kept. From 1 pm add 12 to the hour; the hour after midnight is 00.
- **2.3.2** WST to UTC: subtract 8 hours. If you go below 0000, add 24 and move the date back a day: any WST time before 0800 is the previous UTC date.
- **2.3.3** UTC to WST: add 8 hours. If you reach 2400 or more, subtract 24 and move the date forward a day: any UTC time from 1600 onward is the next WST date. WA has no daylight saving; CST is UTC + 9½ and EST is UTC + 10.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Expect a 12-hour to 4-figure conversion and at least one UTC conversion in each direction, often with the date in the options. Before you choose, run the sanity check: Perth is ahead of UTC, so the WST number is the bigger one unless you crossed midnight; WST before 0800 is yesterday in UTC; UTC from 1600 is tomorrow in WST. If the question involves another state, it means standard time unless it says otherwise.
