## What the exam expects (BAKC 6.1)

- **6.1.1** Name the documents that tell you whether an aircraft is serviceable, and use them to decide whether it is.
- **6.1.2** Describe how an aircraft is certified as fit for flight: the daily inspection, who may do it and how it is recorded, and how it differs from the pilot's pre-flight inspection.
- **6.1.3** Describe how a defect is recorded on the maintenance release, the release to service document, and what happens to the aircraft afterwards.

## Why this matters

Early on a Saturday at Jandakot you are handed the keys to a club aeroplane that someone else flew last night. You did not see it maintained and you have no idea what the last pilot noticed. The only way you can know it is fit to fly is through a short chain of documents and inspections, and the law makes you, the pilot in command, the person who must be satisfied that the chain is complete before you start the engine. The same chain works in the other direction: when you find something wrong, the way you record it is what stops the next pilot flying it unknowingly. The exam asks which document shows serviceability, who may sign a daily inspection, how it differs from a pre-flight, and what you do with a defect. All of those make sense once you see the chain as a whole, so this lesson builds it link by link. The regulation references here are from the cited sources as they stood when the note was written; CASA has been moving these rules between the older CAR 1988 and the newer CASR Parts, so verify the current regulation numbers.

## A chain of certificates

Think about what has to be true for an aeroplane to be safe to fly today. Its **design** must be sound. This particular airframe must have been **built to that design** and kept to it. It must be **legally on the register**, so that someone is accountable for it. Its **maintenance** must be up to date. Nothing must have **gone wrong since** the last maintenance. And nothing must be wrong **right now**, on this walk-around, with this fuel.

Each of those is answered by a different document or inspection, and each covers a different length of time. The design and conformity are certified once and stay in force while the aircraft is maintained. The maintenance is certified for a period of months or hours. The daily inspection is certified for a day. The pre-flight covers one flight. Lay them out in that order and you have the whole of this subtopic.

## The documents that show serviceability

**The certificate of airworthiness.** This confirms that the aircraft type meets an approved design standard and that this individual aircraft conforms to it. It is the foundation: without one the aircraft cannot be flown as a normal certificated aircraft. It does not expire on a date. It **remains in force as long as the aircraft is maintained as required**, which is the link to the next document: the moment the maintenance lapses, the certificate of airworthiness no longer means the aircraft is airworthy.

**The certificate of registration.** This shows the aircraft is on the **Australian register** under its VH- marks, and who the registered operator is. It is a legal document rather than an engineering one: it tells you the aircraft may lawfully operate in Australia and who is responsible for it.

**The approved flight manual or POH.** The **pilot's operating handbook** must be the one for this aircraft, with its **current amendments**, and it must include the **weight and balance data or loading system for this particular aircraft**. That last part matters: two aeroplanes of the same type have different empty weights and CG positions because their equipment differs, so a POH borrowed from the hangar next door gives you the wrong numbers (see [BAKC 6.4](/notes/BAKC/6.4)). The limitations section of the POH is also where the speed limits of [BAKC 6.3](/notes/BAKC/6.3) and the structural weights come from.

**Airworthiness directives and the maintenance schedule.** An **airworthiness directive** (AD) is a mandatory instruction to inspect or modify something, issued when a problem is found in a type. The **maintenance schedule** sets out the periodic inspections. Both are the engineer's business to track, not yours, but they reach you through the maintenance release: **if the maintenance release shows an item overdue, the aircraft is unserviceable**.

**Logbooks** (the aircraft, engine and propeller logbooks) hold the full maintenance history. They are **not carried in the aircraft**, and you do not normally inspect them before flight. Their contents are summarised, for your purposes, in the maintenance release.

**The maintenance release.** This is the key document, the one you actually check before every flight, and it is worth understanding in detail.

??? check "Check yourself: the certificate of airworthiness has no expiry date. Why does that not mean the aircraft is airworthy for ever?"
    Because it remains in force only while the aircraft is maintained as required. If the maintenance release has expired or required maintenance is overdue, the aircraft no longer meets the condition on which the certificate rests, so it is not airworthy whatever the certificate says.

## The maintenance release: what it certifies and how you read it

After a periodic inspection, a licensed aircraft maintenance engineer certifies that the required maintenance has been done and **releases the aircraft to service** for a stated period. The document that records this is the **maintenance release**. Its existence says "this aircraft was inspected and found fit, and its maintenance is current until the limits written on it".

The release is valid for a stated period, commonly **100 hours or 12 months, whichever comes first, for a private or training aeroplane** (verify against the aircraft's maintenance schedule and the current regulations). Both limits are written on it: an **expiry date**, and an expiry **time in service**, the aircraft's hours at which it lapses. Whichever is reached first ends the release.

The maintenance release has more than one part, and each part has a job:

- The release itself, with the **period of validity** and any **maintenance due** within it (an item due at a certain hours figure, an AD inspection due by a date).
- The **daily inspection certifications**, a line for each day the aircraft flies.
- The **defect section**, **Part 2**, where defects are endorsed and where any **permissible unserviceabilities** are recorded with their conditions.

![Where on the maintenance release is each item?](diagram:maintenance-release-anatomy "What to notice: expiry by date and hours and the maintenance due are in the release itself, defects in Part 2, today's daily inspection in Part 3: four questions, four places to look. A schematic, not a facsimile.")

Reading it before flight is a short routine with four questions, the four that the airworthiness flow diagram earlier in this section ends on:

1. **Is it current in both hours and date?** Compare today's date with the expiry date, and the aircraft's hours now with the expiry time in service.
2. **Has the daily inspection been certified for today?**
3. **Is there an endorsed defect that grounds the aircraft?** A defect that has not been cleared or accepted as a permissible unserviceability does. A permissible unserviceability may carry conditions (for example a limitation on the kind of flight) that you must meet.
4. **Is any required maintenance due before the end of the flight you intend?** Not just "is anything overdue now", but "will anything fall due while I am airborne". Add the planned flight time to the current hours and compare.

A "no" to the first two, or a "yes" to the last two, means the aircraft is **not serviceable for that flight**.

!!! example "Worked example: is this aeroplane serviceable for your flight?"
    Given: today is 3 October. The maintenance release was issued on 12 March this year at **3,412.5 hours** time in service, valid for **100 hours or 12 months**. The aircraft now shows **3,498.7 hours**. You plan a **1.5 hour** flight. Today's daily inspection is certified. Part 2 has one entry: a cabin light unserviceable, endorsed by an engineer as a permissible unserviceability with no conditions relevant to a day VFR flight.

    1. Expiry by hours: $3412.5 + 100 = \mathbf{3{,}512.5}$ hours.
    2. Expiry by date: **12 March next year**. Today is well inside it.
    3. Hours at the end of your flight: $3498.7 + 1.5 = \mathbf{3{,}500.2}$ hours, below 3,512.5.
    4. Daily inspection: **certified today**.
    5. Defects: one, **accepted as a permissible unserviceability**, with no condition that affects your flight.

    All four answers are the right ones: the aeroplane is serviceable for this flight. Notice that the hours limit, 12.3 hours after you land, is much closer than the date limit; on a busy training aeroplane it usually is.

Now try one before opening the solution: the same aeroplane, two weeks later. It now shows **3,511.0 hours**, the daily is certified, and you plan a **2.0 hour** cross-country to Northam and back. Can you go?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    The maintenance release expires at 3,512.5 hours. You would land at $3511.0 + 2.0 = 3{,}513.0$ hours, half an hour past the expiry. The release is current now, but required maintenance falls due **before the end of the flight**, so the aircraft is **not serviceable for that flight**. You could do a flight of under 1.5 hours, or wait for the periodic inspection and a new maintenance release.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    "Current" is not enough. The question to ask of the maintenance release is whether it will still be current **when you land**, and whether any item of maintenance falls due during the flight. An aircraft that is legal on the ground can become one that should not be flying halfway through a long cross-country.

## Certifying the aircraft for flight: the daily inspection

The maintenance release covers months. A lot can happen in a day: a bird's nest in the cowl, a tyre cut on a stone, a fuel cap left loose by a refueller. So on top of the periodic maintenance there is a daily check, and it is formally certified.

A **daily inspection** must be completed and **certified on the maintenance release before the first flight of each day**. It is a thorough inspection to the **manufacturer's or operator's daily inspection schedule**, a written checklist, not just a look around. It is signed by an **authorised person**: a **licensed engineer**, or **a pilot who holds a pilot licence and is authorised by the operator to carry out daily inspections on that type**. Once certified, it is **valid for the rest of that day**, so the second and third flights of the day do not need another one.

The reason the daily is certified is accountability. Someone has signed, on the document that travels with the aircraft, that the inspection was done to the schedule. If you are the first to fly the aeroplane today and that line is blank, the aircraft is not ready to fly until an authorised person completes it.

## The pre-flight inspection: every flight, your responsibility

Separately, the **pilot in command** carries out a **pre-flight inspection** before **every flight**. This is the walk-around in the POH: fuel quantity and a fuel sample, oil, control surfaces and their full and free movement, tyres, the propeller, the pitot cover off, the tie-downs off. It **does not need to be certified**: nothing is written on the maintenance release.

What makes it binding is the pilot in command's wider responsibility. Under the Part 91 rules the **pilot in command is responsible for being satisfied that the aircraft is airworthy, correctly loaded, and has the required documents and equipment on board** before flight. The daily inspection can have been certified by someone else at 0700; if you find a fuel cap missing at 1400, that responsibility makes it your problem, not theirs.

![How does the daily inspection differ from the pre-flight inspection?](diagram:daily-vs-preflight-inspection "The daily is once a day, to a schedule, and certified on the maintenance release; the pre-flight is before every flight and not certified.")

| | Daily inspection | Pre-flight inspection |
|---|---|---|
| When | Once a day, before the first flight | Before every flight |
| To what | The daily inspection schedule | The POH walk-around |
| By whom | A licensed engineer, or an authorised pilot | The pilot in command |
| Recorded | Certified on the maintenance release | Not certified |
| Valid for | The rest of the day | That flight |

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The daily inspection and the pre-flight inspection are different things, and doing one does not replace the other. An authorised pilot who does the daily before the first flight has still to do a pre-flight before each later flight, and the pre-flight is never written on the maintenance release.

??? check "Check yourself: you are the third pilot to fly the club aeroplane today. Which inspection must you do, and what must you check on the maintenance release about the other one?"
    You must do the pre-flight inspection yourself, because it is required before every flight. On the maintenance release you check that today's daily inspection has been certified by an authorised person; it was done before the first flight and is valid for the rest of the day, so you do not repeat it.

## Recording a defect

Now the other direction. During the walk-around you find the nosewheel tyre worn through to the cords, or after landing you realise the alternator warning light flickered for the last twenty minutes. Whatever the defect, the next pilot has no way of knowing about it unless you write it down where they will look. That place is the maintenance release, the document they must check before they fly.

A defect found before or after a flight must be **recorded on the maintenance release in the defect section (Part 2)** with:

- a **clear description** of the defect, written so that the next pilot and the engineer understand exactly what is wrong;
- the **date** and the aircraft **time in service**;
- the **signature and licence number** of the person making the endorsement.

If the defect appears **in flight**, the pilot **endorses it after landing**.

Once the defect is endorsed, the aircraft **cannot fly again until a licensed engineer either rectifies the defect and signs it off, or determines it is a permissible unserviceability** that does not affect safety and endorses it as such, **with any conditions**. A pilot in command **must not fly with a known defect that has not been dealt with in this way**.

![What happens after a defect is endorsed?](diagram:defect-endorsement-flow "The aircraft is grounded until an engineer rectifies it or endorses it as a permissible unserviceability, with any conditions.")

The logic is simple once you see it. The endorsement is a lock. It is placed by whoever finds the problem, which can be any pilot, and it can be released only by an engineer, who either fixes the problem or takes professional responsibility for saying that it does not matter for safety, perhaps with a condition attached. Neither the pilot who found it nor the next pilot can decide alone that it is "probably fine".

Some defects are also **reportable**: reporting a defect that affects safety is an obligation under the transport safety reporting rules, separate from endorsing it on the maintenance release. The endorsement protects the next pilot of this aircraft; the report lets the safety system see patterns across many aircraft.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Telling the next pilot, or the instructor on duty, is not recording the defect. The defect must be endorsed on the maintenance release, in Part 2, with a description, date, time in service, signature and licence number. Only that endorsement reaches every future pilot through the document they are required to check.

??? check "Check yourself: on your pre-flight you find a crack in the exhaust pipe. What do you do, and when may the aeroplane fly again?"
    Do not fly. Endorse the defect in Part 2 of the maintenance release with a clear description, the date, the time in service, and your signature and licence number. The aeroplane may fly again only when a licensed engineer has rectified it and signed it off, or has endorsed it as a permissible unserviceability, with any conditions.

## Bringing it together

You arrive for a solo navigation exercise. The certificate of registration and the certificate of airworthiness are in order, and the POH in the seat pocket is this aircraft's, with its own weight and balance data. On the maintenance release you check the expiry date, then the hours: the aircraft has 4 hours left on the release and your flight is 2.5, so nothing falls due before you land. Today's daily inspection was certified at 0715 by an authorised pilot. Part 2 shows one defect from yesterday, a sticking fuel gauge, rectified and signed off by an engineer this morning. Then you do your own pre-flight, and find a cut in the left main tyre deep enough to show the cords. That breaks the chain at the last link. You endorse the defect in Part 2, and the flight waits until an engineer has replaced the tyre and signed it off. When that is done, the chain is complete again, and every link has a signature on it.

??? check "Check yourself: which single document do you check before every flight to know the aircraft's maintenance is current and its defects are dealt with, and what four things do you look for on it?"
    The maintenance release. It must be current in hours and date, have today's daily inspection certified, show no endorsed defect that grounds the aircraft (any permissible unserviceability with its conditions met), and have no maintenance falling due before the end of your flight.

## Key points

- **6.1.1** The documents: certificate of airworthiness (in force while maintained), certificate of registration, the approved flight manual or POH with this aircraft's weight and balance data, and above all the **maintenance release**, through which ADs and the maintenance schedule reach you. Logbooks hold the history and are not carried. Check the maintenance release for hours, date, today's daily, grounding defects, and maintenance due before you land.
- **6.1.2** The **daily inspection** is done once a day before the first flight, to the daily schedule, by a licensed engineer or an authorised pilot, **certified on the maintenance release**, and valid for the rest of the day. The **pre-flight inspection** is done before every flight by the pilot in command and is not certified; the pilot in command must be satisfied the aircraft is airworthy, loaded correctly and properly equipped.
- **6.1.3** A defect is endorsed in **Part 2** of the maintenance release with a description, the date and time in service, and the signature and licence number. The aircraft is then grounded until an engineer rectifies it or endorses it as a permissible unserviceability, with any conditions. In-flight defects are endorsed after landing.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Daily inspection and pre-flight inspection are different things. The daily is once a day, to a schedule, and certified on the maintenance release by an authorised person. The pre-flight is before every flight, by the pilot, and is not certified. If a question asks which document shows maintenance status and outstanding defects, the answer is the maintenance release, not the certificate of airworthiness or the logbooks. And remember the "before the end of the flight" test: maintenance due during the flight makes the aircraft unserviceable for it.
