## What the exam expects (PMTC 2.5)

- **2.5.1** Explain how pressure differences drive the wind, and use Buys Ballot's law to work out roughly where the high and low pressure lie from the wind you are flying in.
- **2.5.2** Tell (a) a squall from a gust and (b) backing from veering.
- **2.5.3** Compare the surface wind with the gradient wind, in both direction and strength.
- **2.5.4** List what controls the daily (diurnal) change in the wind, and describe how the surface wind typically changes over 24 hours.

## Why this matters

As a private pilot the wind stops being a number on the windsock and becomes a weather map you fly through. Tracking south to Albany you will hold a big drift correction for an hour and need to know what it says about the pressure ahead and your altimeter. Departing Jandakot at dawn you will lift off in a calm and climb into a 25 kt easterly a few hundred feet up; by mid-afternoon the same day the surface wind will be gusty and the Fremantle Doctor will have turned the runway round. All of that follows from two forces and one surface effect, which the RPL lessons ([RMTC 2.1](/notes/RMTC/2.1), [RMTC 2.3](/notes/RMTC/2.3)) used without deriving. The exam asks for the Southern Hemisphere answers, and every distractor is the Northern Hemisphere answer from an overseas textbook, so understanding the mechanism is the surest way not to be caught.

## Why air moves: pressure and the isobars

Air pressure at a place is the weight of the column of air above it. Where the column is heavier, the pressure is higher. On a **synoptic chart**, the Bureau's mean sea level pressure chart, lines called **isobars** join places of equal pressure, usually every 2 or 4 hPa, exactly as contour lines on a topographic map join places of equal height.

The patterns on that chart have names. A **high**, or **anticyclone**, is a region of higher pressure surrounded by lower pressure. Air in a high is slowly sinking, and sinking air warms and dries, so a high usually brings fine, stable weather with light winds near its centre. The sinking can trap haze, smoke and moisture under a **subsidence inversion**, so a high can also give fog, low stratus and poor visibility, especially in winter. A **low**, also called a **depression** (or **cyclone** in the general meteorological sense), is the opposite: air converges into it near the surface and rises, and rising air cools and makes cloud and rain. A **trough** is an elongated extension of low pressure, often with a wind change and cloud along it; a **ridge** is an elongated extension of high pressure, with weather like a high's.

Now picture air sitting between a high and a low. The air on the high side is pushed harder than the air on the low side, so there is a net force towards the low. That force is the **pressure gradient force** (**PGF**). It always acts at right angles to the isobars, from high towards low, and its size depends on how quickly the pressure changes with distance. Close isobars mean a big change in a short distance: a steep gradient, a large force and a **strong wind**. Widely spaced isobars mean a gentle gradient and a light wind. The topographic analogy holds here: close contours mean a steep slope.

If that were the whole story, the wind would blow straight across the isobars and the low would fill within hours. It does not, because the Earth rotates.

## Coriolis: why the wind blows along the isobars

The Earth turns under the moving air. To someone standing on the ground, air that sets off in a straight line appears to curve away. That apparent deflection is the **Coriolis effect**, usually treated as a force so that the sums work on a map. Three facts about it matter:

1. In the **Southern Hemisphere it deflects moving air to the left** of its direction of motion. (In the Northern Hemisphere, to the right. This is where every overseas answer goes wrong for you.)
2. It is **proportional to the wind speed**. Still air feels no Coriolis force; fast air feels a lot.
3. It is **zero at the equator** and greatest at the poles, because it depends on how much the local vertical is tilted by the Earth's spin. Perth, at about 32 degrees south, has plenty of it.

Now follow a parcel of air between a high to its north and a low to its south. The pressure gradient force starts it moving south, towards the low. As soon as it moves, Coriolis pushes it to its left, which for air moving south is towards the east. As it speeds up, Coriolis grows and turns it further left. The turning continues until the air is moving at right angles to the pressure gradient, straight along the isobars towards the east. At that point the pressure gradient force pulls it south, towards the low, and Coriolis, acting to the left of an eastward-moving parcel, pushes it north, towards the high. The two balance, and the air flows steadily along the isobars. With the high to the north and the low to the south, you have a **westerly** wind: the westerlies of the Southern Ocean.

That balanced wind along straight isobars is the **geostrophic wind**; around curved isobars the slightly modified balance gives the **gradient wind**. For the exam they are treated together: above the friction layer the wind blows parallel to the isobars at a speed set by their spacing.

Apply the same reasoning to a whole system. Around a **low**, the pressure gradient force points inwards and Coriolis must point outwards, which in the Southern Hemisphere means the air moves with the low on its right. Air moving with the centre always on its right goes round **clockwise**. Around a **high** the forces swap sides, the high is on the air's left, and the flow is **anticlockwise**.

![Which way does the wind blow round highs and lows in the Southern Hemisphere?](diagram:highs-and-lows-southern-hemisphere "What to notice: anticlockwise round a high, clockwise round a low, because Coriolis pulls moving air to its left until it balances the pull towards the low.")

!!! warning "Common trap"
    "Anticlockwise round a low" is the Northern Hemisphere answer, and it will be one of the options. In Australia: **clockwise round a low, anticlockwise round a high.** A quick check: the westerlies over southern Australia blow between the highs to their north and the big Southern Ocean lows to their south, and only clockwise flow round those lows gives a westerly on their northern side.

??? check "Check yourself: the isobars over the Wheatbelt are twice as close together today as yesterday. What has happened to the gradient wind, and why?"
    It is stronger. Closer isobars mean the pressure changes faster with distance, so the pressure gradient force is larger, and the wind must blow faster for Coriolis, which grows with speed, to balance it.

## Buys Ballot's law: reading the map from the cockpit

The balance you have just built gives a rule you can use in flight. Above the friction layer the wind blows along the isobars with the low on its right. So if you stand with **your back to the wind**, the wind is blowing the way you face, and the **low pressure is on your right** and the high on your left. That is **Buys Ballot's law** for the Southern Hemisphere. (A Dutch meteorologist stated it for the Northern Hemisphere, where the low is on the left.)

In the air you find the wind from your drift: put your back to it, and the low is on your right.

!!! example "Worked example: where is the low on a flight to Albany?"
    Given: flying from Perth to Albany, tracking roughly south, you need a large correction to hold track because the aeroplane is drifting to the **left**.

    1. Drift to the left means the wind is pushing you from the right. On a southerly track, your right is the west: the wind is **westerly**.
    2. Put your back to a westerly: you face **east**.
    3. Facing east, your right hand points **south**: the **low is to the south**, the high to the north.
    4. You are flying towards the low, so the pressure is **falling along your track**.

    With the old QNH still set, the altimeter will **over-read**: you will be lower than it shows. Update the QNH as you go, from the area forecast or the nearest station.

Try one before opening the solution: you are tracking east from Perth to Kalgoorlie and must point the nose well to the left of track, towards the north, to hold it. Where is the low, and will the pressure rise or fall along your track?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    Pointing the nose north to hold an easterly track means the wind is coming from the north: a **northerly**. Back to a northerly, you face south, and your right hand points **west**: the low is to the **west**, behind you, and the high to the east, ahead. Flying away from the low towards the high, the pressure **rises** along your track, so an old QNH makes the altimeter **under-read**: you are higher than it shows, the safe direction. A northerly over the south-west with a low to the west is the classic set-up ahead of a cold front ([PMTC 2.6](/notes/PMTC/2.6)).

The law is exact for the gradient wind above the friction layer. At the surface, as the next sections show, friction turns the wind partly across the isobars towards the low, so with your back to the *surface* wind the low is on your right and a little ahead of you.

??? check "Check yourself: in Perth, with your back to the wind, which hand points to the high pressure, and why?"
    The left. In the Southern Hemisphere air moves along the isobars with the low on its right, because Coriolis, acting to the left, balances the pressure gradient force pulling it towards the low. So the high is on the left.

## Gusts and squalls

The wind you feel on the ground never blows at a steady speed. Look at the windsock on a hot afternoon at Northam: it fills, sags, swings, fills again. Those short bursts are gusts, and they come from the same thing that makes the ride bumpy: **turbulence**, whether **mechanical** (air tumbling over rough ground, trees and buildings) or **thermal** (rising bubbles of heated air mixing faster air down from above).

A **gust** is a brief rise in wind speed, lasting only a few seconds, above the mean wind. In a TAF or METAR the gusts are reported when they exceed the mean speed by **10 kt or more** (AIP GEN 3.5; verify). The code adds a `G` and the gust speed to the wind group: `25015G27KT` is a mean wind from 250 degrees true at 15 kt with gusts to 27 kt. Gusts are the everyday case. They make the approach rough, they briefly change your airspeed (and so your margin over the stall), and they are why you add half the gust factor to the approach speed ([RMTC 2.3](/notes/RMTC/2.3)).

A **squall** is a different animal, and the difference is duration and size. A squall is a sudden increase in wind speed of at least **16 kt**, rising to **22 kt or more**, that lasts **at least a minute**, often with a sharp change of direction, a drop in temperature and heavy rain. A gust is a few seconds of turbulence in an otherwise steady wind; a squall is a new wind that arrives and stays for minutes. Squalls come with organised weather: the passage of a cold front, a line of thunderstorms (a squall line) and the gust front spreading out from beneath a storm. The weather code for a squall is `SQ`.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    A squall is not just "a big gust". The exam's distinguishing feature is **duration**: a gust lasts seconds, a squall lasts at least a minute. A 15 kt wind gusting 30 kt is a gusty wind, not a squall.

## Backing and veering

Wind direction is always named for where the wind blows **from**, in degrees: a wind of 270 degrees comes from the west and blows towards the east.

![Which way does a 270 degree wind blow?](diagram:wind-velocity-from "Wind is named for where it comes from: 270/15 blows from the west, towards the east.")

When the direction changes, there are two words for which way it changes, and they describe the movement round the compass, not the weather that causes it.

- **Backing**: the wind direction changes **anticlockwise**, to a smaller number of degrees. A wind going from 180 to 150 degrees, south to south-south-east, has backed.
- **Veering**: the wind direction changes **clockwise**, to a larger number of degrees. A wind going from 320 degrees to 010 degrees has veered, through north.

Crossing north is where people slip, so read it as movement round the dial: from 320 the wind moves clockwise past 360 to 010. That is a veer of 50 degrees, even though the number got smaller.

The definitions are the same in both hemispheres; what differs is *which one happens*. At a Southern Hemisphere cold front the wind backs, north-west to south-west; at a Northern Hemisphere one it veers. Learn the words as compass directions and work out the situation from the physics.

??? check "Check yourself: the Jandakot ATIS wind changes from 090 degrees to 040 degrees over an hour. Has it backed or veered?"
    Backed. From 090 to 040 the direction has moved anticlockwise round the compass, from east towards north-east, by 50 degrees.

## Surface wind and gradient wind

The balance between the pressure gradient force and Coriolis holds in the free air, high enough that the ground does not interfere. Close to the ground the air drags over paddocks, trees, buildings and hills, and that **friction** slows it. The layer in which friction matters is the **friction layer**, and over land it is roughly **2,000 to 3,000 ft** deep. Above it blows the **gradient wind**, parallel to the isobars. At the bottom of it, at the 10 m height of the windsock and the anemometer, blows the **surface wind**.

![How does friction change the wind near the ground?](diagram:wind-gradient-on-approach "Friction slows the wind close to the ground: it is weakest at the surface and reaches its full strength a few hundred feet up.")

Friction does more than slow the wind; it changes its direction. Reason it from first principles:

1. Friction **reduces the wind speed** near the ground.
2. Coriolis is proportional to speed, so it **weakens** too. The pressure gradient force does not depend on the wind speed at all, so it is **unchanged**.
3. The pressure gradient force now wins the tug of war, and the wind turns partly **across the isobars towards the low**.
4. In the Southern Hemisphere, with your back to the wind the low is on your right. Turning towards the low is turning **right**, which is **clockwise**.

So in the Southern Hemisphere the **surface wind is veered** from the gradient wind, and **lighter** than it. Turn it round and you have what happens as you climb: leaving the surface and climbing out of the friction layer, the wind **backs and increases** with height. The Bureau of Meteorology's gradient level wind guidance gives the same result.

How much depends on the surface. Rough land slows the air far more than open water, so the speed loss and the turn are both bigger over land.

| | Over land | Over the sea |
|---|---|---|
| Surface speed | About half to two-thirds of the gradient wind | About two-thirds to three-quarters |
| Direction | Veered about **30 degrees** | Veered about **10 to 15 degrees** |

These are rules of thumb, and the next section shows how much they change between day and night.

!!! example "Worked example: surface wind from the 2,000 ft wind over land"
    Given: the forecast 2,000 ft wind over the Wheatbelt is 320 degrees at 24 kt. Estimate the surface wind at Cunderdin.

    1. Direction: over land, veered about 30 degrees: $320° + 30° = \mathbf{350°}$.
    2. Speed: half to two-thirds of the gradient: $24 \times \tfrac{1}{2} = 12$ kt and $24 \times \tfrac{2}{3} = 16$ kt, so **12 to 16 kt**.

    Expect the windsock at Cunderdin to show about **350/12 to 350/16**, nearly northerly: a different crosswind on each runway from the one the 2,000 ft wind suggests.

Try one before opening the solution: the 2,000 ft wind off the coast near Rottnest is 200 degrees at 20 kt. What surface wind would you expect over the water?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    Over the sea the veer is about 10 to 15 degrees: $200° + 10°$ to $15°$ gives **210 to 215 degrees**. The speed is about two-thirds to three-quarters: $20 \times \tfrac{2}{3} \approx 13$ kt and $20 \times \tfrac{3}{4} = 15$ kt, so **13 to 15 kt**. Over smooth water the surface wind stays much closer to the gradient wind than over land.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Northern Hemisphere textbooks say the surface wind is **backed** from the gradient wind, and that answer will be among the options. Here the low is on the right, friction turns the wind towards the low, so the surface wind is **veered** and lighter. Climbing, it backs and strengthens.

??? check "Check yourself: climbing out of Jandakot in a 12 kt northerly, what do you expect the wind to do by 2,500 ft, and why?"
    Back towards the north-west and strengthen, to something like double the surface speed. Above the friction layer Coriolis is no longer weakened by drag, so the wind turns back anticlockwise to blow along the isobars at the full gradient speed.

## How the wind changes through the day

The friction layer is not a fixed thing. How well its air is stirred together with the faster air above depends on the temperature profile, and that changes every day with the sun. That is the **diurnal variation** of the wind.

**By day**, the sun heats the ground, the ground heats the air touching it, and the lowest layer becomes **unstable**: warm bubbles rise as thermals and cooler air sinks to replace them. The sinking air comes down from higher up, where the wind is faster and closer to the gradient direction. The thermals act as a mixer, bringing gradient-level momentum to the surface. So through the morning the surface wind **increases** and **backs** towards the gradient wind, and it becomes gusty, because the mixing is done by turbulent eddies. It is strongest and gustiest in the **early to mid afternoon**, when heating peaks.

**At night**, the ground cools by radiating heat to a clear sky, the air touching it is chilled, and a **radiation inversion** forms: a layer of cold, dense air under warmer air. Cold air under warm air is stable, the mixing stops, and the surface air is cut off from the wind above. With no supply of momentum from above and friction still acting, the surface wind **decreases** and **veers**, often to calm by dawn. It is lightest around **dawn**. Meanwhile the wind just above the inversion, no longer dragged on by the surface, can blow faster than it does by day: the **nocturnal low-level jet**, the source of the dawn wind shear in [RMTC 2.3](/notes/RMTC/2.3).

### What makes the daily swing bigger or smaller

The size of the swing depends on how strongly the day heats and the night cools, and on how well the air mixes anyway.

- **Heating and cooling.** Clear skies give strong sunshine by day and strong radiation at night, and so a large variation. An overcast sky blankets both and damps it.
- **The surface.** Over land the swing is large. Over the sea it is very small, because the sea's temperature hardly changes in a day.
- **The strength of the gradient.** A strong gradient wind stirs the lowest layers mechanically day and night, so no inversion can form and the surface wind stays close to its average.
- **Terrain and local winds.** Slopes, coastlines and valleys add their own daily winds on top: sea and land breezes, anabatic and katabatic winds.

??? check "Check yourself: which night gives the calmest dawn at Northam: clear with a weak gradient, or overcast with a strong gradient? Why?"
    Clear with a weak gradient. Clear skies let the ground cool fast and form a strong inversion, and a weak gradient cannot stir it away, so the surface air is cut off from the wind above and goes calm. Cloud and a strong gradient both keep the layer mixed.

## Local winds

The last of those factors deserves its own section, because in Western Australia the local winds often matter more than the gradient wind.

The **sea breeze** is driven by the difference in heating between land and sea. By day the land heats much faster than the sea, the air over the land warms and expands upwards, and the pressure pattern that results pushes cool sea air in at the surface, with a weak return flow out to sea a few thousand feet up. A typical sea breeze is **10 to 20 kt**, a few thousand feet deep, and reaches **20 to 50 km or more inland** by late afternoon. Its leading edge, the **sea breeze front**, is a line of convergence where the cool air undercuts the warm: often a line of cumulus, with wind shear and turbulence along it (the full mechanism is in [RMTC 2.1](/notes/RMTC/2.1)).

![What drives the sea breeze, and where is its front?](diagram:sea-breeze-front "Cool sea air pushes inland under the warm land air as a shallow wedge: the wind shift, the shear and any cumulus line are at its leading edge.")

The **land breeze** is the reverse at night: the land cools below the sea, and a weaker offshore breeze of about **5 to 10 kt** develops, strongest around dawn.

The **Fremantle Doctor** is Perth's summer sea breeze, a south-westerly that typically arrives on the coast in the late morning to mid-afternoon and drops the temperature by several degrees. On the hottest days the west coast **heat trough** lies near the coast with hot easterlies (more in [PMTC 2.6](/notes/PMTC/2.6)), the gradient easterly holds the sea breeze offshore, and the Doctor arrives late or not at all. When it does arrive, the change is abrupt: Jandakot and Rottnest often swing from a light easterly to a **15 to 20 kt south-westerly within minutes**. Expect a runway change and a new crosswind in the afternoon, and expect the wind on final to differ from the wind you planned for.

The slopes have their own daily breezes. By day, a slope heated by the sun warms the air in contact with it, and that air, lighter than the air at the same level away from the slope, flows **up** the slope: an **anabatic wind**, usually gentle. At night the slope cools the air in contact with it, which becomes dense and drains **down** the slope into the valleys: a **katabatic wind**. Katabatic air pools in valley floors as cold air, frost and fog, and at the foot of a long, steep slope it can blow strongly. The Avon valley's winter fogs owe something to air draining off the hills on either side.

??? check "Check yourself: why does a strong gradient easterly delay the Fremantle Doctor?"
    The sea breeze is a weak, shallow flow driven by the land-sea temperature difference. A strong opposing easterly holds it offshore until the afternoon heating builds the temperature difference enough to push it inland, and on the hottest days it may not make it at all.

## Bringing it together

A January day with a high in the Bight: back to the easterly, the low is on your right, to the north, and the high to the south, as the chart shows. At 0600 the Jandakot windsock hangs limp under a radiation inversion, but climbing out you meet a 25 kt easterly at about 800 ft, the low-level jet, with a sharp bump as you pass through the top of the inversion. By 1000 the inversion has burned off, thermals mix the easterly down, and the surface wind is 090 at 15 gusting 25, backed towards the gradient direction and gusty. At 1330 a line of small cumulus near the coast marks the sea breeze front; at 1400 the wind at Jandakot swings to a 15 kt south-westerly in a few minutes, and runway 24 comes into use. At dusk the Doctor fades, the ground cools, and the surface wind dies to calm again. One weather map, four surface winds, each predictable from the mechanism.

## Key points

- **2.5.1** The pressure gradient force pushes air from high to low across the isobars, harder where they are close; Coriolis deflects it to the **left** in the Southern Hemisphere until the wind blows along the isobars. Clockwise round a low, anticlockwise round a high. **Back to the wind, low on the right.**
- **2.5.2 (a)** A gust lasts seconds and comes from turbulence (reported at 10 kt or more above the mean); a squall is an increase of at least 16 kt to 22 kt or more lasting at least a minute, from fronts, squall lines or storm outflow.
- **2.5.2 (b)** Backing is an anticlockwise change of direction; veering is clockwise. Same definitions in both hemispheres.
- **2.5.3** Friction slows the surface wind, Coriolis weakens, and the wind turns towards the low: in the Southern Hemisphere the surface wind is **veered and lighter**: over land about 30 degrees and half to two-thirds, over the sea about 10 to 15 degrees and two-thirds to three-quarters. Climbing, the wind backs and increases.
- **2.5.4** By day thermal mixing makes the surface wind stronger, backed and gusty, strongest in the early to mid afternoon; at night an inversion makes it lighter and veered, lightest around dawn. The swing is largest with clear skies, over land and with a weak gradient; local winds (sea and land breezes, the Fremantle Doctor, anabatic and katabatic flows) add their own daily cycle.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Every wind question has a Northern Hemisphere distractor. Have the Australian answers by heart: **clockwise round a low, anticlockwise round a high; back to the wind, low on the right; surface wind veered and lighter than the gradient wind (about 30 degrees and half to two-thirds over land); the wind backs at a cold front.** Backing and veering are compass directions only: anticlockwise and clockwise. For the daily cycle: strongest and gustiest in the early to mid afternoon, lightest around dawn.
