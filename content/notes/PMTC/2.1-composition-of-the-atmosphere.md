## What the exam expects (PMTC 2.1)

- **2.1.1** Give the sea level temperature and pressure of the International Standard Atmosphere (ISA).
- **2.1.2** Give the ISA rates at which temperature and pressure fall with height in the troposphere.
- **2.1.3** Describe how the atmosphere is divided vertically into the troposphere, the tropopause and the stratosphere.
- **2.1.4** Explain why nearly all weather happens below the stratosphere.

## Why this matters

Every number on your aeroplane's performance charts, every altimeter reading and every density height you calculate is measured against one imaginary day: the International Standard Atmosphere. When the ATIS at Jandakot gives 34 degrees on a summer afternoon, the reason your take-off roll is long is that the real day is far warmer than that standard day, and you can only say how much warmer if you know the standard by heart. The same lesson explains why a February thunderstorm over the wheatbelt grows to a flat anvil top and stops, and why the airliners cruising above it sit in smooth, clear air. The exam asks for the ISA figures exactly, for an ISA temperature or deviation at a given height, and for the reasons the weather lives in the lowest layer. You met the outline of all this in the RPL lessons ([RMTC 2.1](/notes/RMTC/2.1)); here you learn the reference figures and the physics behind them.

## What the air is made of

Take a breath at Rottnest and another at 10,000 ft over the Stirling Range. The air in both is the same mixture. Dry air is about **78 percent nitrogen**, **21 percent oxygen** and 1 percent other gases, mostly argon with a small amount of carbon dioxide. Winds and turbulence stir the lower atmosphere so thoroughly that these proportions stay the same up to well above any height a light aeroplane can reach.

What changes with height is the **pressure**: the total weight of air pressing down from above. Fewer molecules are packed into each litre of air at altitude, so each breath holds fewer oxygen molecules even though oxygen is still 21 percent of the mix. That is the whole hypoxia story, told in [PHFC 2.11](/notes/PHFC/2.11) and [PHFC 2.12](/notes/PHFC/2.12).

![How do pressure and oxygen change as you climb?](diagram:atmosphere-pressure-oxygen-vs-altitude "Oxygen stays about 21 percent of the air at every height, but total pressure, and so the oxygen in each breath, halves by about 18,000 ft.")

The one gas whose share does change is **water vapour**: water in its invisible gas form, not droplets. Over the dry interior there is almost none; in hot, humid tropical air it can make up about **4 percent** of the air by volume. Nearly all of it sits in the lowest few kilometres, for two reasons. It comes from evaporation at the surface, from the ocean, lakes, wet ground and plants. And as air carrying it is lifted, it cools, and the vapour condenses out as cloud and falls back as rain long before it can reach great heights.

Water vapour matters out of all proportion to its amount. It is the raw material of every cloud, fog, shower and thunderstorm. When it condenses it releases the heat it absorbed when it evaporated, and that heat is the fuel that drives thunderstorms and tropical cyclones (the mechanism is in [PMTC 2.2](/notes/PMTC/2.2)).

The air also carries **solid particles**: dust from the wheatbelt and the interior, salt from sea spray along the coast, smoke from bushfires and burn-offs, and pollen. They do two jobs. They give water vapour something to condense onto, so they are the **condensation nuclei** at the heart of every cloud droplet. And when there are enough of them they scatter light and reduce visibility, which is the haze and smoke story of [PMTC 2.4](/notes/PMTC/2.4).

??? check "Check yourself: at 10,000 ft, is oxygen a smaller percentage of the air than at sea level?"
    No. Oxygen is still about 21 percent of the air. What has fallen is the total pressure, so each breath contains fewer molecules of everything, including oxygen.

## Why we need a standard atmosphere

The real atmosphere is different every day and every hour. On a winter morning at Albany the air at the surface might be 8 degrees and the pressure 1028 hPa; on a summer afternoon at Meekatharra it might be 42 degrees and 1004 hPa. An altimeter, which measures pressure and displays height, has to be built to some rule that turns pressure into feet. A performance chart has to give take-off distances for some baseline condition. Two pilots comparing their aeroplanes' climb rates need to know what day they are comparing.

So the aviation world agreed on one imaginary, average atmosphere and calibrated everything to it: the **ICAO International Standard Atmosphere**, or **ISA**. It is a mathematical model of how temperature, pressure and density change with height on an average day in the mid-latitudes. It is a **reference, not a forecast**. No real day matches it exactly, and that is the point: once you know the standard, you can describe any real day by how far it differs from it.

That difference is the **ISA deviation**. A day that is 10 degrees warmer than the standard at a given height is called **ISA +10**; one that is 5 degrees colder is **ISA −5**. You will use ISA deviation for density height and true airspeed in [PMTC 2.2](/notes/PMTC/2.2) and the PAKC and navigation units, and performance charts often have a separate line for each ISA deviation.

## The ISA at sea level (2.1.1)

The ISA starts at mean sea level with these values:

| ISA value | Figure |
|---|---|
| Mean sea level pressure | **1013.25 hPa** (29.92 inHg) |
| Mean sea level temperature | **+15 °C** |
| Mean sea level density | 1.225 kg/m³ |

The pressure, 1013.25 hPa, is the weight of the whole column of air above each square metre of the sea. It is why the altimeter subscale is set to 1013 (more exactly 1013.2) when you fly on **flight levels**: everyone is then measuring against the same standard pressure, whatever the real sea level pressure is. The QNH you hear on an ATIS is a real day's sea level pressure, scattered either side of that standard. In the exam, quote the figure exactly: 1013.25 hPa and +15 °C.

The temperature, +15 °C, is a sensible average of mid-latitude surface temperatures. A mild 15 degree day at Jandakot, which is close to sea level, is close to an ISA day; a 40 degree February afternoon there is around ISA +25.

The density, 1.225 kg per cubic metre, follows from the pressure and temperature. You will not be asked to calculate with it, but it is the number the aeroplane actually cares about: lift, drag, engine power and propeller thrust all depend on how many kilograms of air pass over and through the aeroplane each second.

## How temperature falls with height (2.1.2)

Climb out of Jandakot on any day and the air gets colder as you go up. That seems backwards at first: you are getting closer to the sun. The reason is that the sun barely heats the air directly. Sunlight passes through clear air and is absorbed by the **ground**, and the ground then heats the air touching it. The troposphere is a pot heated from the bottom, so the warmest air is at the bottom.

A second effect adds to it. When warm air near the ground rises, it moves into lower pressure, expands, and cools as it expands. Air at height is both further from the heat source and has been cooled by expansion on the way up. The rate at which temperature falls with height is a **lapse rate**.

The ISA uses a single average lapse rate through the whole troposphere: **1.98 °C per 1,000 ft**, which is 6.5 °C per kilometre. For mental arithmetic, and in nearly every exam question, use **2 °C per 1,000 ft**. So the ISA temperature at any height in the troposphere is:

$$T_{\text{ISA}} = 15 - 2 \times \frac{h}{1{,}000}$$

where $h$ is the height in feet and the answer is in degrees Celsius.

!!! example "Worked example: ISA temperature at 8,500 ft"
    Given: height 8,500 ft, ISA lapse rate 2 °C per 1,000 ft.

    1. Temperature fall: $8.5 \times 2 = \mathbf{17\ °C}$.
    2. ISA temperature: $15 - 17 = \mathbf{-2\ °C}$.

    On a standard day you would cruise at 8,500 ft just below freezing. Any real day is described by how far it differs from that −2.

!!! example "Worked example: ISA deviation at 6,000 ft"
    Given: height 6,000 ft, outside air temperature +9 °C.

    1. ISA temperature at 6,000 ft: $15 - 6 \times 2 = 15 - 12 = \mathbf{+3\ °C}$.
    2. Deviation: $9 - 3 = \mathbf{+6}$, so the day is **ISA +6**.

    Warmer than ISA means lower density than the charts' standard day: longer take-off rolls and a poorer climb than the ISA figures promise.

Now try one where the day is colder than standard. Cruising at 11,500 ft over the Goldfields on a winter morning, the outside air temperature gauge reads −12 °C. What is the ISA deviation?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    ISA temperature at 11,500 ft $= 15 - 11.5 \times 2 = 15 - 23 = -8$ °C. Deviation $= -12 - (-8) = -4$, so the day is **ISA −4**. Subtract the ISA value from the actual value and keep the sign: an actual temperature below ISA gives a negative deviation, and colder-than-standard air is denser, so the aeroplane performs slightly better than the book.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The exam often offers **3 °C per 1,000 ft** as the ISA lapse rate. That is the **dry adiabatic** lapse rate, the rate at which a rising parcel of unsaturated air cools ([PMTC 2.2](/notes/PMTC/2.2)). The ISA lapse rate is 1.98, about 2 °C per 1,000 ft. Two different numbers for two different things: one describes the average atmosphere, the other describes a bubble of air moving through it.

## How pressure falls with height (2.1.2)

Pressure at any level is simply the weight of all the air above it. Climb 1,000 ft and you leave 1,000 ft of air below you, so there is less air above pressing down. Pressure always falls with height, on every day, everywhere.

Near the surface the fall is about **1 hPa for every 30 ft**. More exactly, at sea level 1 hPa is about **27 ft**, and the number of feet per hectopascal grows as you go higher. That 30 ft per hPa figure is the one you use for every altimetry and pressure height calculation in the exam, and you will meet it again and again in [PMTC 2.2](/notes/PMTC/2.2).

Why does the figure change with height? Because air is **compressible**. The air near the bottom of the column is squashed by the weight of everything above it, so it is dense: a thin slice of it holds a lot of mass. Higher up, the air is less squashed and less dense, so you must climb through a thicker slice to leave the same weight of air below you. The pressure fall per foot gets smaller as you climb, and the feet per hectopascal get larger.

The result is that the pressure fall is **not linear**. Pressure roughly **halves every 18,000 ft**: about 500 hPa at 18,000 ft and about 300 hPa at 30,000 ft. Half the mass of the atmosphere lies below 18,000 ft.

!!! example "Worked example: where the 30 ft rule stops working"
    Given: ISA sea level pressure 1013 hPa, rule of thumb 30 ft per hPa.

    1. Pressure fall from sea level to 1,200 ft by the rule: $1{,}200 \div 30 = \mathbf{40\ hPa}$, so about 973 hPa. Close to the true ISA value.
    2. Pressure fall from sea level to 18,000 ft by the rule: $18{,}000 \div 30 = \mathbf{600\ hPa}$, which would give $1013 - 600 = \mathbf{413\ hPa}$.
    3. True ISA pressure at 18,000 ft: about **500 hPa**.

    The linear rule is fine for the few thousand feet that matter for altimeter settings and aerodrome pressure heights, and badly wrong at high altitude. Use it near the ground, where the exam uses it.

??? check "Check yourself: why does the pressure fall more slowly per 1,000 ft at 15,000 ft than near sea level?"
    Because air is compressible. Near the surface it is squashed by the weight above and is dense, so a thin layer contains a lot of air; higher up the air is thinner, so you must climb further to leave the same weight of air below you.

## The layers of the atmosphere (2.1.3)

If you could take a thermometer straight up from Perth, you would see the temperature fall steadily for about 36,000 ft, then stop falling, stay steady for a while, and then begin to rise. Those changes in the way temperature behaves with height are what divide the atmosphere into layers. The layers are defined by **temperature**, not by height.

### The troposphere

The **troposphere** is the lowest layer, from the surface up to the tropopause, and it is where you will do all your flying. Temperature generally **decreases** with height through it, for the reason you have just seen: it is heated from below. It holds about three-quarters of the mass of the whole atmosphere and nearly all its water vapour.

Its depth is not fixed. It is deepest where the surface is hottest, because strong heating drives powerful convection that lifts the top of the layer:

- about **8 km (26,000 ft) over the poles**;
- about **11 km (36,000 ft) in mid-latitudes** such as Perth;
- up to **16 to 18 km (52,000 to 59,000 ft) over the equator**.

For the same reason it is higher in summer than in winter. A thunderstorm over the Kimberley in the wet season can therefore grow far taller than a winter storm off Albany.

### The tropopause

The **tropopause** is the boundary at the top of the troposphere, where the temperature stops falling. In the ISA it sits at **36,090 ft** (11 km), with a temperature of **−56.5 °C**. You can check that figure from the lapse rate: 36.09 thousand feet at 1.98 degrees per thousand is a fall of about 71.5 degrees, and 15 minus 71.5 is −56.5.

The tropopause behaves like a **lid**. Air rising through the troposphere arrives at a layer where the surrounding air is no longer getting colder, and loses its buoyancy. A growing thunderstorm cell hits that lid and spreads out sideways, which is why a mature cumulonimbus has a flat **anvil** top that can stretch tens of kilometres downwind. Only the strongest updraughts punch a short way above it as an overshooting top.

The tropopause is not a smooth, continuous surface. It steps down from the high tropical tropopause to the lower polar one, and at the breaks between them are found the **jet streams**, narrow ribbons of very strong wind, and **clear air turbulence**. Those are for high-flying aircraft, but the exam may ask where they live.

### The stratosphere

The **stratosphere** lies above the tropopause to about 50 km. In its lower part the temperature is constant, and the ISA assumes **−56.5 °C** all the way from the tropopause to about **65,600 ft** (20 km). Higher up, temperature **increases** with height, because the ozone layer absorbs ultraviolet radiation from the sun and is heated directly.

The stratosphere is very dry, because the water vapour froze or rained out long before reaching it, and it is very **stable**: a layer in which temperature stays constant or rises with height resists any vertical movement. Above the stratosphere are the mesosphere and the thermosphere, which are not in the PPL syllabus.

| Layer | Temperature with height | Water vapour | Character |
|---|---|---|---|
| Troposphere | Falls, about 2 °C per 1,000 ft | Nearly all of it | Convection, cloud, weather |
| Tropopause | Stops falling | Very little | The lid; anvils, jet streams |
| Stratosphere | Constant, then rising | Almost none | Stable, dry, clear |

![How does temperature change through the layers of the atmosphere?](diagram:atmosphere-layers-and-tropopause "What to notice: temperature falls about 2 degrees per 1,000 ft to −56.5 °C at the tropopause, then stops falling: that lid is where a Cb spreads into its anvil.")

??? check "Check yourself: why is the tropopause higher over Darwin than over Hobart?"
    Because the surface near the equator is heated far more strongly. The vigorous convection that results carries warm air higher and lifts the top of the troposphere, to 16 to 18 km over the equator against about 11 km in mid-latitudes and 8 km over the poles.

## Why the weather lives below the stratosphere (2.1.4)

Weather is mostly the story of air going up and coming down: rising, cooling, making cloud and rain, and sinking again. Three facts about the troposphere make that possible, and the exam may ask for any of them.

**The water is there.** Almost all the atmosphere's water vapour is in the troposphere, close to its source at the surface. With no moisture there can be no cloud and no precipitation, however much the air moves. The stratosphere is a desert.

**The troposphere is heated from below.** Warm air underneath cooler air is top-heavy. A bubble of warm air near the ground is lighter than the air around it and rises, the way a hot-air balloon does, while cooler air sinks to take its place. That overturning, called **convection**, keeps the troposphere constantly mixing, lifting moist air to where it cools and condenses. The name says it: *tropos* is Greek for turning.

**The stratosphere is stable.** A layer in which temperature does not fall, or rises, with height is an **inversion**, and an inversion is the most stable arrangement the air can have. Push a parcel of air up into it and the parcel cools as it rises, while the air around it does not get colder. The parcel ends up colder and heavier than its surroundings and sinks back to where it started. No vertical motion means no cloud and no weather. Rising air stops at the tropopause, so cloud tops (except the strongest storm overshoots) stop there too.

You will meet the same idea, stability decided by how the air around a rising parcel changes with height, near the ground in [PMTC 2.2](/notes/PMTC/2.2): a morning inversion over the Avon valley traps fog and smoke under its lid in exactly the way the tropopause caps a thunderstorm.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    "The stratosphere has no weather because it is too cold" is the distractor. The stratosphere is no colder than the top of the troposphere. The reasons are that it is **dry** and **stable**: there is no water to make cloud, and the temperature structure stops air rising.

??? check "Check yourself: a parcel of air is pushed up into a layer where temperature is constant with height. What happens to it, and why?"
    It sinks back. Rising, the parcel expands and cools, but the surrounding air is no colder higher up, so the parcel becomes colder and denser than its surroundings and returns to its original level. That is a stable layer, which is why the stratosphere has almost no weather.

## Bringing it together

A February afternoon, and you are planning a flight from Jandakot to Kalgoorlie at 8,500 ft. The forecast gives +12 °C at 8,500 ft. The ISA temperature there is 15 minus 17, so −2 °C, and the day is **ISA +14**: the air is thinner than on the standard day, so expect less power and a slower climb than the standard-day figures for that height. Ahead, over the eastern wheatbelt, a cumulonimbus is building. Its top has spread into a wide anvil well above your height, because it has reached the summer tropopause and cannot go higher: everything above that lid is dry, stable stratosphere. The cloud itself, the rain shaft and the gust front beneath it are all in the troposphere, where the water vapour is and where heating from the ground drives the air upwards. Every part of that picture is this lesson: the ISA to describe the day, the lapse rate to work out the deviation, and the layers to explain why the storm looks the way it does.

??? check "Check yourself: the OAT at 4,000 ft is 22 °C. Is the air there more or less dense than the ISA standard, and roughly by how many degrees is it off standard?"
    Less dense. The ISA temperature at 4,000 ft is 15 minus 8, so 7 °C; the actual 22 °C is ISA +15, and warmer air at the same pressure is less dense, so the aeroplane performs worse than its ISA figures.

## Key points

- **2.1.1** ISA at mean sea level: **1013.25 hPa** (29.92 inHg) and **+15 °C** (density 1.225 kg/m³). It is an agreed average used to calibrate altimeters and performance charts, not a forecast; a real day is described by its ISA deviation.
- **2.1.2** ISA temperature falls **1.98 °C per 1,000 ft**, use **2 °C**, to **−56.5 °C** at the tropopause; ISA temperature $= 15 - 2 \times$ (height in thousands of feet). Pressure falls about **1 hPa per 30 ft** near the surface (27 ft at sea level), more slowly higher up, roughly halving by 18,000 ft.
- **2.1.3** Troposphere: temperature falls with height, nearly all the water, about 8 km deep at the poles, 11 km in mid-latitudes, 16 to 18 km at the equator, higher in summer. Tropopause: where the fall stops, ISA **36,090 ft at −56.5 °C**, the lid that flattens anvils. Stratosphere: temperature constant then rising, dry, stable.
- **2.1.4** Weather lives in the troposphere because the water vapour is there and heating from below drives convection; the stratosphere is a dry inversion that stops air rising.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Quote the ISA exactly: 1013.25 hPa, +15 °C, 1.98 °C per 1,000 ft (2 °C for arithmetic), tropopause 36,090 ft at −56.5 °C. The usual distractor is 3 °C per 1,000 ft, which is the dry adiabatic lapse rate. For ISA temperature questions, work out $15 - 2 \times$ (height in thousands) and then subtract it from the actual temperature, keeping the sign. For "why is there no weather in the stratosphere", choose the answer that mentions the lack of water vapour, stability or an inversion.
