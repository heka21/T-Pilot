## What the exam expects (PMTC 2.6)

- **2.6.1** Describe the typical flying weather that comes with (a) a cold front, (b) a warm front, (c) a wave depression, (d) an occluded front, (e) a tropical cyclone and (f) the equatorial trough.
- **2.6.2** Describe that flying weather under four headings: (a) temperature, warmer or colder; (b) the wind change, backing or veering, stronger or weaker; (c) stability and turbulence; (d) the type and rough amount of cloud, and the precipitation.

## Why this matters

Every few days through a Perth winter a cold front sweeps in from the Indian Ocean: a freshening north-westerly and lowering cloud, then squalls and showers, then a cold, gusty afternoon with the Darling Scarp in and out of cloud. In summer the weather turns on the west coast trough instead, and if you fly north to the Pilbara you meet the monsoon trough and the cyclone season. As a private pilot planning a flight to Albany or Geraldton you will read these systems off the synoptic chart and need to know what each one will do to your day. The exam asks you to describe their flying weather using four headings, and every wind question has a Northern Hemisphere distractor waiting for you. This lesson builds each system from the air masses it separates, so that you can work out the weather rather than memorise it.

## Air masses: where the weather comes from

Air that sits for days over a large, uniform surface, a desert, a warm sea, the cold Southern Ocean, takes on that surface's temperature and moisture. The result is an **air mass**: a body of air hundreds to thousands of kilometres across with roughly uniform temperature and humidity. The place where it acquires those properties is its **source region**.

Air masses carry two labels: **maritime** (m, formed over sea, moist) or **continental** (c, over land, dry), and **tropical** (T, warm) or **polar** (P, cold). Three shape Australian weather.

| Air mass | Source | Typical Australian weather |
|---|---|---|
| Continental tropical (cT) | The inland in summer | Hot, dry, dusty; high-based Cu and dry storms; big diurnal temperature range |
| Maritime tropical (mT) | Coral Sea, Timor Sea, Indian Ocean | Warm, humid; the wet season; low cloud and drizzle when cooled, storms when heated |
| Maritime polar (mP) | The Southern Ocean | Cool, moist; arrives behind cold fronts, mostly May to October in southern WA |

An air mass does not keep its properties for ever. As it moves it is changed by the surface beneath it, and the key change is to its **stability**. Recall from [PMTC 2.2](/notes/PMTC/2.2) that air is **unstable** when a parcel pushed upwards stays warmer than its surroundings and keeps rising on its own, and **stable** when a lifted parcel ends up colder than its surroundings and sinks back.

**Cold air moving over a warmer surface** is heated from below. The bottom of the column warms while the top stays cold, so the temperature falls quickly with height and the air becomes **unstable**. Thermals rise freely and build **cumulus**, which grows into showers; the vertical mixing gives **gusts** and **thermal turbulence**, and because the mixing carries dust and haze away upwards, the **visibility is good** between showers. Southern Ocean air crossing the warmer sea to reach Albany or Perth in winter is the classic unstable, showery stream.

**Warm air moving over a colder surface** is cooled from below. The bottom of the column becomes colder than the air above it, which is an **inversion**, and the air becomes **stable**. Nothing rises, so the cloud forms in flat layers: **stratus** and **drizzle** where the cooling reaches the dew point, and **fog** at the surface. Smoke, haze and moisture are trapped in the cool layer, so the **visibility is poor** and the air is smooth.

Hold onto that pair: most of the rest of this lesson is that pair applied to different situations.

??? check "Check yourself: warm, humid tropical air drifts south over the cool winter sea off the Gascoyne coast. What weather do you expect in it, and why?"
    Stable weather: low stratus, drizzle and possibly sea fog, with poor visibility and smooth air. The air is cooled from below by the colder sea, which forms an inversion at its base, so nothing rises and the moisture condenses in flat layers near the surface.

## A front, and the four questions to ask of it

Where two air masses meet they do not mix easily, because they differ in density: cold air is heavier than warm. The boundary between them is a **front**, and it slopes, with the cold air lying under the warm air as a wedge. Along the front the warm air is forced upwards, and lifted air cools, condenses and makes cloud and rain. That is why fronts are where the weather is.

Fronts lie in **troughs**, elongated extensions of low pressure, so the isobars kink sharply at the front. That kink is what makes the wind change as a front passes, and you can work out which way it changes from the circulation you learnt in [PMTC 2.5](/notes/PMTC/2.5). In the Southern Hemisphere air flows clockwise round a low, with the low pressure on its right. On the forward side of a trough the isobars run roughly north-south and the wind blows along them from the north or north-west; on the rear side they turn and the wind blows from the south-west. As the trough passes over you, the wind swings from north-west to south-west: **anticlockwise**, which is **backing**. In the Southern Hemisphere the wind **backs** as a front or trough passes. Northern Hemisphere textbooks say it veers, because there the flow round a low is anticlockwise.

The syllabus asks you to describe each system's **flying weather** under four headings, and those four headings are the questions to ask of any front:

1. **Temperature.** Which air mass replaces which? If colder air arrives behind it, it gets colder; if warmer, warmer.
2. **Wind.** Does it back or veer as the system passes, and does it strengthen or weaken? A tight kink in the isobars means a sharp, strong change.
3. **Stability and turbulence.** Is the lifted air stable or unstable, and is the air behind it heated or cooled from below?
4. **Cloud and precipitation.** How fast is the warm air lifted? **Fast, steep lifting** makes **cumuliform** cloud, Cu, TCu and Cb, with **showers**: heavy and short. **Slow, gentle lifting** over a large area makes **layered** (stratiform) cloud, Ci, Cs, As, Ns and St, with **continuous** rain or drizzle: lighter and long-lasting.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    "The wind veers as a cold front passes" is the Northern Hemisphere answer and it will be among the options. In Australia the front lies in a trough, the flow round it is clockwise, and the wind **backs**: north-west to south-west at a cold front.

## Cold fronts

A **cold front** is the leading edge of cold air advancing into warmer air. The cold air is dense and moves along the ground, so it pushes under the warm air like a snowplough and lifts it steeply. The frontal surface slopes at about **1 in 50 to 1 in 100**: for every 50 to 100 units you go horizontally from the surface front, back into the cold air, the boundary rises by one. In southern WA cold fronts sweep in from the west, mostly from autumn to spring.

Picture it in cross-section, moving west to east: warm air ahead, the cold wedge driving in under it at the surface front, and the frontal surface climbing above the cold air behind.

![What happens as a cold front passes?](diagram:cold-front-cross-section "What to notice: the steep wedge lifts the warm air fast into a narrow band of Cb, and the wind backs from north-west to south-west as the front goes through.")

!!! example "Worked example: how high is a cold frontal surface 50 nm behind the surface front?"
    Given: a cold front with a slope of 1 in 50, and 1 nm is about 6,000 ft.

    1. Height of the frontal surface at 50 nm: $50 \div 50 = \mathbf{1\ nm}$.
    2. In feet: $1 \times 6{,}000 \approx \mathbf{6{,}000\ ft}$.

    Within 50 nm the boundary climbs from the ground to about 6,000 ft, which is why the cloud and weather of a cold front are packed into a narrow band.

Now work through the four questions in the order you meet them.

**Ahead of the front.** The pressure falls and the isobars tighten. The wind is **north-westerly and strengthening**, often hot and dusty in summer and autumn. Lifted warm air spreads ahead as **As and Ac**, with some Cu, and a **pre-frontal trough** can trigger storms of its own.

**At the front.** The steep lifting of warm, moist air gives a narrow band of **Cu, TCu and Cb**: heavy showers, thunderstorms and sometimes hail. The wind changes suddenly, often in a **squall**, with **moderate to severe turbulence** and **wind shear** where the two airstreams meet. The temperature drops sharply.

**Behind the front.** The air is now **maritime polar**, **colder** than what it replaced, and the wind has **backed** to the **south-west**. That cold air is crossing a warmer sea and then warmer land, so it is heated from below and **unstable**: scattered Cu and showers, **gusts and thermal turbulence**, and **good visibility** between showers. The pressure rises.

| | Ahead | At the front | Behind |
|---|---|---|---|
| Temperature | Warm, often hot north-westerlies | Sharp drop | **Colder** |
| Wind | **North-westerly, strengthening** as the front approaches | Squalls; sudden change | **South-westerly**: the wind has **backed** |
| Stability, turbulence | Pre-frontal trough may trigger storms | Moderate to severe turbulence, wind shear | Unstable air: gusts and thermal turbulence |
| Cloud and precipitation | As, Ac, some Cu | A narrow band of Cu, TCu and **Cb**: heavy showers, thunderstorms, hail | Scattered Cu and showers, **good visibility** between them |

The band of bad weather is narrow, often **50 nm or less**, but intense and fast-moving. The thunderstorms in it carry all the hazards of any storm: severe turbulence, hail, lightning, icing in the cloud and microbursts beneath it, and they may be hidden in the frontal cloud (thunderstorm flying in [PMTC 2.7](/notes/PMTC/2.7)).

![What makes a thunderstorm dangerous, and how far away should you stay?](diagram:thunderstorm-hazards "The Cb in a cold front carries every storm hazard, and the gust front and microburst reach well beyond the cloud.")

For a VFR pilot the worst of it is low cloud. As the moist westerly behind the front rides up the Darling Scarp and the hills of the south-west, it cools further and the **cloud bases lower** on the high ground, exactly where the terrain rises to meet them. A VFR pilot should not try to cross a front at low level, and should not try to sneak under the post-frontal cloud over the hills.

![Why turn back early when the cloud base is lowering?](diagram:low-cloud-and-rising-terrain "Behind a front the base lowers on the hills just as the ground rises: turn back while there is room.")

??? check "Check yourself: why is the weather behind a winter cold front showery and gusty but with good visibility?"
    The cold Southern Ocean air behind the front is heated from below by the warmer sea and land, which makes it unstable. Thermals build Cu that grow into showers and mix gusts down to the surface, and the same mixing carries haze and dust away upwards, so the air between showers is clear.

## Warm fronts

A **warm front** is the leading edge of warm air advancing into retreating cold air. Warm air is lighter, so it cannot push the cold air out of the way. Instead it **rides up over** the cold wedge on a very shallow slope, about **1 in 150**. The lifting is slow and spread over a huge area, so the cloud is **layered** and the weather extends hundreds of nautical miles ahead of the surface front.

!!! example "Worked example: how far ahead of a warm front is the frontal surface at 12,000 ft?"
    Given: a warm front with a slope of 1 in 150, and 1 nm is about 6,000 ft.

    1. 12,000 ft in nautical miles: $12{,}000 \div 6{,}000 = \mathbf{2\ nm}$.
    2. Horizontal distance for a rise of 2 nm: $2 \times 150 = \mathbf{300\ nm}$.

    Cloud forming on the frontal surface at 12,000 ft lies about 300 nm ahead of the surface front: the first high cloud of a warm front can appear a day before it arrives.

That shallow slope gives a warm front its signature: a sequence of cloud that lowers and thickens as the front approaches. First comes **cirrus** (Ci), wisps of ice cloud high on the frontal surface; then **cirrostratus** (Cs), a thin veil that gives a **halo** round the sun or moon; then **altostratus** (As), a grey sheet through which the sun looks as if through frosted glass; then **nimbostratus** (Ns), thick, dark and raining, with ragged **stratus fractus** beneath it. The base lowers steadily the whole time.

![Why does warm-front cloud arrive a day early?](diagram:warm-front-cross-section "What to notice: on a 1 in 150 slope the frontal surface is still about 12,000 ft up some 300 nm ahead, so the cloud lowers from Ci to Ns over a day, with rain and frontal fog at the end.")

Ask the four questions:

- **Cloud and precipitation.** The sequence Ci, Cs, As, Ns, with long periods of **continuous rain** from the As and Ns. The rain falling into the cold air beneath saturates it, so visibility is poor and **frontal fog** can form ahead of the front.
- **Stability and turbulence.** The warm air is usually **stable** as it is lifted, so the ride is mostly **smooth**. There is moderate **icing** in the As and Ns above the freezing level. If the warm air is unstable, though, **Cb can be embedded** in the layer cloud, hidden from view in the grey: the one ugly surprise a warm front can spring.
- **Temperature.** **Warmer** after the front passes, because warm air has replaced cold.
- **Wind.** Ahead of the front, **northerly**; after it passes, **north-westerly**. North to north-west is anticlockwise: the wind **backs**, and it is often lighter after passage.

True warm fronts are uncommon over the Australian mainland but common over the Southern Ocean; in WA you are more likely to meet the warm-front sequence ahead of a wave or an occlusion.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    A warm front is not "mild weather". Its rain and fog can hold the visibility below VMC for hours and it can hide Cb: often a longer no-go than a cold front, which passes in an hour or two.

??? check "Check yourself: why does warm-front cloud spread hundreds of miles ahead of the surface front, while cold-front cloud is a narrow band?"
    The warm front's slope is very shallow, about 1 in 150, so the frontal surface is still at cloud level hundreds of miles ahead and the lifting is slow and widespread, making layer cloud. The cold front's slope is steep, 1 in 50 to 1 in 100, so all the lifting happens in a narrow zone and makes cumuliform cloud.

## Wave depressions and occlusions

### The wave depression

Picture a long, slow-moving front with cold polar air on its poleward side and warm tropical air on its equator side, the two flowing past each other. A small disturbance puts a kink in the front, a **wave**. At the tip of the kink the pressure falls and a **low** forms, the air begins to circulate round it, and the circulation pushes the warm air poleward on the forward side and the cold air towards the equator on the rear side. The front is now in two parts: a **warm front** ahead of the low and a **cold front** behind it, with a tongue of warm air between them, the **warm sector**. That system is a **wave depression**.

As it passes, a place on the warm-sector side of the low gets the whole sequence of flying weather in order:

1. **The warm front's weather**: lowering layer cloud and continuous rain.
2. **The warm sector**: warm, humid, **stable** maritime air, often cooled from below by the sea, so **low St, drizzle and poor visibility**, with light turbulence.
3. **The cold front's weather**: the band of Cu and Cb, squalls and showers.
4. **The clearing, showery cold air** behind.

The wind **backs** at each front. The system **deepens** as it moves east: the pressure at its centre falls, the isobars tighten and the winds strengthen.

### The occluded front

Cold fronts move faster than warm fronts, because the dense cold air drives along the ground while the warm air has to climb. As the wave matures, the cold front catches up with the warm front, starting near the low, and the warm sector between them is lifted off the ground. Where that has happened the front is an **occluded front**, or **occlusion**, and it is the sign of a mature or decaying low.

![How does a wave become an occlusion?](diagram:wave-depression-life-cycle "What to notice: the low forms at the poleward tip of the kink, the air goes clockwise round it, and the faster cold front catches the warm front near the low and lifts the warm sector off the ground.")

An occlusion combines the weather of both fronts. It has the **widespread layer cloud and continuous rain** of a warm front, with **embedded Cb** and turbulence from the lifted warm sector, like a cold front, plus **low cloud, poor visibility and icing**. Because the warm air is now aloft, two cold air masses meet at the surface, so the **temperature change at the surface is smaller** than at either front alone. The wind **backs** as it passes.

The defining hazard is the thunderstorm you cannot see: embedded Cb hidden in a wide area of layer cloud and rain. Occlusions are a poor choice for VFR flight.

## Tropical cyclones

A **tropical cyclone** is an intense low that forms over warm tropical seas. It runs on the heat released when water vapour, evaporated from the warm sea, condenses in towering cloud: the heat warms the column, the surface pressure falls further and more moist air converges. To get going it needs:

- a sea surface of about **26.5 degrees** or warmer, to supply the moisture and heat;
- a position at least about **5 degrees from the equator**, because the rotation needs the Coriolis effect, which is zero at the equator ([PMTC 2.5](/notes/PMTC/2.5));
- a disturbance to start it, usually in the monsoon trough.

The WA season is **November to April**, and the **Pilbara coast** is the most cyclone-prone coast in Australia.

Its flying weather under the four headings:

- **Wind.** It rotates **clockwise** in the Southern Hemisphere, like any low here. Sustained winds are **gale force (34 kt) or more**, and **over 100 kt** in a severe cyclone. At the centre the **eye**, **10 to 50 km** across, is nearly calm with little cloud; around it the **eyewall** of Cb carries the strongest winds.
- **Cloud and precipitation.** **Spiral rain bands** of Cb, torrential rain and visibility near zero.
- **Stability and turbulence.** Extremely unstable: severe turbulence and wind shear.
- **Temperature.** Warm, saturated tropical air.

![What is inside a cyclone?](diagram:tropical-cyclone-structure "What to notice: a calm eye 10 to 50 km across, the strongest winds in the Cb eyewall around it, and spiral rain bands turning clockwise in the Southern Hemisphere.")

A cyclone **weakens over land or cooler water**, because it loses its supply of warm, moist air. SIGMETs and Bureau warnings cover it, and the only sensible plan for a light aeroplane is to be on the ground, tied down, or flown well away before it arrives.

??? check "Check yourself: a cyclone off Port Hedland crosses the coast and moves inland. Why does it weaken?"
    It loses its fuel. Over land there is no warm sea to evaporate moisture into the converging air, so less vapour condenses, less heat is released, and the low fills, although the rain and flooding can last for days.

## The equatorial trough

Between the hemispheres the trade winds converge in a band of low pressure, the **equatorial trough**, also called the **intertropical convergence zone** (**ITCZ**). In the southern summer it moves south over northern Australia, where it is called the **monsoon trough**. North of it blow **north-westerly monsoon winds**, moist from the warm seas; south of it blow the **south-east trades**.

Converging air must rise, and this air is warm, moist and unstable, so the trough is a belt of convection:

- **Cloud and precipitation.** Lines and clusters of **Cu and Cb**, heavy showers and thunderstorms, often **at night over the sea** and **in the afternoon over land**, with St and Ns in the heaviest areas.
- **Stability and turbulence.** **Unstable**, with turbulence and wind shear near the storms, but light winds otherwise.
- **Wind.** **Light and variable** near the trough axis, with squalls near storms. Close to the equator Coriolis is too weak to organise the air into strong winds round the pressure pattern.
- **Temperature.** Hot and humid, with a high freezing level, about **15,000 to 16,000 ft**.

## The west coast trough

The trough that matters most to a Perth pilot in summer is not a front at all. In summer the land heats intensely and a trough of low pressure forms down the west of the continent: the **west coast trough**. Its position each day decides Perth's weather.

When the trough lies **off or near the coast**, Perth is on its eastern side, where the flow is from the interior: **hot, dry easterly to north-easterly** winds of continental tropical air. The gradient easterly holds the sea breeze offshore and temperatures climb past 40 degrees.

When the trough moves **inland**, Perth is on its western side. The wind swings **south to south-westerly** behind it, the **Fremantle Doctor** arrives ([PMTC 2.5](/notes/PMTC/2.5)), and the temperature drops. Inland, the convergence along the trough line can lift the hot air enough to set off **high-based storms and dust**. Treat the trough's passage as you would a weak front: a wind change, turbulence and shear along it, and storms to the east.

![Where is the trough, and what wind does Perth get?](diagram:west-coast-trough-positions "What to notice: trough offshore means hot easterlies at Perth; trough inland means the south-westerly sea breeze at Perth and storms and dust inland.")

![What drives the sea breeze, and where is its front?](diagram:sea-breeze-front "When the trough moves inland the sea breeze pushes ashore, and the wind change, shear and cumulus line are at its leading edge.")

??? check "Check yourself: the west coast trough moves inland during the morning. What happens to the wind and temperature at Jandakot, and what should you expect inland?"
    Jandakot is now west of the trough, so the hot easterly gives way to a south-westerly, the sea breeze comes in and the temperature drops, with shear and turbulence as the change passes. Inland, convergence along the trough line can trigger high-based storms with dust and gusty outflow.

## Bringing it together

A June morning. The chart shows a deep low south of Cape Leeuwin with a cold front 300 nm west of Perth, moving east at about 30 kt. You plan Jandakot to Albany and back. Ask the four questions. Ahead of the front the wind is a strengthening north-westerly, so expect a crosswind and some mechanical turbulence, but the cloud is still high As and Ac. The front will reach the coast in roughly 10 hours, so the Cb, squalls and the backing to the south-west arrive in the evening, at Albany a little later. Behind it come gusty south-westerlies, showers and lowering cloud on the Stirling Range and the Scarp. Go early and be home well before the front. If the low were a wave with a warm front ahead, the layer cloud and rain would arrive hours before the cold front and could close the route sooner.

??? check "Check yourself: during the flight above, the wind at Albany backs from north-west to south-west, the temperature drops 6 degrees and a squall line passes. What has happened, and what weather follows?"
    The cold front has passed. Behind it comes colder, unstable maritime polar air: gusty south-westerlies, scattered Cu and showers with good visibility between them, and cloud lowering on the high ground.

## Key points

- **Air masses**: maritime or continental, tropical or polar. Heated from below: unstable, Cu, showers, gusts, good visibility. Cooled from below: stable, St, drizzle, fog, poor visibility.
- **2.6.1 (a) Cold front**: steep (1 in 50 to 1 in 100). Strengthening north-westerly ahead; a narrow band (often 50 nm or less) of Cb, squalls, turbulence and shear; colder, unstable, showery south-westerlies behind. The wind backs.
- **2.6.1 (b) Warm front**: shallow (about 1 in 150). Ci, Cs, As, Ns with lowering bases, continuous rain, frontal fog, mostly smooth, possible embedded Cb; warmer after. North to north-west: backs, often lighter.
- **2.6.1 (c) Wave depression**: a low on a kink in a front; warm front, warm sector (low St, drizzle, poor visibility), cold front, clearing showers. Backs at each front; deepens moving east.
- **2.6.1 (d) Occlusion**: the cold front lifts the warm sector off the ground; layer cloud and rain with embedded Cb, icing and low cloud; small surface temperature change; backs.
- **2.6.1 (e) Tropical cyclone**: sea 26.5 degrees or warmer, 5 degrees or more from the equator, November to April, Pilbara most prone. Clockwise, 34 kt or more (over 100 kt severe), calm eye 10 to 50 km, eyewall and spiral bands of Cb. Weakens over land or cool water.
- **2.6.1 (f) Equatorial trough**: converging moist unstable air; Cb and storms (night over sea, afternoon over land); light variable winds with squalls; freezing level about 15,000 to 16,000 ft.
- **2.6.2** Temperature: which air replaces which. Wind: backs here, stronger with tighter isobars. Stability: heated or cooled from below. Cloud: fast lifting gives Cu and showers, slow lifting gives layers and continuous rain.
- **West coast trough**: offshore, hot easterlies and no sea breeze; inland, a south-westerly change and storms inland.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Southern Hemisphere: the wind **backs** as a front passes (north-west to south-west at a cold front). Warm front: layer cloud, continuous rain, gets warmer. Cold front: Cb, showers, squalls, gets colder, clears behind. Expect a cloud-sequence question (Ci, Cs, As, Ns means a warm front), an occlusion question (embedded Cb), the warm sector (low St, drizzle) and the cyclone recipe: 26.5 degrees, 5 degrees from the equator, clockwise, November to April.
