## What the exam expects (PMTC 2.2)

- **2.2.1** Say how surface air temperature is measured, and how the temperature just above the runway can differ from it.
- **2.2.2** Explain (a) a temperature inversion; (b) saturated air, relative humidity and dew point; (c) evaporation, condensation and freezing.
- **2.2.3** List how changes in temperature, pressure and humidity change air density.
- **2.2.4** Calculate an ISA temperature and a pressure height.
- **2.2.5** Explain height, elevation, altitude, QNH and QFE.

## Why this matters

This is the lesson that turns weather into numbers the aeroplane cares about. On a 38 degree afternoon at an inland strip the METAR temperature, the QNH and the elevation together decide whether you clear the trees at the end of the runway. On a still winter evening at Northam the temperature and dew point decide whether there will be fog in the Avon valley at dawn. Every altimeter setting you dial in, QNH in the circuit and 1013 on the flight levels, rests on the definitions in the last section. The exam asks for definitions (dew point, inversion, QFE), for the direction of an effect (does humidity raise or lower density), and for two calculations: pressure height and ISA temperature.

## How the air is heated

The air gets its heat in four ways, and each one appears later in this unit.

**Radiation.** The sun's energy arrives as radiation, passes mostly straight through clear air and is absorbed by the ground. The ground also radiates its own heat away, day and night, to the sky. By day it gains more than it loses and warms; on a clear night it only loses, and cools.

**Conduction.** Heat passes by contact from a warmer thing to a cooler one. The ground warms, or chills, only the thin layer of air actually touching it. Air is a poor conductor, so conduction alone affects only the bottom few feet.

**Convection.** Air warmed at the ground becomes lighter than the air around it and rises in bubbles (thermals), carrying the heat upwards, while cooler air sinks to replace it. This vertical stirring is what spreads the day's heating through thousands of feet. Moving air sideways, so that warm air flows over cold ground or the reverse, is called **advection**.

**Latent heat.** Water absorbs heat when it evaporates and gives the same heat back when it condenses. Moist air carries this hidden store of energy with it.

## Measuring surface temperature (2.2.1)

The official air temperature in a METAR is measured in the **shade, about 1.2 m above short grass**, in a ventilated, white, louvred instrument shelter (the Stevenson screen) or in the radiation shield of an automatic weather station. Each detail has a reason. Shade stops sunlight heating the thermometer itself. White paint reflects sunlight from the shelter. The louvres let air flow through so the thermometer reads the air rather than the box. Grass gives every station the same standard surface.

The air you take off through is often different.

On a **sunny afternoon** the dark bitumen of the runway absorbs far more sunlight than grass and heats the air touching it by conduction. The layer just above the runway can be several degrees **hotter** than the reported temperature. Hotter air is less dense, so take-off performance is worse than the METAR temperature suggests.

On a **clear, calm night** the opposite happens. The ground radiates its heat to the open sky and chills the air in contact with it. The air at runway level can be **colder** than the screen reading, sometimes enough for frost or fog to form when the reported temperature is still a few degrees above the dew point.

So use the reported temperature for performance charts, which were built for it, but treat it as **optimistic on hot days**.

??? check "Check yourself: why might fog form on a runway when the METAR shows a 2 degree dew point spread?"
    The temperature is measured 1.2 m above grass. On a clear, calm night the ground chills the air touching it below the screen temperature, so the air at runway level can reach its dew point while the screen still reads 2 degrees above it.

## Water in the air (2.2.2 b)

Leave a glass of iced water on the table on a humid Perth summer day and droplets form on the outside: the air touching the glass has been cooled until it cannot keep all its water as vapour. Every cloud and every fog forms the same way.

Air at any temperature can hold only so much water vapour. When it holds that maximum it is **saturated**, and any more vapour, or any further cooling, makes some condense into droplets. The maximum depends very strongly on temperature: warm air can hold far more vapour than cold air, roughly doubling for every 10 degrees or so of warming.

**Relative humidity (RH)** is the amount of vapour the air actually holds, as a percentage of the most it could hold at its present temperature. The word *relative* is the key: RH depends on the temperature as well as on the water. Cool a parcel of air without adding or removing any water, and its RH rises, because the maximum it can hold has fallen while the water in it has not changed. At 100 percent the air is saturated.

The **dew point** is the temperature to which air must be cooled, at constant pressure and with no change in its water content, to become saturated. Unlike RH, the dew point measures the water itself: moist air has a high dew point, dry air a low one, and the dew point stays the same as the air warms and cools through the day. The gap between the air temperature and the dew point is the **dew point spread**, and it is the quick measure of how close the air is to making cloud or fog. A METAR group `12/11` is a 1 degree spread and a strong hint of fog or low cloud; `35/05` is a 30 degree spread and very dry air. You first met the dew point spread in [RMTC 2.1](/notes/RMTC/2.1); in [PMTC 2.3](/notes/PMTC/2.3) you use it to estimate the base of cumulus.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    A rising relative humidity does not mean water has been added. Overnight the RH climbs towards 100 percent simply because the air is cooling. The dew point is the measure of the water content; the spread is the measure of how close the air is to saturation.

??? check "Check yourself: during the evening the temperature falls from 20 to 14 degrees and the dew point stays at 12. What happens to the relative humidity and to the spread?"
    The RH rises, because cooler air can hold less vapour while the water in it is unchanged. The spread closes from 8 to 2 degrees, so the air is getting close to saturation, and fog or low cloud becomes possible if it keeps cooling.

## Evaporation, condensation and freezing (2.2.2 c)

Water changes between three states: vapour, liquid and ice. Each change either takes heat in or gives heat out, and that heat, which changes the state without changing the temperature, is called **latent heat** ("hidden" heat).

**Evaporation**, liquid to vapour, **absorbs** heat from the surroundings, so it cools them. You feel this stepping out of the surf into a breeze. In the atmosphere, rain falling into dry air below a cloud evaporates, chills that air, makes it heavy and drives it downwards: the cold, gusty **downdraughts** under showers and thunderstorms.

**Condensation**, vapour to liquid, **releases** the same heat back into the air. That is the hidden fuel of the atmosphere. Each cloud droplet that forms warms the air around it a little, which is why saturated air cools more slowly as it rises (the next section), and why a big thunderstorm keeps growing: the more vapour condenses, the more heat is released, and the more buoyant the rising air becomes.

**Freezing**, liquid to ice, also **releases** heat; melting absorbs it. Going straight from vapour to ice or back (sublimation, and its reverse, deposition) moves the most heat of all.

| Change | Latent heat | Example in flight |
|---|---|---|
| **Evaporation** (liquid to vapour) | Absorbed, cools the surroundings | Rain falling into dry air cools it and drives downdraughts |
| **Condensation** (vapour to liquid) | Released, warms the air | Why saturated air cools more slowly as it rises; the fuel of thunderstorms |
| **Freezing** (liquid to ice) | Released | Supercooled droplets freeze on contact with an airframe |
| Melting and sublimation | Absorbed | |

Water in cloud often stays liquid well below 0 °C, because tiny droplets need something to freeze onto. These **supercooled** droplets freeze the moment they strike a wing or propeller, and they are the source of airframe icing ([PMTC 2.7](/notes/PMTC/2.7)).

??? check "Check yourself: why is the air under a shower from a high-based cloud often colder and gustier than the air around it?"
    The rain evaporates as it falls through the dry air below the cloud. Evaporation absorbs latent heat, cooling that air; the cooled air is denser, so it sinks and spreads out at the ground as a cold, gusty downdraught.

## Lapse rates and stability

To understand inversions, and every cloud in [PMTC 2.3](/notes/PMTC/2.3), you need one more idea: what happens to a bubble of air, a **parcel**, when it moves up or down.

When a parcel rises it moves into lower pressure and expands. Expanding takes energy, which comes from the parcel's own heat, so it cools. When it sinks it is compressed and warms. If no heat passes between the parcel and the air around it, which is close to true for a large parcel moving quickly, the process is **adiabatic**.

An unsaturated parcel cools at the **dry adiabatic lapse rate (DALR)**, **3 °C per 1,000 ft**. Once it is saturated, every further degree of cooling condenses some vapour and releases latent heat, which offsets part of the cooling. A saturated parcel therefore cools at the smaller **saturated adiabatic lapse rate (SALR)**, about **1.5 °C per 1,000 ft**.

Neither of these is the temperature of the air around the parcel. The real temperature profile on the day, measured by a balloon-borne radiosonde, is the **environmental lapse rate (ELR)**, and it can be anything.

| Lapse rate | Value | What it describes |
|---|---|---|
| **DALR** (dry adiabatic) | **3 °C per 1,000 ft** | An unsaturated parcel rising or sinking |
| **SALR** (saturated adiabatic) | About **1.5 °C per 1,000 ft** (smaller in warm air, closer to the DALR in very cold air) | A saturated parcel: condensation releases latent heat |
| **ELR** (environmental) | Whatever the real air does that day | The surrounding air, measured by radiosonde |
| ISA | 1.98 °C per 1,000 ft | The reference average ELR |

**Stability** compares the parcel with the air around it. Give a parcel a nudge upwards. If it ends up **colder** than the air around it at its new level, it is denser and sinks back: the air is **stable**. If it ends up **warmer**, it is lighter and keeps rising on its own: the air is **unstable**.

So stability depends on whether the environment cools faster or slower with height than the parcel does. If the ELR is **less** than the parcel's lapse rate, the surrounding air stays relatively warm aloft, the lifted parcel ends up colder than it, and the air is **stable**. If the ELR is **greater** than the DALR, the surrounding air gets cold so quickly with height that even a dry parcel, cooling at 3 degrees per 1,000 ft, stays warmer than it and keeps rising: **unstable**. In between, with the ELR between the SALR and the DALR, the air is **conditionally unstable**: stable while the rising air is dry, unstable once it saturates.

![Lapse rates and stability: does a lifted parcel sink back or keep rising?](widget:lapse-rate-stability "Compare the parcel's line with the environment's: where the parcel is warmer it keeps rising, and once it saturates it cools more slowly and can break free.")

!!! example "Worked example: is a lifted parcel stable?"
    Given: surface temperature 26 °C, ELR 2.5 °C per 1,000 ft, parcel unsaturated, lifted 2,000 ft.

    1. Parcel temperature at 2,000 ft (DALR): $26 - 2 \times 3 = \mathbf{20\ °C}$.
    2. Environment at 2,000 ft (ELR): $26 - 2 \times 2.5 = \mathbf{21\ °C}$.
    3. Comparison: the parcel is **1 °C colder** than the air around it, so it sinks back.

    The air is stable for dry parcels. But 2.5 °C per 1,000 ft is between the SALR and the DALR: if the parcel were saturated it would cool at only about 1.5 °C per 1,000 ft, reach 2,000 ft at about 23 °C, 2 degrees warmer than its surroundings, and keep going. That is conditional instability, the classic setting for cumulus that grows once the thermals reach their condensation level.

The exam often describes a day and asks which kind of air it is.

| Stable air | Unstable air |
|---|---|
| Stratiform cloud (St, Sc, As, Ns) | Cumuliform cloud (Cu, TCu, Cb) |
| Continuous rain or drizzle | Showers, thunderstorms, hail |
| Poor visibility: haze, smoke, fog | Good visibility outside showers |
| Smooth air (except near terrain) | Bumpy, thermals, gusty surface wind |

## Temperature inversions (2.2.2 a)

Most of the time temperature falls as you climb. A **temperature inversion** is a layer where it does the opposite and **increases** with height. It is the most stable arrangement there is: any parcel lifted into it cools while the air around it gets warmer, so it sinks straight back. An inversion acts as a lid on everything beneath it.

Four processes make inversions, and each has a setting you can picture:

- **Radiation inversion.** On a clear, calm night the ground radiates its heat away and chills the air touching it, so the coldest air is at the bottom with warmer air above. This is the inversion of a winter dawn in the Avon valley, and the one that makes radiation fog.
- **Subsidence inversion.** In a high pressure system the air slowly sinks over a wide area and warms by compression as it descends, leaving a warm layer sitting on cooler surface air. The long summer highs over WA make this one common.
- **Frontal inversion.** At a front, warm air rides over a wedge of cold air, so climbing through the frontal surface you pass from cold into warm.
- **Turbulence inversion.** Strong wind over rough ground mixes the lowest layer thoroughly, giving it the DALR; at the top of the mixed layer the temperature can jump back up into the undisturbed air above.

![What happens near the ground on a clear, calm night?](diagram:radiation-fog-overnight "The ground loses its heat to the clear sky and chills the air touching it: the coldest air lies at the bottom, under warmer air, and if it reaches its dew point it becomes fog.")

Under an inversion smoke, haze, dust and fog are trapped and cannot mix upwards, so visibility is poor. The air below is smooth. At the top of the inversion there can be a sharp change of wind, because the light surface wind is cut off from the stronger wind above: **wind shear** that can catch you on climb-out or approach ([RMTC 2.3](/notes/RMTC/2.3)). Climbing through the top of a morning inversion you will often see the haze end in a sharp line and the visibility open up.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    An inversion is defined by temperature **rising** with height, not just falling more slowly. An **isothermal** layer, where the temperature stays constant, is also very stable, but it is not an inversion.

??? check "Check yourself: why does smoke from a morning burn-off spread out flat at a few hundred feet instead of rising?"
    It has reached the top of a radiation inversion. The rising smoke cools as it climbs, but the air in the inversion gets warmer with height, so the smoke becomes colder and denser than its surroundings and stops rising, spreading sideways under the lid.

## Air density (2.2.3)

The aeroplane cares about **density**, the mass of air in each cubic metre, because lift, drag, engine power and propeller thrust all depend on it. Three things set it.

**Temperature.** Heat a gas and its molecules move faster and spread apart. At a given pressure, **warmer air is less dense**.

**Pressure.** Squeeze a gas and the same molecules occupy less space. At a given temperature, **higher pressure means denser air**. Climbing reduces pressure, which is why density falls with altitude.

**Humidity.** This one surprises people, because humid air feels heavy. A water vapour molecule is lighter than the nitrogen and oxygen molecules it replaces: its molecular weight is about 18 against about 29 for dry air. Each litre of air holds the same number of molecules at the same temperature and pressure, so swapping heavy molecules for light ones makes it lighter. **Humid air is less dense** than dry air. The effect is small next to temperature, but it always points the same way.

| Change | Density | Aeroplane performance |
|---|---|---|
| Temperature up | Down | Worse |
| Pressure up | Up | Better |
| Humidity up | Down (slightly) | Worse |
| Altitude up (pressure falls) | Down | Worse |

High, hot and humid is the worst case: a summer afternoon at an inland WA strip, or a muggy wet-season day in the Kimberley. Cold, high-pressure, dry air is the best.

??? check "Check yourself: two days have the same temperature and QNH, but one has a dew point of 22 and the other of 2. Which day gives the better take-off performance?"
    The dry day, with the dew point of 2. The humid day has more water vapour, whose molecules are lighter than the nitrogen and oxygen they replace, so its air is slightly less dense.

## Pressure height and ISA temperature (2.2.4)

Performance charts do not know about QNH. They are drawn for the ISA, so they want to know how high the aerodrome is **in the standard atmosphere**. That is the **pressure height**: the height in the ISA at which today's actual pressure would be found. It is also what the altimeter reads with **1013** set on the subscale.

If today's QNH is exactly 1013, the standard atmosphere and the real one agree at sea level, and the pressure height equals the elevation. If the QNH is lower, say 1003, then the real pressure everywhere is about 10 hPa lower than standard, and in the ISA you would have to climb about $10 \times 30 = 300$ ft to find that pressure. The aerodrome "feels" 300 ft higher. Using the 30 ft per hPa rule from [PMTC 2.1](/notes/PMTC/2.1):

$$\text{Pressure height} = \text{elevation} + (1013 - \text{QNH}) \times 30$$

When QNH is **below** 1013 the pressure height is **above** the elevation; when QNH is **above** 1013 it is **below**.

The **ISA temperature** at that pressure height comes from [PMTC 2.1](/notes/PMTC/2.1): $15 - 2 \times$ (pressure height in thousands of feet). Each degree of ISA deviation (actual minus ISA) moves the **density height**, the height in the ISA with the same density as today's air, by about **120 ft**:

$$\text{Density height} \approx \text{pressure height} + 120 \times (\text{OAT} - T_{\text{ISA}})$$

![Work out pressure height and density height for any aerodrome and day](widget:density-height "Heat is the big one: every degree above ISA adds about 120 ft of density height, while each hectopascal of QNH is worth only 30 ft.")

!!! example "Worked example: a hot inland afternoon"
    Given: aerodrome elevation 1,200 ft, QNH 1008 hPa, temperature 38 °C.

    1. Pressure height: $1{,}200 + (1013 - 1008) \times 30 = 1{,}200 + 150 = \mathbf{1{,}350\ ft}$.
    2. ISA temperature at 1,350 ft: $15 - 1.35 \times 2 = 15 - 2.7 = \mathbf{12.3\ °C}$.
    3. ISA deviation: $38 - 12.3 = \mathbf{+25.7}$, so about **ISA +26**.
    4. Density height: $1{,}350 + 120 \times 25.7 = 1{,}350 + 3{,}084 \approx \mathbf{4{,}400\ ft}$.

    The aeroplane performs as if the strip were at 4,400 ft. The charts in [BAKC 6.2](/notes/BAKC/6.2) do this correction for you once you enter pressure height and temperature.

!!! example "Worked example: a cold, high-pressure morning"
    Given: aerodrome elevation 800 ft, QNH 1021 hPa, temperature 12 °C.

    1. Pressure height: $800 + (1013 - 1021) \times 30 = 800 - 240 = \mathbf{560\ ft}$.
    2. ISA temperature at 560 ft: $15 - 0.56 \times 2 = 15 - 1.1 = \mathbf{13.9\ °C}$.
    3. ISA deviation: $12 - 13.9 = \mathbf{-1.9}$, about **ISA −2**.
    4. Density height: $560 + 120 \times (-1.9) = 560 - 228 \approx \mathbf{330\ ft}$.

    High pressure and cool air: the aeroplane performs as if the strip were at about 330 ft, better than its 800 ft surveyed elevation suggests.

Try one yourself before opening the solution: a wheatbelt strip at 650 ft elevation, QNH 1006 hPa, temperature 35 °C. Find the pressure height, the ISA temperature and the density height.

??? solution "Show the solution"
    Pressure height $= 650 + (1013 - 1006) \times 30 = 650 + 210 = 860$ ft. ISA temperature at 860 ft $= 15 - 1.7 = 13.3$ °C. Deviation $= 35 - 13.3 = +21.7$. Density height $= 860 + 120 \times 21.7 = 860 + 2{,}604 \approx$ **3,460 ft**. A strip of 650 ft is behaving like one at nearly 3,500 ft, almost all of it because of the heat.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Get the sign of the QNH term right by asking what the altimeter would do. Low pressure means the altimeter, set to 1013, reads **higher** than the elevation, so a QNH below 1013 **adds** to the elevation. The common mistake is computing $\text{QNH} - 1013$ and getting the sign backwards.

## Height, elevation, altitude, QNH and QFE (2.2.5)

Three words describe vertical distance, and the difference between them is simply where you measure from.

**Height** is the vertical distance from a specified datum, usually the ground or the aerodrome. It is written AGL (above ground level). Your 1,000 ft circuit is a height.

**Elevation** is the vertical distance of a point **on the surface**, such as a runway, a hilltop or a mast base, above mean sea level. Jandakot's runway has an elevation; an aeroplane never does.

**Altitude** is the vertical distance of an aircraft, or a level, above **mean sea level** (AMSL). Your cruise at 4,500 ft is an altitude.

In the circuit the three are tied together: altitude equals the aerodrome's elevation plus your height above it.

![How do height, altitude and elevation relate?](diagram:height-altitude-elevation "At 1,300 ft altitude you are 1,000 ft above a 300 ft aerodrome, but only 400 ft above a 900 ft ridge: the altimeter on QNH tells you altitude, not height.")

The altimeter is a barometer. It measures the pressure outside and converts it to feet using the ISA, counting from whatever pressure you set on the subscale. Choose the subscale setting and you choose the datum.

**QNH** is the setting that makes the altimeter read **altitude**. On the ground it reads the aerodrome **elevation**. It is the pressure the forecaster works out for mean sea level at that place, and it is used in Australia below the transition altitude of 10,000 ft. You get it from the ATIS, AWIS, METAR or, for the area, the forecast ([RMTC 2.2](/notes/RMTC/2.2)). Because charts give terrain and obstacle elevations above sea level, QNH lets you compare your altimeter directly with the chart.

**QFE** is the setting that makes the altimeter read **zero** on the aerodrome, so in flight it shows **height above the aerodrome**. It is the actual pressure at aerodrome level. Since pressure falls about 1 hPa per 30 ft, the QFE is lower than the QNH by the elevation divided by 30:

$$\text{QFE} \approx \text{QNH} - \frac{\text{elevation}}{30}$$

With **1013.2** set, the altimeter reads **pressure height**, which is how flight levels above the transition level are flown. You may also see **QFF** on weather maps: the station pressure reduced to mean sea level using the actual temperature, which is what the isobars on a synoptic chart show. It is a forecaster's quantity, not an altimeter setting.

!!! example "Worked example: QFE at a coastal aerodrome"
    Given: aerodrome elevation 300 ft, QNH 1015 hPa.

    1. Pressure difference: $300 \div 30 = \mathbf{10\ hPa}$.
    2. QFE: $1015 - 10 = \mathbf{1005\ hPa}$.

    With 1005 set, the altimeter reads zero on the runway and 1,000 ft at circuit height.

!!! example "Worked example: QFE at an inland aerodrome"
    Given: aerodrome elevation 1,200 ft, QNH 1010 hPa.

    1. Pressure difference: $1{,}200 \div 30 = \mathbf{40\ hPa}$.
    2. QFE: $1010 - 40 = \mathbf{970\ hPa}$.

    The higher the aerodrome, the further the QFE sits below the QNH.

Setting the wrong QNH, or flying into lower pressure without updating it, gives an error of about 30 ft for every hectopascal. Fly from high pressure towards low pressure with the old, higher QNH still set, and the altimeter **over-reads**: you are lower than it says.

![Explore what the subscale setting does to the reading](widget:altimeter-subscale "Each hectopascal of difference between the subscale and the real QNH moves the reading about 30 ft; set too high a number and you are lower than the altimeter says.")

??? check "Check yourself: on the ground at an aerodrome of elevation 500 ft, what does the altimeter read with QNH set, with QFE set, and with 1013 set on a day when QNH is 1003?"
    With QNH, 500 ft, the elevation. With QFE, zero. With 1013, the pressure height: $500 + (1013 - 1003) \times 30 = 800$ ft, higher than the elevation because the pressure is below standard.

## Bringing it together

A winter evening at Northam after a day of showers. The 1800 observation reads `09/07`, wind calm, skies clearing, QNH 1022. The 2 degree spread and the clear sky tell you the ground will radiate freely overnight; the chilled air at runway level will drop below the screen temperature and reach its dew point, and a radiation inversion will trap the result in the Avon valley as fog at dawn. Your 0700 departure is unlikely; plan for mid-morning, when the sun has heated the ground, the inversion has broken and the fog has cleared. When you do go, the day is kind for performance: with elevation about 500 ft and QNH 1022 the pressure height is about 230 ft, and a 12 degree morning is close to ISA, so the density height is a few hundred feet at most. Climbing out on QNH, your altitude is what you compare with the Darling Scarp's elevations on the chart.

## Key points

- **2.2.1** Surface temperature is measured in the shade 1.2 m above grass in a ventilated screen. Above sunlit bitumen the air is hotter (worse performance than the METAR suggests); on clear calm nights the runway air is colder (frost and fog before the screen shows saturation).
- **2.2.2 (a)** An inversion is a layer where temperature rises with height: very stable, traps haze, smoke and fog, smooth beneath, shear at its top. Radiation, subsidence, frontal and turbulence inversions. Stability compares the ELR with the DALR (3 °C) and SALR (about 1.5 °C per 1,000 ft).
- **2.2.2 (b)** Saturated air holds all the vapour it can; RH is the vapour present as a percentage of that maximum and rises as air cools; dew point is the temperature at which the air would saturate, and the spread measures how close it is.
- **2.2.2 (c)** Evaporation absorbs latent heat and cools; condensation and freezing release it. Supercooled droplets cause airframe icing.
- **2.2.3** Density falls with higher temperature, lower pressure and higher humidity. High, hot and humid is the worst case.
- **2.2.4** Pressure height $=$ elevation $+ (1013 - \text{QNH}) \times 30$; ISA temperature $= 15 - 2 \times$ pressure height in thousands; density height adds about 120 ft per degree above ISA.
- **2.2.5** Height above a datum (AGL), elevation of a surface point AMSL, altitude of an aircraft AMSL. QNH reads altitude (elevation on the ground); QFE reads zero on the aerodrome, about QNH minus elevation ÷ 30; 1013 reads pressure height.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Have 30 ft per hPa, 2 °C per 1,000 ft, 3 °C DALR, about 1.5 °C SALR and 120 ft per degree by heart. "High to low, look out below": flying from high to low pressure without resetting QNH, the altimeter over-reads and you are lower than indicated, about 30 ft per hectopascal.
