## What the exam expects (PMTC 2.4)

- **2.4.1** Work out the visibility, either by what you can see from the cockpit or from a meteorological forecast or report.
- **2.4.2** List the weather factors that reduce visibility in flight.

## Why this matters

Visibility is the one VMC figure you cannot check against the altimeter: you judge it with your own eyes, and it can fall below the minimum while the sky above stays blue. On a still winter morning the Avon valley around Northam and York can lie under fog while the hills either side are in sunshine. On a February afternoon a dust-laden northerly can take Kalgoorlie from 10 km to 2,000 m in minutes, and in autumn a burn-off south of Perth can spread smoke over the whole coastal plain. Poor visibility hides terrain and other aircraft and, worse, can take away the horizon you fly by. The exam asks you to read visibility from a TAF, METAR or GAF, to estimate it from what you can see in flight, to name the things that reduce it, and to say how each kind of fog forms and clears. You met the local picture in [RMTC 2.1](/notes/RMTC/2.1); this lesson adds the physics and the numbers.

## What "visibility" means (2.4.1)

Look out across the airfield and pick out a dark hangar against the sky. Walk away from it and it fades, its edges blur, and at some distance you can no longer tell what it is. That distance is the visibility. Formally, **meteorological visibility** is the greatest horizontal distance at which a dark object of suitable size can be **seen and recognised** against the sky by day, or at which lights can be seen at night. The word "recognised" matters: a vague shape you cannot identify does not count.

A pilot meets three versions of it.

**Reported or forecast visibility** is the observer's or sensor's figure at the aerodrome. In a TAF or METAR it is four digits in **metres** (`4000` is 4,000 m), and `9999` means **10 km or more**. A TAF or METAR gives the prevailing value; the **GAF** gives the visibility for each subdivision of its area, often split into a general figure and a figure in showers (for example 3000 m in SHRA). **CAVOK** includes visibility 10 km or more.

**Flight visibility** is the visibility forward from the cockpit in flight, and it is the figure the VMC rules use: **5,000 m** below 10,000 ft AMSL (Part 91 MOS; verify against the current VFRG, and see the VMC table in [RFRC 2.3](/notes/RFRC/2.3)). It is your judgement, made in the air, and it can differ from the aerodrome report in either direction.

![What visibility and cloud clearance does VMC need below 10,000 ft?](diagram:vmc-minima-by-airspace "Flight visibility is 5,000 m in every class of airspace below 10,000 ft; only the distance from cloud changes.")

**Slant visibility** is what you see looking down at the ground or along the approach. Under a haze or smoke layer the horizontal visibility at the surface may be fine, but looking down from above the layer you look through its whole depth at a shallow angle, and the ground can almost disappear. Looking **into the sun** is worse again, because the particles scatter sunlight towards you and the haze glows.

??? check "Check yourself: the METAR at your destination shows 9999, yet as you descend towards it through a smoke layer you can barely make out the runway. Why the difference?"
    The METAR is horizontal visibility at the surface, measured below or within the bottom of the layer. Descending from above, you look down through the whole depth of the smoke at a slant, so far more particles lie along your line of sight. Slant visibility can be much worse than the reported figure, especially into the sun.

## Judging visibility from the cockpit (2.4.1)

You cannot measure visibility in flight; you estimate it from known distances. The method is simple: pick a feature ahead whose distance you can measure on the chart, a town, a dam, a silo or a road junction, and note where you are when it first becomes **recognisable**. The distance between the two is the flight visibility.

To use it, you need the VMC minimum in the units you navigate in. One nautical mile is 1,852 m, so 5,000 m is about **2.7 nm**. If the feature you expect at 3 nm is not yet in sight, the visibility is below VMC.

!!! example "Worked example: what 5,000 m looks like in nautical miles and in time"
    Given: VMC minimum 5,000 m, 1 nm = 1,852 m, groundspeed 100 kt.

    1. Distance in nautical miles: $5{,}000 \div 1{,}852 = \mathbf{2.7\ nm}$.
    2. Time to cover it: $2.7 \div 100 \times 60 = \mathbf{1.6\ minutes}$, about **1 minute 40 seconds**.

    At 100 kt, a feature that first becomes recognisable less than about 1 minute 40 seconds before you reach it means the visibility is under 5,000 m. In practice you want much more than that: the minimum is a legal floor, not a comfortable working figure.

Timing works the other way too. Suppose you first recognise the York silos, start the stopwatch, and pass over them 2 minutes 30 seconds later at a groundspeed of 105 kt. What was the flight visibility, and is it above VMC?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    Distance flown: $105 \times 2.5 \div 60 = 4.4$ nm. In metres: $4.4 \times 1{,}852 \approx$ **8,100 m**, so about 8 km. That is above the 5,000 m minimum. Remember that a stopwatch figure is only as good as the moment you judged the silos "recognisable"; it gives you a rough check, not a measurement.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Visibility in TAFs and METARs is in **metres**, while you navigate in nautical miles. Mixing them is how a pilot reads "4000" as comfortably more than 2.7 nm and decides it is VMC. It is not: 4,000 m is below the 5,000 m minimum, even with no cloud at all.

## Reading visibility from the forecast (2.4.1)

Before flight, the visibility comes from the forecasts and reports ([RMTC 2.2](/notes/RMTC/2.2) teaches the full decode). The skill the exam tests is reading **every** group, not just the first line. The opening conditions of a TAF are only the background; the change groups can put the visibility below VMC for the very hour you plan to arrive.

![How do the change groups sit over the TAF's validity period?](diagram:taf-change-groups "The main line sets the background; TEMPO and INTER groups lay temporary spells over it for the hours they cover.")

!!! example "Worked example: visibility from a TAF"
    Given: a Kalgoorlie TAF:

    ```
    TAF YPKG 030500Z 0306/0318 25015G25KT 9999 SCT040
      TEMPO 0308/0312 2000 DS
    ```

    1. Main conditions: `9999`, visibility **10 km or more**, from 0600 to 1800 UTC.
    2. `TEMPO 0308/0312`: between **0800 and 1200 UTC**, temporary periods of under an hour each.
    3. `2000 DS`: during those periods, visibility **2,000 m** in **dust storm**.
    4. Compare with VMC: 2,000 m is below 5,000 m.

    During the TEMPO periods the visibility is below VMC, so a VFR arrival should be planned outside 0800 to 1200 UTC (1600 to 2000 WST), or with the holding fuel the TEMPO requires (see [RMTC 2.2](/notes/RMTC/2.2)).

The GAF works the same way for the route. A subdivision might show visibility 10 km generally but 3000 m in showers of rain. If showers are forecast along your route, the 3,000 m figure is the one you will meet when you fly into one, and it is below VMC.

??? check "Check yourself: the TAF for your destination is 9999 throughout, but the GAF for the route shows 3000 m in SHRA. Which matters for the flight, and why?"
    Both: the TAF covers the aerodrome, the GAF covers the route between. En route, any shower you fly into will take the visibility to about 3,000 m, below the 5,000 m minimum, so you plan to fly round the showers or not go, whatever the destination TAF says.

## Why visibility falls: particles and droplets (2.4.2)

Light travels from an object to your eye. Anything suspended in the air between, a water droplet, a dust grain, a smoke particle, scatters some of that light out of the beam and scatters other light into it from the side. The object loses contrast against its background until you can no longer recognise it. The more particles along the path, and the bigger they are, the shorter the visibility.

Two things follow straight from that picture. First, visibility depends on **what is in the air and how much of it**, which is why the codes below name the cause. Second, anything that **traps** particles near the ground makes visibility worse. A stable layer, especially an inversion ([PMTC 2.2](/notes/PMTC/2.2)), stops the air mixing upwards, so smoke, dust, haze and fog collect underneath it. Unstable air mixes everything through a deep layer and carries it away. So visibility is generally **poor in stable air** and **good in unstable air**, outside the showers.

### The factors one by one

**Fog (FG)** is cloud at the surface: the air is saturated and full of water droplets, and the visibility is **under 1,000 m**.

**Mist (BR)** is the same process, less advanced: fewer or smaller droplets, with high humidity, about **95 percent or more**, and visibility **1,000 to 5,000 m**. Mist often comes before fog on a cooling evening and after it as fog clears. The code comes from the French *brume*.

**Haze (HZ)** is made of **dry** particles, fine dust, salt and pollution, too small to see one at a time but numerous enough to take the edge off the horizon. It collects under an inversion and is at its worst looking into the sun.

**Smoke (FU)** from bushfires and burn-offs is the big seasonal problem in the south-west in late summer and autumn. A large fire can cover hundreds of kilometres, and under a morning inversion the smoke spreads flat at a few hundred feet.

**Dust and sand (DU, SA, DS, SS)** are raised by strong winds over the dry interior and the wheatbelt: ahead of fronts, in hot northerlies, and in the outflow of thunderstorms. A **dust storm (DS)** or **sandstorm (SS)** can drop the visibility to a few hundred metres in minutes.

**Precipitation (DZ, RA, SHRA, TSRA)** reduces visibility in proportion to how hard it falls. Drizzle is fine but dense and often comes with low stratus. Heavy showers and thunderstorm rain can drop the visibility to a few hundred metres, and water streaming across the windscreen makes it worse still.

**Low cloud** has no code of its own here but does the same job: cloud on the hills or a lowering base forces you to choose between flying into it and flying below it, close to the ground.

**Sea spray** in strong winds over the sea reduces the visibility at low level along the coast.

| Factor | Code | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Fog** | FG | Visibility **under 1,000 m**, air saturated |
| **Mist** | BR | Visibility 1,000 to 5,000 m, high humidity (about 95 percent or more) |
| **Haze** | HZ | Dry particles; trapped under an inversion; worse looking into the sun |
| **Smoke** | FU | Bushfires and burn-offs, late summer and autumn in WA; can cover hundreds of km |
| **Dust and sand** | DU, SA, DS, SS | Raised by strong winds, ahead of fronts and in storm outflows over the dry interior |
| **Precipitation** | DZ, RA, SHRA, TSRA | Heavy showers and thunderstorm rain can drop visibility to a few hundred metres |
| **Low cloud** | | Cloud on the hills or a lowering base, forcing you into or below it |
| **Sea spray** | | Strong winds over the sea at low level |

Some things that make visibility worse are not weather at all: flying **into the sun**, a dirty, scratched or misted windscreen, rain on the screen, and darkness. The exam may offer them as distractors in a question about *meteorological* factors.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Mist and haze both blur the horizon, but they are different things. Mist (BR) is water droplets in very humid air; haze (HZ) is dry particles. The humidity tells you which: a small dew point spread means mist, a large one means haze.

## Fog: how each kind forms and clears (2.4.2)

Fog forms when the air at the surface is **cooled to its dew point**, or has moisture **added** until it saturates. The kinds of fog differ only in what does the cooling or the moistening, and that also decides what clears them.

### Radiation fog: the Avon valley in winter

On a clear night the ground radiates its heat to space with no cloud to send it back, and chills the air resting on it. If the air is humid, with a small dew point spread at sunset, the chilled layer reaches its dew point and fog forms. Four conditions have to come together: a **clear sky**, a **light wind**, about **2 to 5 kt**, **moist air**, and **long nights**, so autumn and winter.

![Why does fog form on still, clear, humid winter mornings?](diagram:radiation-fog-overnight "The ground loses its heat to the clear sky, the air touching it cools to its dew point, and the fog lies in the valleys until the sun clears it.")

The wind is the subtle part. Radiation fog needs **some** wind: in a dead calm only the thinnest layer touching the ground cools, and you get dew or frost rather than fog. A light breeze stirs the cooling through a layer deep enough to make fog. Too much wind, above about 10 kt, mixes the chilled air with warmer air from above through a deep layer, and the result is low stratus instead of fog, or nothing.

Cold air is dense, so on sloping ground it drains downhill overnight and pools in the valleys. That is why the fog lies in the **Avon valley** around Northam and York, and on river flats, while a strip on the hills nearby stays clear. The fog forms around **dawn**, the coldest time of the night, not at sunset, and often thickens just after sunrise. It clears as the sun heats the ground, burning off from the edges and often lifting into low stratus first, or when the wind strengthens and mixes it out. The warning signs are there the evening before: a clear sky, a light wind and a small spread that is closing, such as `14/12` at sunset.

??? check "Check yourself: why does fog lie over Northam at dawn while a farm strip on the hills a few miles away is clear?"
    Air chilled by the ground overnight is denser than the air around it, so it drains downhill and pools in the valley. The valley floor collects the coldest, most saturated air, which is where the fog forms; the hilltop air never gets as cold.

### Advection fog: the coast

**Advection** means horizontal movement. **Advection fog** forms when warm, moist air **moves over a colder surface** and is cooled from below to its dew point. Because the cooling comes from the surface the air is flowing over, not from the night sky, advection fog can form **day or night**, **with moderate winds**, and over large areas. It clears only when the **wind changes direction** or a different air mass arrives. Sunshine does little to it.

**Sea fog** is advection fog over the sea: moist air moving over cooler water. It can form offshore and then drift onto a coastal aerodrome on an onshore wind, and since the sun cannot warm the sea much in a day, heating does little to clear it. Only a change of wind direction removes it.

### Four more kinds

**Frontal fog** forms ahead of a warm front or a slow-moving front. Rain falling from the warm air above the frontal surface drops into the colder air below, evaporates and adds moisture until that air saturates. It clears with the passage of the front.

**Steaming fog**, or sea smoke, forms when very cold air flows over much warmer water. Water evaporates into the cold air and saturates it, so fog rises from rivers, lakes and the sea on cold mornings, usually in a shallow layer. Heating, or the air warming, clears it.

![What makes each kind of fog, and what clears it?](diagram:fog-types "What to notice: radiation fog burns off with the sun, but advection fog needs a wind change, frontal fog the front to pass, and steaming fog the air to warm.")

**Hill fog**, or upslope fog, is cloud formed by orographic lifting ([PMTC 2.3](/notes/PMTC/2.3)) that sits on high ground: moist onshore winds forced up the coastal ranges cool to their dew point. It clears with a wind change or as the air dries out.

| Type | How it forms | Conditions | Dispersal |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Radiation fog** | The ground radiates heat on a clear night and cools the air above it | **Clear sky, light wind (about 2 to 5 kt)**, moist air, long nights: autumn and winter, inland valleys and river flats | Sun heating after dawn ("burns off" from the edges), or the wind strengthening and mixing it into low stratus |
| **Advection fog** | Warm, moist air moves over a colder surface and is cooled from below | Can form with moderate winds, day or night, and over large areas | Only when the wind changes direction or the air mass changes |
| **Sea fog** | Advection fog over the sea: moist air over cooler water | Can drift onto coastal aerodromes | Change of wind direction; heating does little over water |
| **Frontal fog** | Rain from the warm air above a front falls into colder air below, evaporates and saturates it | Ahead of a warm front or a slow-moving front | Passage of the front |
| **Steaming fog** (sea smoke) | Very cold air flows over much warmer water; evaporation saturates the cold air | Rivers, lakes and the sea on cold mornings; usually shallow | Heating, or the air warming |
| **Hill (upslope) fog** | Moist air forced up a slope cools to its dew point | Moist onshore winds on the coastal ranges | Wind change or drying out |

![Why is cloud on the hills a visibility problem as well as a cloud problem?](diagram:low-cloud-and-rising-terrain "Hill fog is cloud sitting on the ground: as the terrain rises into it, the visibility goes to nothing.")

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The exam separates radiation and advection fog by the clues in the question. Clear night, light wind, inland valley, cleared by mid-morning sun: radiation fog. A moderate wind, over or beside the sea, persisting through a sunny day: advection or sea fog. Sunshine clears radiation fog because it heats the ground that made it; it cannot clear fog made by a cold sea.

??? check "Check yourself: why does steaming fog form over a river on a cold morning when the air above it is far from saturated?"
    The cold air is being moistened, not cooled. Water from the much warmer river evaporates into it faster than the cold air can hold, so the lowest layer saturates and the excess condenses as wisps of fog rising off the surface.

## Bringing it together

A Sunday in June, and you plan a 0800 WST departure from Jandakot to Northam. On Saturday evening the Northam observation is `11/09` with a light easterly and the sky clearing after a front. A 2 degree spread, a clear sky, a light wind and a long winter night: everything radiation fog needs, in the valley where it pools. The Northam TAF on Sunday morning confirms it with `0300 FG` in the opening conditions and `BECMG` to `9999` by mid-morning. Three hundred metres is far below the 5,000 m VMC minimum, so you plan to arrive late morning, when the sun has heated the ground and burned the fog off. On the way, climbing over the Scarp, you meet a layer of haze trapped under the morning inversion: horizontal visibility is fine, but looking east into the low sun the ground ahead fades. You check it by timing: the Northam silos first become recognisable about 3 minutes out at 100 kt, roughly 5 nm, about 9 km, comfortably above VMC. Every part of that flight used this lesson: the fog type and when it clears, the forecast groups, slant visibility into the sun, and an estimate made from the cockpit.

??? check "Check yourself: why is the visibility often poor for days under a winter high, but excellent in the showery air behind a cold front?"
    Air sinking in a high warms and forms a subsidence inversion, a stable lid that traps haze, smoke and fog beneath it. The cold air behind a front is unstable, so it mixes particles up through a deep layer and carries them away; only the showers themselves reduce the visibility.

## Key points

- **2.4.1** Visibility is the greatest distance at which a dark object can be seen and recognised (lights at night). Reports and TAFs give it in metres, `9999` meaning 10 km or more; the GAF gives it by subdivision, often separately for showers. Flight visibility for VMC below 10,000 ft is 5,000 m, about 2.7 nm (Part 91 MOS; verify). Estimate it in flight from a feature at a known distance; read every TAF change group, not just the first line. Slant visibility through haze or smoke, and into the sun, can be far worse than the reported figure.
- **2.4.2** Visibility is reduced by fog (FG, under 1,000 m), mist (BR, 1,000 to 5,000 m, humid), haze (HZ, dry particles), smoke (FU), dust and sand (DU, SA, DS, SS), precipitation, low cloud and sea spray; it is poor in stable air under an inversion and good in unstable air outside showers. Radiation fog: clear, light wind, humid, long nights, valleys, cleared by the sun. Advection and sea fog: moist air over a cold surface, any time, cleared only by a wind change. Frontal, steaming and hill fog complete the set.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    `9999` means 10 km **or more**, not 9,999 m. Visibility in TAFs and METARs is in metres; the VMC minimum is 5,000 m, so `4000` is below VMC even with no cloud at all. Radiation fog: clear, calm (light wind), humid, land, night, cleared by sun. Advection fog: moving air over a cold surface, can persist all day, cleared by a wind change. If the question says "persisted through the day despite sunshine" or "formed with a 12 kt wind", it is advection.
