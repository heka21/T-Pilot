## What the exam expects (PMTC 2.7)

- **2.7.1** For six phenomena, (i) turbulence, (ii) wind shear, (iii) mountain waves, (iv) land and sea breezes, (v) thunderstorms and (vi) down-draughts from terrain and cloud: say what makes them form and what makes them die away, how to recognise them, what they do to the aeroplane, and what the pilot does about it.
- **2.7.2** Know when the rules make it compulsory to obtain a forecast.
- **2.7.3** Decode the GAF (the MOS still calls it the ARFOR), TAF, TTF, METAR, SPECI, AIRMET and SIGMET into plain language, decide whether a forecast is valid for your flight, and use it to plan and fly.
- **2.7.4** List the conditions that oblige you to make a special AIREP.

## Why this matters

The RPL lessons taught you to recognise the hazards close to home ([RMTC 2.1](/notes/RMTC/2.1), [RMTC 2.3](/notes/RMTC/2.3)) and to decode a TAF group by group ([RMTC 2.2](/notes/RMTC/2.2)). As a private pilot you take that further: a flight to Busselton as a front comes through, a crossing of the Stirling Range in a strong northerly, a February afternoon with storms building over the wheatbelt. Now the question is not "what does this group mean?" but "can I go, when, and what will I do if the forecast is wrong?" The exam tests the causes and signs of each hazard, the legal duty to get a forecast, the decoding of real messages against an arrival time, and the special AIREP list. This lesson takes each in turn and ends with a go or no-go decision.

## Turbulence and down-draughts

**Turbulence** is irregular motion of the air, and each kind has its own cause, so each has its own conditions, signs and cure. You met the types in [RMTC 2.1](/notes/RMTC/2.1); here they are as the exam's three questions.

**What makes it, and what ends it.** **Mechanical** turbulence comes from strong wind over rough ground, hills and buildings, and eases as the wind drops. **Thermal** turbulence comes from strong surface heating of unstable air, and dies away in the evening as the heating stops. **Frontal** and **wind shear** turbulence come where two airstreams meet, and pass with the front or the shear.

**The signs.** Turbulence groups in the GAF, gusts in the TAF and METAR, cumulus (taller and more ragged means stronger), dust devils, and ragged, fast-moving low cloud ([RMTC 2.3](/notes/RMTC/2.3)).

**The effect and the cure.** Turbulence gives height, airspeed and attitude excursions, and every gust changes the angle of attack and so the load factor. Fast, a gust can **overstress** the airframe; slow, it can stall the wing. So slow to the turbulence speed, **at or below VA**, the speed at which the wing stalls before it can be overstressed. **Hold attitude, not altitude**: chasing height with the elevator adds your loads to the gust's. Keep the belts tight and avoid the lee of hills in strong wind.

A **down-draught** is the sinking half of the same motions, and two kinds can overpower a light aeroplane. In the **lee of hills and ridges** in a strong wind, air pours down the far side. **Beneath and beside Cb and large Cu**, and under **virga** and rain shafts, rain-cooled air sinks. The signs are the lee of a ridge, lenticular cloud, dark rain shafts, virga, and a falling airspeed and climb rate at full power. The sink **can exceed the aeroplane's climb**, so cross ridges with height in hand, keep away from the lee in strong wind, and stay clear of storm cells and virga.

## Wind shear

**Wind shear** is a sudden change in wind speed or direction over a short distance. Its danger rests on one idea from [RMTC 2.3](/notes/RMTC/2.3): the aeroplane's inertia holds its groundspeed for a moment, so a change in wind becomes a change in **airspeed**, and with it lift. A loss of headwind costs airspeed and the aeroplane sinks; a gain of headwind adds airspeed and it floats.

**What makes it, and what ends it.** Thunderstorms and **microbursts**; **fronts**; **low-level inversions** with a nocturnal jet above a calm surface; **sea breeze fronts**; and **terrain**. It disperses with its cause: the inversion burns off by mid-morning, the front passes, the storm dies.

**The signs.** Cb and virga, a frontal wind change, a strong wind above a calm surface (the GPWT shows 25 kt at 2,000 ft while the windsock hangs limp), and ATIS or pilot reports of shear.

**The effect and the cure.** Sudden airspeed and lift changes near the ground, where there is no height to recover. Add a speed margin, be ready to go around at full power, and never land through a storm outflow.

![What happens to the approach when the headwind dies away near the ground?](diagram:wind-shear-on-approach "Inertia keeps the groundspeed, so the airspeed drops and the aeroplane sinks below the path with no change in attitude or power.")

## Mountain waves

When a strong wind blows across a ridge in **stable** air, the air pushed up and over the crest is denser than its new surroundings and sinks back down the lee side, overshoots, rises again and sinks again: a **standing wave** downwind of the ridge, like water flowing over a rock in a stream. The waves stay put while the air rushes through them.

**What makes it.** **Stable** air, a wind of about **20 kt or more**, within about **30 degrees** of perpendicular to the ridge, **increasing with height and steady in direction**. Stable air supplies the restoring force; a strong cross-ridge wind supplies the push. When the wind drops or turns along the ridge, or the air becomes unstable and mixes, the waves die away.

**The signs.** Smooth, lens-shaped **lenticular** cloud sitting still at the wave crests; a **cap cloud** draped on the ridge; ragged **rotor** (roll) cloud in the lee at about ridge height; dust lifted downwind. In dry air there may be no cloud at all.

**The effect.** In the lee the **down-draughts can exceed the climb rate** of a light aeroplane, and beneath the crests the **rotor** gives severe turbulence. Over the crest the strong wind is accelerated and its pressure lowered, so the **altimeter may over-read**: you are lower than it shows, just when you need the margin.

**The pilot action.** Cross ridges **well above them, 2,000 ft or more**, at **45 degrees** to the ridge so that you can turn away downhill quickly, and stay **upwind of the rotor**. In WA, waves and rotor form over the **Stirling Range** and in the lee of the **Darling Scarp** in strong easterlies.

![Where are the lift, sink and rotor downwind of a ridge?](diagram:mountain-wave-lenticular-rotor "What to notice: lift on the upwind side of each wave crest, sink on the downwind side and down the lee slope, and rotor under the crests at about ridge height.")

??? check "Check yourself: why do mountain waves need stable air, when thunderstorms need unstable air?"
    In stable air a parcel pushed up the ridge is colder than its surroundings and sinks back, overshoots and rises again, setting up the wave. In unstable air the lifted parcel keeps rising on its own, so the energy goes into convection and storms instead of a standing wave.

## Land and sea breezes

You know the mechanism from [PMTC 2.5](/notes/PMTC/2.5) and [RMTC 2.1](/notes/RMTC/2.1): by day the land heats faster than the sea and cool sea air flows in at the surface; at night the land cools below the sea and a weaker land breeze blows offshore.

**What makes them, and what ends them.** A sunny day, a **weak pressure gradient** and a land-sea temperature contrast. The sea breeze fades at sunset and the land breeze takes over at night. A **strong gradient wind suppresses both**: it holds the sea breeze offshore, which is why the Fremantle Doctor is late on the hottest easterly days.

**The signs.** A **line of Cu** inland along the sea breeze front, smoke and windsocks reversing, ripples on the water, and a sudden drop in temperature.

**The effect and the cure.** A **wind change at coastal aerodromes**, the Fremantle Doctor at Jandakot and Rottnest, so a crosswind or tailwind on the old runway; **shear and turbulence** at the front; and **haze** trapped under the marine inversion. Expect a runway change and check the ATIS or AWIS before joining.

![What drives the sea breeze, and where is its front?](diagram:sea-breeze-front "Cool sea air pushes inland under the warm land air: the wind shift, shear and any cumulus line are at its leading edge.")

## Thunderstorms

A thunderstorm needs **moisture, instability and a trigger**: heating, a front, a trough, terrain or convergence ([RMTC 2.1](/notes/RMTC/2.1)). It lives through three stages, and it dissipates when its own down-draughts cut off the warm inflow that feeds the updraught.

| Stage | Air motion | What you see |
|---|---|---|
| **Cumulus** (about 15 min) | Updraughts throughout | Growing TCu, no rain at the surface yet |
| **Mature** (about 15 to 30 min) | Updraughts and **down-draughts** side by side; begins when precipitation reaches the ground | Cb with anvil, heavy rain, hail, lightning, gust front: **the most dangerous stage** |
| **Dissipating** | Down-draughts throughout | Rain easing, anvil left behind |

![Which stage of a thunderstorm is most dangerous?](diagram:thunderstorm-life-cycle "What to notice: the mature stage begins when rain reaches the ground, with updraughts and down-draughts side by side and a gust front spreading out ahead.")

**The signs.** TCu and Cb, an anvil, lightning, `TS` and `CB` in the TAF, METAR and GAF, SIGMETs, and crackling static on the ADF.

**The hazards.** Severe turbulence and structural overstress; **hail**, including in clear air under the anvil; lightning; **severe icing** above the freezing level; heavy rain and near-zero visibility; squalls and the **gust front**, which can run **15 to 20 nm** ahead of the storm; and **microbursts**.

![What makes a thunderstorm dangerous, and how far away should you stay?](diagram:thunderstorm-hazards "The danger reaches well outside the cloud: keep at least 10 nm from a mature cell and never fly under the anvil.")

A **microburst** is a concentrated down-draught **less than about 4 km across** that hits the ground and spreads out. It lasts only about **5 to 15 minutes**, with outflow winds that can **exceed 50 kt**. Look for virga, a rain shaft with a curling foot, or a ring of blowing dust. Flying through one on approach you meet a **headwind** first, so the airspeed rises and the aeroplane climbs above the path; then the **down-draught**; then a **tailwind** that takes the airspeed away near the ground.

![What happens to an aeroplane that flies through a microburst on approach?](diagram:microburst-encounter "Headwind, then down-draught, then tailwind: the first airspeed gain is the warning, not a gift.")

**The pilot action.** Stay **10 nm or more** from a mature cell, never fly under the anvil or through a line of storms, and do not take off or land under or near a storm. If you meet the first airspeed gain near a storm, **go around immediately with full power**. Reducing power to correct the float is the instinctive response, and it leaves you slow and low when the tailwind arrives.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The mature stage starts when **rain reaches the ground**, not when the anvil appears. A cell that has just started raining is at its most dangerous, even if it looks less dramatic than the old anvil drifting off a dying storm.

??? check "Check yourself: you are 5 nm from a storm, in clear air under its anvil. Why is that not a safe place to be?"
    Hail can fall from the anvil into clear air well away from the rain shaft, and the down-draughts and gust front spread out beyond the visible cloud. That is why the stand-off is 10 nm from the cell, never under the anvil.

## Icing

Icing is not one of the six phenomena, but it is a flight consideration in every forecast you read, because the GAF gives the freezing level and the icing in cloud.

**Structural icing** needs **visible moisture**, cloud or rain, at temperatures **at or below 0 degrees** on the airframe. Cloud droplets can stay liquid well below freezing, **supercooled**, and they freeze the moment they strike the aeroplane. What builds depends on the drop size:

| Type | Conditions | Character |
|---|---|---|
| **Clear ice** | Large supercooled drops: **0 to −10 °C** in Cu and Cb, freezing rain | Hard, heavy, spreads back, the most dangerous |
| **Rime ice** | Small drops: about **−10 to −20 °C**, stratiform cloud | White, brittle, builds forward on leading edges |
| **Hoar frost** | Clear air: aeroplane parked overnight, or descending from cold into warm moist air | Feathery; on a parked aeroplane it must be removed before flight |

Large drops freeze slowly, so the water runs back before it sets into a hard, clear sheet over the wing. Small drops freeze instantly, trapping air, so rime is white and builds forward into a rough shape. Either way ice adds weight and drag, **destroys lift** and **raises the stall speed** ([RBKA 3.6](/notes/RBKA/3.6)). A VFR pilot in a light aeroplane keeps out of cloud and notes the **freezing level** in the GAF.

![Why is clear ice worse than rime?](diagram:airframe-icing-types "What to notice: large drops run back over the wing before they freeze into hard, heavy clear ice; small drops freeze on impact into white rime on the leading edge.")

**Carburettor icing** is a different problem: it forms inside the engine, in **clear air**, on a warm humid day, because the fuel evaporating and the pressure drop at the throttle cool the air well below the outside temperature. It is possible over roughly **−10 to +30 degrees** OAT, worst with high humidity, and most likely at low power. The full story is in [BAKC 3.3](/notes/BAKC/3.3); use carburettor heat as your POH directs.

![At what outside temperatures can engine ice form?](diagram:carburettor-ice-temperature-ranges "Carburettor ice needs humidity, not freezing air: it forms from well below zero to about 30 degrees.")

??? check "Check yourself: a clear, humid autumn morning, OAT 18 degrees, and a long descent at low power into Busselton. Which icing should you be thinking about, and why?"
    Carburettor icing. There is no visible moisture and the air is above freezing, so structural icing is impossible, but evaporating fuel and the pressure drop at the nearly closed throttle can cool humid air below freezing inside the carburettor, and low power makes it most likely.

## When a forecast is mandatory

Under the Part 91 MOS (chapter 7) the pilot in command must, **before every flight**, study **authorised weather forecasts and reports** for the route, the departure aerodrome, the destination and any alternate. If you studied them **more than 1 hour** before departure, you must obtain and review an update. The destination forecast must cover at least **30 minutes before to 60 minutes after your ETA**.

The one exception: if no authorised forecast is available, you may depart if you reasonably consider that the weather at the departure aerodrome will allow you to **return and land there within 1 hour** of take-off. You must then obtain a forecast for the destination **within 30 minutes** of take-off, or return. Verify these figures against the current Part 91 MOS before the exam.

The logic of each figure is protection against stale or missing information: weather changes, so an hour-old briefing is updated; an ETA is an estimate, so the forecast must cover a window around it.

??? check "Check yourself: you study the forecasts at 0700 and depart at 0830. What must you do before you go?"
    Obtain and review an update. More than 1 hour has passed since you studied the forecasts, and the Part 91 MOS requires a fresh look so that you are not flying on stale information.

## Using the products

Every product has a job, a datum and a validity, and a forecast is only useful if it covers your flight.

- **GAF** (it replaced the ARFOR in 2017; the MOS still says ARFOR): the area forecast for en route weather, cloud heights **AMSL**, **6-hour** validity.
- **TAF**: the aerodrome forecast, cloud **above aerodrome level**. Check that its validity period covers your ETA window.
- **TTF** (trend forecast): attached to the METAR or SPECI at major aerodromes, valid for **3 hours** from the observation, and it **supersedes the TAF** for that period.
- **METAR and SPECI**: observations, not forecasts. They tell you whether the forecast is coming true.
- **SIGMET**: significant en route hazards (thunderstorms, severe turbulence, severe icing, severe mountain waves, volcanic ash, tropical cyclones), valid **up to 4 hours**. **AIRMET**: hazards to low-level flights **below 10,000 ft** not already in the area forecast.

The decoding itself is in [RMTC 2.2](/notes/RMTC/2.2). Two definitions decide most go or no-go questions: **TEMPO** spells last **30 to 60 minutes** each, and **INTER** spells **less than 30 minutes**. In both, the worse conditions can be there at any moment in the period, and you cannot choose to arrive in a gap.

![When does each part of the TAF apply?](diagram:taf-change-groups "FM is permanent, TEMPO spells last 30 to 60 minutes, INTER less than 30: plan for the worst group that touches your window.")

Applying a TAF is three steps. First, **validity**: does the TAF cover the ETA window? Second, **conditions**: for each group that touches the window, is it VMC for your class of airspace? Third, **hazards**: is there TS, CB, a gust or a wind change that matters even if it is VMC?

![What are the VMC minima in each class of airspace?](diagram:vmc-minima-by-airspace "In Class G at or below 3,000 ft AMSL or 1,000 ft AGL, whichever is higher, you need 5,000 m and to stay clear of cloud in sight of the ground or water.")

!!! example "Worked example: is a VFR arrival possible at Busselton?"
    Given: Busselton (Class G, elevation about 60 ft), ETA 1130 UTC, and this TAF:

    ```
    TAF YBLN 140500Z 1406/1418 34015G25KT 9999 -RA BKN025
      FM141000 27020G32KT 6000 SHRA BKN012
      TEMPO 1411/1415 2000 TSRA SCT008 BKN015CB
    ```

    1. ETA window, 30 minutes before to 60 minutes after: **1100 to 1230 UTC**.
    2. Validity: 1406/1418 is 0600 to 1800 UTC on the 14th, so the TAF **covers the window**.
    3. Main conditions from 1000: visibility **6,000 m**, above the 5,000 m minimum; cloud **BKN at 1,200 ft** above the aerodrome. Below 3,000 ft AMSL in Class G you need only stay clear of cloud in sight of the ground or water, so this is **VMC**, but a 1,000 ft circuit is only **200 ft** under the base.
    4. TEMPO 1411/1415: from **1100 to 1500**, spells of **2,000 m in thunderstorms with rain** and Cb: **below VMC** and a storm hazard.
    5. The window, 1100 to 1230, sits **inside the TEMPO**.
    6. Wind: from 340 at 15 gusting 25 to 270 at 20 gusting 32. That is anticlockwise, so it **backs**: a cold front in Southern Hemisphere terms ([PMTC 2.6](/notes/PMTC/2.6)).

    Either arrive **before 1000**, delay, or carry the holding fuel and alternate that AIP ENR 1.1 and the Part 91 MOS require. With a cold front and thunderstorms at the destination, the sensible plan is to be on the ground well before it.

Try one before opening the solution. A different day, the same kind of front:

```
TAF YBLN 150500Z 1506/1518 33012G22KT 9999 -RA BKN030
  FM150800 26018G28KT 7000 SHRA BKN014
  TEMPO 1509/1512 3000 TSRA SCT010 BKN018CB
```

Your ETA is 0815 UTC. Is the arrival VMC throughout your window, and what is the latest ETA that keeps the window clear of the TEMPO?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    The window is 0745 to 0915 UTC, and the TAF, valid 0600 to 1800 UTC on the 15th, covers it. Before 0800 the main conditions are 10 km or more in light rain with a broken base at 3,000 ft: VMC. From 0800, 7,000 m in showers with a broken base at 1,400 ft: VMC in Class G below 3,000 ft AMSL, though gusting 28 kt. But the TEMPO starts at 0900, inside the window, with 3,000 m in thunderstorms and Cb: below VMC. So no, you must plan for the TEMPO. The window ends 60 minutes after the ETA, so the latest ETA that keeps it clear of the TEMPO is **0800 UTC** (window 0730 to 0900, ending just as the TEMPO begins); allow a margin and aim earlier. The wind backs from 330 to 260 with the front.

On the day, the latest **METAR or SPECI**, and the **TTF** where there is one, tell you whether the front is early or late. A SPECI showing thunderstorms already over the destination settles the question.

## Special AIREPs

Routine AIREPs are not normally required of VFR pilots, but you are often the only observer of the weather where you are. A **special AIREP** must be made as soon as practicable when you meet (AIP GEN 3.5):

- **severe turbulence**;
- **severe icing**;
- **severe mountain wave**;
- **thunderstorms**, with or without hail, that are obscured, embedded, widespread or in squall lines;
- **heavy dust storm or sand storm**;
- **volcanic ash** cloud, or pre-eruption volcanic activity.

The pattern is that each is a hazard that could bring down the next aircraft, and most are things the forecasters cannot see from the ground: embedded storms, severe icing in cloud, wave over a range. You should also report any other weather that may affect other aircraft, for example moderate turbulence or icing, wind shear, or unforecast low cloud or poor visibility, but those are a should, not a must. The report goes to ATC or FLIGHTWATCH: aircraft identification, position, time, level, the phenomenon and, if possible, the outside air temperature and wind.

??? check "Check yourself: why does the special AIREP list include embedded thunderstorms but not an isolated Cb you can see from 30 nm?"
    An isolated, visible Cb is already seen by every pilot nearby and is usually in the forecast. Embedded or obscured storms are hidden in cloud, so a pilot's report may be the only warning the next aircraft and the forecasters get.

## Bringing it together

A Saturday in May: Jandakot to Busselton, departing 0830 WST (0030 UTC). The GAF shows a cold front reaching the coast in the afternoon, with Cb and moderate turbulence along it. You study the forecasts at 0715 WST and check them again just before departure, because more than an hour has passed. The Busselton TAF covers your arrival window with a strengthening north-westerly and broken cloud at 3,000 ft: VMC. Its TEMPO for thunderstorms starts in the afternoon, so you plan to return by early afternoon. En route the north-westerly over the Darling Range is bumpy: you slow to the turbulence speed and hold attitude. On the way home the line of Cb is visible to the west; you keep well over 10 nm from it, and at Jandakot the ATIS shows a gusty wind, so you add half the gust factor. Had you met severe turbulence, you would have made a special AIREP.

## Key points

- **2.7.1 (i) Turbulence**: mechanical (strong wind over rough ground), thermal (heating) or frontal; eases as the wind drops or the heating stops. Slow to VA or below, hold attitude, avoid the lee of hills.
- **2.7.1 (ii) Wind shear**: storms, fronts, inversions, sea breeze fronts, terrain. Inertia turns a wind change into an airspeed change; add margin and be ready to go around at full power.
- **2.7.1 (iii) Mountain waves**: stable air, 20 kt or more within about 30 degrees of perpendicular, increasing with height. Lenticular, cap and rotor cloud. Sink beyond the climb rate, rotor turbulence, altimeter over-reads; cross 2,000 ft or more above, at 45 degrees, upwind of the rotor.
- **2.7.1 (iv) Land and sea breezes**: sunny, weak gradient; a strong gradient suppresses them. Cu line, reversing smoke and windsocks. Expect a runway change, shear at the front and trapped haze.
- **2.7.1 (v) Thunderstorms**: moisture, instability, trigger. Mature stage (rain at the ground) is worst. Gust front 15 to 20 nm ahead; microburst under 4 km, 5 to 15 minutes, over 50 kt. Stay 10 nm away, never under the anvil; at the first airspeed gain, go around at full power.
- **2.7.1 (vi) Down-draughts**: lee of ridges and beneath Cb, Cu, virga and rain shafts; can exceed the climb rate.
- **Icing**: structural needs visible moisture at or below 0 degrees (clear 0 to −10, rime −10 to −20, hoar frost in clear air); carburettor ice from about −10 to +30 degrees in humid air.
- **2.7.2** Study authorised forecasts and reports before every flight; update if more than 1 hour old; destination forecast covers 30 minutes before to 60 minutes after the ETA; the 1-hour return and 30-minute exception.
- **2.7.3** GAF AMSL, 6 hours; TAF above the aerodrome; TTF 3 hours, supersedes the TAF; SIGMET up to 4 hours; AIRMET below 10,000 ft. Validity, then conditions, then hazards, for every group touching the window.
- **2.7.4** Special AIREP: severe turbulence, severe icing, severe mountain wave, obscured, embedded, widespread or squall-line thunderstorms, heavy dust or sand storm, volcanic ash.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Mountain waves need **stable** air and a strong wind across the ridge; thunderstorms need **unstable** moist air and a trigger. The worst thunderstorm stage is the mature stage, which starts when rain reaches the ground. For TAF questions, work out the ETA window (30 minutes before to 60 minutes after) and test every TEMPO and INTER group that touches it. The special AIREP list is about **severe** conditions: severe turbulence, severe icing, severe mountain wave, significant thunderstorms, heavy dust or sand storm, volcanic ash. "Moderate turbulence" alone is a should, not a must.
