## What the exam expects (PMTC 2.3)

- **2.3.1** Name and classify clouds by the height of their base and by the ten genera.
- **2.3.2** Give the standard abbreviation for each cloud type, and explain how cloud amount is reported.
- **2.3.3** Describe the weather that goes with each type of cloud.

## Why this matters

The sky is a weather report you can read without a briefing. A morning of turreted altocumulus over the wheatbelt warns of storms by afternoon; a halo round the sun over Rottnest says a front is on its way; a flat sheet of stratus clinging to the Darling Scarp says the route to York is closed for now. Each cloud is the visible end of a process, air rising and cooling in a particular way, and once you know the process you know the weather that comes with it: the bumps, the rain, the icing and the visibility. The exam asks you to classify clouds by height and genus, to decode cloud groups, to match each cloud with its weather, and to estimate a cumulus base from the temperature and dew point.

## How cloud forms: cooling air to its dew point

You met the essentials in [PMTC 2.2](/notes/PMTC/2.2): air can hold only so much water vapour, the **dew point** is the temperature at which it would become saturated, and cooling it below that point makes some of the vapour condense. A cloud is simply a mass of tiny water droplets or ice crystals, each formed this way and each so small that the slightest upward movement of air keeps it aloft.

The droplets need something to form on. The air is full of microscopic particles of sea salt, dust and smoke, and vapour condenses readily onto these **condensation nuclei** ([PMTC 2.1](/notes/PMTC/2.1)), so cloud forms as soon as the air saturates.

So the question "why is there a cloud here?" always becomes "what cooled this air to its dew point?". Near the ground the answer can be contact with cold ground, which gives fog ([PMTC 2.4](/notes/PMTC/2.4)). In the free air the answer is almost always **lifting**. Rising air moves into lower pressure, expands and cools adiabatically: at the dry adiabatic lapse rate (**DALR**) of **3 °C per 1,000 ft** while it is unsaturated, and once cloud has formed at the saturated rate (**SALR**) of about **1.5 °C per 1,000 ft**, because condensation releases latent heat. The level at which a rising parcel first reaches its dew point is the **condensation level**, and that is the cloud base.

## Five ways to lift air

Air rises for one of five reasons, and each leaves a signature in the shape of its cloud.

**Convection.** On a hot afternoon the sun heats the ground, the ground heats the air touching it, and bubbles of warm, light air break away and rise as **thermals**. Each thermal cools as it rises, and where it reaches its dew point a cumulus cloud forms on top of it. Because the thermals over one area start with much the same temperature and moisture, they reach their dew point at much the same height: a field of cumulus has flat bases at one level, with blue sky between the clouds where the air is sinking. This is the cloud of a summer afternoon inland, from fair-weather cumulus (Cu) to towering cumulus (TCu) and cumulonimbus (Cb).

![What do thermals look like from the cockpit?](diagram:visible-signs-of-thermals "Every cumulus sits on top of a thermal, and the flat bases mark the level where the rising air reaches its dew point.")

**Orographic lifting.** When wind meets a slope or a range, the air has to go up and over. A moist westerly behind a winter front meets the Darling Scarp, is forced up a thousand feet or so, and if it reaches its dew point on the way it caps the hills in a layer of stratus or stratocumulus while the coastal plain stays clear. The cloud sits over the high ground on the windward side, which is exactly where the terrain is closest to you. In the lee of a range in a strong wind, the air can oscillate in standing waves, and smooth, lens-shaped **lenticular** clouds form in the crests ([RMTC 2.3](/notes/RMTC/2.3) explains the turbulence they mark).

![Why is low cloud on the hills so dangerous?](diagram:low-cloud-and-rising-terrain "Orographic cloud forms where the ground rises, so the gap between base and terrain closes just where you need it.")

**Frontal lifting.** Where warm and cold air masses meet, the lighter warm air ends up on top. At a **warm front** it slides gently up over a wedge of cold air, over hundreds of kilometres, and gives widespread layer cloud. At a **cold front** the advancing cold air drives under the warm air like a snowplough and lifts it steeply, giving a narrow band of cumulus and cumulonimbus. The details belong to [PMTC 2.6](/notes/PMTC/2.6).

**Convergence.** When air flows in from different directions it cannot pile up at the surface, so it goes up. This happens in lows and troughs, and on a small scale along the **sea breeze front**, where cool sea air pushes inland under the warm land air and lifts it. A line of cumulus a few miles inland from the coast on a summer afternoon marks the sea breeze front.

![Where does the cumulus line along the coast come from?](diagram:sea-breeze-front "The sea breeze wedges under the warm inland air and lifts it, so a line of cumulus marks where the two meet.")

**Turbulence (mechanical mixing).** A strong wind over rough ground stirs the lowest few thousand feet thoroughly. Moist air from near the surface is carried up, cools at the DALR, and if the mixing reaches the condensation level, a layer of cloud forms at the top of the mixed layer. With an inversion above acting as a lid, the result is a flat, often lumpy sheet of stratus or stratocumulus.

### Layer or heap: stability decides

The lifting process starts the air moving; **stability** decides what it does next. In [PMTC 2.2](/notes/PMTC/2.2) you saw that a parcel in stable air, nudged upwards, ends up colder than its surroundings and wants to sink back. Lift stable air and it rises only as far as it is pushed, so the cloud that forms spreads sideways in a flat sheet: **stratiform** cloud. Lift unstable air and, once it is warmer than its surroundings, it keeps rising on its own and the cloud grows upwards in heaps and towers: **cumuliform** cloud.

That one distinction carries through the rest of the lesson. Layer cloud means stable air: smooth flying, steady precipitation, often poor visibility. Heap cloud means unstable air: bumps, showers, good visibility between them. The same orographic lifting over the Scarp gives a flat cap of stratus on a stable winter day and towering cumulus on an unstable one.

??? check "Check yourself: why does a field of fair-weather cumulus have flat bases all at one height?"
    Each cloud sits on a thermal, and the thermals over the same ground start with roughly the same temperature and dew point. Rising and cooling at the same rate, they all reach their dew point at about the same height, and that condensation level is the flat base.

## Estimating the cumulus base from the dew point spread

The METAR gives you two numbers that together predict the base of the afternoon's cumulus: the temperature and the dew point.

A thermal leaves the ground at the surface temperature, $T$, carrying air whose dew point is $T_d$. As it rises unsaturated it cools at the DALR, **3 °C per 1,000 ft**. Its dew point also falls, but only slowly, about **0.5 °C per 1,000 ft**, because the expanding air spreads its vapour through a larger volume. The temperature is therefore catching up on the dew point at about $3 - 0.5 = 2.5$ °C per 1,000 ft. When the gap reaches zero, the air is saturated and the cloud base is there.

So every degree of spread at the surface takes $1{,}000 \div 2.5 = 400$ ft of climb to close, which gives the rule of thumb:

$$\text{Cloud base (ft AGL)} \approx (T - T_d) \times 400$$

![Why 400 ft per degree of spread?](diagram:dew-point-and-cloud-base "What to notice: the temperature falls 3 degrees per 1,000 ft but the dew point falls 0.5, so the spread closes 2.5 degrees per 1,000 ft: 24/16 gives a base of about 3,200 ft.")

!!! example "Worked example: the cumulus base on a spring morning"
    Given: a METAR temperature and dew point of `24/16`.

    1. Dew point spread: $24 - 16 = \mathbf{8\ °C}$.
    2. Rate at which the spread closes: $3 - 0.5 = \mathbf{2.5\ °C}$ per 1,000 ft, so **400 ft** per degree.
    3. Cloud base: $8 \times 400 = \mathbf{3{,}200\ ft}$ AGL.

    When the thermals get going, expect cumulus bases about 3,200 ft above the aerodrome. Cruising at 4,500 ft would put you in or among the cloud; plan to cruise below it, or above it with the VMC cloud clearances in mind.

!!! example "Worked example: a dry afternoon on the coast"
    Given: Jandakot early afternoon, METAR `29/13`.

    1. Spread: $29 - 13 = \mathbf{16\ °C}$.
    2. Cloud base: $16 \times 400 = \mathbf{6{,}400\ ft}$ AGL.

    A high base and plenty of room under it. The drier the air, the higher the base.

Now try one in the Goldfields. On a summer afternoon Kalgoorlie reports `36/06`. Estimate the base of any convective cloud, and say what that base suggests about the storms that might form.

??? solution "Show the solution"
    Spread $36 - 6 = 30$ °C; base $\approx 30 \times 400 = $ **12,000 ft AGL**. Storms with bases that high are typical of the dry interior. Their rain falls through thousands of feet of very dry air and evaporates before it reaches the ground (**virga**), and the evaporation chills the air and drives strong, dry downdraughts that raise dust at the surface.

Some texts simplify the rule to $(T - T_d) \div 3 \times 1{,}000$, about 330 ft per degree. That version uses the DALR alone and ignores the fall in dew point, so it gives a slightly lower base: in the Jandakot example, $16 \div 3 \times 1{,}000 \approx 5{,}300$ ft instead of 6,400 ft. Use the method the question states; if it states none, 400 ft per degree is the physically sound figure.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The rule works only for **convective** cloud: cumulus formed by thermals rising from the surface where $T$ and $T_d$ were measured. It tells you nothing about the base of frontal or orographic cloud, and the answer is height **above the ground** at that station, not above sea level. A small spread, 2 or 3 degrees, means a low base and possible fog.

??? check "Check yourself: why does each degree of spread give 400 ft of cloud base rather than about 330 ft?"
    The rising air cools at 3 degrees per 1,000 ft, but its dew point also falls, by about 0.5 degrees per 1,000 ft. The spread therefore closes at only about 2.5 degrees per 1,000 ft, which is 400 ft per degree.

## The ten cloud genera (2.3.1, 2.3.2)

Cloud names rest on four Latin words. **Cumulus** means a heap: cloud built upwards by rising air. **Stratus** means a layer: cloud spread sideways. **Cirrus** means a curl or a wisp of hair: the fibrous look of ice crystals. **Nimbus** means rain-bearing. To these, the prefix **alto-** (literally "high", but used for the middle level) marks a cloud of the middle layer.

Combine form with the height of the base and you get the **ten genera**, each with a two-letter abbreviation. The height bands below are approximate for Australian latitudes; the bands are higher in the tropics, where the troposphere is deeper ([PMTC 2.1](/notes/PMTC/2.1)).

![Where does each of the ten cloud genera sit?](diagram:cloud-families-by-height "What to notice: three bands by base height, high above about 20,000 ft, middle about 6,500 to 20,000 ft, low below 6,500 ft, plus Cu and Cb, which start low and grow tall.")

### High cloud: above about 20,000 ft

At these heights the air is far below freezing, so high cloud is made of **ice crystals**. It is thin, white and silky, and you can usually see the sun through it.

- **Cirrus (Ci)**: white wisps, streaks and hooks, sometimes called mares' tails.
- **Cirrocumulus (Cc)**: small white ripples or grains with no shading.
- **Cirrostratus (Cs)**: a thin white veil. Its ice crystals bend sunlight into a **halo** round the sun or moon.

### Middle cloud: about 6,500 to 20,000 ft

Middle cloud is mostly water droplets, often **supercooled** below 0 °C.

- **Altocumulus (Ac)**: grey-white rolls or patches, larger than cirrocumulus and shaded. With small turrets rising from a common base it is **altocumulus castellanus**.
- **Altostratus (As)**: a grey or bluish sheet. The sun shows through it dimly, "as through ground glass", with **no halo**, because the cloud is made of droplets, not ice.
- **Nimbostratus (Ns)**: a thick, dark, featureless layer with continuous rain. Classed as middle cloud, but its base often lowers to near the ground.

### Low cloud: below about 6,500 ft

- **Stratocumulus (Sc)**: a grey layer of lumps, rolls or patches, often with blue sky between them.
- **Stratus (St)**: a uniform grey layer with a very low base. Where it touches high ground the result is **hill fog**.

### Cloud with a low base and great vertical extent

- **Cumulus (Cu)**: separate heaps with flat bases and cauliflower tops. When the tops grow tall and bubbling it is towering cumulus, reported as **TCu**.
- **Cumulonimbus (Cb)**: the thunderstorm cloud, a huge tower that can reach the tropopause, where it spreads into a flat **anvil** top.

![What is in and under a cumulonimbus?](diagram:thunderstorm-hazards "The cumulonimbus is the one cloud you never fly near: turbulence, hail, lightning and icing in it, wind shear and microbursts under it.")

| Level (base) | Genus | Abbreviation | Look |
|---|---|---|---|
| **High**, above about 20,000 ft | Cirrus | **Ci** | White wisps and hooks of ice crystals |
| | Cirrocumulus | **Cc** | Small white ripples or grains, no shading |
| | Cirrostratus | **Cs** | Thin white veil, produces a **halo** round the sun or moon |
| **Middle**, about 6,500 to 20,000 ft | Altocumulus | **Ac** | Grey-white rolls or patches, larger than Cc |
| | Altostratus | **As** | Grey sheet, sun seen "as through ground glass", no halo |
| | Nimbostratus | **Ns** | Thick dark layer with continuous rain; base often lowers to near the ground |
| **Low**, below about 6,500 ft | Stratocumulus | **Sc** | Grey lumpy layer or rolls |
| | Stratus | **St** | Uniform grey layer, very low base, hill fog |
| **Low base, great vertical extent** | Cumulus | **Cu** | Flat base, cauliflower top; **TCu** when towering |

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Telling cirrostratus from altostratus: both are featureless sheets with the sun showing through. The halo decides it. Cirrostratus is ice and makes a halo; altostratus is mainly water droplets and does not, so the sun looks blurred as if through frosted glass.

??? check "Check yourself: why is high cloud white and thin enough to see the sun through, while nimbostratus is dark?"
    High cloud is made of sparse ice crystals, because the air up there is far below freezing, so light passes through and is scattered white. Nimbostratus is a deep, dense mass of water droplets that absorbs and blocks most of the light from above.

## Reporting cloud amount (2.3.2)

Two things are reported for each cloud layer: **how much** of the sky it covers, and **how high** its base is.

The amount is measured in **oktas**, eighths of the sky. Imagine the dome of the sky divided into eight equal slices and count how many slices the cloud would fill if you pushed it all together. The oktas are then grouped into four codes:

| Code | Meaning | Oktas |
|---|---|---|
| **FEW** | Few | 1 to 2 |
| **SCT** | Scattered | 3 to 4 |
| **BKN** | Broken | 5 to 7 |
| **OVC** | Overcast | 8 |

![How much sky does each cloud code mean?](diagram:cloud-amounts-in-oktas "FEW 1 to 2, SCT 3 to 4, BKN 5 to 7, OVC 8 oktas: only BKN and OVC make a ceiling.")

The base follows the amount as three digits in **hundreds of feet**: `SCT035` is scattered cloud, 3 to 4 oktas, based at 3,500 ft. A layer of BKN or OVC is a **ceiling**, because at that amount the sky is more cloud than gap and you cannot count on finding a hole to climb or descend through.

The datum depends on the product, and this is where pilots slip. In a **TAF or METAR** the base is **above aerodrome level**: the people using it are about to land or take off there. In the **GAF** (graphical area forecast) it is **above mean sea level**, because the GAF covers a wide area of varying terrain and your altimeter on QNH reads altitude.

![Is that cloud base above the aerodrome or above sea level?](diagram:cloud-height-datum "The same base is a smaller number in the TAF than in the GAF, by the aerodrome elevation.")

Only two cloud types are named in a TAF or METAR, because only two are a direct hazard: **CB** and **TCU**, added after the height (`SCT050CB`). Every other cloud is just amount and height. When there is no cloud that matters, the report says **NSC**, nil significant cloud; an automatic station that sees nothing reports **NCD**, no cloud detected. Decoding a whole TAF or METAR is practised in [RMTC 2.2](/notes/RMTC/2.2).

!!! example "Worked example: is there a ceiling, and where is it above sea level?"
    Given: a METAR for Northam (elevation about 500 ft) with cloud groups `FEW020 BKN045`.

    1. `FEW020`: 1 to 2 oktas based at **2,000 ft** above the aerodrome. Not a ceiling.
    2. `BKN045`: 5 to 7 oktas based at **4,500 ft** above the aerodrome. This is the **ceiling**.
    3. Ceiling above sea level: $4{,}500 + 500 = \mathbf{5{,}000\ ft}$ AMSL.

    To compare it with a GAF or a cruising altitude on QNH, add the elevation first.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    A TAF and a GAF giving "the same" base number do not describe the same cloud height unless the aerodrome is at sea level. At an inland aerodrome the TAF figure is smaller than the GAF figure for the same base, by the aerodrome elevation.

## Weather from each cloud (2.3.3)

Every cloud's weather follows from two things you now know: whether it formed in stable or unstable air, and whether it is made of ice or water.

**The cirrus family** is thin ice cloud, so it gives no precipitation that reaches the ground and no icing: ice crystals do not stick to an airframe. Its value is as a **warning**: cirrus thickening into cirrostratus over a few hours is often the first sign of a warm front or a jet stream.

**Middle cloud** is water, often supercooled, so it can ice an airframe. Altocumulus is usually dry with light bumps, but **altocumulus castellanus** shows that the middle layer is unstable: seen in the morning, it warns of thunderstorms once surface heating adds its push from below. Altostratus that thickens and lowers through the day means a front is approaching, and it becomes nimbostratus, the deep rain cloud of stable air: hours of **continuous rain**, poor visibility, icing in the deep supercooled cloud and ragged **stratus fractus** forming in the moist air beneath.

**Low layer cloud** is stable and shallow. Stratus gives **drizzle** and smooth air, but its threat is its base, low on the hills. Stratocumulus is usually fair, with a bumpy ride below.

**Heap cloud** is unstable. Small cumulus gives only bumps; large cumulus gives **showers**, with good visibility between them because unstable air carries haze away. Cumulonimbus brings the lot: **heavy showers, hail and thunderstorms**, severe icing and turbulence, lightning, microbursts and gust fronts ([PMTC 2.7](/notes/PMTC/2.7)).

| Cloud | Precipitation | Icing | Turbulence | Visibility and other |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ci, Cs | None | No (ice crystals) | Nil (Cc may give light) | Increasing Ci then Cs often heralds a warm front or a jet stream |
| Ac | Usually none | Light | Light | **Ac castellanus** (turreted) in the morning warns of afternoon thunderstorms |
| As | Light continuous rain | Light to moderate | Little | Thickening and lowering As means a front approaching |
| Ns | **Continuous moderate to heavy rain** | Moderate to severe | Little | Visibility poor, ragged low cloud (St fractus) beneath |
| Sc | Light drizzle or light showers | Light | Light | Usually fair, bumpy below |
| St | **Drizzle** | Light | Smooth | Very low base, hills in cloud, poor visibility |
| Cu | Fair-weather Cu none; large Cu **showers** | Moderate when large | Moderate below and in it | Good visibility outside showers |
| Cb | **Heavy showers, hail, thunderstorms** | Severe | Severe | Lightning, microbursts, squalls, gust fronts ([PMTC 2.7](/notes/PMTC/2.7)) |

### What the precipitation tells you

The type of precipitation is a fingerprint of the cloud, and so of the air.

**Drizzle** is very fine droplets falling close together. Small drops can only grow in shallow cloud with weak lift, so drizzle comes **only from stratus and stratocumulus**. It is a sign of a stable, moist, low-based layer, and often of poor visibility.

**Continuous rain** falls steadily for hours from **nimbostratus and thick altostratus**. Deep layer cloud, lifted gently over a wide area by a front, keeps producing it. It means stable air and a large-scale system.

**Showers** start and stop suddenly, are often heavy while they last, and alternate with clear spells. They fall from **cumulus and cumulonimbus**, each shower being one cell. Showers mean unstable air.

**Hail** is ice that has been carried up and down through a cloud by strong updraughts, adding a layer each trip, until it is too heavy to hold. Only a **cumulonimbus** has updraughts strong enough to do that, so hail means Cb.

**Virga** is precipitation that **evaporates before it reaches the ground**, visible as grey streaks hanging below a cloud base. It means dry air beneath the cloud, and, because evaporation absorbs latent heat ([PMTC 2.2](/notes/PMTC/2.2)), it marks chilled air sinking: a downdraught.

??? check "Check yourself: it has rained steadily all morning, but after lunch the rain turns into heavy bursts with sunny spells between. What has changed about the air?"
    Steady rain comes from layer cloud in stable air, typically ahead of or at a front. Showers come from heap cloud in unstable air. The change suggests the front has passed and you are now in the colder, unstable air behind it.

## Bringing it together

A winter Saturday, and you plan to fly from Jandakot to Albany on Sunday. In the afternoon wisps of cirrus spread from the west, and by evening they have merged into a milky veil with a halo round the moon: cirrostratus, ice well above 20,000 ft, telling you a front is coming. By Sunday morning the halo has gone and the sun is a blur behind a grey sheet of altostratus. By mid-morning the base is low and dark, it is raining steadily and stratus fractus is forming underneath: nimbostratus, with poor visibility, the hills in cloud and icing above the freezing level. The flight is off. By Monday the front has passed: cumulus with sunny gaps and sharp showers, excellent visibility outside them, and Jandakot reporting `17/09`. The 8 degree spread puts the cumulus base at about 3,200 ft above the ground, so you plan to cruise below it, where the terrain allows, and steer round the showers, provided no `CB` appears in the TAF. Every cloud in that sequence is a lifting process and a stability state you can now name.

??? check "Check yourself: a TAF contains `SCT040CB`. What kind of air does that indicate, and what three hazards would you expect near the cloud?"
    Unstable air, because cumulonimbus is cumuliform cloud growing upwards on its own buoyancy. Expect severe turbulence, hail and heavy showers, along with lightning, severe icing and microburst wind shear: the reason only CB and TCU are named in a TAF.

## Key points

- **2.3.1** Cloud forms when lifting (convection, orographic, frontal, convergence, turbulence) cools air to its dew point; stable air gives layer cloud, unstable air heap cloud. The ten genera by base: high (above about 20,000 ft) Ci, Cc, Cs; middle (about 6,500 to 20,000 ft) Ac, As, Ns; low (below about 6,500 ft) Sc, St; low base with great vertical extent Cu and Cb.
- **2.3.2** Abbreviations Ci, Cc, Cs, Ac, As, Ns, Sc, St, Cu (TCu), Cb. Amount in oktas: FEW 1 to 2, SCT 3 to 4, BKN 5 to 7, OVC 8, base in hundreds of feet; BKN and OVC are a ceiling. TAF and METAR bases are above the aerodrome, GAF bases above sea level. Only CB and TCU are named; NSC means nil significant cloud, NCD no cloud detected. Convective cloud base is roughly (T − Td) × 400 ft AGL, a rule of thumb.
- **2.3.3** Ci and Cs herald fronts; Ac castellanus warns of storms; Ns gives continuous rain and icing; St drizzle and low bases; Cu showers and bumps; Cb heavy showers, hail, thunderstorms, severe icing and turbulence. Drizzle from St and Sc, continuous rain from Ns and As, showers from Cu and Cb, hail only from Cb, virga means dry air and downdraughts.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Showers come from heap cloud, continuous rain from layer cloud, drizzle from stratus. "Hail" in a question means cumulonimbus, every time. A halo means cirrostratus. For a cloud base question, multiply the spread by 400 unless the question gives another method, and remember the answer is above the ground, for convective cloud only. In a TAF or METAR the base is above the aerodrome; in the GAF it is above sea level.
