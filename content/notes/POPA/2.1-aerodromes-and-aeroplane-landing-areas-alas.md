## What the exam expects (POPA 2.1)

- **2.1.1** Explain the terms take-off safety speed, take-off distance available (TODA), take-off distance required (TODR), landing distance available (LDA) and landing distance required (LDR), and use them to decide whether a runway is long enough.
- **2.1.2** Decide whether a given aeroplane landing area (ALA) is suitable for a safe take-off and landing, using the guidelines in CAAP 92-1 (now AC 91-02).

## Why this matters

Much of Western Australian private flying ends on a strip with no ERSA entry: a farm runway near York for a family lunch, a station strip in the Murchison, a mate's paddock outside Esperance. There is no aerodrome operator, no published distances and no one to tell you the grass is long or the far end is soft. The pilot in command decides whether the place is suitable, and the decision is made with five defined distances and speeds and a short list of guidelines from CASA. The exam asks both halves: what each term means and where it is measured from and to, and whether a described strip passes. This lesson builds the terms from a picture of an actual take-off and landing, then works through the ALA guidelines one at a time, with the reason behind each.

## The take-off, from brake release to 50 ft

Picture a take-off from the threshold of a 700 m grass strip. You release the brakes at full power and the aeroplane accelerates along the ground until it reaches lift-off speed. That part is the **ground run**. Then it lifts off and climbs, still accelerating, until it is 50 ft above the runway surface. That second part is the **airborne segment**. Together they are the take-off distance.

Why stop counting at 50 ft? Because the aeroplane is not safe from the ground until it is clear of the things that sit on the ground: fences, stock, a parked tractor, the trees at the end of the paddock. Performance data is measured to an imaginary **screen** 50 ft high, and every take-off chart in the CASA workbook and every POH take-off table stops there. Above 50 ft the problem becomes the climb gradient, which is a separate question (see [POPA 2.5](/notes/POPA/2.5)).

### Take-off safety speed

At the 50 ft screen the aeroplane must be flying at a speed that gives it a safe margin above the stall. That speed is the **take-off safety speed** (TOSS). For light aeroplanes it is at least **1.2 times the stall speed in the take-off configuration**, and the performance charts are based on reaching it at the screen height.

The margin is there because the first few hundred feet after take-off are where the aeroplane is most exposed. A gust that drops the airspeed, a wind shear as you climb out of the shelter of the trees, or a turn to avoid a bird all raise the speed at which the wing stalls (see [RBKA 3.6](/notes/RBKA/3.6)). Twenty percent above the stall speed is enough to absorb an ordinary gust, or to bank to about 45 degrees in a level turn, before the wing reaches its critical angle. That figure follows from the turn formula: a stall speed 1.2 times higher means a load factor of $1.2^2 = 1.44$, and $1/\cos\theta = 1.44$ at a bank of about 46 degrees.

The CASA charts print the TOSS in their conditions box. Fig 3 in the workbook, for example, gives **60 KIAS** with 10 degrees of flap and full throttle; the ECHO chart (Fig 12) gives a scale on which the TOSS varies with weight. A heavier aeroplane stalls at a higher speed, so it needs a higher TOSS.

!!! example "Worked example: the minimum take-off safety speed"
    Given: the POH gives a stall speed of 47 kt IAS with take-off flap at maximum weight.

    1. Minimum TOSS: $1.2 \times 47 = \mathbf{56.4\ kt}$.
    2. Round up, never down: **57 kt** at the very least.

    If the POH or the chart gives a higher figure, fly that. The 1.2 factor is a floor, not a target, and the manufacturer's number already includes it.

??? check "Check yourself: why is the take-off distance measured to 50 ft and not to the point of lift-off?"
    Because lifting off is not the same as being safe. The aeroplane is clear of fences, stock and vehicles only once it has climbed above them, so performance is measured to a 50 ft screen at the take-off safety speed.

## Required against available

Every distance comparison in this subtopic has the same shape. One number comes from the aeroplane: how much it **needs** today. The other comes from the runway: how much it **has**. The first must never exceed the second.

### Take-off distance required

The **take-off distance required** (TODR) is the distance from the start of the take-off run to the point where the aeroplane reaches **50 ft** at the take-off safety speed. You get it from the performance chart for the day's weight, pressure height, temperature, wind, slope and surface ([POPA 2.4](/notes/POPA/2.4) shows how to work the charts). It includes the safety factor built into the chart.

That last phrase matters. The distance a test pilot achieves in a new aeroplane on a perfect day is the **measured** or **unfactored** distance. You are not a test pilot, your aeroplane is not new and your strip is not a test runway, so the measured figure is multiplied by a **factor** to give a distance a line pilot can rely on. The CASA charts use a factored distance: each prints its own factor in the conditions box (1.15 on Fig 3, for instance), and the chart output already includes it. The POH figures are typically unfactored, and AC 91-02 applies a factor to them. Read the chart or table before you use it, so you know which kind of number you have.

### Take-off distance available

The **take-off distance available** (TODA) is the length of runway, the **take-off run available**, plus any **clearway**. A clearway is an area beyond the end of the runway, free of obstacles, over which the aeroplane may complete its climb to 50 ft. It is not for rolling on; it is airspace over flat, clear ground. At a licensed aerodrome the TODA is published in ERSA (the runway distances supplement). At an ALA there is no publication, so the pilot measures it.

![Where do you find a runway's published length and surface?](diagram:ersa-entry-anatomy "The runway table gives length and width in metres, surface and slope; for an ALA you measure these yourself.")

The rule is simple: **TODR must not exceed TODA.** If it does, the aeroplane will not be at 50 ft and the safety speed by the end of the available distance, and whatever is beyond the end of the strip is in the way.

!!! example "Worked example: is the runway long enough to take off?"
    Given: the take-off chart gives a TODR of 640 m for today's conditions. The strip is 700 m long with no clearway.

    1. TODA: $700 + 0 = \mathbf{700\ m}$.
    2. Margin: $700 - 640 = \mathbf{60\ m}$; TODR is less than TODA.

    The take-off is legal with 60 m to spare. If the strip were only 600 m, the TODR of 640 m would exceed the TODA, and the weight would have to be reduced until the chart gave a TODR of 600 m or less.

Now try one yourself: the chart gives a TODR of 580 m, and the strip is 650 m with no clearway. Is the take-off legal, and with what margin?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    TODA $= 650 + 0 = 650$ m. Margin $= 650 - 580 =$ **70 m**. TODR is less than TODA, so the take-off is legal with 70 m to spare. A margin of about 10 percent leaves little room for a soft patch, a late rotation or a gust that dies at lift-off; it is legal, not generous.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    TODR is measured to **50 ft**, not to lift-off. An answer that compares the ground run with the runway length, or that says the take-off distance ends "when the wheels leave the ground", is wrong. The other trap is counting a clearway as runway: a clearway lengthens the TODA, but you cannot roll on it, so it does nothing for a ground run or a rejected take-off.

### Landing distance required

The landing is the take-off run backwards. The aeroplane crosses the threshold at 50 ft at the **threshold speed**, the approach speed the chart is based on (Fig 6 prints 58 KIAS; Fig 13 gives a scale that varies with weight), descends to touch down, and is braked to a stop. The **landing distance required** (LDR) is the distance from **50 ft** over the threshold to a full stop, taken from the landing chart for the weight, pressure height, temperature, wind, slope and surface, and factored like the take-off distance.

### Landing distance available

The **landing distance available** (LDA) is the length of runway from the threshold that is available for the landing. Usually that is the whole runway. But if the threshold is **displaced**, moved along the runway to give a clearer approach over an obstacle or because the first part of the surface is unfit, the LDA starts at the displaced threshold and is shorter than the runway. You can often still use the full length for take-off, so a runway can have a TODA and an LDA that differ.

The rule mirrors the take-off: **LDR must not exceed LDA.** Because the aeroplane crosses the threshold at 50 ft, the airborne part of the landing is already inside the LDA.

!!! example "Worked example: is the runway long enough to land?"
    Given: the landing chart gives an LDR of 520 m. The runway is 700 m with the threshold at the end.

    1. LDA: **700 m**. Margin: $700 - 520 = \mathbf{180\ m}$. LDR is less than LDA: the landing is fine.
    2. Now a displaced threshold reduces the LDA to **500 m**. $520 > 500$: LDR **exceeds** LDA by 20 m.

    With the displaced threshold you may not plan to land on that runway at that weight in those conditions, even though the bitumen is still 700 m long.

The farm strip in "Bringing it together" below makes the same point the other way: an LDR of 520 m against an LDA of 650 m is legal with 130 m to spare.

![Where do TODR, TODA, LDR and LDA start and end?](diagram:takeoff-landing-distances-ala "What to notice: both required distances include the 50 ft segment, and a displaced threshold shortens the LDA while the strip stays 700 m long.")

![What makes the landing distance longer or shorter?](diagram:takeoff-landing-factors "Everything that lowers density, adds weight, adds a tailwind or slows the roll lengthens the distances; only a headwind shortens both.")

??? check "Check yourself: a runway's threshold is displaced. Does that change the TODA, the LDA, or both?"
    Normally only the LDA. The landing must begin at the displaced threshold, so the length available for landing is shorter; the pavement before the threshold can usually still be used for the take-off run. Check the published distances, which give each one separately.

## What an ALA is, and whose decision it is

An **aeroplane landing area** (ALA) is any place that is not a certified or registered aerodrome and is used for taking off and landing: a farm strip, a station strip, a beach or salt-lake surface used regularly. Nobody certifies it and nobody maintains it to a standard. The **pilot in command** is responsible for deciding that it is suitable for the aeroplane, the conditions and the operation.

CASA's guidance for that decision is **AC 91-02**, the advisory circular that replaced CAAP 92-1 (the syllabus still uses the old name). It applies to aeroplanes with a maximum take-off weight up to 5,700 kg and sets out what a suitable place looks like. It is guidance, not a rule with a pass mark, but if you ignore it and something goes wrong, you will be asked why. The figures below are as this note has them from AC 91-02; **verify them against the current AC** before relying on them.

Think of the guidelines as answering four questions in turn: is there enough room, is the way in and out clear, is the ground fit to roll on, and can you identify and use it safely?

## Enough room: length and width

**Length.** The strip must provide at least the TODR and the LDR for your aeroplane in the expected conditions. That is the whole of the previous section applied to a field: take the chart distances for the weight, pressure height, temperature, wind, slope and surface, and compare them with the measured strip.

**Width.** The AC recommends minimum runway widths: about **15 m** for light single-engine aeroplanes, more for larger or faster types (AC 91-02; verify). Width is your margin for a swing on take-off, a crosswind gust at touchdown or a tyre that grabs on wet grass. A 15 m strip leaves a high-wing trainer with a wingspan of about 11 m only 2 m of grass beyond each wingtip if it is on the centreline.

**Runway strip.** Beyond the edges and ends of the runway itself the AC calls for a cleared **runway strip**, an area free of obstacles that could strike the aeroplane if it runs off the side or the end. Think of it as the space a mistake needs. A fence post 3 m off the edge of a runway is fine until the day the aeroplane swings 4 m.

![What does AC 91-02 ask of an ALA?](diagram:ala-plan-view "What to notice: the runway, the cleared strip around it and the obstacle-free surfaces at each end are separate tests; the figures are to verify against the current AC.")

??? check "Check yourself: why does an ALA need a cleared strip either side of the runway as well as a runway wide enough for the wheels?"
    Because the wings are much wider than the wheel track and the aeroplane does not always stay on the centreline. The cleared strip gives room for a swing, a crosswind drift or a run-off without the wing or the wheels hitting anything.

## The way in and out: approach and take-off areas

Length tells you the aeroplane can reach 50 ft on the strip. It says nothing about what is beyond the end. The AC therefore calls for **obstacle-free approach and take-off areas** at each end: imaginary surfaces sloping up and out from the end of the strip, with nothing (trees, poles, power lines, sheds) poking through them. For day operations it gives a gradient of about **5 percent**, which is **1 in 20** (AC 91-02; verify). It also calls for **lateral transitional surfaces** clear of trees, poles and fences along the sides.

A 1 in 20 slope rises 1 unit for every 20 out. So a 50 ft tree must be at least $50 \times 20 = 1{,}000$ ft, about 300 m, beyond the start of the surface to stay below it. A line of tall gums 150 m past the end of a farm strip breaks the surface, however long the strip.

![What does a 1 in 20 obstacle surface rule out?](diagram:ala-obstacle-surface "What to notice: the surface is only about 25 ft up 150 m out, so a line of gums there fails the strip however long it is.")

Why 5 percent? Because the aeroplane has to climb faster than the surface rises, with some to spare. A light trainer climbing at 500 ft per minute at a groundspeed of 70 kt covers 70 nm in 60 minutes, which is 1.17 nm per minute, so it gains about 429 ft for every nautical mile. A nautical mile is about 6,080 ft, so 429 ft per nautical mile is a gradient of about 7 percent. That clears a 5 percent surface, but not by much, and a tailwind or a hot day narrows the gap.

![How does wind change the climb gradient over the ground?](diagram:climb-gradient-and-wind "The rate of climb is the same in all three cases; the tailwind flattens the gradient over the ground towards the obstacle surface.")

!!! example "Worked example: does the climb clear a 5 percent take-off surface?"
    Given: rate of climb 500 ft/min, climb TAS 70 kt, a 15 kt tailwind on the take-off.

    1. Groundspeed: $70 + 15 = \mathbf{85\ kt}$.
    2. Ground covered per minute: $85 \div 60 = \mathbf{1.42\ nm}$.
    3. Height per nautical mile: $500 \div 1.42 = \mathbf{353\ ft/nm}$.
    4. Gradient: $353 \div 6{,}080 = \mathbf{5.8\%}$.

    The aeroplane still out-climbs a 1 in 20 surface, but by less than 1 percent, and only if it really makes 500 ft per minute. On a hot afternoon at a lower rate of climb, the same tailwind take-off may not. Take off into wind, and check the climb rate the POH gives for today's density height.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    A strip long enough for the TODR is not therefore suitable. The approach and take-off areas are a separate test, and a strip with trees or power lines inside the obstacle surface fails it even when the length is generous. Power lines are the classic killer: thin, hard to see, often strung across the end of a paddock.

## Ground fit to roll on: surface and slope

**Surface.** The AC calls for a surface that is firm, even and free of holes, ruts, loose stones and long grass, with drainage so it is still usable after rain, and grass kept short. Each item maps to a force from [RBKA 4.1](/notes/RBKA/4.1): long grass and soft ground add rolling resistance, which lengthens the take-off; wet grass reduces braking, which lengthens the landing; holes and ruts can collapse a nosewheel; loose stones damage the propeller. Check the surface on foot if you possibly can. If you are arriving for the first time, make a **low pass** and inspect the strip from the air before committing to land, looking for stock, washouts, soft patches and the windsock.

**Slope.** The **longitudinal slope**, along the strip, should be within the chart limits; the AC suggests not more than about **2 percent** for most light aeroplanes (AC 91-02; verify). The **transverse slope**, across the strip, should be small, so the aeroplane does not drift towards the low side and rainwater drains off without cutting gullies. An uphill take-off lengthens the run and a downhill landing lengthens the roll, so on a sloping strip with light wind you usually land uphill and take off downhill.

![Uphill or downhill?](diagram:runway-slope-take-off-and-landing "Taking off uphill, part of the thrust climbs the slope; landing downhill, gravity fights the brakes.")

The AC also reminds you of what the book figures assume. POH distances are for a **sealed, level, dry runway** flown by a **test pilot**. A grass ALA, soft ground and an average pilot all add distance, and the AC gives the factors to apply for each. If the chart or the AC factors show that the ALA cannot provide the TODR or LDR with the required margin, the ALA is **not suitable at that weight**. The fix is usually less weight (fuel or passengers), a cooler time of day, or a different strip.

??? check "Check yourself: an ALA has a 2.5 percent longitudinal slope and is otherwise ideal. What does the guideline suggest, and why does slope matter?"
    It exceeds the AC's suggested maximum of about 2 percent (verify), so it should be treated as unsuitable unless the POH and charts allow that slope. Slope adds a component of weight along the strip: uphill it slows the take-off, downhill it fights the brakes on landing.

## Finding it and using it safely

**Wind indication.** The AC calls for a **windsock** or other reliable wind indicator, so you know the wind component on the strip itself rather than at an aerodrome 40 nm away. Plan for the prevailing wind, and know the crosswind your aeroplane can handle.

![What crosswind will this strip give you today?](widget:crosswind-component "A strip has only one runway direction; thirty degrees off the strip already puts half the wind across it.")

**Markings.** The usable area should be marked (cones, gables, drums, tyres) so you can identify the strip and its boundaries from the air, and you must know what the markings mean ([PFRA 2.5](/notes/PFRA/2.5)). A grass strip in a grass paddock is surprisingly hard to find, and harder to line up on accurately.

![How are the edges and boundaries of an unsealed strip marked?](diagram:runway-markings-and-markers "Cones mark the runway edges, gables mark the strip boundary, and a white cross closes an unserviceable section.")

**Other matters.** The AC's list ends with the things that make an ALA an ALA rather than an aerodrome:

- **Stock and wildlife**: kangaroos at dawn and dusk, sheep that wander onto the strip after the low pass.
- **Power lines** on or near the approach.
- **The owner's permission**.
- **Location relative to populous areas**: the minimum height rules still apply on approach and departure.
- **Communications and search and rescue**: there is no CTAF or AWIS, so plan how you will tell someone where you are and when you are due.

## Bringing it together

A friend invites you to land at the family farm near Wongan Hills. On the phone you learn the strip runs north-south, is 650 m of short dry grass, and has a windsock. Satellite imagery shows a fence across the southern end and a line of trees about 300 m beyond the northern end; a powerline runs along the road to the west.

Your chart for the expected weight, pressure height and temperature, corrected for short dry grass and the forecast 10 kt southerly, gives a TODR of 580 m and an LDR of 520 m. Against 650 m that is 70 m spare for take-off and 130 m for landing: both legal. You ask about width (about 20 m mown, with a cleared strip each side) and slope (the farmer thinks the north end is "a bit higher", which you note: in calm air you would land uphill to the north, but a 10 kt headwind outweighs a gentle slope). You check the trees against a 1 in 20 surface: 300 m out, they must be below about 50 ft. Tall gums would not be, so you plan to depart to the south over the fence, into the southerly. On arrival you make a low pass to check the surface and the stock, confirm the windsock, and land to the south, into wind, crossing the northern trees with height in hand.

Then the decision changes. You arrive after rain: the grass is wet and long. Long wet grass is the worst surface on the CASA charts, the TODR rises well above 580 m, and the 70 m margin is gone. The answer is the one the AC leads you to: wait for the strip to dry, or reduce weight and rework the chart before you plan a take-off.

## Key points

- **2.1.1** TOSS is at least 1.2 times the stall speed in the take-off configuration, reached by 50 ft. TODR runs from brake release to 50 ft at TOSS; TODA is runway plus clearway; TODR must not exceed TODA. LDR runs from 50 ft over the threshold to a stop; LDA is the runway from the threshold, shortened by a displaced threshold; LDR must not exceed LDA. Know whether your distances are factored.
- **2.1.2** The pilot in command decides whether an ALA is suitable, using AC 91-02 (formerly CAAP 92-1): length for the TODR and LDR, width about 15 m plus a cleared strip, obstacle-free approach and take-off areas at about 1 in 20, a firm short-grass surface, longitudinal slope not more than about 2 percent, a windsock, markings, and the stock, power lines, permission and SAR (figures to verify against the current AC).

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    TODR and LDR are measured from or to 50 ft and must not exceed TODA and LDA. For an ALA question, check length against the chart distances, width, slope, surface, approach gradient, windsock and markings, in that order, and say no if any one fails.
