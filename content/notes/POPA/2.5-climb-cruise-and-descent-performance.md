## What the exam expects (POPA 2.5)

- **2.5.1** From typical charts or tables, extract or work out for the climb, the cruise and the descent: (a) time, speed, distance and fuel flow or fuel quantity; (b) the engine settings to use; (c) rates of climb and descent; and (d) the conditions under which the aeroplane achieves maximum range and maximum endurance.

## Why this matters

Every fuel plan you write as a private pilot rests on three numbers you take from section 5 of the POH: how long and how much fuel the climb takes, what the aeroplane burns in the cruise, and what happens on the way down. On a flight from Jandakot to Kalbarri the climb is a small part of the distance but a large part of the fuel per minute, and on a hot day it takes longer than the book's standard-day figure. The exam gives you a table, usually a time, fuel and distance to climb table or a cruise table, and asks you to subtract, interpolate and correct for temperature and wind; then it asks why the aeroplane flies a different speed for maximum range than for maximum endurance. The fuel plan that uses these numbers is in [POPC 2.4](/notes/POPC/2.4); the climb and descent aerodynamics behind them are in [RBKA 3.3](/notes/RBKA/3.3) and [RBKA 3.4](/notes/RBKA/3.4).

## The climb table: time, fuel and distance

A light aeroplane POH gives climb performance as a **time, fuel and distance to climb** table. Each row is a pressure altitude, and against it are three cumulative figures: the minutes, litres (or pounds or US gallons) and nautical miles taken to climb **from sea level to that altitude**. Above the table, in small print, are the conditions it assumes: a stated **weight** (usually the maximum), a **climb speed**, the **power** (usually full throttle) and the **mixture** setting, **standard temperature**, and **still air**. Those are the engine settings element (b) asks for: you climb at the power, mixture and speed the table states, or its figures do not apply.

### Climbing between two altitudes: subtract

Because the figures are cumulative from sea level, the climb from one altitude to another is the **difference** between the two rows. That is the single most important rule, and the most common exam slip is to read off the higher row alone.

!!! example "Worked example: climb from 3,000 to 7,000 ft"
    Given: the table gives to 3,000 ft, 5 min, 3 L, 6 nm; to 7,000 ft, 13 min, 7 L, 17 nm. Standard temperature, still air.

    1. Time: $13 - 5 = \mathbf{8\ min}$.
    2. Fuel: $7 - 3 = \mathbf{4\ L}$.
    3. Still-air distance: $17 - 6 = \mathbf{11\ nm}$.

    Those are the figures for the 4,000 ft climb itself. The first 3,000 ft belonged to an aerodrome at sea level, which yours is not.

![Why do you subtract the rows of a climb table?](diagram:climb-table-subtract "What to notice: each row runs from sea level, so the 3,000 to 7,000 ft climb is the difference: 8 min, 4 L, 11 nm.")

### When your altitude falls between rows: interpolate

The table may jump in steps of 1,000 or 2,000 ft, and your aerodrome or cruising level will rarely sit on a row. Then you **interpolate**: assume the figures change evenly between the two rows on either side, and take the same fraction of the way between them.

!!! example "Worked example: departing from a 1,000 ft aerodrome"
    Given: the table gives sea level 0 min, 0 L, 0 nm; to 2,000 ft, 3 min, 2 L, 4 nm; to 6,000 ft, 11 min, 6 L, 15 nm. The aerodrome pressure height is 1,000 ft and you will cruise at 6,000 ft.

    1. 1,000 ft is halfway from sea level to 2,000 ft, so take half the 2,000 ft figures: **1.5 min, 1 L, 2 nm**.
    2. Climb 1,000 to 6,000 ft, time: $11 - 1.5 = \mathbf{9.5\ min}$.
    3. Fuel: $6 - 1 = \mathbf{5\ L}$.
    4. Still-air distance: $15 - 2 = \mathbf{13\ nm}$.

    Interpolating is slightly generous, because the aeroplane climbs faster near sea level than higher up, but the error over a 2,000 ft step is a fraction of a minute.

The rows are **pressure altitudes**, so enter the table with the pressure height of the aerodrome and of the cruising level, not their elevations. On most days the difference is small; on a low-QNH day it is a few hundred feet.

### Hotter than standard: correct

The table assumes ISA. On a warmer day the density height is higher than the pressure height, the engine makes less power and the climb is slower, so every figure grows. The POH gives a correction in a note under the table; a typical one, and the one this lesson uses, is to **add 10 percent for every 10 degrees above ISA** to the time, the fuel and the distance. Your POH's note governs.

!!! example "Worked example: the same climb on a warm day with a tailwind"
    Given: climb 3,000 to 7,000 ft, ISA figures 8 min, 4 L, 11 nm. Temperature ISA + 10. A 20 kt tailwind; climb TAS 75 kt.

    1. Temperature correction, 10 percent for 10 degrees: time $8 \times 1.1 = \mathbf{8.8\ min}$; fuel $4 \times 1.1 = \mathbf{4.4\ L}$; still-air distance $11 \times 1.1 = \mathbf{12.1\ nm}$.
    2. Groundspeed in the climb: $75 + 20 = \mathbf{95\ kt}$.
    3. Ground distance: $\dfrac{8.8}{60} \times 95 = \mathbf{13.9\ nm}$.

    The top of climb (TOPC) is about 14 nm along track, not 11. The wind changes where you reach your level, not how long it takes or how much fuel you burn.

### The wind: from still-air distance to ground distance

The table's distance is through the air. To find where along your track you reach the top of climb, you need the ground distance. The reliable way is the one in the example: multiply the climb **time** by the climb **groundspeed** (TAS minus the headwind component, or plus the tailwind). This is the same TOPC calculation as in [PNVC 2.4](/notes/PNVC/2.4). An alternative is to scale the still-air distance by the ratio of groundspeed to TAS. The two agree when the TAS you are given matches the table's own average climb speed, and differ a little when it does not, so use the time and groundspeed when you can.

![How does wind change groundspeed?](diagram:groundspeed-headwind-tailwind "The same TAS covers more ground with a tailwind and less with a headwind; the climb time and fuel do not change.")

Try one before opening the solution. The table gives to 2,000 ft, 3 min, 2 L, 4 nm; to 6,000 ft, 11 min, 6 L, 15 nm. You climb from 2,000 to 6,000 ft on a day 20 degrees above ISA, into a 15 kt headwind, at a climb TAS of 80 kt. Find the time, fuel and ground distance.

??? solution "Show the solution"
    ISA figures: $11 - 3 = 8$ min, $6 - 2 = 4$ L, $15 - 4 = 11$ nm. Twenty degrees above ISA is 20 percent: 9.6 min, 4.8 L, 13 nm still air. Groundspeed $80 - 15 = 65$ kt. Ground distance by time: $9.6 \div 60 \times 65 = 10.4$ nm; by the ratio method, $13 \times 65 \div 80 = 10.6$ nm. **About 9.6 min, 4.8 L and 10 to 11 nm** over the ground.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Reading the higher row alone gives the climb from sea level, not from your aerodrome. And the temperature correction applies to all three figures, but the wind applies only to the distance over the ground: a headwind does not make the climb take longer or burn more fuel.

??? check "Check yourself: why does a climb table need a temperature correction but not a wind correction to the time and fuel?"
    Temperature changes the air density, so it changes the engine's power and the rate of climb, and therefore the time and fuel to reach the level. Wind moves the whole air mass; the aeroplane climbs through it at the same rate, so only the distance over the ground changes.

## Rates of climb and descent

The **rate of climb** is the height gained per minute, in feet per minute. It comes from the surplus of power available over power required ([RBKA 3.3](/notes/RBKA/3.3)), and both ends of that surplus move with height: a normally aspirated engine loses roughly 3 percent of its full-throttle power per 1,000 ft of density height, while the power required to fly at the climb speed rises. So the rate of climb **falls with altitude**, and on a hot day it falls faster. Typical trainer figures are 700 ft/min at sea level at a climb IAS of 75 kt, falling to 400 ft/min at 7,000 ft on a warm day.

![Why does the climb rate fade with height?](diagram:power-loss-with-altitude "Full-throttle power falls roughly 3 percent per 1,000 ft; the surplus that drives the climb shrinks faster than the power itself.")

The POH gives the rate of climb either as a **rate of climb chart** (pressure altitude and temperature in, feet per minute out) or as a column in the climb table. You can also work out the **average** rate of climb from the climb table: height gained divided by time. For the 3,000 to 7,000 ft climb, $4{,}000 \div 8 = 500$ ft/min at ISA, and $4{,}000 \div 8.8 \approx 455$ ft/min at ISA + 10.

The climb speed matters too. The POH's climb figures assume a stated speed, usually the **best rate of climb speed** (Vy), the speed that gives the most height per minute. The **best angle** speed (Vx) is slower and gives the most height per mile, for clearing an obstacle. Climb faster or slower than Vy and the rate falls.

![Where are best rate and best angle?](diagram:climb-rate-vs-airspeed "The top of the curve is Vy, best rate; the steepest line from the origin touches it at Vx, best angle. Vx is always below Vy.")

The **rate of descent** is the same idea in reverse, and it is usually something you choose, not something the POH dictates. Most light-aeroplane POHs give little descent data beyond a recommended power setting and speed. You plan the descent with the rate of descent and the groundspeed: $\text{time} = \text{height to lose} \div \text{rate of descent}$, and $\text{distance} = \text{time} \times \text{groundspeed}$.

!!! example "Worked example: planning the descent"
    Given: cruising at 6,500 ft, joining the circuit at 1,500 ft. Planned rate of descent 500 ft/min; descent groundspeed 110 kt; cruise fuel flow 30 L/h (illustrative figure).

    1. Height to lose: $6{,}500 - 1{,}500 = \mathbf{5{,}000\ ft}$.
    2. Time: $5{,}000 \div 500 = \mathbf{10\ min}$.
    3. Distance: $\dfrac{10}{60} \times 110 = \mathbf{18.3\ nm}$.
    4. Fuel, at cruise flow: $\dfrac{10}{60} \times 30 = \mathbf{5\ L}$.

    Start down about 18 nm before the joining point (top of descent, TOPD). Fuel in the descent is typically at or a little below the cruise flow if power is kept on to avoid shock cooling the engine, so planning it at cruise flow is the conservative choice.

![Where is the top of descent?](diagram:descent-planning-topd "What to notice: 5,000 ft at 500 ft/min is 10 minutes, and 10 minutes at 110 kt is about 18 nm.")

Some POHs do give a descent table in the same cumulative form as the climb table. Then you subtract rows exactly as for the climb.

![What do power and flap do to a descent at the same IAS?](diagram:powered-descent-vs-glide "Power flattens the path and reduces the rate of descent; flap steepens it. The rate of descent is the pilot's choice.")

??? check "Check yourself: the average rate of climb from a table comes out lower on a hot day. Which way does that move the top of climb along your track, and why?"
    Further along track. The climb takes longer at the same groundspeed, so the aeroplane covers more ground before it reaches the level.

## The cruise table: power, speed and fuel flow

The **cruise performance table** is entered with **pressure altitude** and **RPM** (or, for a constant-speed propeller, **manifold pressure and RPM**). For each combination it gives the **percent power**, the **TAS** and the **fuel flow**, usually in three columns: a temperature below standard, standard, and above standard. These are the cruise engine settings element (b) asks for: you choose a power, set the RPM the table gives for it at your altitude and temperature, and lean the mixture as the table assumes.

The method:

1. Find the **pressure altitude** row, interpolating if you cruise between rows.
2. Choose the **power setting**: 65 to 75 percent is usual.
3. Read across to the **temperature column** for today.
4. Read the **RPM** that gives that power, the **TAS** for navigation, and the **fuel flow** for the fuel plan.

Two patterns are worth understanding because the exam asks about them. At a **fixed RPM**, the percent power **falls** as altitude or temperature rises, because the engine breathes thinner air. But for a **given percent power**, the **TAS rises** with altitude, because the same power drives the aeroplane through thinner air with less drag at the same true speed. That is why a normally aspirated aeroplane often cruises fastest for its fuel at a middle altitude, where it can still make 65 to 75 percent power.

The table's fuel flow is only true for the **mixture** it assumes, often "leaned to peak EGT" or "best economy". Cruise at full rich and the fuel flow will be noticeably higher than planned, and the reserve will shrink without any warning on the fuel gauge.

![What does leaning do to power and fuel burn?](diagram:mixture-rich-to-lean "Power peaks near the correct mixture: too rich wastes fuel, too lean risks overheating and detonation.")

!!! example "Worked example: time and fuel for a cruise leg"
    Given: from the cruise table at your level and power you read TAS 105 kt and fuel flow 30 L/h (illustrative figures). The leg is 140 nm with a 15 kt headwind component.

    1. Groundspeed: $105 - 15 = \mathbf{90\ kt}$.
    2. Time: $140 \div 90 = 1.56$ h, **93 min**.
    3. Fuel: $1.56 \times 30 = \mathbf{47\ L}$.

    The wind does not change the fuel flow, but it changes the time, and fuel is flow times time. The same leg with a 15 kt tailwind would take 70 min and burn 35 L.

??? check "Check yourself: you cruise at the RPM the table gives for 65 percent power but leave the mixture rich. What happens to your fuel plan?"
    The fuel flow is higher than the table's figure, which assumed a leaned mixture, so you burn more fuel than planned on every leg. The power and TAS barely change, so nothing in the cockpit tells you until the gauges or the time-based fuel log disagree.

## Maximum range and maximum endurance

These two ideas answer different questions, so they need different speeds.

**Maximum endurance** is the longest **time** in the air on the fuel you have: holding while fog clears at Albany, searching, waiting for a runway. Fuel burned per hour depends on the engine power, so endurance is greatest at the speed that needs the **least power** to stay level: the **minimum-power speed**. It is slow, slower than the minimum-drag speed, and close to the best angle of climb speed. Fly it low in a normally aspirated aeroplane, leaned: height gains nothing when distance does not matter, and the power required to fly at a given indicated speed is lowest in dense air.

**Maximum range** is the greatest **distance** on the fuel you have. Now the measure is fuel per nautical mile, which is fuel flow divided by groundspeed. In still air that is best at the speed with the best ratio of TAS to fuel flow, which is the **minimum-drag speed**, the speed of the **best lift/drag ratio** at the bottom of the total drag curve. It is faster than the endurance speed. Range improves by flying **higher** in a normally aspirated aeroplane (a higher TAS for the same power), by **leaning**, and by being **lighter**.

![Where is the minimum-drag speed?](diagram:drag-curves-vs-airspeed "The bottom of the total drag curve is the best lift/drag speed: the still-air range speed. The endurance speed is slower than this.")

**Wind changes the range speed** but not the endurance speed. Range is fuel per **ground** mile. Into a headwind, every minute spent in the air is a minute the wind pushes you back, so you **fly faster** than the still-air range speed, accepting more fuel per hour to spend fewer hours exposed to the wind. With a tailwind the wind is doing some of the work, so you **fly slower** and let it carry you longer. Endurance is measured in time, and the wind does not change time in the air, so the endurance speed stays the same in any wind.

![Where are the endurance and range speeds on the power curve?](diagram:power-required-range-endurance "What to notice: endurance is the bottom of the curve; range is where a line from the origin touches it, a faster speed, and a headwind moves that line's start and the range speed up.")

| Objective | Fly at | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Maximum endurance | Minimum power speed (low) | Least fuel per hour |
| Maximum range, still air | Minimum drag speed (best L/D) | Most nautical miles per litre |
| Range in a headwind | Faster than still-air range speed | Less time in the headwind |
| Range in a tailwind | Slower | More time with the tailwind's help |
| Range | Higher altitude (normally aspirated), lean mixture, lower weight | Higher TAS for the same power, less fuel per mile |
| Endurance | Low altitude, lean mixture, lower weight | Least fuel per hour; height gains nothing when distance does not matter |

The POH range and endurance charts give the range or endurance at each altitude and power setting for a full fuel load, with and without the reserve. They show the same pattern as the table: more range with height and lower power, more endurance at low power and low level.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Endurance and range are not the same speed, and the exam's distractor swaps them. Endurance (time) is the slower one, at minimum power; range (distance) is faster, at minimum drag. And wind only affects range: fly faster into a headwind, slower with a tailwind.

??? check "Check yourself: why does the maximum range speed go up in a headwind, while the maximum endurance speed does not?"
    Range is measured in ground distance, and a headwind costs ground distance for every minute in the air, so flying faster reduces the minutes exposed to it. Endurance is measured in time, which the wind cannot change, so the speed for least fuel per hour stays the same.

## Bringing it together

You plan Jandakot to Albany at 6,500 ft on a day 10 degrees above ISA. The climb table gives the climb from the aerodrome's pressure height to 6,500 ft after you subtract and interpolate; you add 10 percent to the time, fuel and distance for the temperature, and multiply the climb time by the climb groundspeed to place the top of climb. The cruise table at 6,500 ft, at your chosen 65 percent power and today's temperature column, gives the RPM to set, the TAS for the navigation log and the fuel flow for the fuel plan, provided you lean as the table says. Ten minutes and about 18 nm out you start a 500 ft/min descent and plan its fuel at cruise flow. Then the forecast changes: a 25 kt headwind on the way home. For the return you fly a little faster than the still-air range speed to cut the time in the headwind. And if Albany's fog is slow to lift on arrival, you slow to the endurance speed, low and leaned, to make the fuel last longest while you wait.

## Key points

- **2.5.1 (a)** Climb tables are cumulative from sea level: subtract the lower altitude's row from the higher, interpolate between rows, correct for temperature (typically 10 percent per 10 degrees above ISA; the POH governs), then use climb time × groundspeed for the ground distance. Cruise tables give TAS and fuel flow; leg fuel = flow × time.
- **2.5.1 (b)** Climb at the power, mixture and speed the table states. Cruise at the RPM (or manifold pressure and RPM) the table gives for the chosen power at your pressure altitude and temperature, leaned as the table assumes.
- **2.5.1 (c)** Rate of climb falls with altitude and temperature; the average rate is height gained ÷ time. Descent: time = height ÷ rate of descent, distance = time × groundspeed, fuel at cruise flow to be conservative.
- **2.5.1 (d)** Maximum endurance: minimum-power speed, slow, low, leaned. Maximum range: minimum-drag (best L/D) speed in still air, higher, leaned, lighter; faster into a headwind, slower with a tailwind.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Climb table arithmetic is "subtract the lower altitude's figures from the higher's", then correct for temperature and then for wind, in that order, and the wind changes only the ground distance. Cruise questions want the pressure altitude row, the temperature column and the RPM for the chosen power. For range and endurance, remember that endurance speed is slower than range speed, and that a headwind means fly faster for range and a tailwind means fly slower.
