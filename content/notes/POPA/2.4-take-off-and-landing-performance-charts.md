## What the exam expects (POPA 2.4)

- **2.4.1** Find the maximum structural take-off and landing weights in the flight manual.
- **2.4.2** Given a flight scenario, work the performance charts to find the maximum take-off weight and maximum landing weight (chart A), the take-off and landing distances required (chart B), the climb weight limit, the take-off parameters (power, flap setting, take-off safety speed) and the landing parameters (flap, threshold speed), and say what conditions those parameters assume.

## Why this matters

"Correct use of the take-off and landing charts" is first on CASA's list of PPLA weak areas. The charts are not hard, but they are unforgiving: one input entered on the wrong scale, a wind speed used where the chart wants a component, or a slope applied the wrong way, and the answer is a few hundred metres or fifty kilograms out with nothing to warn you. In real flying the same skill decides whether you can take four people out of a 900 m grass strip at York on a February afternoon, or whether two of them drive home. The workbook charts are handed to you in the exam; this lesson teaches the order of work and the checks that make the answer reliable. Have the [workbook](/reference/workbook) open beside you as you read, and work each step on the real chart with a ruler.

## Where the structural limits come from

Before any chart, write down the limits that no chart can raise. The **maximum structural take-off weight** (MTOW) and **maximum structural landing weight** (MLW) are set by the strength of the airframe and undercarriage, as [POPA 2.2](/notes/POPA/2.2) explains, and they live in **section 2 (limitations)** of the POH. Section 2 may also give a maximum ramp weight, the heaviest weight for taxiing, a little above the MTOW to allow for the fuel burned before take-off, and for some types a maximum zero fuel weight. For a light trainer the MTOW and MLW are often the same figure.

In the exam the "flight manual" is either a limitations extract printed in the question or the loading system page in the workbook, which prints the MTOW on the loading envelope. The workbook figures, as this note has them, are:

| Loading system | Structural limit printed |
|---|---|
| ALPHA | MTOW 1,633 kg |
| BRAVO | MTOW 1,000 kg (normal category) |
| CHARLIE | MTOW 1,115 kg (normal category) |
| ECHO | MTOW 2,950 kg, MLW 2,725 kg |

Read the limit, write it down, and treat it as a ceiling over every chart result. A performance chart can tell you that the runway would allow 1,700 kg; if the structure allows 1,633 kg, the answer is 1,633 kg.

ECHO is the one with different take-off and landing limits, and that difference creates a question type of its own: a take-off at the MTOW is not legal if the aeroplane will still be above the MLW when it lands.

!!! example "Worked example: the landing weight caps the take-off weight"
    Given: aircraft ECHO, MTOW 2,950 kg, MLW 2,725 kg. Planned take-off weight 2,950 kg; planned fuel burn to destination 250 L of avgas at 0.72 kg/L.

    1. Fuel burned: $250 \times 0.72 = \mathbf{180\ kg}$.
    2. Landing weight: $2{,}950 - 180 = \mathbf{2{,}770\ kg}$.
    3. Compare with MLW: $2{,}770 - 2{,}725 = \mathbf{45\ kg}$ over.
    4. Maximum take-off weight for this trip: $2{,}725 + 180 = \mathbf{2{,}905\ kg}$.

    The structure would allow 2,950 kg at take-off, but the landing limit brings the take-off weight down to 2,905 kg for this trip. The landing weight chart for the destination (Fig 13) may lower it again.

![How can the landing limit cap the take-off weight?](diagram:landing-weight-caps-takeoff "What to notice: taking off at the MTOW lands 45 kg over the MLW; the trip limit is the MLW plus the fuel burned, 2,905 kg.")

??? check "Check yourself: a performance chart gives a runway-limited take-off weight above the structural MTOW. What is your maximum take-off weight?"
    The structural MTOW (or a lower climb or landing limit if one applies). A chart can only tell you what the runway and the air allow; it cannot make the airframe stronger.

## The charts in the workbook

The workbook contains two kinds of chart, and the syllabus labels them. The **weight charts** answer "what is the heaviest I can be for this runway today?": in the syllabus wording these are **chart A**, for the maximum take-off weight and the maximum landing weight. The **distance charts** answer "how much runway does my weight need today?": **chart B**, for the take-off distance required (TODR) and the landing distance required (LDR). The definitions of TODR, TODA, LDR and LDA, and the rule that required must not exceed available, are in [POPA 2.1](/notes/POPA/2.1); in brief (verify against your chart's own definitions), TODR runs from brake release to 50 ft at the take-off safety speed, and LDR from 50 ft over the threshold to a stop.

|---|---|---|
| Fig 3 | 5 | Take-off weight chart |

| Fig 5 | 7 | Take-off weight chart |
| Fig 6 | 8 | Landing chart |
| Fig 12 | 20 | Take-off weight chart, aircraft ECHO |
| Fig 13 | 21 | Landing weight chart, aircraft ECHO |

Each chart is drawn for **one aeroplane in one configuration**, and the configuration is printed on it. Version 3 of the workbook also prints a **crosswind limit** on the take-off and landing charts, and a question can hinge on it.

Every chart has the same anatomy. It is a chain of panels joined by guide lines. The first panel turns the air into a single reference value; each later panel applies one correction, with sloping guide lines that show how much that factor changes the result; the last panel gives the answer on a scale in kilograms or metres. You enter on the left, follow the arrows, and never skip a panel: a factor that does not apply today (zero wind, level runway) is still a panel you cross, by going straight through to the reference line without moving up or down.

## Entering the chart: the order of work

Whatever the chart, the inputs come in the same order. Prepare all of them before you put the ruler down, so the chart work is mechanical.

1. **Pressure height.** From the elevation and QNH: $\text{elevation} + (1013 - \text{QNH}) \times 30$. Not density height: the chart applies the temperature itself (see [POPA 2.3](/notes/POPA/2.3)).
2. **Temperature.** The actual air (shade) temperature in degrees Celsius, entered on the chart's own temperature scale.
3. **Weight or distance.** For a weight chart, the distance available (TODA for take-off, LDA for landing). For a distance chart, the weight.
4. **Wind component.** The headwind or tailwind **component** along the runway, not the reported wind speed. Note which side of the reference line the chart puts tailwind on.
5. **Slope.** Percent, and whether it is up or down **in the direction you will be rolling**.
6. **Surface.** Sealed, gravel, short dry grass, long or wet grass, as the chart provides.
7. **Obstacles.** Not a chart panel, but the last check: the chart gets you to 50 ft at the end of the TODR; whatever is beyond that is a climb gradient question ([POPA 2.5](/notes/POPA/2.5) and the obstacle surfaces in [POPA 2.1](/notes/POPA/2.1)).

![Split the reported wind into the component the chart wants](widget:crosswind-component "The chart takes the headwind or tailwind component; the crosswind part goes against the crosswind limit printed on the chart.")

![Which way does the slope go for take-off and for landing?](diagram:runway-slope-take-off-and-landing "Upslope is a penalty for the take-off and a help for the landing; downslope is the reverse.")

!!! example "Worked example: preparing the take-off inputs at Northam"
    Given: Northam, elevation 1,120 ft, QNH 1006, 33 degrees. Runway 32, TODA 1,100 m, 1 percent up, grass. Wind 290 degrees at 15 kt.

    1. Pressure height: $1{,}120 + (1013 - 1006) \times 30 = 1{,}120 + 210 = \mathbf{1{,}330\ ft}$.
    2. Temperature: **33 degrees**, entered as it is.
    3. Distance: TODA **1,100 m**.
    4. Wind angle: $320 - 290 = 30$ degrees. Headwind component $15 \times \cos 30° = 15 \times 0.87 = \mathbf{13\ kt}$. Crosswind $15 \times \sin 30° = \mathbf{7.5\ kt}$ from the left, to check against the chart's crosswind limit.
    5. Slope: **1 percent up** (a take-off penalty).
    6. Surface: **grass**.

    Now enter Fig 3 at 1,330 ft and 33 degrees, through the TODA of 1,100 m, a headwind of 13 kt, 1 percent upslope and grass, and read the runway-limited take-off weight. Read the climb weight limit for 1,330 ft and 33 degrees. The planned take-off weight from the loading sheet must be no more than the lowest of those two and the structural MTOW. The exact numbers depend on the chart, which is why the workbook is provided in the exam.

Try preparing the landing inputs yourself before opening the solution. Later the same day you return to Northam to land on runway 14, the reverse direction of the same runway, so it slopes 1 percent down. The QNH is now 1010, the temperature 24 degrees and the wind 180 degrees at 12 kt.

??? solution "Show the solution"
    Pressure height $= 1{,}120 + (1013 - 1010) \times 30 = 1{,}120 + 90 = 1{,}210$ ft. Temperature 24 degrees. Wind angle $180 - 140 = 40$ degrees: headwind $12 \times \cos 40° = 12 \times 0.77 = 9$ kt; crosswind $12 \times \sin 40° = 12 \times 0.64 = 7.7$ kt from the right. Slope **1 percent down**, which is a landing penalty because gravity now opposes the brakes. Surface grass. Enter the landing chart in that order with the weight (for the LDR) or the LDA (for the maximum landing weight).

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Three input errors cause most wrong chart answers: entering the full wind speed instead of the component, entering the density height where the chart wants pressure height, and getting the slope backwards. A runway that is 1 percent up in one direction is 1 percent down in the other, and upslope is a penalty for the take-off but a help for the landing.

## Working a take-off weight chart (chart A)

Start at the **pressure height and temperature** grid. Go up from the temperature to the pressure height line, interpolating between lines, then across to the first reference line. From there the chart takes you through the corrections in the order its arrows show: typically **take-off distance available** (enter the TODA in metres), **wind component**, **runway slope** and **surface**, to the **take-off weight** scale. The result is the **maximum take-off weight the runway allows** today.

![How do you work a take-off weight chart, panel by panel?](diagram:takeoff-weight-chart-walkthrough "What to notice: in every correction panel the line runs parallel to the guide lines from the reference line to the input; an illustrative chart, so use the workbook or your POH for numbers.")

The same chart, or a separate line on it, gives the **climb weight limit**: the weight at which the aeroplane can still achieve the required climb gradient at that pressure height and temperature. It is read **without** the runway corrections. That makes sense once you see what each limit protects: the runway-limited weight depends on how much runway there is and what the wind and surface do to the run; the climb gradient after take-off depends only on the air the aeroplane is climbing through, and the wind does not change the rate of climb through the air.

Your permitted take-off weight is the **lowest** of: the chart's runway-limited weight, the climb weight limit, and the structural MTOW (and, as the ECHO example showed, any take-off weight forced down by the landing limit).

??? check "Check yourself: why is the climb weight limit read without the runway length, wind, slope and surface corrections?"
    Because it protects the climb after take-off, which depends on the aeroplane's power and drag in today's air, set by pressure height and temperature. The runway factors change how far the aeroplane rolls, not how well it climbs through the air once airborne.

## Working a distance chart (chart B)

The take-off distance chart runs the other way. Enter the same **pressure height and temperature**, then the **weight**, then **wind**, **slope** and **surface**, and read the **take-off distance required** in metres to the 50 ft screen height. Compare it with the TODA: the TODR must not exceed it.

## Working the landing charts

The landing charts follow the same pattern. Enter **pressure height and temperature**, then **weight** (or the **landing distance available**), wind, slope and surface, and read the **landing distance required** from 50 ft to a stop (Fig 4), or the **maximum landing weight for the LDA** (Fig 13 for aircraft ECHO). Compare the LDR with the LDA, and the landing weight with the structural MLW.

## The parameters and the conditions printed on the charts

Every chart states the technique and configuration it assumes. The figures it gives are only true if you fly that way, which is why the exam asks you to read them off and state them.

**Take-off parameters.** The conditions box gives the **power** (full throttle, maximum power), the **flap setting** (for example 10 degrees of take-off flap, or flaps up), and the **take-off safety speed** to be reached by 50 ft. The TOSS is either a single IAS (Fig 3 gives **60 KIAS** with 10 degrees of flap and full throttle) or a small table or scale on which it varies with weight (Fig 12): a heavier aeroplane stalls at a higher speed, so it needs a higher TOSS. The box also states the surface type the basic chart is drawn for, and that the distances are **factored**. The CASA charts include a safety factor of **1.15** on the measured distance for take-off, and the landing charts a larger factor, measured to or from the 50 ft screen (verify on the chart in front of you).

**Landing parameters.** The **flap** setting (usually full flap) and the **threshold speed**, the IAS at 50 ft over the threshold (Fig 6 prints **58 KIAS**; Fig 13 gives a scale that varies with weight). The landing distances also assume **power off from the threshold**, **maximum braking**, a **dry surface** unless corrected, and the factored distance.

**Both.** The pressure height and temperature are ISA-based inputs, and each correction is from a baseline: **still air** before the wind correction, a **level runway** before the slope correction, and a **sealed surface** before the surface correction.

| | Take-off | Landing |
|---|---|---|
| Power | Full throttle | Off from the threshold |
| Flap | As printed (for example 10 degrees) | Usually full |
| Speed | TOSS by 50 ft: fixed IAS or by weight | Threshold speed at 50 ft: fixed IAS or by weight |
| Braking | Not applicable | Maximum |
| Distances | Factored (1.15 on the CASA take-off charts) | Factored (a larger factor) |

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The chart's distance is only valid for the chart's technique. Take off with less flap than the chart assumes, rotate late, or cross the threshold 10 kt fast, and the real distance is longer than the chart says, however carefully you read it. In the exam, a question that asks for "the conditions on which the take-off figures are based" wants the power, flap, TOSS and factoring from the conditions box, not the day's weather.

??? check "Check yourself: why does the take-off safety speed on the ECHO chart vary with weight?"
    Because the stall speed rises with weight, and the TOSS must keep the same margin above the stall. A heavier aeroplane needs a higher speed at 50 ft to have the same protection against a gust or a turn.

## Reading accurately

Most marks lost on charts are lost in the reading, not the understanding.

- **Use a ruler and a sharp pencil.** Draw every line; do not sight across the chart by eye.
- **Follow the guide lines exactly parallel.** In each correction panel, run your line parallel to the nearest guide lines, not straight across. A line drawn flat through a sloping panel is the commonest single error.
- **Read the scales to the nearest division and interpolate** between the printed lines.
- **Take the wind component from the table** or the trigonometry, never the full wind speed, and check whether the chart wants it as a head or a tail component.
- **Confirm the slope direction**: up for take-off is a penalty, up for landing is a benefit.
- **Do not round intermediate readings.** Carry each value through and round the answer.
- **Write the answer with its units**, kilograms or metres, and check it against the structural limit and the distance available.

Then ask whether the answer makes sense. A hot, high, tailwind, grass, upslope take-off should give a lower weight or a longer distance than a cool, sealed, into-wind one; if your answer moved the other way, a line went the wrong way in one of the panels.

??? check "Check yourself: in a wind correction panel the guide lines slope down to the right. Your line arrives at the reference line and the headwind is 13 kt. Do you draw straight across?"
    No. You follow the slope of the guide lines from the reference line until you reach the 13 kt headwind position, then continue to the next panel's reference line. Drawing straight across ignores the wind correction entirely.

## Bringing it together

You plan to take three friends from Northam to Rottnest for lunch on a hot day. The loading sheet puts you at a take-off weight 10 kg below the structural MTOW from the limitations page. At the Northam inputs worked above, you enter Fig 3 at 1,330 ft and 33 degrees, cross the TODA, 13 kt headwind, 1 percent upslope and grass panels, and read the runway-limited weight; it comes out above your planned weight, so the runway is long enough. Then you read the climb weight limit for 1,330 ft and 33 degrees, with no runway corrections. Suppose it is below your planned weight. The runway would let you get airborne, the structure would carry you, but the aeroplane could not then make the required climb gradient: the climb weight limit governs, and you remove fuel or baggage until you are under it. Finally you check the conditions box: the figures assume full throttle, the stated flap and the TOSS by 50 ft, so that is how you fly the take-off. On arrival you would repeat the process on the landing chart for Rottnest's pressure height, temperature, wind and runway.

## Key points

- **2.4.1** The structural MTOW and MLW are in POH section 2 (limitations), or on the loading system page in the workbook (ALPHA 1,633 kg; BRAVO 1,000 kg normal; CHARLIE 1,115 kg normal; ECHO 2,950 kg, MLW 2,725 kg). They cap every chart answer, and the MLW can cap the take-off weight through the fuel burn.
- **2.4.2** Enter every chart in the same order: pressure height and temperature, then distance available (weight chart, chart A) or weight (distance chart, chart B), then wind component, slope and surface. The weight chart gives the runway-limited weight; the climb weight limit is read from pressure height and temperature alone; take the lowest with the MTOW. The distance charts give TODR to 50 ft and LDR from 50 ft, to compare with TODA and LDA. The conditions box gives the take-off power, flap and TOSS, the landing flap and threshold speed, and the assumptions: factored distances, power off from the threshold, maximum braking, still air, level, sealed and dry baselines.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Practise the workbook charts until the entry order is automatic: pressure height and temperature, then distance or weight, then wind, slope and surface. Use the wind component, not the wind speed. Always compare the chart weight with the structural limit and the climb weight limit, and always compare the required distance with the distance available. Know where the TOSS and threshold speed are printed (Fig 3 60 KIAS, Fig 6 58 KIAS, ECHO by weight) and that the CASA distances are already factored.
