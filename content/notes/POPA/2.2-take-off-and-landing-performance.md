## What the exam expects (POPA 2.2)

- **2.2.1** Say whether each factor increases or decreases take-off distance, landing distance and take-off climb performance: the headwind or tailwind component, air temperature, QNH, density height, aerodrome elevation, runway slope and surface (wet and slushy included), ground effect and wind shear, and frost on the aeroplane.
- **2.2.2** Tell pressure height and density height apart.
- **2.2.3** Describe how the altimeter on the ground gives you the local QNH, the aerodrome's pressure height and its elevation.
- **2.2.4** Explain maximum structural take-off and landing weight, and the climb weight limit.
- **2.2.5** State what is likely to happen if you exceed the weight limits.

## Why this matters

As a private pilot you will fill four seats and the tanks and set off for Kalbarri in January, in an aeroplane that is heavier, slower to accelerate and slower to climb than the trainer you soloed in. The RPL lessons [BAKC 6.2](/notes/BAKC/6.2) and [RBKA 4.1](/notes/RBKA/4.1) taught each factor on its own. This lesson puts them together the way a PPL pilot meets them, several at once on the same afternoon, and adds the one element the RPL did not cover: what actually happens when you load past the limits. The PPLA exam asks the same increase-or-decrease questions as the RPLA, but it sets them in an operational frame: given today's conditions, which limit governs, and does the planned flight comply? It builds on BAKC 6.2 rather than repeating its arithmetic.

## Two heights the aeroplane might care about

**Elevation** is the surveyed height of the aerodrome above mean sea level, printed in ERSA. It never changes, and the aeroplane is indifferent to it.

**Pressure height** is the height in the **International Standard Atmosphere** (ISA) at which the pressure equals today's pressure at the aerodrome. ISA is the agreed model atmosphere: 1013.25 hPa and 15 degrees at sea level, temperature falling 2 degrees per 1,000 ft, pressure falling about 1 hPa per 30 ft near the ground. If today's QNH is 1013, pressure height equals elevation. If a trough has dropped the QNH to 1003, the pressure at the aerodrome is what ISA has 300 ft higher, so the pressure height is 300 ft above the elevation.

**Density height** is the height in ISA at which the air density equals today's density at the aerodrome. Density depends on pressure and on temperature, so density height is pressure height corrected for temperature: about **120 ft for every degree** the air is warmer than ISA at that pressure height, and the same amount lower for every degree colder.

![Which height is which?](diagram:height-altitude-elevation "Elevation is fixed by survey; what the altimeter shows depends on the subscale setting, and none of these is the density height.")

Each serves a different customer. **Pressure height** serves the altimeter and the airspace system: it is what an altimeter reads with 1013 set, and it is one of the two inputs to every CASA take-off and landing chart. **Density height** serves the aeroplane: the wing, the propeller and the engine all respond to the number of air molecules they meet, not to the pressure alone. The calculation itself, by four methods, is the whole of [POPA 2.3](/notes/POPA/2.3).

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Pressure height and density height are equal only when the temperature is exactly ISA for that pressure height. An exam option that says "density height is the altimeter reading with 1013 set" is describing pressure height. Another favourite distractor says a high QNH raises the density height; it lowers it, because high pressure packs more air into each cubic metre.

??? check "Check yourself: two aerodromes have the same pressure height today. One is at 12 degrees, the other at 36 degrees. Which has the higher density height, and does the altimeter on the ground tell you?"
    The 36 degree aerodrome, by roughly 24 × 120 = 2,880 ft. The altimeter cannot tell you: with 1013 set both read the same pressure height. Only the temperature correction reveals the density height.

## What the altimeter tells you on the ground

An altimeter is a barometer graduated in feet: whatever pressure is in the subscale window, the needles show height above the level where that pressure exists, assuming ISA. Each hectopascal you wind onto the subscale moves the needles about 30 ft, in the same direction (a higher setting gives a higher reading). Sitting on the ground at a known elevation, that gives you three readings from one instrument.

**Local QNH.** Wind the subscale until the needles show the aerodrome elevation. The number in the window is now the pressure that, set on the subscale, makes the altimeter read true height above mean sea level at this spot: the local QNH.

**Pressure height.** Set **1013** (1013.2 is the same setting to the precision of the subscale). The needles now show height above the 1013.25 hPa level, which is the definition of pressure height.

**Elevation.** Set the **aerodrome QNH** from the ATIS, AWIS or tower. The needles should show the aerodrome elevation. This is also the pre-flight accuracy check: an error within **100 ft** is acceptable for VFR (AIP ENR 1.7; verify the current figure). Larger than that and the altimeter is unserviceable for the flight.

![Explore what the subscale setting does to the reading](widget:altimeter-subscale "Each hectopascal of difference between the subscale and the real QNH moves the reading about 30 ft; with 1013 set you are reading pressure height.")

!!! example "Worked example: three readings at a farm strip"
    Given: you know the strip's elevation is 960 ft. With the needles set to 960 ft the subscale shows 1004 hPa. Later you hear an area QNH of 1005 hPa.

    1. Local QNH: the subscale reading with the needles at the elevation, **1004 hPa**.
    2. Pressure height: set 1013, a change of $1013 - 1004 = 9$ hPa upward, so the reading rises by $9 \times 30 = 270$ ft: $960 + 270 = \mathbf{1{,}230\ ft}$.
    3. Area QNH set: $1005 - 1004 = 1$ hPa above the local value, so the altimeter reads $960 + 30 = \mathbf{990\ ft}$, an error of 30 ft.

    The 30 ft error is well inside the 100 ft VFR tolerance (verify), and the pressure height of 1,230 ft is the number you take to the take-off chart.

??? check "Check yourself: you set 1013 on the subscale at an aerodrome of elevation 400 ft and the altimeter reads 160 ft. Is the QNH above or below 1013, and roughly what is it?"
    Above. The pressure height is 240 ft below the elevation, so the sea-level pressure is higher than standard by $240 \div 30 = 8$ hPa: QNH about 1021.

## Density: temperature, QNH, elevation and density height

The syllabus lists temperature, QNH, density height and elevation separately, but they are one factor seen four ways: each of the first three lowers the density, and density height combines them.

Thin air hurts three parts of the aeroplane at once:

- **The engine.** A normally aspirated piston engine draws in a fixed volume of air each stroke. Thinner air means less oxygen per stroke, less fuel can be burned, and full-throttle power falls by roughly 3 percent per 1,000 ft of density height.
- **The propeller.** It is a rotating wing, and in thinner air each blade makes less thrust at the same RPM.
- **The wing.** Lift depends on dynamic pressure, $\tfrac{1}{2}\rho V^2$. With a lower density $\rho$ the wing needs a higher true airspeed to make the same lift. The airspeed indicator responds to the same dynamic pressure, so you still lift off at the same indicated airspeed, but the true airspeed, and therefore the groundspeed the aeroplane must reach, is higher.

![How much power does a normally aspirated engine lose with height?](diagram:power-loss-with-altitude "Roughly 3 percent per 1,000 ft of density height; a 5,000 ft density height on a hot afternoon leaves about 85 percent.")

Put those together for the take-off: less thrust, accelerating the aeroplane to a higher groundspeed. **Take-off distance increases.** For the landing: the same indicated approach speed is a higher true airspeed and groundspeed, so there is more kinetic energy for the brakes to absorb. **Landing distance increases.** For the climb: the rate of climb depends on the surplus of power available over power required, and thin air shrinks the power available while raising the power required. **Take-off climb performance decreases**, and it suffers most of the three.

So for the exam: **higher temperature, lower QNH, higher elevation and higher density height all increase both distances and reduce the climb.** The reverse of each improves performance: a cold, high-pressure morning at a coastal aerodrome can have a density height below sea level.

??? check "Check yourself: why does a high density height lengthen the landing distance when you fly the same indicated approach speed as always?"
    Because the same IAS is a higher TAS in thin air, so the aeroplane touches down at a higher groundspeed with more kinetic energy for the brakes to absorb. The IAS keeps the wing safe; it does not keep the energy the same.

## Wind: what the component does

The aeroplane only benefits from the part of the wind along the runway, the **headwind component** (or suffers from the **tailwind component**); the part across the runway, the **crosswind component**, does nothing for performance and everything to your workload. The workbook charts take the along-runway component as their wind input, not the reported wind speed.

![Split any wind into headwind and crosswind components for a runway](widget:crosswind-component "Thirty degrees off the runway already puts half the wind across it, but the headwind component is still nearly nine-tenths of the wind.")

The aeroplane lifts off at a fixed airspeed. A headwind supplies part of that airspeed before the aeroplane moves, so lift-off happens at a lower groundspeed; a tailwind means the aeroplane must reach a higher groundspeed before the air over the wing is fast enough. The runway used depends on the energy built up, and kinetic energy goes with the **square** of the groundspeed.

![Why does a small change in groundspeed cost so much runway?](diagram:kinetic-energy-speed-squared "10 percent faster carries 21 percent more energy: the square is why tailwinds are so expensive.")

!!! example "Worked example: an 8 kt wind component on the ground roll"
    Given: lift-off at 55 kt TAS. Compare an 8 kt headwind component with an 8 kt tailwind component, ground roll only.

    1. Headwind: groundspeed at lift-off $55 - 8 = 47$ kt; energy ratio $(47/55)^2 = \mathbf{0.73}$, about **27 percent shorter**.
    2. Tailwind: groundspeed at lift-off $55 + 8 = 63$ kt; energy ratio $(63/55)^2 = \mathbf{1.31}$, about **31 percent longer**.

    The same 8 kt costs more as a tailwind than it saves as a headwind. That asymmetry is why the CASA charts penalise tailwind more steeply than they credit headwind, and why the rule of thumb is that a tailwind of 10 percent of the lift-off speed adds roughly 20 percent to the take-off distance.

The landing roll obeys the same square law: a headwind shortens it, a tailwind lengthens it markedly. For the take-off climb, wind changes the climb **gradient over the ground** but not the **rate of climb**. The aeroplane gains the same feet per minute in the moving air; into a headwind it covers less ground in that minute, so it is higher over any given tree.

![Does wind change how fast or how steeply you climb?](diagram:climb-gradient-wind "Same 600 ft per minute in every case: the headwind steepens the path over the ground, the tailwind flattens it.")

## Slope and surface, including wet and slush

Now the ground itself. Performance charts are drawn for a level, dry, sealed runway; every departure from that has a direction and a reason ([RBKA 4.1](/notes/RBKA/4.1) has the full treatment).

**Slope.** On an upslope, a component of the aeroplane's weight acts down the runway, roughly the weight times the slope as a fraction. It opposes the thrust during the take-off and helps the brakes during the landing. So **upslope increases the take-off distance and decreases the landing distance; downslope does the reverse.** As a rule of thumb a 2 percent upslope adds around 10 percent to the take-off distance. The climb through the air is unchanged, but rising ground beyond an upslope strip reduces the clearance.

![Uphill or downhill?](diagram:runway-slope-take-off-and-landing "Taking off uphill, part of the thrust climbs the slope; landing downhill, gravity fights the brakes. With light wind, land uphill and take off downhill.")

**Grass and gravel.** Higher rolling resistance than bitumen: the take-off is longer. On landing, the extra drag helps a little, but braking on grass is poorer, so the landing distance is also longer on most charts.

**Wet sealed runway.** Water barely changes the rolling resistance, so the **take-off changes little**. It does lubricate the tyre contact, so braking falls away and the **landing distance increases**, often by 15 percent or more.

**Wet grass.** Both distances **increase**: the soft surface drags at the wheels on take-off and the slippery grass robs the brakes on landing.

**Slush and standing water.** The worst case. The wheels have to push the slush aside and the spray strikes the airframe, and both drags grow with speed, so the net accelerating force shrinks just as you approach lift-off speed. **Take-off distance increases greatly**, and the aeroplane may never reach lift-off speed. On landing the tyres can aquaplane, losing braking and steering together: **landing distance increases greatly**. Most light-aeroplane POHs give no slush figures at all, and that absence is the answer.

![What do slope and surface do to each distance?](diagram:runway-slope-and-surface-effects "Wet sealed hurts the landing only; wet grass hurts both; slush hurts both greatly and the climb starts later.")

On a contaminated surface the climb through the air is unchanged, but the aeroplane leaves the ground further down the strip, so it starts climbing later and closer to whatever is beyond the end. That is how the exam describes it: climb **unchanged, but starting later**.

??? check "Check yourself: a sealed runway is wet after a shower. Which distance does that affect most, and why?"
    The landing distance. Water hardly adds rolling resistance, so the take-off is little changed, but it lubricates the tyre contact and the brakes can no longer stop the aeroplane as quickly.

## Ground effect and wind shear

**Ground effect.** Within about one wingspan of the ground, the ground blocks the downwash behind the wing and squashes the wingtip vortices. The wing's induced drag, the drag that comes with making lift, falls sharply. Two consequences follow. On take-off the aeroplane can become airborne at a speed and angle of attack at which it cannot fly once clear of the ground. Pulled off early, a heavily loaded aeroplane on a hot day lifts to 10 or 15 ft, leaves ground effect, meets its full induced drag, and either sinks back or hangs there unable to accelerate or climb. Ground effect therefore **shortens the ground run but can destroy the take-off climb**: the protection is to hold the aeroplane down until the POH rotation speed. On landing, an aeroplane arriving a little fast **floats** in ground effect, using up runway before it settles: **landing distance increases**.

**Wind shear** is a change in wind speed or direction over a short distance. The aeroplane's inertia keeps its groundspeed for a moment, so a sudden change in wind becomes a sudden change in airspeed. A sudden loss of headwind, or a gain in tailwind, drops the indicated airspeed and the lift with the attitude unchanged. On the take-off climb that means a loss of airspeed and climb rate just when height is scarce; on the approach it means sinking below the path and undershooting. In WA the classic case is the sea-breeze front arriving at Jandakot on a summer afternoon.

![What happens to the approach when the headwind dies near the ground?](diagram:wind-shear-on-approach "The groundspeed holds for a moment, so the IAS drops and the aeroplane sinks below the path with no change in attitude or power.")

The defence is the same in both directions: speed in hand above the minimum, a margin added to the approach speed in gusty or sheared conditions, and power ready. On take-off, the exam answer for shear is that it **reduces take-off climb performance** and can be dangerous; for the landing its effect is variable, usually an undershoot.

??? check "Check yourself: why can an aeroplane lift off in ground effect but then fail to climb?"
    In ground effect the induced drag is much lower, so the wing can fly at a speed too low to support it in free air. As it climbs above about a wingspan the induced drag returns in full, and if the speed is still low the aeroplane cannot accelerate or climb.

## Frost on the aeroplane

A thin frost on a winter morning at Narrogin looks harmless. It is not. Frost roughens the upper surface of the wing, especially near the leading edge where the airflow is most sensitive. The boundary layer becomes turbulent and thick early, and the flow separates at a lower angle of attack. The maximum lift coefficient falls and drag rises: the stall speed goes up, sometimes by a large margin, while the thrust is unchanged.

So **take-off distance increases**, because the aeroplane must reach a higher speed before it can lift off, and it may not lift off at the normal rotation speed at all. Pulled off at the usual speed, it can stall in ground effect or just after leaving it. **Take-off climb performance decreases**, because the wing is giving less lift for more drag. Frost forms on a parked aeroplane, so the exam treats it as a take-off problem; the rule is simple: remove all frost before flight, every time.

## The factors side by side

The exam is built from this table. Cover the right-hand columns and supply each answer, with its reason, before you look.

| Factor | Take-off distance | Landing distance | Take-off climb |
|---|---|---|---|
| Headwind component | Decreases | Decreases | Steeper over the ground |
| Tailwind component | Increases markedly | Increases markedly | Flatter over the ground |
| Higher temperature | Increases | Increases | Decreases |
| Lower QNH | Increases | Increases | Decreases |
| Higher density height | Increases | Increases | Decreases |
| Higher elevation | Increases | Increases | Decreases |
| Upslope | Increases | Decreases | Reduced relative to rising ground |
| Downslope | Decreases | Increases | Unchanged, ground falls away |
| Wet sealed runway | Little change | Increases | Unchanged |
| Wet grass | Increases | Increases | Unchanged but starts later |
| Slush | Increases greatly | Increases greatly | Unchanged but starts later |
| Ground effect | Lift-off possible before climb is | Float | Sags on leaving it |
| Wind shear (loss of headwind) | Longer | Variable | Dangerous loss of airspeed and climb |
| Frost | Increases, may prevent lift-off | Not applicable | Decreases |

![What lengthens the take-off and landing distances?](diagram:takeoff-landing-factors "Everything that lowers density, adds weight, adds a tailwind or roughens the surface lengthens the distances; only a headwind shortens both.")

The factors also stack. Each one multiplies the distance, so a combination of a moderate tailwind, a hot day and a grass strip can double the book figure even though no single factor looks alarming. That is why the charts take each correction in turn, and why [POPA 2.4](/notes/POPA/2.4) insists on working every step.

## Structural weights and the climb weight limit

There are two quite different reasons an aeroplane can be too heavy, and the exam wants you to keep them apart.

**Maximum structural take-off weight** (MTOW) is the heaviest weight at which the aeroplane may begin a take-off. It is set by the strength of the airframe: the wing spar, the attachments, the undercarriage, all certified to carry a stated load at a stated load factor. **Maximum structural landing weight** (MLW) is the heaviest weight at which it may land, where the undercarriage has to absorb the vertical speed of touchdown. For most light trainers the two are equal. Both are printed in section 2 of the POH and never change with the weather.

The **climb weight limit** is a performance limit, not a structural one. It is the heaviest weight at which the aeroplane can still achieve the **required climb gradient after take-off** at today's pressure height and temperature. It is sometimes called the **WAT limit**: weight, altitude, temperature. On a cool morning at a coastal aerodrome it is usually above the structural MTOW and does not bite. On a 40 degree afternoon inland, with the density height thousands of feet above the elevation, it can fall below the MTOW, and then it is the governing limit, however strong the airframe. The workbook take-off weight chart gives it as a separate reading, alongside the weight the runway allows.

Your permitted take-off weight is the **lowest** of four numbers:

1. the structural MTOW;
2. the climb weight limit for today's pressure height and temperature;
3. the runway-limited weight from the take-off chart for the TODA, wind, slope and surface;
4. the take-off weight that will burn down to no more than the MLW (and the landing-chart weight for the destination) by the time you land.

!!! example "Worked example: which limit governs?"
    Given: structural MTOW 1,150 kg, MLW 1,150 kg. Today's take-off chart gives a runway-limited weight of 1,185 kg and a climb weight limit of 1,095 kg. Planned weight from the loading sheet 1,120 kg.

    1. Candidates: 1,150 kg (structural), 1,095 kg (climb), 1,185 kg (runway).
    2. Lowest: **1,095 kg**, the climb weight limit.
    3. Excess: $1{,}120 - 1{,}095 = \mathbf{25\ kg}$.

    The runway is long enough and the structure is strong enough, but the aeroplane cannot meet the required climb gradient at 1,120 kg today. You must offload 25 kg, about 35 L of avgas at 0.72 kg/L, or a bag, or wait for cooler air.

![Which weight limit governs today?](diagram:climb-weight-limit-governs "What to notice: the runway and the structure would both allow 1,120 kg, but the climb weight limit is lower and governs.")

??? check "Check yourself: why can the climb weight limit change during the day when the structural MTOW never does?"
    The climb weight limit depends on how much the aeroplane can climb in today's air, which depends on pressure height and temperature. As the day warms the density height rises, climb performance falls, and the heaviest weight that still meets the required gradient falls with it. The structure does not care about the air.

## What happens when you exceed the limits

This is the element the RPL lessons leave out, and it has a clear physical answer for each limit.

**Performance.** More weight needs more lift, which needs more speed. Stall speed rises with the square root of the weight, so every speed the POH gives (rotation, take-off safety speed, threshold speed) is too low for an overweight aeroplane. Accelerating more mass to a higher speed takes much longer: as a rough rule, take-off distance grows with about the square of the weight, so 10 percent overweight costs about 20 percent more runway ($1.1^2 = 1.21$). The landing distance grows too, because there is more kinetic energy to absorb. And the climb shrinks: the extra weight both raises the power required and adds a component of weight along the climb path ([RBKA 3.3](/notes/RBKA/3.3)), so the rate and the gradient fall, which is exactly the margin the climb weight limit exists to protect.

!!! example "Worked example: the stall speed of an overloaded aeroplane"
    Given: stall speed 48 kt at the MTOW of 1,150 kg; the aeroplane has been loaded to 1,265 kg, 10 percent over.

    1. Weight ratio: $1{,}265 \div 1{,}150 = \mathbf{1.10}$.
    2. Stall speed scales with $\sqrt{W}$: $48 \times \sqrt{1.10} = 48 \times 1.049 = \mathbf{50.3\ kt}$.

    Every published speed now carries less margin. A take-off safety speed meant to be 1.2 times the stall speed is now only about 1.14 times it.

**Structure.** The airframe is certified to a limit load factor at the MTOW: in the normal category, +3.8 g. That really means the structure can carry 3.8 times the maximum weight. Load it 10 percent heavier and the same structure reaches its limit at $3.8 \div 1.1 \approx 3.45$ g. A gust in turbulence, a firm pull-out or a heavy landing that the aeroplane would shrug off at normal weight can now bend something permanently. The undercarriage, tyres and brakes are certified for the MLW; landing heavier overloads them, and a hard landing overweight is where most of the damage happens.

![What load can the structure take in each category?](diagram:category-load-limits "Limit load factors are set at maximum weight; overload the aeroplane and the same structure reaches its limit at a lower g.")

**Handling.** Weight usually comes with a centre of gravity problem: the extra bags go in the back, the extra passenger in the rear seat. An aft CG gives light, over-sensitive pitch and poor stall and spin recovery; a forward CG may leave too little elevator to flare. [BAKC 6.4](/notes/BAKC/6.4) covers the loading side.

![What does a CG outside the limits do?](diagram:cg-position-effects "Forward: nose heavy, higher stall speed, may not flare. Aft: over-sensitive, poor stall and spin recovery.")

**The law.** Flying above a structural or performance limit is a breach of the aeroplane's operating limitations and therefore of the regulations; the certification no longer describes the aeroplane you are flying.

![What does 10 percent overweight cost?](diagram:overweight-ten-percent "What to notice: one overload hits speed, distance and strength at once: about 5 percent on the stall speed, about 20 percent on the runway, and the g limit falls to about 3.45.")

!!! warning "Common trap"
    "The aeroplane will simply use a bit more runway" is the answer the exam wants you to reject. Exceeding weight limits affects everything at once: longer take-off and landing distances, lower climb rate and gradient, higher stall and approach speeds, reduced cruise speed, range and manoeuvrability, structural and undercarriage loads beyond certification, tyres and brakes beyond their limits, and a breach of the regulations, with control problems on top if the CG is out.

## Bringing it together

It is the first week of January and you plan to fly three friends from Northam to Kalbarri for a long weekend. Before you leave home the AWIS gives Northam 37 degrees and QNH 1005. You set the subscale to 1013 in the aeroplane and the altimeter shows about 240 ft above the elevation, confirming a pressure height of elevation + 240 ft; with the air nearly 25 degrees above ISA, the density height is about 3,000 ft above that. The wind is a light easterly, a tailwind component of 4 kt on the runway you would otherwise use, and the far end of the runway is a little higher than the near end.

Every factor so far lengthens the take-off and weakens the climb: high temperature, low QNH, a tailwind and a slight upslope. Four people and full tanks put you 15 kg below the structural MTOW, but the workbook-style chart for today gives a climb weight limit 40 kg below your planned weight. Your options: take off from the other end, downslope and out of the tailwind, which helps the distance but not the climb weight limit; offload about 55 L of fuel and plan a stop at Geraldton; or leave at dawn tomorrow at 22 degrees. What you may not do is go at the planned weight and hope, because the aeroplane would be over a limit that exists precisely for the hot afternoon you are about to fly into.

## Key points

- **2.2.1** Headwind shortens both distances and steepens the climb; tailwind lengthens both markedly. Heat, low QNH, high elevation and high density height all mean lower density: both distances longer, climb poorer. Upslope lengthens the take-off and shortens the landing; downslope the reverse. Wet sealed lengthens the landing; wet grass both; slush both greatly. Ground effect lets you lift off before you can climb and makes you float. Wind shear that removes headwind drops the IAS and the climb. Frost raises the stall speed and lengthens or prevents the take-off.
- **2.2.2** Pressure height is the ISA height with today's pressure (altimeter with 1013 set). Density height is the ISA height with today's density, pressure height corrected about 120 ft per degree from ISA. Performance follows density height.
- **2.2.3** Needles set to the elevation: the subscale shows local QNH. Subscale 1013: the needles show pressure height. Subscale at aerodrome QNH: the needles show elevation, within 100 ft for VFR (AIP ENR 1.7, verify).
- **2.2.4** Structural MTOW and MLW are airframe limits that never change. The climb weight limit is the heaviest weight that still meets the required climb gradient today. The permitted take-off weight is the lowest of all the limits.
- **2.2.5** Over the limits: longer distances, poorer climb, higher stall and approach speeds, structure and undercarriage beyond certification, CG and control problems, and a breach of the regulations.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Expect increase-or-decrease questions, usually with one factor that cuts the other way to catch you: upslope shortens the landing, a wet sealed runway barely changes the take-off. For weight questions the answer is always the lowest limit, and the climb weight limit is the one that falls on a hot day. Have by heart: 30 ft per hectopascal, 120 ft per degree from ISA, ISA 15 degrees falling 2 per 1,000 ft, and the 100 ft altimeter tolerance.
