## What the exam expects (POPA 2.3)

- **2.3.1** Work out density height from (a) the outside air temperature and the pressure height, and (b) on the aeroplane itself, from the cockpit temperature gauge with 1013.2 hPa set on the altimeter.
- **2.3.2** Do it by any of four methods: a density altitude chart, the manual navigation computer, the flight manual charts, and mathematics.

## Why this matters

Density height is the single number that tells you how the aeroplane will perform today. At Kalgoorlie on a summer afternoon it can be 3,500 ft above the aerodrome's elevation; at Albany on a frosty winter morning it can be below sea level. [BAKC 6.2](/notes/BAKC/6.2) taught the arithmetic. The PPLA goes further: it expects you to get the inputs from the aeroplane itself, to use whichever tool the question names (a chart, the navigation computer, the POH or the formula), and to get the same answer each way. CASA lists density height and pressure height among the most common PPLA errors, almost always sign slips and the wrong ISA temperature, so this lesson spends as long on checking an answer as on finding it.

## What density height measures, and where the 120 ft comes from

Density height is the height in the **International Standard Atmosphere** (ISA) at which the air would have the same density as the air around you now. The ISA is the reference atmosphere that every performance chart and altimeter is built on: 1013.25 hPa and 15 degrees at sea level, with the temperature falling **2 degrees per 1,000 ft**. If the air today is exactly ISA for its pressure, density height equals pressure height. If it is warmer, the air is thinner than ISA at that pressure, and the density height is higher.

The size of the correction follows from two facts you can check. First, air density depends on absolute temperature: at 15 degrees, which is 288 kelvin, warming the air by 1 degree reduces its density by $1 \div 288$, about **0.35 percent**. Second, in the lower atmosphere ISA density falls by about **2.9 percent per 1,000 ft**. So one degree of warming thins the air as much as climbing

$$\frac{0.35}{2.9} \times 1{,}000 \approx 120\ \text{ft}$$

That is the **120 ft per degree** rule. It is a rule of thumb, good to within about 100 ft at the heights and temperatures the exam uses, and it works in both directions: each degree **colder** than ISA lowers the density height by about 120 ft.

**Pressure height** is the other input. It is the ISA height with today's pressure: what the altimeter reads with **1013.2 hPa** set, or the elevation corrected at 30 ft per hectopascal of QNH below 1013 (see [POPA 2.2](/notes/POPA/2.2) for the difference between the two heights and the altimeter procedure).

??? check "Check yourself: why must the ISA temperature be worked out at the pressure height, not at the elevation?"
    Because density height is the correction of pressure height for non-standard temperature. The question is how warm the air is compared with ISA at the same pressure, and the ISA height with today's pressure is the pressure height. Using the elevation gives the wrong "standard" temperature whenever the QNH is not 1013.

## Getting the two inputs

The exam gives you the inputs in one of three ways, and element 2.3.1 names the first two.

**(a) OAT and pressure height given.** The simplest: the question states, for example, "pressure height 2,500 ft, OAT 30 degrees". Go straight to the method.

**(b) From the aeroplane, with 1013.2 set.** Sitting in the aeroplane, set **1013.2 hPa** on the altimeter subscale. Whatever the needles now show, on the ground or in flight, is the **pressure height**. Read the temperature from the aeroplane's **outside air temperature gauge**. On the ground give it time: a gauge in a parked aeroplane on a sunny apron reads the heat soaked into the airframe, not the air. Take the reading once it has settled with the aeroplane in the shade or with air moving over the probe, never from the sun-heated cabin, which can be far hotter than the air outside.

**(c) Elevation and QNH given.** Work out the pressure height first, $\text{elevation} + (1013 - \text{QNH}) \times 30$, then carry on as for (a).

![What does the altimeter read with 1013.2 set?](widget:altimeter-subscale "With 1013 set the needles show pressure height, whatever the real QNH; that is input (b).")

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The cabin is not the air. A cockpit thermometer in a closed aeroplane on a 35 degree day can read 50. Use that and you add nearly 2,000 ft of density height that does not exist. The reverse error is worse: a temperature taken from an air-conditioned building or an early-morning forecast understates the afternoon's density height.

## Method 1: mathematics

Three lines, in order. Do this method even when the question names another, as a check. Work each line to the nearest 10 ft and keep one decimal place in the temperatures; round only the final answer. A numeric exam question allows for the small differences that rounding produces, but not for a sign error, which typically moves the answer by hundreds or thousands of feet.

$$\text{pressure height} = \text{elevation} + (1013 - \text{QNH}) \times 30$$

$$T_\text{ISA} = 15 - 2 \times \frac{\text{pressure height}}{1000}$$

$$\text{density height} = \text{pressure height} + 120 \times (\text{OAT} - T_\text{ISA})$$

!!! example "Worked example: density height from OAT and pressure height"
    Given: pressure height 3,000 ft, OAT 30 degrees.

    1. ISA temperature at 3,000 ft: $15 - 2 \times 3 = \mathbf{9}$ degrees.
    2. Deviation from ISA: $30 - 9 = \mathbf{+21}$ degrees.
    3. Correction: $120 \times 21 = \mathbf{2{,}520\ ft}$.
    4. Density height: $3{,}000 + 2{,}520 = \mathbf{5{,}520\ ft}$.

    The aeroplane will perform as it would at 5,500 ft on a standard day: roughly 16 percent less power than at sea level and a markedly longer take-off run.

!!! example "Worked example: below ISA, from the cockpit in flight"
    Given: cruising over the Stirling Range in winter, you set 1013.2 and the altimeter reads 6,500 ft. The OAT gauge shows −4 degrees.

    1. Pressure height: the reading with 1013.2 set, **6,500 ft**.
    2. ISA temperature at 6,500 ft: $15 - 2 \times 6.5 = 15 - 13 = \mathbf{2}$ degrees.
    3. Deviation: $-4 - 2 = \mathbf{-6}$ degrees, colder than standard.
    4. Density height: $6{,}500 + 120 \times (-6) = 6{,}500 - 720 = \mathbf{5{,}780\ ft}$.

    The cold air is denser than ISA, so the aeroplane performs as if 720 ft lower than its pressure height. The sign of the deviation carries straight through.

Now try one before opening the solution, the kind of number you meet in the Goldfields: Kalgoorlie, elevation 1,200 ft, QNH 1008, OAT 40 degrees.

??? solution "Show the solution"
    Pressure height $= 1{,}200 + (1013 - 1008) \times 30 = 1{,}200 + 150 = 1{,}350$ ft. ISA temperature at 1,350 ft $= 15 - 2.7 = 12.3$ degrees. Deviation $= 40 - 12.3 = 27.7$ degrees. Density height $= 1{,}350 + 120 \times 27.7 = 1{,}350 + 3{,}324 =$ **about 4,700 ft** (4,674). The aeroplane performs as if at nearly 5,000 ft, on a strip only 1,200 ft above the sea.

![How do the two corrections stack at Kalgoorlie?](diagram:density-height-kalgoorlie "What to notice: the QNH adds 150 ft; the heat adds over 3,300 ft. Temperature is the big correction.")

??? check "Check yourself: with 1013.2 set the altimeter reads 2,000 ft and the OAT is exactly 11 degrees. What is the density height, and why?"
    2,000 ft. ISA at 2,000 ft is $15 - 4 = 11$ degrees, so the deviation is zero and the density height equals the pressure height.

## Method 2: the navigation computer

The computer side of a manual navigation computer (the E6B or the CRP type) has a window for converting calibrated airspeed to true airspeed, and that conversion needs exactly the same information as density height: pressure height and temperature. That is why most computers carry a **density altitude window** beside the airspeed window.

The method:

1. In the **airspeed (or density altitude) window**, set the **pressure height** against the **OAT**. The pressure-altitude scale and the temperature scale are side by side in the window; turn the disc until the two values line up.
2. Without moving the disc, read the **density altitude** in the density altitude window, against its index mark.
3. Check the answer against the mathematics.

For the 2,500 ft, 30 degree case the computer gives about 4,900 ft, the same as the arithmetic: ISA at 2,500 ft is 10 degrees, the deviation 20 degrees, and $2{,}500 + 2{,}400 = 4{,}900$ ft. The computer is quick but its scales are crowded at the hot end, and the commonest error is reading the wrong window or setting the elevation instead of the pressure height. Each make lays out its scales slightly differently, so practise with the computer you will take into the exam.

The link with true airspeed is worth seeing, because it explains why density height matters in the cruise as well as on the runway. The airspeed indicator measures dynamic pressure, which depends on density as well as speed. In thin air the needle shows less than the speed through the air, so the true airspeed is higher than the indicated: roughly 2 percent per 1,000 ft of density height, as a rule of thumb. The same disc setting that gives you the density altitude also lines up the CAS scale against the TAS scale, so one setting answers both questions. A high density height means a faster true airspeed for the same indicated speed, which on the runway means a faster groundspeed at lift-off and touchdown.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The computer's density altitude scale expects **pressure** altitude. Setting the aerodrome elevation against the temperature skips the QNH correction, and on a low-pressure day that understates the density height by 30 ft for every hectopascal below 1013.

## Method 3: a density altitude chart

A **density altitude chart** is a graph that does the arithmetic for you. Temperature runs along the horizontal axis, density altitude up the vertical axis, and a family of sloping lines, one for each pressure altitude, crosses the grid. Some versions use aerodrome elevation lines with a separate QNH correction scale instead of pressure altitude lines.

To use one:

1. Find the **temperature** on the horizontal axis.
2. Go **straight up** to the line for your **pressure altitude**, interpolating between lines if it falls between two.
3. Go **straight across** to the vertical axis and read the **density altitude**.

The lines slope because density altitude rises with temperature at a fixed pressure altitude, and the slope is the 120 ft per degree you met in the mathematics. A chart answer should therefore agree with the formula to within the width of a line. The CASA workbook does not include a density altitude chart, but the POH may, and the exam may provide one in the question.

![How do you read a density altitude chart?](diagram:density-altitude-chart "What to notice: up from 30 degrees to the 3,000 ft line and across gives 5,520 ft, the same answer as the arithmetic.")

??? check "Check yourself: on a density altitude chart your pressure altitude falls halfway between the 2,000 ft and 3,000 ft lines. What do you do?"
    Interpolate: go up from the temperature to a point halfway between the two lines, then across. The lines are evenly spaced for equal steps of pressure altitude, so halfway between them is 2,500 ft.

## Method 4: the flight manual charts

Most POH take-off and landing charts, and every chart in the CASA workbook, take **pressure altitude and temperature as separate inputs** and do the density correction inside the chart. You never see the density height as a number: you enter the pressure height on one scale, the temperature on another, and the chart's guide lines carry the combination through to the result. For these charts the right procedure is to stop after the pressure height and read the temperature on the chart's own scale; working out the density height first and entering that as a pressure height would count the temperature twice.

Some POH tables are indexed by **density altitude** instead, giving take-off distance or rate of climb at sea level, 2,000 ft, 4,000 ft and so on. Then you work out the density height first, by any of the other three methods, and enter the table with it, interpolating between rows. Read the column headings: "pressure altitude" and "density altitude" are different inputs, and the wrong one can be thousands of feet out on a hot day.

![What does a higher density height cost the engine?](diagram:power-loss-with-altitude "Roughly 3 percent of full-throttle power per 1,000 ft of density height: the 5,520 ft example is down about 16 percent.")

??? check "Check yourself: a POH take-off chart has a pressure altitude scale and a temperature scale. Should you enter it with the density height?"
    No. The chart applies the temperature correction itself, so you enter the pressure height and the actual temperature separately. Entering the density height as the pressure height would apply the temperature correction twice.

## Checks that catch most errors

Every method should land on the same number, so the best check is to do the mathematics alongside whatever the question names. Beyond that, four habits catch most mistakes:

- **The ISA temperature falls with height.** At 6,000 ft ISA is 3 degrees, so a "mild" 20 degree day up there is already 17 degrees above standard, about 2,000 ft of correction.
- **A negative pressure height is normal.** With a high QNH at a coastal aerodrome, say 1025 at a 20 ft elevation, the pressure height is $20 - 360 = -340$ ft. Carry the sign, and work the ISA temperature at −340 ft: 15.7 degrees.
- **Sanity-check the size.** On a hot WA afternoon inland the density height is typically 3,000 to 4,000 ft above the elevation; on the coast in winter it can be below sea level. An answer far outside that range for the conditions given is probably a slip.
- **Humidity** adds a little to the density height, because water vapour is lighter than dry air, but the exam methods ignore it.

## Bringing it together

You are about to leave a station strip in the Murchison at midday. With 1013.2 set the altimeter reads 1,850 ft, and the OAT gauge, after five minutes of taxiing with the vents open, settles on 34 degrees. That is your input (b): pressure height 1,850 ft, OAT 34.

By the mathematics: ISA at 1,850 ft is $15 - 3.7 = 11.3$ degrees, the deviation is $34 - 11.3 = 22.7$ degrees, the correction is $120 \times 22.7 = 2{,}724$ ft, and the density height is about **4,570 ft**. You check it on the navigation computer: pressure altitude 1,850 against 34 degrees, read the density altitude window, and it agrees to within the scale's resolution. Your POH take-off chart wants pressure altitude and temperature, so you enter it at 1,850 ft and 34 degrees, not at 4,570 ft. The climb table, though, is laid out by density altitude, so there you use 4,570 ft and interpolate. Three tools, one atmosphere, and the arithmetic is the referee.

## Key points

- **2.3.1** You need pressure height and temperature. (a) Given directly. (b) From the aeroplane: 1013.2 on the subscale gives pressure height; the OAT gauge, settled and shaded, gives the temperature, never the hot cabin. (Or calculate pressure height from elevation and QNH.)
- **2.3.2** Mathematics: ISA = 15 − 2 per 1,000 ft of pressure height; density height = pressure height + 120 × (OAT − ISA). Navigation computer: pressure height against OAT in the airspeed window, read the density altitude window. Density altitude chart: up from temperature to the pressure altitude line, across to density altitude. Flight manual charts: usually enter pressure height and temperature separately and let the chart correct; tables indexed by density altitude need it worked out first.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Whatever method the question names, the answer must agree with the arithmetic to within the reading accuracy of the chart or computer, so do the mathematics as a cross-check. The usual errors are working the ISA temperature at the elevation instead of the pressure height, losing the sign of a below-ISA deviation or a negative pressure height, and feeding a density height into a chart that wants pressure height. Know 15 degrees, 2 degrees per 1,000 ft, 30 ft per hectopascal and 120 ft per degree by heart.
