## What the exam expects (PAKC 3.9)

- **3.9.1** Describe the controls you use to transmit and receive on an aeronautical radio (the COM set, the press-to-talk switch and the transponder) and what each audio panel selection does.

## Why this matters

A radio call that never left the aeroplane looks exactly like one that nobody answered. Most of the "radio problems" a student meets at Jandakot or on a Northam CTAF are not faults at all but a knob in the wrong place: the tower frequency still in standby, the MIC selector left on COM 2 after a call to Centre, the squelch wound so tight that a weak station is cut out, the intercom swallowing the radio. Each control does one job in a chain that runs from your mouth to the antenna and back to your ears, and once you can picture that chain you can set it up correctly before taxi and find the fault in seconds when something goes quiet. The exam asks you to describe the controls and predict what happens with a given set of selections, especially on the audio panel.

## The chain from your mouth to the antenna

Start with what has to happen when you speak. Your voice goes into the **microphone** on your headset, which turns it into an electrical signal. That signal goes to the **audio panel**, a switchboard between the headsets and the radios. The audio panel sends it to whichever **COM** (communication) radio is selected to transmit. The COM radio, keyed by the **press-to-talk (PTT)** switch, turns the voice into a radio-frequency signal on the selected frequency and feeds it to its **antenna**, which radiates it.

Receiving runs the same chain backwards. The antenna picks up signals; the COM radio tuned to the frequency extracts the voice; the audio panel passes that audio to your headset (or the cabin speaker) if you have selected that radio for listening.

Two consequences follow, and they explain most of this lesson:

- Every link has a control, and every control can be set wrong. Silence tells you that a link is broken, not which one.
- Aviation VHF voice is **simplex**: one frequency carries both directions, and only one station can usefully transmit at a time. While your PTT is pressed your own receiver on that frequency is silent, so you cannot hear anyone else, and if two stations transmit together, listeners hear a squeal or garble, or only the stronger one.

??? check "Check yourself: why can't you hear another station that transmits while you are holding the PTT down?"
    Aviation VHF voice is simplex: one frequency carries both directions, and while your transmitter is keyed your own receiver on that frequency is muted. Two stations transmitting at once also block each other for everyone else listening.

## The COM radio face

A typical light-aircraft COM radio, whether a stand-alone set or the COM half of a combined COM/NAV or GPS unit, has the same handful of controls. Your POH supplement (section 9) describes your exact unit; the functions are common to all of them.

| Control | Function |
|---|---|
| On/off and **volume** knob | Powers the radio and sets audio level |
| **Squelch** (or pull-to-test) | Mutes background noise when no signal is present; pulling or turning opens it to set volume and test reception |
| **Frequency selector** (outer knob MHz, inner kHz) | Sets the frequency in the **standby** window |
| **Flip-flop** (transfer) button | Swaps standby into **active**; you transmit and receive on the active frequency only |
| Memory / channel recall | Stored frequencies on some units |
| **TX indicator** | Shows when the transmitter is keyed; a steady TX with no PTT pressed means a stuck microphone |
| 121.5 recall | Many radios recall the emergency frequency with a long press of the flip-flop |

The table is the recap; here is why each control is there.

### Volume

The volume knob (usually combined with the on/off switch) sets how loud the received audio is before it reaches the audio panel. On many sets it is the only on/off switch, so "volume fully down" and "radio off" can be one click apart. If you hear nothing, the volume is the first thing to check, and the squelch is how you check it.

### Squelch

A receiver with nothing to receive does not stay quiet: it amplifies whatever random electrical noise is around and you hear a constant **hiss** (static). Listening to that for an hour is tiring and it hides real calls. The **squelch** is a gate that keeps the audio muted until an incoming signal is stronger than a threshold, then opens to let the voice through.

Setting the threshold is a trade-off. Too low and the hiss leaks through between calls. Too high and weak stations (a distant aircraft, a ground station near the edge of its range) never open the gate, so you do not hear them at all. Many radios set the threshold automatically and give you a **pull-to-test** or push button that opens the squelch fully. Opening it is useful twice over: if you hear hiss, the receiver, volume and audio path are working, and the hiss is a known sound to set the volume against. On a radio with a manual squelch knob, turn it until you hear the hiss, then back it off until the hiss *just* stops.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    A squelch set too tight looks like a quiet frequency. If a busy CTAF seems strangely silent, open the squelch before assuming nobody is about.

### Frequency selection, active and standby

Most modern COM radios show two frequencies. The **active** frequency (on the left or top, often labelled ACT or COM) is the one the radio is transmitting and receiving on now. The **standby** frequency (STBY) is a scratch pad. The concentric frequency knobs change only the standby: the larger outer knob steps whole **megahertz** and the smaller inner knob steps the **kilohertz** part. When the standby is ready, the **flip-flop** (transfer) button swaps the two. The old active frequency moves to standby, which makes going back one press away.

This design lets you dial the next frequency without losing contact on the current one, and it is why a frequency you have dialled but not transferred does nothing: you transmit and receive on the active frequency only. A standard habit is to pre-set the next frequency in standby as soon as you know it (the tower while you are on the ATIS, the area frequency while you are on the CTAF) and transfer it at the right moment.

![Which window do you talk on, and what do the knobs change?](diagram:com-radio-active-standby "What to notice: the knobs change only the standby window; you transmit and receive on the active frequency until you press the flip-flop.")

Australian VHF COM uses 25 kHz spacing; a frequency like 120.025 MHz is a valid 25 kHz channel. Some radios also offer 8.33 kHz spacing (used in Europe); Australia does not require it.

### Memory, 121.5 and the TX light

Some units store frequently used frequencies as **memory** channels, saving dial-twisting on regular routes. Many also give a quick way to tune **121.5 MHz**, the international emergency frequency, often a long press of the flip-flop button; check your POH for your unit.

The **TX indicator** is a small "TX" or "T" in the display that lights while the transmitter is keyed. Glance at it when you make a call: it confirms the radio you meant to use is the one transmitting. If it stays lit after you release the PTT, the microphone is stuck in the transmit position. That is more than your problem: a stuck microphone keeps transmitting a carrier (and every word in the cockpit) on the frequency, blocking it for every other aircraft and the ground station. Unplug the offending microphone or cycle the PTT until the light goes out.

??? check "Check yourself: you have dialled the tower frequency and made your call, but the tower does not answer. The frequency is shown on the radio. What should you check first, and why?"
    Whether the tower frequency is in the active window or still in standby. The knobs only change standby, and the radio transmits and receives on the active frequency only, so an untransferred frequency means your call went out on the old one.

## The press-to-talk switch

The **press-to-talk** switch on the control column (or the hand microphone) keys the selected transmitter. Press, pause, speak, release (PAKC 3.1 on clipped transmissions). In two-crew or training aircraft each seat usually has its own PTT.

The pause matters because a transmitter takes a moment to come up to power after it is keyed, and other radios take a moment to open their squelch. Speak before that and the first word, often the call-sign, is lost: a **clipped transmission** ([PAKC 3.1](/notes/PAKC/3.1)). Releasing before you finish clips the end. Release fully afterwards and check the TX light has gone out, or you become the stuck microphone.

The PTT keys whichever transmitter the audio panel's MIC selector has chosen. It does not know which radio you are thinking about.

## The audio panel

The audio panel routes audio between the radios, the headsets and the speaker. It is where the most interesting mistakes happen, because it decouples what you *hear* from where you *transmit*. Typical controls:

- **MIC (transmit) selector**: chooses which COM transmits (COM 1, COM 2, sometimes COM 3/HF). Selecting a COM for transmit normally also selects it for receive.
- **Receiver select buttons**: COM 1, COM 2, NAV 1, NAV 2, ADF, DME, marker. Pressing a button lets you hear that receiver. This is how you **monitor** the area frequency on COM 2 while transmitting on the CTAF on COM 1, or listen to a navaid **ident**.
- **Speaker / phone** selector: routes audio to the cabin speaker or the headsets.
- **Intercom** volume and **squelch (VOX)** knob, and **isolation** modes: "pilot isolate" (pilot hears only radios), "crew" (pilot and co-pilot talk, passengers hear music), "all".
- **Marker beacon** lights and sensitivity (HI/LO) on many panels.
- **Fail-safe**: if the audio panel loses power, COM 1 is usually connected directly to the pilot's headset so you can still talk and listen. Check your POH.

### Transmit one, listen to several

The key idea is that **you transmit on one radio but can listen to as many as you like**. The MIC selector is a single choice: one COM at a time is connected to the microphones and the PTT. The receiver buttons are independent switches: you can hear COM 1, COM 2 and a NAV ident at once, mixed together in your headset.

That makes **monitoring** possible. On a CTAF departure you can broadcast on the CTAF on COM 1 while listening on COM 2 to the area frequency for traffic and for anyone calling you. It also makes the classic error possible: you hear the CTAF clearly, so you assume you are talking on it, when the MIC selector is still on COM 2 from your last call to Centre and your broadcast has gone out on the area frequency instead.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    You transmit on whichever COM the MIC selector chooses, regardless of which ones you are listening to. Hearing a frequency proves nothing about where your next call will go; the MIC selector and the TX light do.

### Navaid idents and the marker

The NAV, ADF and DME buttons let you hear the Morse identification of a navigation aid, which you must check before using it. Turn the receiver button on to identify the aid, then off again so the Morse does not drown out ATC. The **marker beacon** lights and tone belong to instrument approaches; for VFR you will mostly leave the marker audio off or set to LO sensitivity.

### Intercom and isolation

The **intercom** lets the people on board talk to each other through their headsets. A voice-activated (**VOX**) squelch opens each microphone only when someone speaks loudly enough, so the engine noise is not piped into every headset; set it like the radio squelch, just above the point where engine noise opens it. The **isolation** modes decide who hears what. In **pilot isolate** the pilot hears only the radios and is cut out of the cabin chat, useful during a busy arrival. **Crew** links the front seats while the passengers hear only themselves (and music, if fitted). **All** puts everyone on the same intercom.

### When the audio panel fails

The audio panel is electrically powered and sits between you and every radio, so its failure could silence everything. Most panels are therefore built with a **fail-safe** path: with no power, COM 1 is wired straight through to the pilot's headset and microphone. You lose the selections, the intercom and COM 2, but you can still talk and listen on COM 1. Check your POH for your panel, because this is also why the fault-finding check in [PAKC 3.3](/notes/PAKC/3.3) includes the audio panel switch and its circuit breaker.

??? check "Check yourself: you want to hear the Perth ATIS on COM 2 without losing the tower on COM 1. What audio panel selections do you make?"
    Leave the MIC selector on COM 1 (the tower), and press the COM 2 receiver button so you hear COM 2 as well. Turn COM 2 off again once you have the ATIS, so it does not mask tower calls.

## Setting up before you taxi

Putting the controls together, here is a standard set-up at a non-controlled aerodrome.

!!! example "Worked example: setting up for a CTAF departure"
    Given: a departure from a non-controlled aerodrome, with the CTAF from ERSA and the area frequency from the VNC.

    1. **COM 1** active: CTAF (from ERSA); **COM 2** active: area frequency from the VNC.
    2. **Audio panel**: MIC on COM 1; receive COM 1 and COM 2 both selected.
    3. **Volume and squelch** set on each: open the squelch, set a comfortable volume against the hiss, close it.
    4. **Transponder**: code 1200 and ALT (Mode C/S) before entering the runway (AIP ENR 1.6), unless a different code is assigned.
    5. **Taxi call on COM 1.** If you then need to call Centre, switch the MIC selector to COM 2 and remember to switch it back for the next CTAF broadcast.

    The pattern is: talk on the CTAF, listen on both, and treat every change of the MIC selector as something you must undo.

Now set up for a different case before opening the solution. You are about to leave the Jandakot circuit area on a flight to a country strip. COM 1 has Jandakot tower active; you want the area frequency ready next, and you want to check the identity of a navaid on NAV 1 on the way. Describe the selections.

??? solution "Show the solution"
    1. **COM 1**: tower active; dial the area frequency (or the destination CTAF) into **standby**, ready to flip-flop when the tower releases you.
    2. **MIC on COM 1**, receive COM 1 selected; nothing changes on the audio panel when you flip-flop, because the frequency changes inside the same radio.
    3. **NAV 1**: tune the aid, press the **NAV 1 receiver button** to hear the Morse ident, check it against the chart, then turn the button off.
    4. **COM 2** can hold the next frequency after that, selected for receive if you want to monitor it, with the MIC still on COM 1.

    Every change that affects where you transmit (MIC selector, active frequency) is checked against the TX light on the next call.

## Transponder controls

The **transponder** is a radio too: it receives interrogations from ground radar on 1,030 MHz and replies on 1,090 MHz with a code and, in ALT mode, your pressure altitude. It has three groups of controls.

Code selector (four digits, each 0 to 7), mode selector (**OFF, STBY, ON, ALT**, sometimes GND), and **IDENT** button (press only when ATC asks "squawk ident"). Changing codes, avoid passing through 7500, 7600 and 7700.

The code identifies your aircraft on the controller's screen. **STBY** keeps the transponder warm but silent (used on the ground at many aerodromes until you enter the runway); **ON** replies with the code only; **ALT** adds altitude, which is the normal setting in flight (Mode C/S, AIP ENR 1.6). The **IDENT** button makes your return stand out on the screen for a few seconds so the controller can pick you out, which is why it is used only on request.

The reason to avoid the reserved codes while changing digits is that each intermediate setting is transmitted as you dial. Rolling from 1200 to 7234 by turning the first digit up through 7 first could briefly show 7200, harmless, but other paths pass through 7600 or 7700 and trigger an alarm on the controller's screen.

![Which transponder codes are reserved for emergencies?](diagram:transponder-emergency-codes "7700 emergency, 7600 radio failure, 7500 unlawful interference: never pass through them while changing codes.")

??? check "Check yourself: you are cleared to squawk 3721. Why might you set the first digit last?"
    Each intermediate code is transmitted as you dial. Setting the 7 last means the code never starts with 7 while the other digits pass through 5, 6 or 0, so it cannot briefly show 7500, 7600 or 7700.

## Key points

- **3.9.1** The COM radio: volume (often also power), squelch to mute the hiss (pull-to-test to open it), outer knob MHz and inner knob kHz set the **standby** frequency, the **flip-flop** makes it **active**, and you transmit and receive on the active frequency only; the TX light shows the transmitter is keyed. PTT: press, pause, speak, release. The audio panel: **one MIC selection** decides where you transmit, any number of **receiver buttons** decide what you hear, plus speaker/phones, intercom with VOX squelch and isolate modes, and a fail-safe that connects COM 1 to the pilot's headset. Transponder: four-digit code (0 to 7), OFF/STBY/ON/ALT, IDENT only on request; avoid passing through 7500, 7600, 7700.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    You transmit on whichever COM the MIC selector chooses, regardless of which one you are listening to. Many "no reply" problems are a call made on COM 2 when you meant COM 1. Expect questions that give you a set of selections (MIC on one COM, receivers on several, frequency in standby) and ask what happens when you press the PTT: work along the chain from the microphone to the active frequency.
