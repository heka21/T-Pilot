## What the exam expects (PAKC 3.3)

- **3.3.1** Describe the routine procedure for finding a radio fault, and the corrective action a pilot takes once the fault is found or cannot be cleared.

## Why this matters

Sooner or later the radio goes quiet on you. It might be on the ground at Jandakot when Ground does not answer your taxi call, or at 3,000 ft over the Avon Valley inbound to Northam when the CTAF that was busy a minute ago falls silent. In almost every case the radio itself is fine: a plug has worked loose, a switch is in the wrong place, the frequency is in the standby window, or a breaker has popped. A pilot with a routine finds that in a minute without taking their eyes off the horizon for long; a pilot without one fiddles, gets distracted, and flies through the Serpentine circuit unannounced. The exam asks what to check first for a given symptom, what to do with a breaker that pops, and what to do when nothing works. This lesson gives you the routine, the reasoning behind its order, and the corrective actions that follow.

## Fly first, then fault-find

Before any checklist, one rule: the aeroplane comes first. A dead radio is an inconvenience; a dead radio plus a wandering heading in a busy training area, or a climb into the base of the Class C steps above Jandakot, is a problem. So level off or settle into the circuit, trim, look out, and only then start working through the checks, a step at a time, looking up between each one.

That rule shapes the routine. Every check in it is quick, done from the seat, and can be interrupted and resumed. It finds the simple causes; if they are cleared and the radio still does not work, you stop fault-finding and switch to the radio failure procedure.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Fault-finding is not a reason to stop looking out. In a CTAF circuit with a silent radio, other pilots cannot hear you and you cannot hear them, so your lookout is now the only collision avoidance you have. Do one check, look out, do the next.

## Think of the radio as a chain

A radio message travels along a chain of parts. Coming out, it goes from your voice into the **microphone**, through the **audio panel** to the selected **transmitter**, which needs **electrical power**, then out through the **antenna** to the other station. Coming in, it runs the other way: antenna, **receiver**, audio panel, headset or speaker, ear. [PAKC 3.6](/notes/PAKC/3.6) takes each of those parts one at a time; here you only need their order.

Written as one line, the chain is **power, radio, audio panel, microphone or headset, antenna, the other station**. A break anywhere stops everything downstream of it, so you test one link and the result tells you which half of the chain the fault is in. The routine also puts the commonest causes first: plugs, switches and settings fail far more often than electronics, and they are the only things you can fix in flight. Most "radio failures" in light aircraft are switch or plug problems, not dead radios.

??? check "Check yourself: why does the fault-finding routine follow the signal path rather than starting wherever you first suspect a fault?"
    Because one break in the chain silences everything after it, and testing the links in order tells you which part of the chain is at fault. Starting from power and working outwards also puts the commonest and simplest causes first.

## The routine fault-finding check

Here is the routine, in order. Learn the order, then learn why each item sits where it does.

1. **Power.** Master switch and avionics master on? Radio switched on and display lit? Ammeter or voltmeter normal? A low voltage warning means the alternator has failed and the radios may be next.
2. **Circuit breakers.** Look for a popped breaker for the COM, audio panel or intercom. Reset it **once** only; if it pops again, leave it out (there is a fault, possibly an electrical fire risk).
3. **Frequency.** Is the right frequency in the **active** window, not the standby? Has it been mis-set by one digit (126.7 vs 127.6)?
4. **Volume and squelch.** Volume up; open the squelch. If you hear hiss the receiver and audio path are working; you may simply be out of range or nobody is talking.
5. **Audio panel.** Correct MIC (transmit) selector, and the receiver selected for listening.
6. **Headset and microphone.** Plugs fully in, in the correct sockets (mic and phone plugs swapped is common). Try the other headset socket, a spare headset, or the hand microphone and the cabin speaker.
7. **Stuck microphone.** If the transmit light (often a "T" or "TX" on the display) stays on, the PTT is stuck. You cannot hear anyone, and you are blocking the frequency for all. Unplug or switch to the other COM.
8. **Other radio.** Change to COM 2 and try again.
9. **Range and terrain.** VHF is line of sight; climb if safe, or try a station closer or higher ([PAKC 3.10](/notes/PAKC/3.10)).

### Power and breakers first

Power is first because without it nothing else can be tested. The **ammeter** or **voltmeter** tells you whether the alternator is charging. If it is not, the battery is carrying the whole electrical load and will run down, and the radios will die with it. That is a corrective action in its own right: switch off what you do not need, keep one COM, and plan to land while you still have it (see the battery endurance in [PAKC 3.6](/notes/PAKC/3.6)).

The **circuit breakers** come next because a popped breaker is the commonest cause of a radio that has gone dark on its own. A circuit breaker is a switch that opens itself when too much current flows, to protect the wiring from overheating. A single trip may be a momentary spike, which one reset clears. A real fault, such as a chafed wire touching the airframe, trips it again, and then the breaker is telling you that a circuit behind the panel is overheating. Holding it in, or resetting it again and again, defeats the protection and can start an electrical fire. So: one reset, and if it pops again, leave it out.

??? check "Check yourself: why do you reset a popped circuit breaker only once?"
    A single trip may be a transient; a second trip means there is a real overload or short in that circuit. Resetting it again forces current through a faulty circuit, which can overheat the wiring and start a fire, so you leave it out and treat the radio as failed.

### Frequency, volume and squelch: the hiss test

With power confirmed, look at the frequency. Modern COMs show an **active** frequency, the one you transmit and receive on, and a **standby** frequency that you preset and then flip across. Setting the next frequency in standby and forgetting to flip it is a very common slip, and so is a transposed digit: 127.6 set instead of 126.7. Either leaves you on a perfectly good frequency that nobody you want is using.

Then comes the most useful single test in radio fault-finding. Turn the volume up and open the **squelch**. Squelch is a gate that mutes the receiver's output when no signal is present, so you do not have to listen to background noise all flight (the controls are in [PAKC 3.9](/notes/PAKC/3.9)). When you open it, the gate is forced open. If the receiver, the audio panel and your headset are all working, you will hear **hiss**, the noise of an empty channel.

That hiss splits the chain in two. **Hiss but no stations** means everything from the receiver to your ears works, so the fault is out in front of the radio: the wrong frequency, or you are out of range or shielded by terrain, or simply nobody is talking. **No hiss at all** means the problem is between the radio and your ear: power to the radio, the breaker, the audio panel selection, the headset or its plugs. One action has halved the search.

### Audio panel and headset

The **audio panel** chooses which receivers you hear and which transmitter your microphone keys. With two COMs, it is easy to be listening to COM 1 while the MIC selector is on COM 2. You then hear everyone on the CTAF and nobody hears your broadcasts, because they are going out on the area frequency. Check that the MIC selector and the receiver buttons are where you think they are.

The **headset** plugs are the next weak point. A headset has two plugs, a larger one for the headphones and a smaller one for the microphone, and they go into separate sockets. A plug that is half out, or the two plugs swapped, gives exactly the symptoms of a dead radio. If the plugs look right, try the other headset socket, a spare headset, or bypass the headset entirely with the **hand microphone** and the **cabin speaker**. If the hand mic and speaker work, the radio is fine and the headset is the fault.

### The stuck microphone

One fault hurts everyone else as well as you. If the press-to-talk switch sticks closed, the radio transmits continuously and the TX indicator stays lit. While a radio transmits it cannot receive, so you hear nobody, and your open carrier blocks the frequency for every other aircraft on it. Unplug the PTT or the headset, or switch to the other COM, at once.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    A silent frequency can be a stuck microphone, not a quiet day. If the TX light is on and you are not pressing the button, you are the reason nobody else can talk.

### Other radio, then range

If one COM still will not work, the second one is a complete second chain from the audio panel outwards, with its own transmitter, receiver and antenna. Changing to COM 2 tests all of them at once.

Last comes **range**. VHF travels in straight lines, like light, so a hill or the curve of the earth blocks it: low behind the Darling Scarp you can lose a coastal-plain station that was readable minutes earlier. Climbing, where airspace and cloud allow, extends the line of sight ([PAKC 3.10](/notes/PAKC/3.10)).

!!! example "Worked example: hiss but no replies after leaving the CTAF"
    Situation: you have departed a country strip for the training area and changed frequency. Your call gets no answer, and you have heard nothing for five minutes.

    1. Fly: level at your planned altitude, trimmed, look out.
    2. Power: COM display lit, ammeter showing a charge. **Power is fine.**
    3. Breakers: all in. **No tripped circuit.**
    4. Frequency: the active window shows 127.6; the frequency you meant to set was 126.7. **Transposed digits.**
    5. Correct it, open the squelch: hiss is heard, then other traffic. **Fault cleared.**

    The radio was never faulty. Reading the active window digit by digit would have caught it before the first call.

??? check "Check yourself: you open the squelch and hear a strong hiss, but no stations. What does that tell you, and what do you check next?"
    The receiver, audio panel and headset are all working, so the fault is in front of the radio. Check that the right frequency is in the active window, then consider range and terrain: climb if you can, or try a closer station.

## Matching the symptom to the cause

Each symptom points at a part of the chain. The table gathers the common ones. Read each row as a short piece of reasoning, not a fact to memorise.

| Symptom | Likely cause | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Nothing heard, no hiss with squelch open | Radio or audio off, circuit breaker, headset | Check power, breaker, plugs; try speaker |
| Hiss but no stations | Wrong frequency, out of range | Check frequency, climb, try another station |
| Others cannot hear you, you hear them | Mic plug, wrong MIC selector, PTT, antenna | Check plugs and selector, use hand mic, other COM |
| Constant TX light, no reception | Stuck PTT | Unplug PTT or headset, change radio |
| Weak, noisy reception | Squelch set high, range, antenna | Adjust squelch, climb, report to maintenance |
| Whine that varies with RPM | Alternator noise | Report for maintenance |

Look at the logic. **No hiss** and **hiss but no stations** are the squelch test from above. **They cannot hear you but you hear them** means receive is fine, so the fault is in the parts used only for transmitting: microphone plug, MIC selector, PTT, and possibly the antenna. A **whine that rises and falls with RPM** is electrical noise from the alternator getting into the audio; it does not stop you communicating, but it is a defect to report.

Try this one before opening the solution. You have just swapped headsets with your instructor before departing Murrayfield. You hear the CTAF clearly, but two of your broadcasts get no response and an aircraft in the circuit asks whether anyone is on the runway. Which part of the chain is at fault, and what do you check?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    1. You hear others clearly, so the receive path, the frequency and the power are fine. **The fault is on the transmit side only.**
    2. Check the MIC selector on the audio panel: still on COM 1, the CTAF. **Not the selector.**
    3. Check the headset plugs: the microphone plug is only half in after the swap. **Push it home.**
    4. If that does not fix it, try the other headset socket or the hand microphone, then COM 2.

    The swap disturbed the plug. Until the fix is confirmed, assume nobody has heard you and look out accordingly.

## When the fault cannot be cleared

Sometimes the routine ends with no fix. At that point you stop fault-finding and change from diagnosing to coping. The corrective actions are:

- Squawk **7600** if you have a transponder and are, or were about to be, communicating with ATC.
- Follow the radio failure procedure in **ERSA EMERG** ([PAKC 3.8](/notes/PAKC/3.8)): stay in VMC, land at the most suitable aerodrome, report arrival.
- Transmit "blind" in case your transmitter works even though your receiver does not.
- Afterwards, record the defect on the maintenance release (or in the aircraft's defect log) so the next pilot is not caught out.

![Which transponder code tells ATC your radio has failed?](diagram:transponder-emergency-codes "7600 is radio failure; 7700 emergency and 7500 unlawful interference are the other two, all listed in ERSA EMERG.")

Each one has a reason. **7600** tells a radar controller, without a word, that you have lost communications, so they can keep other traffic clear and expect you to follow the **ERSA EMERG** procedure rather than improvise. **Transmitting blind** recognises that a fault may be on one side of the chain only. If your receiver has failed but your transmitter works, your broadcasts still tell others where you are, even though you cannot hear their replies. **Reporting arrival** stops a search being started for an aeroplane that is safely on the ground.

The last step happens on the ground and is easy to skip when the drama is over. A radio defect not written up is a trap set for the next pilot, who may be a student solo on their first navigation exercise. Write the defect on the maintenance release, or in whatever defect system your operator uses, in plain words: what failed, when, and what you tried.

!!! example "Worked example: alternator failure on the way to Northam"
    Situation: cruising at 4,500 ft east of the Scarp, the low voltage light comes on and the ammeter shows a discharge.

    1. Fly: maintain heading and altitude, look out.
    2. Diagnose: low voltage warning plus discharge means **the alternator has failed and the battery is carrying the load.**
    3. Act on power: switch off non-essential equipment, leave one COM on. **Load shed.**
    4. Decide: the radios will last only as long as the battery, so **plan to land at the nearest suitable aerodrome** while you can still make your calls.
    5. If the radio then dies: squawk 7600 if you had been talking to ATC, follow ERSA EMERG, transmit blind, stay in VMC.
    6. After landing: report arrival if required, and **write the alternator and radio defect on the maintenance release.**

    The fault-finding found the cause early, which bought time to land with the radio still working.

??? check "Check yourself: if your receiver may have failed but your transmitter may still work, why make blind transmissions?"
    Because other aircraft and ATC might still hear you even though you cannot hear them. Prefixing your normal calls with "transmitting blind" tells them where you are and what you intend, so they can keep clear.

## Bringing it together

Returning from a solo in the Jandakot training area, you call the tower at the inbound point and get nothing. You hold clear of the zone boundary, trim and look out. Power: display lit, ammeter charging. Breakers: all in. Frequency: tower is in the standby window, the ATIS in the active. You flip it, call again and still hear nothing. You open the squelch: no hiss. That points behind the radio, so you check the audio panel (COM 1 selected for both MIC and receive) and then the headset: the headphone plug has worked out of its socket. You push it home, the hiss returns, then the tower, asking you to report your position. Two simple faults stacked on one another, both found by working in order. Had the routine found nothing, the next steps were 7600, ERSA EMERG and blind transmissions; either way, a loose socket goes on the maintenance release.

??? check "Check yourself: you have flipped the right frequency into the active window and still hear nothing. Opening the squelch gives no hiss. Which part of the chain do you now look at?"
    The part between the radio and your ears: the radio's power and breaker, the audio panel receiver selection, and the headset plugs. No hiss means the receive audio is not reaching you, so the frequency was not the whole problem.

## Key points

- **3.3.1** Fly first, then work along the chain from the simplest cause: power, breakers, frequency in the active window, volume and squelch, audio panel, headset and plugs, stuck mic, other COM, range. The squelch hiss test tells you whether the fault is behind the radio or in front of it. Reset a breaker once only. If nothing works: 7600 if you were talking to ATC, ERSA EMERG radio failure procedure, transmit blind, stay in VMC, land at the most suitable aerodrome, report arrival, and write the defect on the maintenance release.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    A popped circuit breaker is reset once. If it trips again, do not hold it in: the protective device is telling you there is a fault, and repeated resetting can start an electrical fire. Expect symptom questions: no hiss points to power, breakers, audio panel or headset; hiss without stations points to frequency or range; a constant TX light is a stuck microphone.
