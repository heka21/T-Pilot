## What the exam expects (PAKC 2.3)

- **2.3.1** Explain the pitot-static system, pitot and static pressure, the alternate static source, and pressure error.
- **2.3.2** Say what indicated, calibrated and true airspeed each mean, and how one becomes the next.
- **2.3.3** State how the ASI, VSI and altimeter are affected by blockages and leaks, manoeuvres, lag, a wrong subscale setting and non-standard temperature and pressure, and what an instantaneous VSI gains you.
- **2.3.4** Describe how a direct-reading magnetic compass is built: magnets that seek magnetic north, a damping fluid that must be free of bubbles, and a pendulous magnet system that causes errors.
- **2.3.5** State what turning and acceleration errors do to the compass in the southern hemisphere.
- **2.3.6** Explain the compass correction card and use it to find the heading to steer for a magnetic heading.
- **2.3.7** Describe how you check the primary flight instruments are serviceable before flight.

## Why this matters

[BAKC 3.5](/notes/BAKC/3.5) taught how each instrument works and fails. Flying further and higher as a private pilot, the small errors you could ignore in the circuit start to matter. Cruising at 8,500 ft to Kalgoorlie, the ASI reads nearly 20 kt less than your true airspeed, and if you plan with the wrong one, every time and fuel figure in the plan is wrong. Crossing the Stirling Range on a cold winter morning, the altimeter over-reads by enough to eat into your terrain margin. With the DI drifting, the compass is your heading reference, and in the southern hemisphere it lies in a particular way every time you turn. The exam asks for the airspeed chain, a TAS calculation, the error directions, and the southern-hemisphere compass rules.

## The pitot-static system and its imperfections

### Pitot pressure and static pressure

The **pitot-static system** is the pitot tube, the static vent or vents, the lines joining them, and the three instruments they feed: ASI, altimeter and VSI.

![Which instruments use pitot pressure and which use static?](diagram:pitot-static-system "Pitot pressure goes only to the ASI; static pressure feeds all three, and the alternate static source can stand in for a blocked vent.")

Air ramming into the pitot tube comes to rest, so the tube holds the surrounding pressure plus the pressure of stopping the moving air: **pitot pressure** (total pressure). The flush static vent senses only the surrounding air's own pressure: **static pressure**. The ASI measures the difference, **dynamic pressure**, $\tfrac{1}{2}\rho V^2$, which grows with air density $\rho$ and with the square of speed $V$.

### Pressure error

The static vent has an impossible job: to measure undisturbed air pressure from a hole in an aeroplane that is disturbing the air around it. The fuselage speeds the flow up in some places and slows it in others, and pressure changes wherever flow speed changes. No spot on the fuselage is right at every speed and attitude. The flow pattern changes with **airspeed**, **flap setting** and **angle of attack**, so the error changes too.

That difference between the pressure the vent senses and the true static pressure is **pressure error**, also called **position error** because it depends on where the vent sits. The pitot tube can add a little of its own when the airflow meets it at an angle. Pressure error affects all three pressure instruments, but it shows most on the ASI, and it is largest **at low speed with flap extended**, because that is when the angle of attack is greatest and the flow around the fuselage most distorted. The ASI also carries a small **instrument error** from its own mechanism. The manufacturer measures both in flight testing and publishes the correction as the **airspeed calibration table** in section 5 of the POH.

### The alternate static source

Ice, insects or a wasp's nest can block the static vent. Many aeroplanes therefore have an **alternate static source**: a valve under the panel that lets the instruments take static pressure from inside the cabin instead.

Cabin air is not quite at outside static pressure. Air flowing fast over the cabin lowers the pressure outside the doors and windows, and the cabin leaks towards it, so in an unpressurised aeroplane in flight the cabin pressure is **slightly lower** than true static. Lower static pressure is what the instruments would see if you were higher and faster. So on the alternate source the **altimeter reads slightly high**, the **ASI reads slightly high**, and the **VSI shows a brief climb** at the moment you select it, then settles. The instruments are usable but less accurate; the POH gives the corrections.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Pressure error is not a blockage and not a fault. It is present in every serviceable aeroplane at every speed, it changes with speed and configuration, and the only cure is the POH calibration table.

??? check "Check yourself: why is pressure error usually largest at low speed with full flap?"
    Because the flow around the fuselage, and so the pressure at the static vent, depends on angle of attack and configuration. At low speed with flap down the angle of attack is high and the flow pattern most distorted, so the vent is furthest from sensing true static pressure.

## From indicated to true airspeed

### Three airspeeds

**Indicated airspeed (IAS)** is what the needle shows: dynamic pressure, on a dial that assumes sea-level density. The wing also responds to dynamic pressure, so stall and limit speeds are given as IAS.

**Calibrated airspeed (CAS)** is IAS corrected for pressure error and instrument error, from the POH calibration table. In the cruise most light aeroplanes show CAS and IAS within a couple of knots; the gap opens at low speed with flap down.

**True airspeed (TAS)** is your actual speed through the air. It is CAS corrected for **air density**, which depends on pressure height and temperature. TAS is what navigation uses: add the wind to it and you get **groundspeed**, the speed over the ground.

![How does indicated airspeed become groundspeed?](diagram:airspeed-chain-ictg "Each step has one correction: instrument and position error for CAS, density for TAS, wind for groundspeed.")

### Why TAS is higher than CAS at height

The ASI responds to $\tfrac{1}{2}\rho V^2$. Climb into thinner air and $\rho$ falls, so to make the same dynamic pressure, and the same reading, the aeroplane must be moving faster through the air. So at height the ASI reads less than the true speed. Heat does the same, because warm air is less dense; **density height** ([PAKC 2.1](/notes/PAKC/2.1)) combines both.

The rule of thumb is that **TAS exceeds CAS by about 2 percent for every 1,000 ft of density height**:

$$\text{TAS} \approx \text{CAS} \times (1 + 0.02 \times \text{density height in thousands of feet})$$

It is close enough for planning; the navigation computer gives the exact figure.

!!! example "Worked example: TAS on a warm day"
    Given: pressure height 6,000 ft, outside air temperature +20 degrees, IAS 105 kt, POH calibration table gives CAS 103 kt.

    1. ISA temperature at 6,000 ft: $15 - 2 \times 6 = \mathbf{3}$ degrees.
    2. Deviation from ISA: $20 - 3 = \mathbf{+17}$ degrees.
    3. Density height: $6000 + 120 \times 17 = 6000 + 2040 = \mathbf{8{,}040\ ft}$.
    4. TAS: $103 \times (1 + 0.02 \times 8.04) = 103 \times 1.161 = \mathbf{120\ kt}$ (119.6).

    With 105 kt on the dial you are moving through the air at about 120 kt. Plan the leg with 120 kt, and the flight computer will confirm it within a knot or two.

Now try one before opening the solution: pressure height 4,000 ft, outside air temperature +23 degrees, CAS 100 kt. What is the TAS?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    ISA at 4,000 ft is $15 - 2 \times 4 = 7$ degrees, so the deviation is $23 - 7 = +16$ degrees. Density height $= 4000 + 120 \times 16 = 5{,}920$ ft. TAS $\approx 100 \times (1 + 0.02 \times 5.92) = 100 \times 1.118 =$ **about 112 kt**. The flight computer gives about the same.

![Find the density height for any pressure and temperature](widget:density-height "Heat pushes density height up fast, and every 1,000 ft of density height adds about 2 percent to your TAS.")

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The chain runs in a fixed order: IAS, then CAS (instrument and pressure error), then TAS (density), then groundspeed (wind). A distractor will define CAS as "TAS corrected for wind" or "IAS corrected for density". Density belongs to the CAS-to-TAS step only.

??? check "Check yourself: at the same CAS, is your TAS higher on a 35 degree day or a 15 degree day at the same pressure height, and why?"
    Higher on the hot day. Warm air is less dense, so the density height is greater, and the aeroplane must move faster through the air to produce the same dynamic pressure and the same reading.

## How the pressure instruments err

[BAKC 3.5](/notes/BAKC/3.5) worked through the blockages one by one; the diagram is the recap. This section adds leaks, manoeuvres, lag, the IVSI, and temperature error.

![What does each blockage do to the three pressure instruments?](diagram:pitot-static-blockages "A static blockage hits all three instruments; a pitot blockage hits only the ASI; the alternate static makes them read slightly high.")

### The ASI

**Blockages.** Pitot blocked with the drain clear: the trapped pressure leaks away and the ASI falls towards zero. Pitot and drain both blocked: the ASI acts as an altimeter, over-reading in a climb and under-reading in a descent. Static vent blocked: the ASI under-reads in a climb and over-reads in a descent.

**Leaks.** A leak lets the wrong pressure in. A **static line that leaks inside the cabin** feeds the instruments with cabin pressure, so they behave as if the alternate static source were selected: slightly high. A **pitot line that leaks** loses some of its ram pressure, so the difference the ASI measures is too small and the **ASI under-reads**.

**Manoeuvre-induced errors.** The pressure at the static vent depends on the flow around the fuselage, and an abrupt manoeuvre changes that flow suddenly: a sharp pull-out from a dive, a push-over, a sideslip. For a few seconds afterwards the ASI, altimeter and VSI may all read incorrectly; fly attitude and let them settle.

### The VSI

**Static blockage.** Capsule and case both hold the same trapped pressure, so the **VSI reads zero** whatever the aeroplane does.

**Lag.** The case pressure catches up with the capsule only through a calibrated leak, so the VSI is always slightly behind. It shows the trend at once but takes about **6 to 9 seconds** to settle on the true rate. On entering a climb or descent abruptly it may briefly show the **opposite** trend, because the pitch change itself disturbs the static pressure. Use it for the trend and confirm with the altimeter.

**The instantaneous VSI (IVSI).** The IVSI adds an **accelerometer**: a small weighted piston in a cylinder, held by springs, connected to the capsule line. When the aeroplane starts to climb or descend, the piston's inertia moves it within its cylinder, pumping a small pressure change into the capsule at once, before the static pressure has changed much. As the vertical acceleration stops the piston recentres and the calibrated leak takes over. The IVSI removes most of the lag and the reverse indication. It is still a static instrument: a blocked vent stops it too.

### The altimeter

**Static blockage.** The case pressure stops changing, so the **altimeter freezes** at the height where the vent blocked.

**Lag.** The capsules and linkage take a moment to follow a pressure change, so in a rapid climb or descent the altimeter lags by a few seconds. Level off with that in mind.

**Incorrect subscale setting.** Each hectopascal of error moves the reading about **30 ft**, and a setting too high makes the altimeter read too high. Flying from high to low pressure without resetting does the same: "from high to low, look out below". The arithmetic is in [BAKC 3.5](/notes/BAKC/3.5).

![What does a wrong subscale setting do to the reading?](widget:altimeter-subscale "Each hectopascal between the subscale and the real QNH moves the reading about 30 ft; set too high a number and you are lower than the altimeter says.")

**Non-standard temperature.** The altimeter is calibrated with the **ISA**, which assumes a particular temperature at every height. With the correct QNH set it reads correctly at the station that supplied the QNH, but the air between the station and you may be colder or warmer than ISA.

Cold air is denser and more compact, so the pressure falls off faster with height and the pressure levels are squeezed closer together. The pressure level your altimeter calls 4,000 ft sits physically lower than 4,000 ft. In **cold air you are lower than indicated**. In warm air the levels are stretched apart and you are higher than indicated. The memory aid extends the pressure one: **"from high to low, or hot to cold, look out below"**.

The size of the error, as a rule of thumb, is about **4 percent of the height above the QNH station for every 10 degrees** the air differs from ISA. It is small near the ground and grows with height, so it matters most for terrain clearance on a cold day. In a Western Australian summer the error is in the safe direction.

!!! example "Worked example: a cold morning over the Stirling Range"
    Given: a winter morning, air about 10 degrees colder than ISA; correct QNH set from a station near sea level; indicated altitude 4,500 ft; rule of thumb 4 percent of height above the station per 10 degrees.

    1. Height above the QNH station: about $\mathbf{4{,}500\ ft}$.
    2. Error: $4500 \times 0.04 \times \tfrac{10}{10} = \mathbf{180\ ft}$.
    3. Direction: cold air, so you are lower than indicated.
    4. True altitude: $4500 - 180 = \mathbf{4{,}320\ ft}$.

    The altimeter is set correctly and serviceable, and you are still 180 ft lower than it says. Over peaks of about 3,600 ft, the clearance you think is 900 ft is nearer 720 ft.

Now try the opposite case before opening the solution: on a summer afternoon the air is 15 degrees warmer than ISA, the QNH is correct and comes from a station near sea level, and you are indicating 6,500 ft. Roughly what is your true altitude?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    Error $= 6500 \times 0.04 \times \tfrac{15}{10} = 6500 \times 0.06 = 390$ ft. Warm air stretches the pressure levels apart, so you are higher than indicated: true altitude **about 6,890 ft**. On a hot day the error is in the safe direction.

**Non-standard pressure.** The altimeter only knows the datum you give it. When the pressure changes along your route, the subscale error applies until you reset the area QNH.

??? check "Check yourself: the area QNH is 1009 but the subscale still shows the 1016 you set at departure. Is the altimeter reading high or low, and by about how much?"
    High, by about $7 \times 30 = 210$ ft. The subscale is set too high, so the altimeter over-reads and you are about 210 ft lower than it shows.

## How the magnetic compass is built

### Magnets that seek magnetic north

Under the card of the **direct-reading magnetic compass** are two or more small **magnetised needles**, fixed to the card and balanced on a **pivot**. A freely suspended magnet lines up with the Earth's field, so the needles, and the card, align with the horizontal part of that field and point to **magnetic north**. The aeroplane turns around the card, and you read the heading against the **lubber line**.

### The fluid

The bowl is filled with a light, clear **fluid**, usually a kerosene-like oil. It **damps oscillation**: a dry card would swing for a long time after every turn or bump, but the fluid resists the motion so the card settles quickly. And it **reduces friction**: the card floats, taking weight off the pivot, so it turns freely.

### No bubbles

The fluid must fill the bowl and be **free of bubbles**. A bubble means fluid has leaked; the card is no longer fully damped and floated, so it swings and sticks, and the pivot can corrode. A compass with a bubble is **unserviceable**.

### Pendulosity, and why it causes errors

The Earth's field does not lie flat. Away from the equator its lines slope into the ground, steeply near the magnetic poles: this slope is **dip**. In the southern hemisphere the **south-seeking** end of a free magnet is pulled downwards. A card tilted by dip would rub, stick, and read wrongly.

The cure is to make the magnet assembly **pendulous**: it hangs below its pivot, like a pendulum, so that gravity holds it roughly level against the pull of dip. To balance that pull, its **centre of gravity sits a little to the equator side of the pivot**, which in the southern hemisphere means the north end.

That pendulous design is also the cause of the compass's errors. Whenever the aeroplane accelerates, turning or changing speed, it pushes the pivot, but the assembly's centre of gravity lags behind like a passenger thrown sideways in a car. Because the centre of gravity is offset from the pivot, that lag rotates the magnets, and the card shows a heading change that has not happened.

## Turning and acceleration errors in the southern hemisphere

All the rules below follow from one fact: in the southern hemisphere the assembly's centre of gravity is on the **north** side of the pivot, and it lags behind whenever the aeroplane accelerates.

### Acceleration errors

Fly due east and accelerate. The pivot is pushed forward, towards the east; the centre of gravity, on the north side of the pivot, lags behind, towards the west. That swings the north end of the magnets backwards, the card rotates, and the compass shows a heading change towards **south**. Fly due west and accelerate, and the same lag again shows a turn towards south. Decelerate and the centre of gravity surges forward instead, showing a turn towards **north**.

The southern-hemisphere memory aid is **SAND**: **S**outh on **A**cceleration, **N**orth on **D**eceleration. The errors are **largest on east and west headings** and **nil on north and south**, because heading north or south the centre of gravity is directly ahead of or behind the pivot, and pushing it back or forward produces no rotation.

### Turning errors

In a turn the aeroplane is accelerating towards the centre of the turn, and the lagging centre of gravity swings outwards.

**Turning through south**, the north end of the assembly is behind the pivot. The outward swing rotates the magnets the same way the aeroplane is turning, so the card turns with the aeroplane and the reading changes **too slowly**. The compass is **sluggish: it lags**, and at higher latitudes may even start by showing a turn the wrong way. You must **roll out early**, before the compass shows the heading you want: **undershoot south**.

**Turning through north**, the north end is ahead of the pivot. Now the swing rotates the magnets against the turn, the card moves the opposite way to the aeroplane, and the reading changes **too fast**. The compass is **lively: it leads**. You must **roll out late**, after it has passed the heading you want: **overshoot north**.

The errors are **largest when turning through north or south**, and **nil when turning through east or west**, where the outward swing is along the magnets' axis. The usual allowance is about **30 degrees at a rate one turn in mid latitudes**, less at lower latitudes; on headings between north or south and east or west, allow a proportion of it. Northern-hemisphere books give the opposite rules (UNOS and ANDS), so check which hemisphere a text was written for.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Learn the **action**, not just a slogan: in the southern hemisphere, roll out **early** when turning onto south and **late** when turning onto north. Some sources say "overshoot south, undershoot north", describing the card rather than the pilot, and mix up which way it leads or lags. An answer that rolls out early through south and late through north is the southern-hemisphere answer, whatever words surround it.

Because of both errors, the compass is read only in **steady, straight, level, unaccelerated flight**, and the directional indicator is reset to it only then.

??? check "Check yourself: in level flight heading 270 near Perth you open the throttle to climb power. What does the compass do, and what is the real heading doing?"
    It swings towards south (SAND: South on Acceleration), so it shows a turn to the left onto a lower heading, even though the real heading has not changed. Wait until the speed is steady before reading it.

## The compass correction card

The compass also feels the aeroplane itself: the steel in the engine, the magnetos, the radios and the currents in the wiring make magnetic fields of their own. The result is **deviation**: the difference between the heading the compass shows and the true magnetic heading. Because the aeroplane's own field turns with it, deviation changes with heading.

![How do true, magnetic and compass headings differ?](diagram:true-magnetic-compass "Variation separates true and magnetic north; deviation, from the aeroplane itself, separates magnetic and compass north.")

An engineer measures deviation in a **compass swing**, lining the aeroplane up on known magnetic headings and adjusting small compensating magnets; what is left is recorded on the **compass correction card** (deviation card) mounted beside the compass. Its purpose is to tell you which compass heading to steer to achieve a given magnetic heading. It reads like this:

| For (magnetic) | 000 | 030 | 060 | 090 | 120 | 150 | 180 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Steer (compass)** | 002 | 033 | 064 | 092 | 120 | 148 | 176 |

Where the compass reads more than magnetic, deviation is **west**; where it reads less, deviation is **east**. The memory aid is **"deviation east, compass least; deviation west, compass best"**. The card must be current; the compass is re-swung after avionics changes or any work that alters the aeroplane's magnetic field.

!!! example "Worked example: the compass heading to steer"
    Given: the card above; your flight plan gives a magnetic heading of 075.

    1. The card entries either side: for 060 steer 064, for 090 steer 092.
    2. Deviation at 060: $64 - 60 = \mathbf{4}$ degrees (compass reads more, so 4 W). At 090: $92 - 90 = \mathbf{2}$ degrees W.
    3. 075 is halfway between, so deviation is about $\tfrac{4 + 2}{2} = \mathbf{3}$ degrees W.
    4. Compass heading to steer: $075 + 3 = \mathbf{078}$.

    Steer 078 on the compass and the aeroplane is flying 075 magnetic.

The card works in reverse too, when you want to know the magnetic heading you are actually flying. Try one before opening the solution: in steady level flight the compass reads 162. Using the card, what is your magnetic heading?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    The compass reading 162 lies between the steer figures 148 (for 150) and 176 (for 180), exactly halfway. Deviation there runs from 2 degrees E (150 magnetic reads 148) to 4 degrees E (180 reads 176), so halfway it is about 3 degrees E. Compass least, so magnetic is more: $162 + 3 =$ **165 magnetic**.

??? check "Check yourself: why does deviation change with heading when variation does not?"
    Deviation comes from the aeroplane's own magnetic fields, which turn with the aeroplane, so their effect on the compass changes as the heading changes. Variation is a property of the place on the Earth, the same whichever way you point.

## Checking the instruments before flight

[BAKC 3.5](/notes/BAKC/3.5) gave the checks and the reason for each. The PPL exam adds a few figures.

![Which gyro instruments do you check on the taxi, and what drives them?](diagram:gyro-instruments-panel "The attitude and heading indicators are vacuum driven, the turn coordinator electric: check each, and the suction gauge, after start.")

**Pressure instruments, before start.**

- **ASI**: reads zero at rest, or roughly the wind if parked facing it; pitot cover removed, pitot tube and drain clear, no damage. Static vents clear: a blocked vent will not show on the ground. Where the POH calls for it, check the pitot heat warms the tube.
- **Altimeter**: set the QNH and compare the reading with the aerodrome elevation: within **100 ft for VFR** (AIP ENR 1.7; verify against the current AIP). The subscale moves freely. Where two altimeters are fitted, they should agree.
- **VSI**: reads zero on the ground; if it shows a small steady value, note it and treat that as the zero in flight.

**Gyro instruments, after start and on the taxi.**

- **Attitude indicator**: erects within about **5 minutes** of start, no warning flag, shows level with the aeroplane level, and tilts no more than about **5 degrees** in taxi turns. Suction gauge in the green.
- **Directional indicator**: set to the compass; turns the correct way in taxi turns and stays within a few degrees of the compass when straight; no flag.
- **Turn coordinator**: shows a turn in the direction of the taxi turn, with the ball to the outside; no flag.

**Magnetic compass**: bowl full and the fluid clear with **no bubbles**; the card swings freely and settles; it turns the correct way in taxi turns and reads close to the runway direction when lined up; the **deviation card is present and legible**.

**After take-off**: airspeed alive on the roll, altimeter and VSI showing the climb.

??? check "Check yourself: on the taxi the attitude indicator banks about 15 degrees in each taxi turn. Is it serviceable, and what might be the cause?"
    No. A serviceable attitude indicator should stay within about 5 degrees in taxi turns. A gyro that tilts like this may not be up to speed, from low suction or a worn instrument; check the suction gauge and have it looked at before you rely on it.

## Bringing it together

On a June morning you plan Jandakot to Albany at 7,500 ft. The planning: CAS 105 kt, pressure height 7,500 ft, air 5 degrees colder than ISA. ISA at 7,500 ft is 0 degrees, so the temperature is about $-5$ degrees and the density height about $7500 - 600 = 6{,}900$ ft, giving a TAS of about $105 \times 1.138 \approx 119$ kt. Before start the altimeter reads within 30 ft of the Jandakot elevation and the compass is full and clear. En route the cold air puts you about $7500 \times 0.04 \times 0.5 = 150$ ft lower than indicated, and you reset the DI from the compass only in straight, level, unaccelerated flight. Turning onto a southerly heading with the compass alone, you roll out early, because turning towards south the compass lags. Descending towards Albany's higher ground with a stale QNH and cold air, remember that both altimeter errors point the same way: lower than you think.

## Key points

- **2.3.1** Pitot (total) pressure feeds only the ASI; static feeds all three. Pressure (position) error is the vent's failure to sense true static, largest at low speed with flap, removed by the POH table. Alternate (cabin) static reads slightly high.
- **2.3.2** IAS is the dial; CAS is IAS corrected for instrument and pressure error; TAS is CAS corrected for density, about 2 percent more per 1,000 ft of density height; add wind for groundspeed.
- **2.3.3** Pitot leak: ASI under-reads. Abrupt manoeuvres upset all three for a few seconds. VSI lags 6 to 9 seconds; the IVSI's accelerometer removes most of it. Static blockage freezes the altimeter and zeroes the VSI. 30 ft per hPa of subscale error; cold air puts you lower, about 4 percent per 10 degrees: hot to cold, look out below.
- **2.3.4** Magnets point to magnetic north; fluid damps and floats the card; a bubble makes it unserviceable; the pendulous, offset assembly counters dip and causes the errors.
- **2.3.5** Southern hemisphere: SAND on east and west headings; turning, lag through south (roll out early), lead through north (roll out late).
- **2.3.6** The card converts magnetic heading to compass heading to steer: deviation east, compass least; west, compass best.
- **2.3.7** ASI zero, covers off; altimeter within 100 ft of elevation (VFR); VSI zero; attitude indicator erect within about 5 minutes, within about 5 degrees in taxi turns; compass full, no bubbles, card present.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Southern-hemisphere compass rules are the ones examined: SAND for acceleration on east and west headings, and for turns, roll out early through south and late through north. Northern-hemisphere books (ANDS, UNOS) give the opposite. Have the airspeed chain in order (IAS, CAS, TAS, groundspeed), the 2 percent per 1,000 ft rule for TAS, 30 ft per hectopascal for the subscale, and "hot to cold, look out below" for temperature error.
