## What the exam expects (PAKC 3.10)

- **3.10.1** Explain how radio waves behave and how they are sent and received: (a) the MF, HF, VHF and UHF frequency bands; (b) what a radio wave is and how far a transmission reaches; (c) the ground-wave and sky-wave paths; (d) what affects propagation and reception: terrain, the ionosphere, sunspots, electrical interference, thunderstorms and the loss of signal power; (e) how antennas are built and used.
- **3.10.2** Explain what limits VHF and HF communication, and what affects the quality and range of the signal.

## Why this matters

At 2,500 ft over the Swan Coastal Plain you can talk to Perth all day. Fly east past the scarp and descend into a valley near Toodyay, and the same radio on the same frequency can fall silent, although nothing in the aeroplane has changed. Climb a few hundred feet and the controller comes back. That is VHF behaving exactly as physics says it should, and once you understand why, you stop thinking of the radio as unreliable and start flying to keep the signal. Out over the Nullarbor or the Kimberley, beyond any VHF station, aircraft use HF, which reaches thousands of kilometres but hisses, fades and depends on the time of day. The exam asks you to calculate a wavelength and a VHF range, to match each band to its use and its propagation path, and to say why HF works at night on one frequency and not another. This lesson builds all of that from what a radio wave is.

## What a radio wave is

Throw a stone into a pond and ripples spread out. The water does not travel; the disturbance does. A radio wave is a similar travelling disturbance, but in electric and magnetic fields rather than water: an **electromagnetic wave**, the same kind of wave as light, only with a much longer wavelength. It needs no medium, so it crosses empty space, and it travels at the **speed of light**, about **300,000,000 m/s**.

Two numbers describe any wave. The **frequency** is how many complete waves pass a point each second, measured in **hertz** (Hz): one cycle per second. Radio frequencies are large, so they are given in **kilohertz** (kHz, thousands) and **megahertz** (MHz, millions). Kilo means thousand and mega means million, so 126.7 MHz = 126,700 kHz. The **wavelength** is the distance from one wave crest to the next, in metres.

Because every radio wave travels at the same speed, frequency and wavelength are locked together. If more waves pass each second, each must be shorter. Speed equals frequency times wavelength:

$$\lambda = \frac{c}{f} = \frac{300\,000\,000\ \text{m/s}}{f\ \text{(Hz)}}$$

Measure the frequency in megahertz instead and the millions cancel, leaving the working rule:

$$\text{wavelength (m)} = \frac{300}{\text{frequency (MHz)}}$$

!!! example "Worked example: the wavelength of a VHF COM signal and its antenna"
    Given: a COM frequency of 118 MHz.

    1. Wavelength: $\lambda = 300 \div 118 = \mathbf{2.54\ m}$.
    2. Quarter-wave antenna length: $2.54 \div 4 = \mathbf{0.64\ m}$, about 64 cm.

    A VHF COM antenna is a quarter-wave whip or blade, so a little over half a metre is all it needs. That is why COM antennas on a light aeroplane are short.

Now try one before opening the solution: what is the wavelength of an NDB signal at 300 kHz?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    Convert to megahertz first: 300 kHz = 0.3 MHz. Then $\lambda = 300 \div 0.3 =$ **1,000 m**. A wave a kilometre long cannot be matched by a short whip, which is why low-frequency transmitters need large antennas and the aircraft receiver uses loop and sense antennas instead.

The other check: at 125 MHz, the wavelength is $300 \div 125 = 2.4$ m and a quarter-wave antenna is about 0.6 m.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The rule needs the frequency in **megahertz**. Put 300 kHz in as 300 and you get a wavelength of 1 m instead of 1,000 m. Convert kilohertz to megahertz (divide by 1,000) before you divide into 300.

## The frequency bands (3.10.1 a)

The radio spectrum is divided into bands, each ten times higher in frequency than the one below. Aviation uses four of them, and each one behaves differently enough that it has its own job.

| Band | Frequencies | Aviation use | Main propagation |
|---|---|---|---|
| MF | 300 kHz to 3 MHz | NDBs (with LF, roughly 190 to 1,750 kHz) | Ground wave |
| HF | 3 to 30 MHz | Long-range voice (oceanic, remote areas; aviation HF roughly 2.8 to 22 MHz) | Sky wave |
| VHF | 30 to 300 MHz | VHF COM 118 to 137 MHz, VOR and ILS 108 to 118 MHz | Line of sight (space wave) |
| UHF | 300 MHz to 3 GHz | DME, transponder (1,030/1,090 MHz), GNSS, ILS glideslope | Line of sight |

![Which band does what, and how does each travel?](diagram:frequency-band-ladder "What to notice: each band up is ten times the frequency and a tenth of the wavelength, and the path changes from ground wave to sky wave to line of sight.")

The pattern in the last column is the key to the whole lesson. **As frequency rises, waves behave more like light.** Low-frequency waves bend around the curve of the earth and around obstacles, as long ripples spread around a post in a pond. As the frequency rises, the ground wave dies out faster, the ionosphere stops bending the waves back, and they travel in straight lines, stopped by whatever is in the way, like a torch beam. **Lower frequencies** reach further around the earth but **need large antennas and more power**; higher frequencies need only small antennas but reach only as far as they can "see".

That explains each band's job. **MF** (and the LF just below it) carries the NDBs, whose signals follow the ground beyond the horizon. **HF** carries long-range voice by bouncing off the ionosphere. **VHF** carries everyday voice and the VOR, clear but short-ranged. **UHF** carries the transponder, DME and GNSS, which need precise straight-line signals and tiny antennas.

??? check "Check yourself: which band does the COM radio you use in the circuit work in, and how does its signal travel?"
    VHF, 118 to 137 MHz. Its signal travels in straight lines, line of sight (the space wave), so range depends on the aircraft's height and on terrain between you and the station.

## Three paths for a radio wave (3.10.1 c)

A wave leaving an antenna can reach a distant receiver by three routes. Which one dominates depends on the frequency.

![What are the three paths a radio wave can take?](diagram:radio-propagation-paths "What to notice: the ground wave follows the earth, the HF sky wave is bent back by the ionosphere, and VHF and UHF go straight, through the ionosphere and into space.")

### The ground wave

The **ground (surface) wave** **follows the curvature of the earth**. Part of the wave travels along the surface, and the ground drags on it slightly, tilting the wave front forward so it bends around the curve. Every bit of ground it travels over absorbs some energy, so it fades with distance.

The ground wave is **strong at LF and MF** and fades quickly at higher frequencies. Its **range depends on power and frequency**, and it is **greater over sea than over land**, because sea water conducts electricity far better than dry ground and absorbs less of the wave. This is **how NDBs are received**: an NDB's signal reaches you around the curve of the earth, well below the line of sight.

### The sky wave

Between roughly **60 and 400 km** up lies the **ionosphere**, layers of gas that the sun's radiation has **ionised**, stripping electrons from the atoms. A radio wave entering an ionised layer is **refracted**, bent, and if it is bent enough it comes back down to earth far from where it started. That returning wave is the **sky wave**. Each bounce off the ionosphere and back is a **hop**, and HF hops can be thousands of kilometres long. This gives **HF its long range**.

The sky wave creates a strange gap. Close to the transmitter you hear the ground wave, which soon fades. The first sky wave comes back down a long way out. In between lies the **skip zone (dead space)**, where nothing is heard: too far for the ground wave, too close for the sky wave.

![Why can a nearer aircraft hear nothing on HF?](diagram:hf-skip-zone "What to notice: the skip zone lies between where the ground wave fades and where the first sky wave comes down.")

### The space wave

At VHF and UHF the ionosphere is not dense enough to bend the waves back, so they pass **straight through** into space. What remains is the **space (direct) wave**, travelling in a straight line from antenna to antenna. It reaches only as far as the **radio horizon**: **line of sight**. If the receiver is below the horizon or behind a hill, the wave goes over its head.

??? check "Check yourself: an aircraft 150 km from an HF station hears nothing, while one 30 km away and one 1,500 km away both hear it clearly. Why?"
    The aircraft at 150 km is in the skip zone: beyond the range of the fading ground wave but short of where the first sky wave returns. The near aircraft gets the ground wave, the far one the sky wave.

## How far does VHF reach? (3.10.1 b)

Because VHF travels in straight lines, its range is set by geometry: how far can an antenna at your height "see" before the earth curves away? The higher you are, the further the horizon. Radio waves bend slightly more than light does in the lower atmosphere, so the radio horizon is a little beyond the visual one. The rule of thumb for line-of-sight range in nautical miles is:

$$\text{range (nm)} \approx 1.23 \times \sqrt{\text{height in feet}}$$

For two elevated stations, each has its own horizon, and the ranges add:

$$\text{range (nm)} \approx 1.23 \times \left(\sqrt{h_1} + \sqrt{h_2}\right)$$

Use the height above the terrain the ground station sits on.

The square root is the important part. Range grows with height, but more slowly than height does: **double the range needs four times the height**. That is why the first few thousand feet of climb make a large difference and the next few thousand a smaller one.

![How far does VHF reach from your height?](diagram:vhf-line-of-sight-range "What to notice: 1,500 ft gives about 48 nm and 5,000 ft about 87 nm; four times the height only doubles the range.")

!!! example "Worked example: VHF range from 5,000 ft"
    Given: an aircraft at 5,000 ft calling a ground station at sea level.

    1. Square root of the height: $\sqrt{5000} = \mathbf{70.7}$.
    2. Range: $1.23 \times 70.7 = \mathbf{87\ nm}$ (86.97).

    At 5,000 ft over flat country you can expect to work a sea-level station out to about 87 nm, if the transmitter power and the terrain allow.

!!! example "Worked example: a station on a hill"
    Given: an aircraft at 4,000 ft and a ground station on a 1,000 ft hill.

    1. Aircraft's term: $\sqrt{4000} = \mathbf{63.2}$.
    2. Station's term: $\sqrt{1000} = \mathbf{31.6}$.
    3. Range: $1.23 \times (63.2 + 31.6) = 1.23 \times 94.8 = \mathbf{117\ nm}$ (116.6).

    Putting the station on a hill added about 39 nm compared with a sea-level site. That is why VHF repeaters and ATC transmitters sit on hilltops and towers.

Now try one: you are in the circuit area at 1,500 ft, calling a station at sea level. What is the theoretical range?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    $\sqrt{1500} = 38.7$, so range $\approx 1.23 \times 38.7 =$ **48 nm** (47.6). At circuit and low-level training heights, VHF reaches only a few tens of miles, and less behind hills.

**Actual range is often less** than the formula gives. **Terrain** blocks the line of sight: hills between you and a Perth station, flying east of the scarp, cast a radio shadow you can fly into. **Transmitter power** is limited, so a weak signal may fade into noise before the geometric horizon. And the **antenna may be shielded by the airframe** in a turn, with the wing or fuselage between the antenna and the station.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Range does not rise in proportion to height. Doubling your height multiplies the range by only $\sqrt{2}$, about 1.4. To double the range you need **four times** the height.

??? check "Check yourself: a pilot at 1,000 ft behind a ridge near Toodyay cannot raise Perth, although the formula says the station is well within range. Why, and what is the remedy?"
    The formula assumes a clear line of sight; the ridge is blocking it and the aircraft is in its radio shadow. Climbing (if safe and permitted) or moving clear of the ridge restores the line of sight.

## What weakens or spoils the signal (3.10.1 d)

Six factors affect propagation and reception. Each acts on a particular band more than the others, and the pattern follows from the paths above.

**Terrain.** Hills and mountains **block VHF and UHF**, casting a shadow behind them. Terrain also **absorbs the ground wave**, more over land than over sea. And mountains can **reflect** low-frequency signals, which bends an NDB signal and gives the ADF a false bearing.

**The ionosphere.** It is made by the sun, so it changes with the sun. By day, ionisation is strong, and the layers can bend back higher frequencies; by night, ionisation fades and only lower frequencies come back, while higher ones go straight through into space. Its height and density also vary with the season. The practical rule for HF is: **by day use higher HF frequencies; at night lower ones**. The ionosphere also causes the NDB's **night effect**: after dark, sky waves from the NDB come back down and **interfere with the ground wave**, making the ADF needle wander.

**Sunspot activity.** The sun's activity rises and falls over an eleven-year cycle. **Solar flares and high sunspot counts disturb the ionosphere**, causing **HF fade-outs**, noise and **unpredictable range**. VHF, which ignores the ionosphere, is largely unaffected.

**Electrical interference.** Anything electrical can radiate noise: **alternator whine**, **ignition noise**, **faulty strobes** and **personal electronic devices**. It **affects VHF and especially LF and MF**, where the receiver is listening for weak, low-frequency signals in a band where electrical noise is strong. A whine in your headset that rises and falls with engine RPM is usually the alternator.

**Thunderstorms.** Lightning is a huge, brief electrical discharge, and it radiates across a wide range of frequencies. It causes **static crackle**, **worst on LF, MF and HF**. An **ADF may point to the storm**, because the lightning is a stronger signal than the NDB.

**Power attenuation.** Signal strength **falls with distance**. Spreading out from the antenna, the same power covers a growing area, so the strength falls with the square of distance (the **inverse square** law): twice as far, a quarter of the strength. The signal is also **absorbed** by the atmosphere, terrain, water and the airframe. And a **low battery reduces transmit power**, so a pilot on battery alone after an alternator failure may be heard over a shorter range than usual.

| Factor | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Terrain** | Blocks VHF/UHF (shadow behind hills); absorbs ground wave over land more than over sea; mountain reflections cause NDB errors |
| **Ionosphere** | Reflects HF; its height and density vary by day and night and season. By day use higher HF frequencies; at night lower ones. Causes NDB "night effect" when sky waves interfere with the ground wave |
| **Sunspot activity** | Solar flares and high sunspot counts disturb the ionosphere: HF fade-outs, noise, unpredictable range |
| **Electrical interference** | Alternator whine, ignition noise, faulty strobes, personal electronic devices; affects VHF and especially LF/MF |
| **Thunderstorms** | Lightning causes static crackle, worst on LF/MF/HF; an ADF may point to the storm |
| **Power attenuation** | Signal strength falls with distance (inverse square) and is absorbed by the atmosphere, terrain, water and the airframe; low battery reduces transmit power |

??? check "Check yourself: why does a thunderstorm 40 nm away crackle in the HF receiver but barely affect your VHF COM?"
    Lightning radiates strongly at low and high frequencies, and LF, MF and HF receivers pick it up as static; VHF is much less affected. The same effect can swing an ADF needle towards the storm.

## Antennas (3.10.1 e)

An antenna is the part of the system that turns electrical current into radio waves and back ([PAKC 3.6](/notes/PAKC/3.6)). It works best when its length is a set fraction of the wavelength, **half-wave or quarter-wave**, because then the current in it resonates with the wave, like a guitar string whose length matches the note.

Feed that into the wavelength rule and the shapes of aircraft antennas follow. VHF wavelengths are a couple of metres, so **VHF antennas are short whips or blades**. HF wavelengths are tens of metres, so **HF antennas are long wires or use tuned couplers** that make a shorter wire behave electrically like the right length. LF and MF wavelengths are hundreds of metres, far too long for any aircraft, so the **NDB receiver uses loop and sense antennas** that sense the direction and presence of the signal rather than resonating with it.

Antennas are also **polarised**: the wave's electric field lines up with the antenna. **VHF COM antennas are vertical, VOR antennas horizontal**, and a receiving antenna works best when it matches the transmitting one.

In use, antennas must be **clean, secure and undamaged**, and **bonding to the airframe** matters, because a poor electrical connection weakens the signal and adds noise. Remember also that **shielding by the fuselage or wing can blank a signal in a turn**: in a steep turn the antenna on the belly may face away from the station.

??? check "Check yourself: why is a VHF COM antenna a short blade, while an aircraft HF antenna is a long wire or needs a coupler?"
    An antenna works best at a set fraction (a quarter or half) of the wavelength. VHF wavelengths are about 2.5 m, so a quarter is about 60 cm; HF wavelengths are tens of metres, so the antenna must be long or electrically lengthened with a coupler.

## Limits of VHF and HF (3.10.2)

Put the two communication bands side by side and their strengths and weaknesses are mirror images.

**VHF** travels by line of sight. Its range is **limited by height and terrain**, so low down or behind hills you are **out of range**. Within range, though, it is **clear, low-noise and little affected by weather**, because it ignores the ionosphere and suffers less from static. That makes it the band for **normal ATC, CTAF and area frequencies**.

**HF** travels by sky wave, so its range is **thousands of kilometres**. But it is **noisy and fading**, and **affected by time of day, season and solar activity**, because it depends on the ionosphere. It has a **skip zone**, and choosing the **wrong frequency for the time of day gives no contact**. It is used in **remote and oceanic areas beyond VHF coverage**.

| | VHF | HF |
|---|---|---|
| Range | Line of sight, limited by height and terrain | Thousands of km via sky wave |
| Quality | Clear, low noise, little affected by weather | Noisy, fading, affected by time of day, season and solar activity |
| Problems | Out of range at low level or behind hills | Skip zone; wrong frequency for the time of day gives no contact |
| Use | Normal ATC, CTAF, area frequencies | Remote and oceanic areas beyond VHF coverage |

Source: AIP GEN 3.4 for the HF and VHF communications services; verify the current coverage and frequencies.

## Bringing it together

A ferry flight from Perth towards Kalgoorlie in an aeroplane with VHF and HF. At 1,500 ft leaving Perth, the formula gives you about 48 nm to a sea-level station; climbing to cruise widens it, which is why you climb before you lose contact. East of the scarp you descend into a valley and the area frequency goes quiet: terrain shadow, fixed by climbing. Beyond the reach of the VHF stations, you change to HF. In the afternoon a daytime frequency works; after dark it fades away and you change to a lower one, because the weakened ionosphere now bends back only lower frequencies. A thunderstorm to the south fills the HF with crackle and swings the ADF needle towards it, while the VHF stays clean. Every behaviour on that flight came from one fact: frequency decides the path a wave takes.

## Key points

- **3.10.1** Radio waves are electromagnetic waves at 300,000,000 m/s; wavelength (m) = 300 ÷ frequency (MHz). MF 300 kHz to 3 MHz (NDBs, ground wave), HF 3 to 30 MHz (long-range voice, sky wave), VHF 30 to 300 MHz (COM 118 to 137 MHz, VOR and ILS, line of sight), UHF 300 MHz to 3 GHz (DME, transponder, GNSS, glideslope). Higher frequency behaves more like light. Ground wave follows the earth (further over sea); sky wave is refracted by the ionosphere (60 to 400 km), leaving a skip zone; space wave is line of sight. VHF range ≈ 1.23 × √height in ft; two stations add. Terrain blocks VHF; the ionosphere changes day and night; sunspots disrupt HF; electrical noise hits LF/MF; lightning causes static on LF/MF/HF and swings the ADF; signals fall with the square of distance. Antennas are half or quarter wavelength, polarised, and must be clean, secure and bonded.
- **3.10.2** VHF: clear and low-noise but line of sight, limited by height and terrain. HF: thousands of kilometres but noisy, fading, with a skip zone; higher frequencies by day, lower at night.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    VHF range depends mainly on height: double the range needs four times the height. HF needs a frequency chosen for the time of day: high by day, low at night. Have the two formulas by heart, wavelength = 300 ÷ MHz and range ≈ 1.23 × √feet, and convert kilohertz to megahertz before dividing. Expect a matching question: NDB with ground wave, HF with sky wave, VHF and UHF with line of sight.
