## What the exam expects (PAKC 3.7)

- **3.7.1** Explain how a distress message differs from an emergency (urgency) message, and give the standard words used for each.

## Why this matters

Most pilots never make a MAYDAY call, and that is exactly why the words have to be learned on the ground. When the engine goes quiet over the wheatbelt east of Northam, you will have perhaps two minutes between the silence and the paddock, and most of that belongs to flying the aeroplane. There is no time to work out what to say or in what order. The radio part of an emergency has to be a reflex: the right signal word, the right frequency, the items in a fixed order, then back to flying. Just as important is choosing between the two levels of emergency call, because each tells the people listening something different about how fast they need to act. The exam asks you to tell the two apart in a scenario, to put the items of a distress message in order, to pick the right frequency, and to recognise the other standard phrases. Every call format in this lesson is from AIP GEN 3.4 and ERSA EMERG as this lesson gives it; verify it against the current AIP and ERSA.

## Two levels of trouble

Start with what the people on the ground need to know when you call. A controller who hears an aircraft is in trouble has to decide, in seconds, how much to drop: clear the frequency, alert search and rescue, scramble a rescue helicopter, or just give you priority and a bit of help. The first word of your call answers that question for them.

There are two levels, and the test that separates them is one phrase: **grave and imminent danger, requiring immediate assistance**.

**Distress** is the top level. The aircraft is threatened by **grave and imminent danger** and requires **immediate assistance**. The signal is **MAYDAY MAYDAY MAYDAY**. It has **absolute priority over all other traffic**.

**Urgency** is the level below. There is a condition concerning the **safety** of the aircraft or of a person on board (or in sight), which does **not** require immediate assistance. The signal is **PAN PAN, PAN PAN, PAN PAN**. It has **priority over all except distress**.

The word "emergency" in the syllabus covers this second level; AIP GEN 3.4 calls it urgency. Both are emergencies in the everyday sense, but only distress means "help now".

### Applying the test

The useful question is not "how bad is this?" but "do I need help **now**, or do I need people to know and stand by?"

| | Distress | Urgency |
|---|---|---|
| Signal | **MAYDAY MAYDAY MAYDAY** | **PAN PAN, PAN PAN, PAN PAN** |
| Meaning | The aircraft is threatened by **grave and imminent danger** and requires **immediate assistance** | A condition concerning the **safety** of the aircraft or a person on board (or in sight), which does **not** require immediate assistance |
| Examples | Engine failure with forced landing, fire, structural failure, pilot incapacitated with no other pilot, lost and with fuel nearly exhausted | Rough-running engine still producing power, sick passenger, lost but with fuel to spare, alternator failure, bird strike with the aircraft controllable |
| Transponder | **7700** | 7700 if appropriate |
| Priority | Absolute priority over all other traffic | Priority over all except distress |

Look at the pairs in that table, because they show the test working. **Lost with fuel nearly exhausted** is distress; **lost with fuel to spare** is urgency. The problem is the same, being lost, but in the first case the danger is imminent: the engine will stop soon wherever you are. In the second you have time, and what you need is help finding your position, not a rescue. Likewise an **engine failure** forcing a landing is distress, while a **rough-running engine still producing power** is urgency: you are still flying, still in control of where you go, but you want people to know in case it gets worse.

![Do I need help now: MAYDAY or PAN PAN?](diagram:mayday-or-pan-decision "What to notice: the same problem can be either call; what decides it is whether immediate assistance is needed, and a PAN can be upgraded.")

Notice that the urgency definition includes "or in sight". A PAN PAN can be about someone else: an aircraft or vessel you can see that appears to be in trouble.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The test is not how frightening the situation feels but whether **immediate assistance** is needed. A sick passenger, an alternator failure, or being lost with fuel to spare are all serious, but you can still fly and choose where to go: PAN PAN. Engine failure, fire, structural failure, or fuel about to run out: MAYDAY.

??? check "Check yourself: you are lost over the Darling Range with two hours of fuel. Distress or urgency, and what would change your answer?"
    Urgency: the situation concerns the safety of the aircraft, but with two hours of fuel the danger is not imminent, so a PAN PAN. If the fuel became nearly exhausted while you were still lost, the danger would become grave and imminent and you would upgrade to a MAYDAY.

## Declare early, upgrade or cancel

A PAN can be **upgraded** to a MAYDAY if the situation worsens, and a MAYDAY should be **cancelled** once the danger is over. That flexibility is what lets you call early.

**Do not hesitate.** Declaring early gets help moving and **costs nothing if it is cancelled**. The reasoning is lopsided. If you call and the problem clears, you cancel, and the cost is a few minutes of a controller's attention. If you do not call and the problem gets worse, the search and rescue system starts late, perhaps only when your SARTIME expires, and the people who could have helped you are hours behind. Pilots who hesitate usually do so out of embarrassment, and embarrassment is not a safety factor.

## Which frequency, and what else to set

Your engine has just failed. Which frequency do you call on?

Use **the frequency you are already on**: the area frequency, the tower or the CTAF. The reason is simple: **someone there is already listening**. A controller working the area frequency hears you immediately; aircraft on a CTAF can see you and pass on your position. Changing frequency costs time and may land you on a frequency nobody is monitoring.

If there is **no reply**, use **121.5 MHz**, the international emergency frequency, which many aircraft and ground stations monitor.

Two more actions go with the call:

- **Squawk 7700** so radar shows an emergency. The controller's screen flags your aircraft and position even if your call is not heard.
- **Activate the ELT** if a forced landing is imminent or has happened. The emergency locator transmitter sends a signal that satellites and search aircraft can detect, which is how searchers find you after you are on the ground.

![Which transponder code goes with a MAYDAY, and where are all the emergency procedures kept?](diagram:transponder-emergency-codes "7700 means emergency; 7600 is radio failure and 7500 unlawful interference. All three, with the distress and urgency calls, are in ERSA EMERG.")

The full set of emergency procedures, codes and calls is in the **ERSA EMERG** section, the pink pages, as [RFRC 2.7](/notes/RFRC/2.7) explains. A radio failure on its own is not a distress situation; that is 7600 and the procedure in [PAKC 3.8](/notes/PAKC/3.8).

??? check "Check yourself: you are on the area frequency when the engine fails. Why make the MAYDAY there rather than switching straight to 121.5?"
    Because someone on the frequency you are already using is already listening, so you will be heard at once without spending time changing frequency. You use 121.5 MHz if there is no reply.

## What goes in a distress message

When a controller hears MAYDAY, they need enough information to send help to the right place: who you are, what is wrong, what you will do, and where you are. The message delivers those in a fixed order, so that even if it is cut off, the most important items have already gone out.

After **MAYDAY MAYDAY MAYDAY**, give as much as time allows, **in this order**:

1. **Name of the station addressed** (if known and time permits)
2. **Call-sign** (aircraft type helps search)
3. **Nature of the emergency**
4. **Intentions** of the pilot in command
5. **Position**, level and heading
6. **Any other useful information**: persons on board, endurance, aircraft colour

Source: AIP GEN 3.4 and ERSA EMERG; verify against the current text.

Each item earns its place. The **signal** said three times makes certain the call is recognised even through noise and gets everyone else off the frequency. The **station addressed** directs it, but you skip it if you do not know who to call or there is no time. The **call-sign** identifies you, and adding the **type** tells searchers what to look for from the air. The **nature of the emergency** tells responders what kind of help is needed. **Intentions** tell them what you are going to do, which for a forced landing is where you will be. **Position, level and heading** narrow the search area; level and heading let them work out roughly where you will come down. **Other information** comes last because it is useful but not essential: **persons on board** tells rescuers how many people to find, **endurance** how long you have, and the **aircraft colour** helps them spot you.

An **urgency message** has the **same structure**, preceded by **PAN PAN** three times.

!!! example "Worked example: building a MAYDAY for an engine failure near Northam"
    Situation: Cessna 172 Alpha Bravo Charlie, two on board, engine failure 8 miles north-east of Northam, passing 2,000 ft, heading 230, gliding to a paddock. You are on the Melbourne Centre frequency.

    1. **Fly first**: best glide, paddock chosen. Then press the PTT on the frequency you are already on.
    2. **Signal**: "MAYDAY MAYDAY MAYDAY".
    3. **Station addressed**: "Melbourne Centre".
    4. **Call-sign and type**: "Cessna 172 Alpha Bravo Charlie, Alpha Bravo Charlie, Alpha Bravo Charlie".
    5. **Nature of the emergency**: "engine failure".
    6. **Intentions**: "forced landing in a paddock".
    7. **Position, level, heading**: "8 miles north-east of Northam, passing 2,000, heading 230".
    8. **Other useful information**: "two POB".

    Together: "MAYDAY MAYDAY MAYDAY, Melbourne Centre, Cessna 172 Alpha Bravo Charlie, Alpha Bravo Charlie, Alpha Bravo Charlie, engine failure, forced landing in a paddock, 8 miles north-east of Northam, passing 2,000, heading 230, two POB." Then squawk 7700, return to flying the aeroplane, and activate the ELT as the landing becomes certain.

![What goes in a MAYDAY, in what order?](diagram:distress-message-anatomy "What to notice: station, call-sign, nature, intentions, position, the rest: the most essential items go first in case the call is cut short.")

The order is the thing to learn. Say the items in this sequence every time and you will not have to think about it when it matters.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Exam options often swap the order of the middle items. After the signal and the station, it is **call-sign, nature of the emergency, intentions, position**. "Position before nature" and "intentions last" are the usual distractors.

??? check "Check yourself: why does the call-sign come before the nature of the emergency, and the extra details such as persons on board come last?"
    Responders must first know who is calling, then what is wrong, what you will do and where you are; that is what they need to start helping. Persons on board, endurance and colour help the search but are least essential, so they come last in case the message is cut short.

## The urgency message

An urgency message uses exactly the same structure as the distress message, with **PAN PAN** said three times in place of MAYDAY. Because the danger is not imminent, you usually have time to give every item, including endurance, which matters more here: the controller wants to know how long you have to sort the problem out.

!!! example "Worked example: a PAN PAN for a rough-running engine"
    Situation: Cessna 172 Alpha Bravo Charlie, two on board, at 3,500 ft, 10 miles west of York. The engine is running rough but still producing power. You decide to divert to York. Two hours of fuel.

    1. **Signal**: "PAN PAN, PAN PAN, PAN PAN".
    2. **Station**: "Melbourne Centre".
    3. **Call-sign and type**: "Cessna 172 Alpha Bravo Charlie".
    4. **Nature**: "rough-running engine".
    5. **Intentions**: "diverting to York".
    6. **Position** and **level**: "10 miles west of York, 3,500 ft".
    7. **Other information**: "two POB, endurance two hours".

    Together: "PAN PAN, PAN PAN, PAN PAN, Melbourne Centre, Cessna 172 Alpha Bravo Charlie, rough-running engine, diverting to York, 10 miles west of York, 3,500 ft, two POB, endurance two hours." The engine is still working, you are in control and you have a plan, so this is urgency, not distress.

Now try the next step before opening the solution. Five minutes later, over York, the rough engine stops altogether. What do you do on the radio?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    The danger is now grave and imminent: the aircraft cannot stay airborne. **Upgrade to distress.** On the same frequency, after flying the aeroplane: "MAYDAY MAYDAY MAYDAY", then station, call-sign, nature (engine failure), intentions (forced landing, at York if you can reach it), position, level and heading, and persons on board, in the standard order. Squawk 7700 if you have not already, and activate the ELT if the landing will be off-aerodrome. The PAN already gave Centre most of the picture, which is exactly why calling early pays.

## Other standard phrases

Three more phrases complete the set. Each has a fixed form and a fixed meaning.

**"MAYDAY RELAY"** (said three times) is used when you are passing on a distress message from another station that **cannot be heard by ATC**. You are acting as a link. Give the **distressed aircraft's details, not your own**, then identify yourself. The order matters: the controller must not mistake you for the aircraft in trouble. This is the safety exception to the secrecy duty in [PAKC 3.5](/notes/PAKC/3.5): you are expected to pass on a distress message.

**"STOP TRANSMITTING, MAYDAY"** is used by the **controlling station** to impose silence on stations interfering with distress traffic. When you hear it, **do not transmit until it is cancelled**. The frequency belongs to the aircraft in distress.

**"DISTRESS TRAFFIC ENDED"** or "**cancel distress**" means the danger is over. For the pilot who declared the MAYDAY, the usual form is:

> "MAYDAY Alpha Bravo Charlie, cancel distress, engine has restarted, continuing to York."

That cancellation is as important as the MAYDAY. Until it is made, rescue resources may still be on their way.

??? check "Check yourself: you hear the controller say \"STOP TRANSMITTING, MAYDAY\". You were about to report your position. What do you do?"
    Do not transmit until the silence is cancelled. The controller has imposed silence because distress traffic is in progress, and any call, however routine, could block the aircraft in distress.

## After a forced landing

The radio work does not end when the aeroplane stops. If you can, **tell ATS you are safe**: on the radio, by phone, or through another aircraft. Keep the **ELT** available, because if nobody has heard from you it is how searchers will find you.

![What happens if nobody hears from you after the landing?](diagram:sartime-timeline "Silence after the SARTIME starts the alerting phases and then a physical search; a call or phone message that you are safe stops it.")

**A cancelled distress needs no apology; an undeclared one may delay the search.** That single sentence is the attitude the whole lesson is building.

## Bringing it together

A calm Sunday flight from Jandakot to Northam, two on board. Over the hills east of the scarp the engine starts running rough but keeps producing power. You are in control and have fuel, so on the area frequency you make a PAN PAN, in the standard order, with your intention to divert. The engine then stops. Now the danger is grave and imminent: you upgrade to MAYDAY on the same frequency, squawk 7700, and go back to flying the glide. Another pilot hears Centre struggling to read you and makes a MAYDAY RELAY with your details first and their own call-sign after. The controller imposes silence with "STOP TRANSMITTING, MAYDAY". Before you reach the paddock the engine catches and runs smoothly; you climb away and cancel distress. If it had not restarted, you would have landed, activated the ELT and told ATS you were safe as soon as you could. Every phrase used came from the same small set, in the same fixed order.

## Key points

- **3.7.1** **Distress (MAYDAY ×3)**: grave and imminent danger, immediate assistance needed, absolute priority. **Urgency (PAN PAN ×3)**: a safety concern not needing immediate assistance, priority over all but distress. Use the frequency you are on, then 121.5 MHz; squawk 7700; ELT if a forced landing is imminent or has happened. Message order: station addressed, call-sign, nature, intentions, position (level, heading), other information (POB, endurance, colour); urgency uses the same order. Upgrade a PAN if it worsens, cancel a MAYDAY when it is over. MAYDAY RELAY passes on another aircraft's distress; STOP TRANSMITTING, MAYDAY imposes silence; cancel distress or DISTRESS TRAFFIC ENDED ends it.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    The test is "grave and imminent danger requiring immediate assistance". If immediate help is needed, MAYDAY; if the situation is serious but under control, PAN PAN. Use the frequency you are already on first, then 121.5. Learn the message order as a chant: station, call-sign, nature, intentions, position, the rest. Verify the phraseology against the current AIP GEN 3.4 and ERSA EMERG before the exam.
