## What the exam expects (PAKC 3.5)

- **3.5.1** State what an aeronautical radio operator must do about (a) the secrecy of communications they hear and (b) transmissions they are not authorised to make.

## Why this matters

The first time you press the transmit button on a training flight out of Jandakot, you become a radio operator, with duties set by Commonwealth law as well as by CASA. Nobody checks those duties at the holding point; they live in how you behave on the frequency every day. You will overhear a great deal: other pilots' mistakes, company messages, a controller sorting out someone's lost clearance. You will also be tempted to fill the air, with a cheery "thanks, see you" to the tower or a chat with a mate on the CTAF at Murrayfield. This lesson explains what you may and may not do with what you hear, and what you may and may not put on the air, and why each rule exists. The exam tests it with short scenario questions where one answer is the courteous-sounding wrong one, so the reasons matter more than the lists.

## Who counts as a radio operator

Anyone who transmits on an aeronautical frequency is acting as an **aeronautical radio operator**, and the law treats that as a privilege you must hold. A **Part 61 flight crew licence** holder may operate the aircraft radio as part of the licence privileges, subject to the **English language proficiency** requirement. A person who is not a licensed pilot needs an **aeronautical radio operator certificate**. Check CASR Part 61 for the current wording.

The English language requirement exists for a plain reason: the aeronautical band is the one place where pilots and controllers of every background must understand one another, quickly, under pressure, with no chance to look up a word. The privilege is tied to being able to do that.

Either way, using the aeronautical band brings legal duties under two sources. The **Radiocommunications Act 1992** is Commonwealth law that governs all use of the radio spectrum in Australia, aviation included; it is where secrecy and unauthorised transmissions are legal obligations rather than good manners. **AIP GEN 3.4** sets out the radiotelephony procedures: how calls are made, the standard words, and the discipline expected on the frequency.

??? check "Check yourself: your non-pilot friend in the right seat wants to make the inbound call at Northam. May they?"
    Not unless they hold an aeronautical radio operator certificate (or a flight crew licence that includes the radio privilege). Transmitting on the aeronautical band is a privilege that has to be held; it does not come with sitting in an aircraft. Check CASR Part 61 for the current wording.

## Aeronautical radio is a safety service

Before the two rules themselves, one idea explains both. Aeronautical radio is a **safety service**, not a public broadcast. It exists to keep aircraft apart and to get help to those in trouble. Everything on it is either safety information or the routine traffic that supports safe operations, and every second of air time belongs to that purpose.

Two consequences follow. First, a message is meant for the station it is addressed to, and the people who happen to hear it have no right to use it for anything else. That is **secrecy of communications**. Second, a frequency is a shared resource that only one station can use at a time. Aviation VHF voice is simplex, as you will see in [PAKC 3.9](/notes/PAKC/3.9): while one station transmits, nobody else can be heard. So anything that does not need to be said costs everyone else air time, and anything false or misleading poisons the picture that every pilot builds from what they hear. That is the basis of the rules on **unauthorised transmissions**.

## Secrecy of communications (3.5.1 a)

Imagine you are cruising at 3,500 ft south of Serpentine, listening on the area frequency. You hear an exchange between another aircraft and the controller about that pilot's airspace infringement, with their call-sign and a request to phone after landing. You know the aircraft; it belongs to a rival school. The thought crosses your mind that this would make a good story at the club.

It would also break the law. A radio receiver lets you hear everything on the frequency, but hearing a message does not make it yours.

### What the duty is

The operator's responsibility for secrecy, as AIP GEN 3.4 and the Radiocommunications Act frame it, comes down to three things:

- **Do not divulge or publish** the content of a message you overhear that was not intended for you. Divulge means telling anyone; publish means putting it out to the public.
- **Do not use it for personal gain.** Information overheard on the frequency is not yours to profit from.
- **Do not record and broadcast or post** aviation communications in a way that breaches the legislation. Check the Radiocommunications Act for the current detail.

The word "secrecy" can mislead you into thinking you must not listen. That is not the rule. You are expected to listen: a listening watch is how you build your picture of traffic. The duty is about what you **do with** what you hear. You may hear; you may not pass on.

### Why the rule exists

Pilots and controllers need to speak plainly. A pilot reporting a problem, admitting a mistake or asking for help must not hesitate because the call might end up on social media or in a newspaper. If every transmission were public property, people would start guarding their words, and a frequency where people guard their words is a frequency where problems are reported late. The secrecy duty protects the candour that safety depends on.

Some traffic also concerns people's private affairs, and the people involved did not choose to have their business broadcast.

### What secrecy does not stop

Secrecy does not stop you from acting on **information relevant to safety**. Much of what you hear is addressed to you, even though your call-sign is not in it.

- **Broadcasts to all stations.** A CTAF call that begins "Northam traffic" is addressed to every aircraft in the area. Its whole purpose is that you hear it and act on it. Using it to plan your join is exactly what it is for.
- **Traffic you need to avoid.** Hearing a controller give another aircraft a clearance that puts it near you is information you may and should use for your own safety.
- **Distress traffic.** If you hear a distress message, you **should relay** it if the station it was addressed to does not appear to have heard it. Secrecy never stops you helping an aircraft in danger. The phraseology for relaying is in [PAKC 3.7](/notes/PAKC/3.7).

The line, then, runs between **using** information for the safety purpose it was sent for, which is allowed and often required, and **spreading** it beyond the frequency for any other purpose, which is not.

![What may you do with a message you overhear?](diagram:secrecy-use-vs-spread "What to notice: listening is expected and using it for safety is allowed; divulging, publishing or profiting from it is not.")

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Secrecy does not mean "do not listen" and it does not mean "ignore what is not addressed to you". You must keep a listening watch, act on broadcasts to all stations and relay distress traffic. What you must not do is divulge, publish or profit from a message that was not intended for you.

??? check "Check yourself: on the Jandakot tower frequency you hear the controller tell another pilot they landed without a clearance. After landing, a friend asks what all that was about. What may you say?"
    Nothing about the content of that exchange. It was not addressed to you and had no safety purpose for you once you were on the ground, so passing it on, or posting it, would breach the secrecy duty. You may of course have used any traffic information in it while you were flying.

## Unauthorised transmissions (3.5.1 b)

Now turn from listening to talking. A transmission is **unauthorised** when it is one the operator has no right to make: because of its content, because of the frequency it is made on, because it carries no identification, or because the equipment is not approved. There are six kinds. Take them one at a time, each with its reason.

### Unnecessary or superfluous messages

Do not transmit **unnecessary or superfluous** messages: chatting, jokes, "thanks a lot", repeated radio checks.

This is the one most pilots break without thinking, because it feels polite. A tower controller at Jandakot on a busy Saturday may be working a dozen aircraft on one frequency. Each "thanks very much, have a good day" takes two or three seconds, and during those seconds nobody else can be heard. If one of those blocked calls was "entering and rolling" or "going around", the courtesy has cost safety. The correct way to say thanks is a clean readback or a short acknowledgement with your call-sign, then silence.

Repeated **radio checks** are the same. One radio check before departure, made properly, tells you what you need to know ([PAKC 3.2](/notes/PAKC/3.2)). Asking again on every frequency change adds nothing.

### Profane, obscene or offensive language

Do not transmit **profane, obscene or offensive** language. The aeronautical band reaches every receiver in range, including ground stations, other aircraft, and anyone with a scanner. It is a public utility used by professionals, and the law treats it that way.

### False or misleading messages, and a false distress or urgency signal

Do not transmit **false or misleading** messages, and above all never a **false distress or urgency signal**.

A false position report or a misleading intention puts a wrong picture into every listener's head, and other pilots plan their separation around it. A false MAYDAY or PAN PAN is worse. It launches search and rescue resources: aircraft, crews, rescue coordination staff, sometimes police and volunteers. While they are chasing a hoax they are not available for a real emergency, and they themselves take risks to respond. That is why a false distress signal is an **offence**, not just bad practice.

A genuine distress call that turns out to be unnecessary is a different thing entirely. If you declare a MAYDAY for an engine failure and the engine then recovers, you cancel it, and nobody blames you. The rule is against falsehood, not against caution.

### Messages without identification

Every transmission must include your **call-sign**. A transmission without one is unauthorised, because the listener cannot tell who it is from, cannot reply, and cannot judge whether it applies to them. An anonymous "turning base" on a busy CTAF could be any of three aeroplanes, and every pilot in the circuit has to guess.

### The wrong frequency for the purpose

Do not transmit on a **frequency not appropriate to the purpose**. The clearest examples are these: using **121.5 MHz** for routine chat, or using a **CTAF** for company messages.

Each frequency is reserved for a job, and other people are listening on it for that job. 121.5 MHz is the international emergency frequency. Aircraft in trouble call on it, and many aircraft and ground stations monitor it so that a distress call is heard. Chatting on it buries the call it exists for. A CTAF is the frequency every aircraft near that aerodrome uses to keep track of the others, so a long company message about fuel orders or passenger arrangements blocks the position reports that keep people apart.

### Unapproved equipment

Do not transmit from a radio that is **not approved or licensed for aeronautical use**. An approved aircraft radio is built to transmit on the exact frequency selected, with the right power and a clean signal. An unapproved handheld or a modified set can drift off frequency, splatter onto neighbouring channels, or interfere with navigation aids.

!!! example "Worked example: which of these calls may you make?"
    Situation: you are in the Northam circuit on the CTAF with two other aircraft. Five possible transmissions, sorted against the rules above.

    1. "Northam traffic, Cessna Alpha Bravo Charlie, joining crosswind runway 23, Northam." **Authorised**: a broadcast to all stations, identified, on the right frequency, for a safety purpose.
    2. On short final, "Thanks for the heads-up, mate, nice day for it." **Unauthorised**: unnecessary and superfluous, and it blocks the frequency at the busiest point of the circuit.
    3. "Northam traffic, turning base runway 23, Northam." **Unauthorised as made**: no call-sign, so nobody knows which of the three aircraft is turning base. Adding the type and call-sign makes it a correct call.
    4. A message to your school asking them to have the fuel truck ready, sent on the CTAF. **Unauthorised**: a company message on a frequency reserved for traffic separation.
    5. The same fuel request sent on 121.5 MHz "because nobody else is using it". **Unauthorised**: the emergency frequency is reserved for emergencies, and somebody may need it at any moment.

    The test for each is the same: is it necessary, identified, truthful, on the right frequency and from approved equipment?

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The exam likes the polite option. "Thank you, tower, have a good afternoon" sounds like good airmanship but is an unnecessary transmission. Courtesy on the radio is brevity: a correct readback with your call-sign is all the thanks a controller needs.

??? check "Check yourself: why is a false MAYDAY treated as an offence rather than merely poor practice?"
    Because it launches real search and rescue resources, which then are not available for a genuine emergency and whose crews take risks to respond. A false distress signal costs safety elsewhere, so the law treats it as an offence.

## Good practice that follows from the same duty

The rules say what you must not do. Good radio practice follows from the same two ideas, a shared frequency and a safety purpose, and makes it easy to stay inside them.

**Listen first.** Before you press the PTT, listen long enough to know the frequency is clear and that you are not about to answer a question that was asked of someone else. Never cut across a transmission in progress: on a simplex channel two stations transmitting together produce a squeal, and both messages are lost.

**Never transmit over distress traffic.** Once a MAYDAY is on the air, that frequency belongs to the aircraft in distress and the station helping it. Anything else you have to say can wait or go elsewhere. The controlling station can impose silence formally ([PAKC 3.7](/notes/PAKC/3.7)), but you should not need to be told.

**Plan the message before you press the PTT.** Know what you will say, in standard phraseology, in the standard order. Standard phraseology from AIP GEN 3.4 exists so that every listener can decode your call instantly, and the CTAF broadcast format in [PAKC 3.4](/notes/PAKC/3.4) is a good example of how much a short, ordered call can carry.

**Keep messages short, and use the minimum power and time necessary.** The band is shared. Short transmissions leave gaps for others, including the pilot who needs to say something urgent.

!!! example "Worked example: planning a call before pressing the PTT"
    Situation: you are approaching 10 miles south of Northam at 2,500 ft, returning from the training area, and the inbound call is due. The CTAF is busy.

    1. **Listen** for a few seconds: two aircraft are in the circuit on runway 23. The frequency is now quiet.
    2. **Plan** the call in the standard order: location traffic, type, call-sign, position, level, intentions, location.
    3. **Check** it against the rules: necessary (an inbound call by 10 nm is recommended), identified, truthful, on the CTAF where the traffic is.
    4. **Transmit** once, cleanly: "Northam traffic, Cessna 172 Alpha Bravo Charlie, 10 miles south, 2,500, inbound, estimate circuit at 25, Northam."
    5. **Release** the PTT and listen again.

    The call is short, standard and complete, so nobody needs to ask you to repeat it, and the frequency is free again within seconds.

??? check "Check yourself: you are about to report downwind at Jandakot when you hear a MAYDAY from another aircraft on the tower frequency. What do you do with your downwind call?"
    Hold it. A frequency carrying distress traffic belongs to the aircraft in distress and the station helping it, so you do not transmit over it. Keep flying your circuit safely, keep listening, and make your call only when the frequency is free and it will not interfere.

## Bringing it together

A Saturday solo from Jandakot to the training area and back. At the holding point you listen to the tower before calling, and your call is planned and identified. Over the scarp you hear a company aircraft discussing a passenger's missed connection with its base: none of your business, and it stays that way. You notice another student in the area chatting with a friend on the area frequency about lunch plans; that is the unnecessary transmission the rules forbid, and you do not join in. On the way back a pilot on the frequency reports smoke in the cockpit with a MAYDAY. You stop transmitting, keep listening, and hear Centre answer; had Centre not answered, you would have relayed it. After landing you resist the temptation to post a recording of the drama. Every choice in that flight came from two ideas: messages belong to the station they were meant for, and the air time belongs to safety.

## Key points

- **3.5.1** A licence holder (subject to English language proficiency) or a certificated operator may transmit, under the Radiocommunications Act 1992 and AIP GEN 3.4. **Secrecy:** you may hear, but you must not divulge, publish or profit from a message not intended for you; you may act on safety information, broadcasts to all stations, and you should relay distress. **Unauthorised transmissions:** nothing unnecessary, profane, false or misleading (above all no false distress or urgency signal, which is an offence), nothing without your call-sign, nothing on the wrong frequency (121.5 MHz is not for chat, a CTAF is not for company messages), nothing from an unapproved radio. Listen first, never transmit over distress traffic, plan the message, keep it short.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    "Secrecy" means you may hear, but you may not pass on, messages not addressed to you. The exception is safety: distress and urgency traffic, and broadcasts addressed to all stations. For unauthorised transmissions, look for the option that is unnecessary, unidentified, false or on the wrong frequency, and be wary of the polite-sounding distractor: "thanks a lot" is superfluous. A false distress signal is an offence. Check the current Radiocommunications Act and AIP GEN 3.4 wording before the exam.
