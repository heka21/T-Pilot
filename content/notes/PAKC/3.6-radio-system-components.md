## What the exam expects (PAKC 3.6)

- **3.6.1** Say what each part of an aircraft radio installation does: (a) the power source and battery (master) switch; (b) the radio (avionics) master; (c) fuses and circuit breakers; (d) the microphone; (e) the transmitter; (f) the receiver; (g) the antenna; (h) the headphones and speaker.

## Why this matters

When you call Jandakot Ground for taxi, your words make a journey through eight different parts before they reach the controller's ear, and the reply makes the same journey back. Each part does one job, and each can fail in its own way. If you know what each one does, a silent radio stops being a mystery: you can work out which part has stopped doing its job, and you know which you can fix from the seat and which you cannot. The fault-finding routine in [PAKC 3.3](/notes/PAKC/3.3) and the controls in [PAKC 3.9](/notes/PAKC/3.9) both rest on this lesson. The exam asks for the function of a named component, most often the avionics master, the receiver and the type of modulation, so you need a clear one-line answer for each, backed by the reason.

## The system as a chain

On transmit, electrical power feeds the radio; the **microphone** turns your voice into an electrical audio signal; the **transmitter** turns that audio into a radio-frequency signal; and the **antenna** radiates it into the air. On receive, the chain runs the other way: the antenna collects signals, the **receiver** picks out the one you want and turns it back into audio, and the **headset** or **speaker** makes it audible.

Upstream of all of that sits the power supply with its switches and protection: the battery and alternator, the master switch, the avionics master, and the fuses and circuit breakers.

![How does your voice reach the tower?](diagram:radio-system-block-diagram "What to notice: power comes through the master, the avionics master and a breaker; transmit and receive share one box and one antenna but are separate paths.")

Any break in the chain stops communication, and a break anywhere looks the same from the seat: silence. That is why fault finding in [PAKC 3.3](/notes/PAKC/3.3) follows the same path. Notice also that the transmitter and receiver usually live in the same box, the **COM radio** (a **transceiver**), and share one antenna, but they are separate circuits that can fail separately. That is why [PAKC 3.8](/notes/PAKC/3.8) has you transmit blind.

## Power: the battery, the alternator and the master switch (3.6.1 a)

There are two sources of electrical power. The **battery** stores electrical energy chemically. It supplies the large current needed for **starting** the engine, and it is the **reserve** if the alternator fails.

Once the engine runs, the **alternator** (or, in older aircraft, a **generator**) takes over. Driven by the engine, it generates electricity, supplies the whole electrical load, radios included, and **recharges the battery**.

The **master switch** connects the battery to the **main bus**, the bar or wire from which all the aircraft's electrical circuits draw their power. Master off, the bus is dead and so is every radio.

The function that matters most for radio is what happens when the alternator fails. The battery then carries everything alone. With the alternator failed, a light-aircraft battery may run the radios for perhaps **30 to 60 minutes**, less with heavy loads such as lights and pitot heat, so **shed non-essential load early** and check your POH for your aircraft. The ammeter or voltmeter, and any low-voltage warning, tell you when this has happened. [PAKC 3.3](/notes/PAKC/3.3) works through the decision that follows.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    With the alternator failed, the radios do not stop at once, and they do not run indefinitely. They run on the battery for a limited time, roughly 30 to 60 minutes in a typical light aircraft and less with heavy loads. The time is your POH's figure, not a fixed rule.

??? check "Check yourself: in cruise the ammeter shows a discharge and the low-voltage light is on. What is now powering the radio, and what follows from that?"
    The battery alone, because the alternator has stopped supplying the load. The battery is running down, so you shed non-essential equipment at once to stretch the time the radio will keep working, and plan to land within it.

## The radio (avionics) master (3.6.1 b)

Between the main bus and the radios sits a second switch: the **radio master** or **avionics master**. It is a separate switch (or relay) between the main bus and the **avionics bus**, the circuit that feeds the radios, navigation equipment, audio panel and transponder.

It has two jobs.

**Protection during engine start and shutdown.** When the starter motor engages, it draws a very large current, and when it releases, and when the alternator comes on or off line, the system voltage can **spike** or dip sharply. Modern radios are built from **solid-state** circuits: transistors and microprocessors that can be damaged by those transients. The avionics master lets all radios be switched off together for **engine start and shutdown**, so the spike never reaches them. The rule is simple: **avionics master on after start, off before shutdown.**

**One switch in an emergency.** In an electrical emergency, such as smoke from behind the panel or an electrical fire, the avionics master lets the pilot **remove power from all avionics at once** without hunting for individual knobs.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The avionics master's main purpose is to **protect the avionics from voltage transients during engine start and shutdown**. It does not "save the battery" or "isolate the alternator"; those are distractors. Its second purpose is a single switch to kill all avionics in an electrical emergency.

??? check "Check yourself: why is the avionics master switched off before you turn the starter?"
    Because the starter's heavy current causes voltage spikes and dips on the bus, and the solid-state circuits in the radios can be damaged by them. With the avionics bus isolated, the transient never reaches the radios; you switch it on once the engine is running.

## Fuses and circuit breakers (3.6.1 c)

Each radio draws a modest current through its own wiring. If a wire chafes through its insulation and touches the airframe, or a component fails, a **short circuit** or **overload** lets far more current flow than the wire was designed for. The wire heats and can start a fire behind the panel.

**Fuses** and **circuit breakers** protect each circuit from overload and short circuit by **breaking the circuit when the current is excessive**. Each radio and each major circuit has its own, rated for that circuit's normal current.

A **fuse** is a thin link of metal that **melts** when too much current passes. It protects once and must then be **replaced**. If your aircraft uses fuses, carry spares of the **correct rating**; a higher-rated fuse would let a dangerous current through before it blew.

A **circuit breaker** is a switch that opens itself when the current is excessive, and it **pops out** to show it has tripped. It can be **reset** by pushing it back in.

Reset a breaker **once only**. A single trip may be a momentary surge. A breaker that trips again indicates a real fault, and holding it in or resetting it repeatedly forces current through a faulty circuit, the very thing it exists to prevent.

??? check "Check yourself: the COM 1 circuit breaker pops, you reset it, and two minutes later it pops again. What does it tell you, and what do you do?"
    It indicates a real fault in that circuit, such as a short or overload, not a passing surge. Leave it out, treat COM 1 as failed, and use COM 2 if fitted; resetting again risks overheating the wiring.

## The microphone and the press-to-talk switch (3.6.1 d)

Now the signal path itself, starting at your mouth. Sound is a pressure wave in the air. The **microphone** **converts your voice (sound pressure) into an electrical audio signal**, a small voltage that varies in step with the sound.

A cockpit is loud, and the engine and propeller noise reaches the microphone along with your voice. Aviation headset microphones are therefore **noise-cancelling**: they respond to sound arriving close to the mouth and reject the engine noise from all around. That only works when the boom is positioned **close to the lips**. Move it away and your voice weakens while the cabin noise does not.

The microphone does not transmit by itself. The **press-to-talk (PTT)** switch, on the control yoke or stick, **keys the transmitter**: while it is held, the transmitter is on and the microphone's audio goes out on the air. Release it and the radio returns to receiving. A PTT that sticks keeps the transmitter on, which blocks the frequency for everyone; [PAKC 3.9](/notes/PAKC/3.9) covers how to spot it.

## The transmitter (3.6.1 e)

The audio signal from your microphone is a low-frequency electrical wave, the same few hundred to a few thousand cycles per second as the sound of your voice. On its own it cannot travel through the air any distance. It needs a carrier.

The **transmitter** **generates a radio-frequency carrier on the selected frequency** and **modulates** it with the audio from the microphone. The **carrier** is a steady radio wave at, say, 118.1 MHz. To **modulate** it means to vary some property of that carrier in step with the audio, so that the voice rides on the radio wave.

Aeronautical VHF voice uses **amplitude modulation (AM)**: the **strength** of the carrier varies with the voice. Loud parts of your speech make the carrier stronger, quiet parts weaker; its frequency stays fixed.

AM has a property that matters for safety. When two stations transmit together on the same frequency, an AM receiver lets a listener **hear** the clash, as a **squeal or garble**. Listeners know both calls may need repeating. FM receivers tend to lock on to the stronger signal and suppress the weaker one, so a listener might hear one clean call and never know a second pilot had also transmitted. With AM, the blocked call announces itself. That is a useful safety feature.

![How does your voice ride on the carrier?](diagram:am-modulation "What to notice: in AM the carrier's strength follows your voice while its frequency stays fixed, and two stations at once are heard as a squeal.")

??? check "Check yourself: what does the transmitter do to your voice, and why is AM useful when two pilots press the PTT at the same moment?"
    It generates a radio-frequency carrier on the selected frequency and varies the carrier's strength with your voice (amplitude modulation). With AM, two simultaneous transmissions are heard as a squeal or garble, so listeners know a call was blocked and can ask for a repeat.

## The antenna (3.6.1 g)

The **antenna** is where electrical signals become radio waves and back again. On transmit it **converts the transmitter's radio-frequency current into radiated electromagnetic waves**. On receive it **collects passing waves** and delivers the tiny currents they induce to the receiver. A COM radio uses one antenna for both.

An antenna works best when its length is matched to the wavelength of the signal, and [PAKC 3.10](/notes/PAKC/3.10) explains why. VHF COM antennas are typically a **quarter-wavelength whip or blade**, about **60 cm** long at 125 MHz, mounted on top of or under the fuselage. On the walk-around you can match each one to its job:

- **COM antennas**: whips or blades on the top or bottom of the fuselage, one per COM radio.
- **NAV antennas**: often a V-shaped (**cat's whisker**) antenna on the fin, feeding the VOR and ILS receivers.
- **Transponder and DME**: short **UHF stubs** underneath the fuselage, short because their frequencies are high and their wavelengths short.

![Which antenna on the aeroplane does which job?](diagram:aircraft-antenna-locations "What to notice: COM antennas are short blades or whips top and bottom, the NAV cat's whisker sits on the fin, and the shortest stubs belong to the UHF transponder and DME.")

The antenna is the one part of the radio system outside the aeroplane. **Damaged, loose or ice-covered antennas reduce range**, which is why the antennas are on the pre-flight walk-around: look for cracks, bends, a loose base or a missing whip.

## The receiver (3.6.1 f)

At any moment the antenna is collecting a jumble of signals: every station on every frequency within range, plus electrical noise. The **receiver** turns that jumble into one clear voice. Its job has three parts:

1. **Select** the wanted frequency from all the signals the antenna collects. This is what tuning does.
2. **Amplify** it, because the signal arriving from a distant station is extremely weak.
3. **Demodulate** it to **recover the audio**: strip the voice back off the carrier, reversing what the distant transmitter did.

The two words pair up and the exam pairs them too: **the transmitter modulates, the receiver demodulates.**

The receiver has one more circuit. With no station transmitting, an amplified receiver produces a steady **hiss** of background noise. The **squelch** circuit **mutes the output when no signal is present**, so you hear silence between calls, and opens when a signal strong enough arrives. Opening the squelch on purpose to hear the hiss is a quick test that the receiver and the audio path are alive, as [PAKC 3.3](/notes/PAKC/3.3) explains.

??? check "Check yourself: what are the three things the receiver does to the signal arriving from the antenna?"
    It selects the wanted frequency from everything the antenna collects, amplifies the weak signal, and demodulates it to recover the audio. The squelch then mutes the output when no signal is present.

## Headphones and speaker (3.6.1 h)

The last link turns the receiver's audio back into sound. **Headphones** and the **speaker** both **convert the audio signal back into sound**, the reverse of what the microphone did.

**Headphones** (in practice a **headset**, with the microphone on a boom) are far better in a noisy cockpit. They put the sound directly at your ears and seal out much of the engine noise, so you hear the call clearly, and they also give **hearing protection** against hours of engine noise. The **cabin speaker** is a **back-up** if the headset fails: harder to hear over the engine, but it works with nothing plugged in.

![How does a headset cut the engine noise you would otherwise hear?](diagram:anr-noise-cancelling "The ear seal blocks noise passively; ANR electronics also cancel the low engine drone with an opposite sound wave, but only while they have power.")

**Active noise reduction (ANR)** headsets go further. A small microphone inside each ear cup hears the low-frequency engine and propeller drone, and the electronics play an opposite sound wave that cancels much of it. ANR headsets **need their own battery or aircraft power**, so **check them before flight**. If the ANR power fails, many still work as ordinary passive headsets, but the drone returns.

??? check "Check yourself: your headset fails in flight. What can you still use to hear and to transmit?"
    The cabin speaker lets you hear the radio, and the hand microphone (if fitted) lets you transmit. Both are back-ups precisely for this failure; selecting them may need a change on the audio panel.

## Following one call through the system

Put the chain together with a call you will make on almost every flight.

!!! example "Worked example: a taxi call from your mouth to the tower and back"
    Situation: engine running at Jandakot, you call Ground for taxi and Ground replies.

    1. **Power:** master on, alternator charging, avionics master switched on **after** start so the start transient never reached the radios. The breakers for COM 1 and the audio panel are in.
    2. **Microphone:** you hold the boom close to your lips and speak; it turns your voice into an electrical audio signal and rejects the engine noise.
    3. **PTT:** pressing it keys COM 1's transmitter.
    4. **Transmitter:** generates a carrier on the Ground frequency and amplitude-modulates it with your voice.
    5. **Antenna:** the blade on the fuselage radiates the signal; Ground's antenna collects it.
    6. You release the PTT. Ground replies; your **antenna** collects the reply.
    7. **Receiver:** selects the Ground frequency, amplifies the weak signal, demodulates it to recover the controller's voice; the squelch opens because a signal is present.
    8. **Headset:** turns that audio back into sound at your ears.

    Eight links, each doing one job. If any one fails, the conversation stops, and the job that was not done points you at the cause.

Now try one before opening the solution. You are listening to the Perth ATIS on COM 2 before departure and hear nothing at all. List, in signal order, every component the ATIS broadcast passes through on its way to your ears, and for each say in a few words what it does.

??? solution "Show the solution"
    1. **Power** (battery or alternator, master, avionics master, COM 2 breaker): runs the radio.
    2. **Antenna** (COM 2's): collects the ATIS signal from the air.
    3. **Receiver** (COM 2): selects the ATIS frequency, amplifies it, demodulates it to recover the audio; the squelch opens because a signal is present.
    4. **Headset or speaker**: turns the audio back into sound.

    The microphone, PTT and transmitter play no part, because you are only receiving. A silent ATIS therefore points at power, the COM 2 antenna, the COM 2 receiver or its settings, or the headset and audio routing.

## The typical light-aircraft fit

A training aeroplane usually carries more than one radio, all built from the same blocks.

| Item | Function |
|---|---|
| COM 1 / COM 2 | VHF voice transmit and receive, 118.000 to 136.975 MHz |
| NAV 1 / NAV 2 | VOR and ILS receivers, 108.00 to 117.95 MHz (receive only) |
| ADF | Receives NDBs, roughly 190 to 1,750 kHz |
| Transponder | Replies to secondary radar with code and altitude |
| ELT | Emergency beacon on 406 MHz (and 121.5 MHz homing) |
| Audio panel and intercom | Selects what you hear and which radio transmits; crew intercom |

The **NAV** and **ADF** sets are receivers only: they have antennas and receivers but no transmitter, because a navigation aid on the ground does the transmitting. The **transponder** is both: it receives the radar's interrogation and transmits a reply. The **audio panel** is the switchboard between the headsets and all those radios ([PAKC 3.9](/notes/PAKC/3.9)).

## Key points

- **3.6.1** (a) Battery starts the engine and is the reserve, alternator carries the load; master connects the battery to the bus; alternator failed means perhaps 30 to 60 minutes (POH). (b) Avionics master: protects the radios from start and shutdown spikes, kills all avionics at once. (c) Fuses melt, breakers pop; reset once only. (d) Microphone: voice to audio, close to the lips; PTT keys the transmitter. (e) Transmitter: carrier, modulated; VHF voice is AM. (f) Receiver: select, amplify, demodulate; squelch mutes. (g) Antenna: radiates and collects; quarter-wave, about 60 cm. (h) Headset and speaker: audio to sound; speaker is the back-up; ANR needs power.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Remember the reason for the avionics master: it protects the avionics from voltage transients during engine start and shutdown. The receiver demodulates; the transmitter modulates; aeronautical VHF voice is AM. With the alternator failed, the radios run on the battery for a limited time only, so shed load early.
