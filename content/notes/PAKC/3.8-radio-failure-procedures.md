## What the exam expects (PAKC 3.8)

- **3.8.1** Find the radio (communications) failure procedures in ERSA and apply them to a flight: what to set, what to say, where to go and how to arrive.

## Why this matters

A radio that goes quiet is one of the commonest "abnormals" a student pilot meets, and it rarely happens at a convenient moment. It happens on the way back from the training area south of Jandakot with the Class D zone ahead, or halfway between Northam and York with a CTAF full of other aeroplanes you can no longer hear. Nothing about the aeroplane's ability to fly has changed, so this is not an emergency, but your two safety nets around other traffic (hearing them and being heard by them) have gone at once. The procedure in ERSA exists so that controllers and other pilots can predict what you will do without talking to you. The exam gives you an ERSA extract or a scenario and asks what you do next, so you need to know where the text lives and the logic behind each step.

## Finding the procedure in ERSA

The **En Route Supplement Australia (ERSA)** is mostly aerodrome entries, but its front section holds the procedures you might need in a hurry. The **EMERG** section (Emergency Procedures, printed on pink pages so you can find it by feel) holds the distress and urgency calls, the transponder emergency codes, the communications failure procedures, interception, light signals, and search and rescue. The communications failure procedures are set out in separate paragraphs: for VFR and for IFR flights, and for flights in controlled airspace and outside it. You read the paragraph that matches *your* flight rules and *your* airspace, and ignore the rest.

![Where in ERSA do the radio failure procedures live, and what sits beside them?](diagram:transponder-emergency-codes "The EMERG pink pages hold the radio failure procedure, 7600 and the light signals together, so one section answers every question in this lesson.")

Practise finding EMERG in your own copy on the ground, and accept that the text changes with ERSA amendments. What follows is the shape of the procedure as the cited sources gave it when this note was written; the exam and the cockpit both use the current ERSA text, so **verify each step against the current ERSA EMERG and AIP ENR 1.1**.

??? check "Check yourself: you are VFR in Class G and the EMERG section has paragraphs for IFR in controlled airspace and for VFR. Which do you use?"
    The VFR paragraph that applies outside controlled airspace. The procedure differs with flight rules and airspace because what ATC expects of you differs, so the first step in "extracting" the procedure is choosing the paragraph that matches your flight.

## Is it really a failure?

Before you treat the radio as dead, find out whether it is. A radio installation is a chain: microphone and headset, the audio panel that routes the signal, the radio itself with its frequency and volume settings, the power that feeds it, and the antenna outside. A break anywhere in the chain looks the same from the seat: silence. Most breaks are in the parts you can reach.

Run the fault-finding check from [PAKC 3.3](/notes/PAKC/3.3) in order: **volume** up, **squelch** opened (you should hear background hiss), the **frequency in the active window** (not standby, and the right one), **audio panel** selections (the right COM selected for MIC and for receive), headset and microphone **plugs** fully home, **circuit breakers** in, then try **COM 2** and the **hand microphone**. Many apparent failures end here.

If the checks find nothing, think about which half has failed, because it decides how much good each later step does:

- **Receiver failed, transmitter working.** You hear nothing, but everyone hears you. Your broadcasts still let other pilots and ATC know where you are.
- **Transmitter failed, receiver working.** You hear everything, but nobody hears you. You can still listen to the ATIS, the CTAF traffic and anything ATC says to you.
- **Both failed**, as with an electrical failure. You are a silent aircraft and must rely on the transponder code (if it still has power), your lookout and the published procedures.

From the cockpit you usually cannot tell which of the first two you have. The procedure therefore assumes the worst of each half: you **keep listening** in case the receiver works, and you **keep transmitting** in case the transmitter does.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    A radio failure on its own is not a distress situation. The code is **7600**, not 7700, and the procedure is calm and planned. If something else is wrong as well (an engine problem, a sick passenger), the emergency procedures in [PAKC 3.7](/notes/PAKC/3.7) apply on top.

## The VFR radio failure procedure, step by step

Each step answers one question: how do the people around you find out what is going on, and how do you stay safe without them?

1. **Squawk 7600** (if a transponder is fitted). Radar controllers see the radio failure code.
2. **Listen** on the appropriate frequencies, including the ATIS and any navaid voice channel; your receiver may work even if your transmitter does not.
3. **Transmit blind**: make your normal calls prefixed with "**TRANSMITTING BLIND**", repeated, because your transmitter may work even if your receiver does not. "Transmitting blind, Northam traffic, Cessna Alpha Bravo Charlie, ..."
4. **Remain in VMC.** In controlled airspace, a VFR flight that has lost communications should stay in VMC and follow the ERSA procedure for its situation (generally: proceed in accordance with the last clearance received and acknowledged where that is what ERSA requires, or leave controlled airspace by the most direct route, then land).
5. **Land at the most suitable aerodrome.**
6. **Report arrival** to ATS by telephone as soon as possible, so a search is not started (and cancel any SARTIME).

Source: ERSA EMERG, AIP ENR 1.1 and ENR 1.6; verify against the current text.

### Why 7600

A **transponder** answers radar interrogations with a four-digit code (each digit 0 to 7) and, in ALT mode, your pressure altitude. A few codes are reserved so that their meaning appears on every radar screen at once. **7600** means radio failure. The moment you select it, the controller's display flags your aircraft, and they know two things without a word from you: you may not hear instructions, and you will be following the published procedure. They can then move other traffic around you. Remember the three reserved codes as a set: **7500** unlawful interference, **7600** radio failure, **7700** emergency.

### Why listen, and why transmit blind

Listening costs nothing and may give you everything: the ATIS tells you the runway and conditions at your destination, and a working receiver lets you hear other traffic and any instruction ATC tries to give you.

**Transmitting blind** means making a broadcast you cannot confirm anyone received. You make the same calls you would normally make, at the same points (position, intentions, joining, turning final), prefixed "TRANSMITTING BLIND" and sent twice, because without a readback you cannot know if the first one got through. The prefix tells every listener not to reply and expect an answer, and it tells ATC that you will not acknowledge their calls.

### Why remain in VMC and land

In visual conditions you can see and avoid other traffic yourself, which replaces the separation that the radio was helping with. If you flew into cloud you would be invisible to other pilots and unable to coordinate with anyone. Landing at the **most suitable aerodrome** limits the time you spend as a silent aircraft. "Most suitable" is your judgement: often the nearest non-controlled aerodrome with a good runway, sometimes your destination if it is close and you know its procedures well.

### Why telephone afterwards

The search and rescue system is triggered by silence: if you held a **SARTIME** (a time by which you promised to report safe arrival) or were on a flight plan with ATS, the system cannot tell a quiet radio from a crashed aeroplane. Telephoning ATS (or the tower you were dealing with) as soon as you are on the ground closes the loop and stops a search being launched for you.

![What happens if your SARTIME passes with no word from you?](diagram:sartime-timeline "Silence after the SARTIME starts the alerting phases and, eventually, a physical search; a phone call on landing stops it.")

??? check "Check yourself: after the checks you can hear the Jandakot ATIS clearly but the tower never answers you. Which half of the radio is probably working, and does it change what you do?"
    The receiver is probably working, so the transmitter (or microphone) is the suspect. You still squawk 7600 and still transmit blind, because you cannot be sure the transmitter is dead, and you listen closely because any instruction ATC gives you will reach you.

## In controlled airspace

Controlled airspace adds a complication: you were flying on a clearance, and ATC was relying on you to do what you had read back. Step 4 above gives the general shape (stay in VMC; follow the last acknowledged clearance where ERSA requires it, or leave controlled airspace by the most direct route; then land). This is the part most likely to differ in detail between amendments and between classes of airspace, which is why the exam asks you to *extract* it from ERSA rather than recite it: read the paragraph, then apply it to the scenario; verify against the current ERSA EMERG.

The logic behind it is predictability. A controller who has lost contact with you plans around the last thing you agreed to do. If you keep doing it, or leave the controlled airspace by the shortest path, they can keep other traffic clear of you.

## Arriving at a non-controlled aerodrome without a radio

At a non-controlled aerodrome, separation is entirely up to pilots, using lookout and radio broadcasts on the **CTAF** (common traffic advisory frequency). Without a working receiver you do not know who is in the circuit, and without a working transmitter they do not know you are coming. Your arrival therefore has to give you the information the radio normally would.

Make blind broadcasts as usual. Plan to **overfly** the aerodrome above circuit height (check AIP ENR 1.1 for the recommended height) to check the wind indicator, the runway in use and other traffic; then descend on the non-active side and join the circuit, keeping a very good lookout. Other traffic will not know you are there.

![How do you join the circuit from overhead?](diagram:overhead-join "Cross above circuit height, read the windsock and spot the traffic, descend on the dead side away from the circuit, join crosswind at circuit height.")

Each part of that has a reason. Overhead and above the circuit you are clear of the traffic flying it, and from there you can read the windsock and any ground signals to find the runway in use and see the aeroplanes already in the pattern. Descending on the **non-active (dead) side**, the side away from where the circuit is flown, keeps you out of their way while you lose height. Joining at circuit height in the normal direction puts you where other pilots expect to see aircraft. Expect no help from anyone: there is no tower, so no light signals.

??? check "Check yourself: why overfly the aerodrome above circuit height before joining when your radio has failed?"
    Without the radio you do not know the runway in use or where the circuit traffic is. Overhead and above the circuit you are clear of that traffic and can read the windsock and see the other aircraft, then descend on the dead side and join where they expect you.

## Arriving at a controlled aerodrome: light signals

At a towered aerodrome the tower can still control you, without a radio, using a **signal lamp**: a focused light it can aim at a single aircraft and switch between green, red and white, steady or flashing. You cannot reply on the radio, so watch the tower. The colours follow a pattern you met in [RFRC 2.3](/notes/RFRC/2.3): green is permission, red is prohibition, white sends you to a specific place, and a flash is a weaker or preparatory version of the steady light.

| Light | To an aircraft in flight | To an aircraft on the ground |
|---|---|---|
| Steady **green** | Authorised to land (if the pilot is satisfied no collision risk exists) | Authorised to take off |
| Steady **red** | Give way to other aircraft and continue circling | Stop |
| **Green flashes** | Return for landing | Authorised to taxi |
| **Red flashes** | Aerodrome unsafe, do not land | Taxi clear of the landing area in use |
| **White flashes** | Land at this aerodrome after receiving a steady green, and then, after receiving green flashes, proceed to the apron | Return to the starting point on the aerodrome |
| Red pyrotechnic | Notwithstanding any previous instructions, do not land for the time being | |

Source: AIP ENR 1.1 and ERSA EMERG; verify against the current text.

Read the in-flight column as a sequence. **Green flashes** say "come back and set up for a landing"; a **steady green** is the landing clearance itself, and even then you land only if you are satisfied there is no collision risk. A **steady red** says "not yet: give way and keep circling". **Red flashes** are stronger: the aerodrome itself is unsafe, so you do not land there at all. **White flashes** tell you this is where you are to land and that you will be sent to the apron afterwards, with the steady green and then green flashes still to come.

Acknowledging (check AIP ENR 1.1 for the exact wording): in flight by day, **rocking the wings**; at night, flashing the landing lights or navigation lights. On the ground by day, moving the ailerons or rudder; at night, flashing the landing or navigation lights.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The exam pairs **green flashes** and **steady green** as distractors for each other. In flight, green flashes mean *return for landing*; only a steady green authorises the landing. On the ground, green flashes mean *taxi* and a steady green means *take off*.

??? check "Check yourself: in the circuit with a failed radio, you see white flashes from the tower. What do they mean, and what are you still waiting for?"
    Land at this aerodrome; you are still waiting for a steady green before you land, and then for green flashes after landing before you taxi to the apron.

## Using the procedure: two scenarios

Here is the procedure applied twice, once with a tower and once without.

!!! example "Worked example: radio failure inbound to a controlled aerodrome"
    Given: you are VFR, inbound to Jandakot from the training area. After listening to the ATIS you hear nothing on tower. The checks reveal nothing.

    1. Squawk **7600** and keep listening on the tower and ATIS frequencies. The ATIS still gives you the runway in use.
    2. Transmit blind your position and intentions, prefixed **"TRANSMITTING BLIND"** and repeated.
    3. Stay clear of the control zone if ERSA directs it, or continue as ERSA permits for your situation; **verify the current ERSA EMERG paragraph** for VFR in controlled airspace.
    4. If continuing, **watch the tower for light signals** and land only after a **steady green**.
    5. **Telephone the tower** after landing.

    The tower, seeing 7600 on radar and hearing perhaps none of your calls, can still sequence you with the lamp; your job is to be predictable and to watch.

Now try the non-controlled case before opening the solution. You are returning to Northam, a non-controlled aerodrome, from the west. Ten miles out you make your inbound call on the CTAF and hear nothing at all: no replies, no other traffic, although it is a Saturday and you heard three aircraft there when you left. What do you do from here to the hangar?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    1. **Check first**: volume, squelch (hiss present?), active frequency correct, audio panel MIC and receive on the right COM, plugs, circuit breakers, then COM 2 and the hand microphone.
    2. Nothing found: **squawk 7600** if a transponder is fitted.
    3. **Keep listening** on the CTAF in case the receiver recovers or partly works.
    4. **Broadcast blind** at every normal point ("Transmitting blind, Northam traffic, ..."), each call sent twice.
    5. **Stay in VMC** and continue to Northam, the most suitable aerodrome since you are almost there.
    6. **Overfly above circuit height** (check AIP ENR 1.1 for the recommended height), read the windsock, find the runway in use and the traffic, descend on the dead side and join crosswind at circuit height with a heightened lookout. There is no tower, so **no light signals** to expect.
    7. After landing, **telephone ATS** if you hold a SARTIME or flight notification, and cancel it.

    The difference from the Jandakot case is who sequences you: at Northam nobody does, so the overhead join and your eyes replace the CTAF entirely.

## Bringing it together

Picture the worst version: a flat battery takes both radios and the transponder at once, near Serpentine on the way home to Jandakot. You cannot squawk 7600 and you cannot transmit, so ATC has no idea; you are a silent, unidentified aeroplane. The ERSA logic still tells you what to do: stay in VMC, do not enter controlled airspace unless ERSA permits it for your situation, choose the most suitable aerodrome (perhaps Serpentine itself, non-controlled and right below you), overfly, join from the dead side with your eyes working hard, land, and telephone so nobody starts looking for you.

??? check "Check yourself: why does the procedure have you both listen out and transmit blind, when at least one of them is probably useless?"
    From the cockpit you cannot tell whether the receiver or the transmitter has failed. Doing both guarantees that whichever half still works is used: listening catches the ATIS and instructions, transmitting tells others where you are.

## Key points

- **3.8.1** The procedure is in **ERSA EMERG** (the pink pages); pick the paragraph for your flight rules and airspace and verify it against the current text. Check the radio first (volume, squelch, frequency, audio panel, plugs, breakers, COM 2). Then: **squawk 7600**, **listen** (ATIS, tower, CTAF), **transmit blind** ("TRANSMITTING BLIND", twice), **remain in VMC**, follow the ERSA procedure in controlled airspace, **land at the most suitable aerodrome**, **telephone** on arrival and cancel the SARTIME. Non-controlled: overfly above circuit height, descend on the dead side, join with a heightened lookout. Controlled: watch for light signals; steady green to land, green flashes to return for landing, steady red to give way and keep circling, red flashes do not land; rock the wings by day to acknowledge.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    7600 is radio failure, 7700 emergency, 7500 unlawful interference. "Transmitting blind" means you assume your transmitter may work even though you hear nothing. A steady green to an aircraft in flight means cleared to land; flashing green means return for landing. Expect a scenario or an ERSA extract: decide which paragraph applies (VFR or IFR, inside or outside controlled airspace), then read it literally. Verify the procedure and the light signals against the current ERSA EMERG before the exam.
