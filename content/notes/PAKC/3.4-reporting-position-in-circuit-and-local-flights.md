## What the exam expects (PAKC 3.4)

- **3.4.1** Give the standard words for reporting where you are in the circuit, and the calls a local flight makes on departure, in the training area and on return, at both non-controlled and towered aerodromes.

## Why this matters

On a Saturday morning the circuit at Murrayfield can hold four aeroplanes, a gyroplane and someone inbound from Serpentine, and the only thing joining them into one picture is the radio. If every pilot names the same legs with the same words and puts the same items in the same order, a ten-second broadcast tells everyone where you are, how high and what you will do next. If someone invents their own words, the picture breaks and lookout is all that is left. At Jandakot the tower does the sequencing, but it can only do it if your calls give it what it needs, at the points it expects them. The exam asks you to pick the correct form of a broadcast, to know when the inbound call is due and why the aerodrome name is said twice, and what a downwind call at a towered aerodrome contains. Every call in this lesson is from the cited AIP and AC 91-10 wording as the note gives it; check the current AIP and VFRG before relying on any of them.

## Naming the legs of the circuit

Every circuit is the same shape: a rectangle with the runway on one long side. Because the shape is standard, the parts can have standard names, and a name tells other pilots both where you are and roughly how high.

![Which leg is which, and where are you on each?](diagram:circuit-pattern "Upwind straight ahead, crosswind after the first turn, downwind at circuit height parallel to the runway, base descending, final lined up.")

The legs are:

- **Upwind**: straight ahead after take-off, climbing along the runway direction.
- **Crosswind**: after the first turn, at right angles to the runway, still climbing to circuit height.
- **Downwind**: parallel to the runway, flying the opposite way to the landing direction, at circuit height.
- **Base**: at right angles again, descending, setting up for the last turn.
- **Final**: lined up with the runway, descending to land.

Around those five sit a few more terms. **Joining crosswind** and **joining downwind** say you are entering the pattern on that leg rather than flying it from take-off. **Late downwind** means you are past the landing threshold on the downwind leg, so other pilots know you will turn base soon. **Straight-in approach** means you are approaching along the extended centreline without flying the rest of the circuit.

Distances on final are given in nautical miles: "5 miles final". Left or right is added only where the circuit direction is not obvious, for example at an aerodrome where one runway has a right-hand circuit: "right downwind runway 24".

![Fly the circuit in 3D and change the runway in use](widget:circuit-3d "Swap runways and the whole pattern flips; the leg names stay the same, which is why they work as position reports.")

!!! warning "Common trap"
    "Upwind" is not "into wind on approach". It is the leg flown straight ahead after take-off. A pilot who calls "upwind" when they mean final points every listener at the wrong end of the runway.

??? check "Check yourself: you are on the downwind leg, already past the landing threshold. What do you call your position, and why does the distinction help others?"
    "Late downwind". It tells others that your base turn is imminent, so an aircraft about to turn base or join will expect you close ahead rather than at the start of the leg.

## Calls at a non-controlled aerodrome

At a non-controlled aerodrome nobody sequences the traffic. The pilots do it themselves, using two tools: lookout and the **common traffic advisory frequency** (CTAF), the frequency published in ERSA on which everyone near that aerodrome broadcasts. Murrayfield, Serpentine and Northam all work this way; their CTAF frequencies are in ERSA.

Under CASR Part 91 a pilot must broadcast when it is reasonably necessary to avoid a collision risk, and pilots must carry and use a radio at certain aerodromes (check AIP ENR 1.1 and the VFRG for the current list). AC 91-10 then sets out the **recommended broadcasts**, the points at which a call most often prevents a conflict.

### The shape of every broadcast

Every CTAF broadcast follows one format:

**location traffic, type, call-sign, position, level, intentions, location**

Take it item by item. The **location** first tells listeners which aerodrome the call is about. **Traffic** says it is addressed to all aircraft, not to a particular station. **Type** lets others picture what they are looking for and how fast it will be. The **call-sign** identifies you. **Position** and **level** say where you are. **Intentions** say what you will do next, which is the part another pilot needs to plan around. And the **location again** at the end matters because the same frequency (often 126.7) is shared by many aerodromes. A pilot who tunes in halfway through your call missed the first word; the last word tells them whether the call is about their aerodrome or one 40 nm away.

![How does a CTAF broadcast build its picture?](diagram:ctaf-broadcast-anatomy "Location, traffic, type, call-sign, position, level, intentions, location: the aerodrome name brackets the call because many aerodromes share one frequency.")

### The recommended calls

| When | Example |
|---|---|
| Before taxiing | "Northam traffic, Cessna 172 Alpha Bravo Charlie, taxiing runway 23 for the south-west training area, Northam." |
| Before entering the runway | "Northam traffic, Cessna Alpha Bravo Charlie, entering and rolling runway 23, departing to the south-west, Northam." |
| Inbound, by 10 nm (earlier for fast aircraft) | "Northam traffic, Cessna 172 Alpha Bravo Charlie, 10 miles south, 2,500, inbound, estimate circuit at 25, Northam." |
| Joining the circuit | "Northam traffic, Cessna Alpha Bravo Charlie, joining crosswind runway 23, Northam." |
| Straight-in approach (if used) | "... 3 miles final, straight-in approach runway 23 ..." (only when no other traffic is disadvantaged) |
| Base or final | When needed for separation, e.g. if other traffic is about: "... turning base runway 23 ..." |
| Clear of the runway | Optional, useful when traffic is waiting: "... clear of runway 23 ..." |

Each call sits where it does for a reason. The **taxi call** warns aircraft about to land that someone will soon be on the runway, and tells anyone else taxiing where you are going. The **entering the runway** call is the last warning before you occupy the runway; an aircraft on short final that hears it can go around rather than land on top of you. The **inbound call** gives circuit traffic time to look for you. The **joining call** tells them exactly where you will appear. **Base and final** calls are made when other traffic is about and needs to know where you are in the sequence. **Clear of the runway** tells a waiting aircraft it may line up.

### Why 10 nm for the inbound call

The inbound call should be made by **10 nm**, which at 100 kt gives others about 6 minutes' warning. The arithmetic: at 100 kt you cover

$$\frac{10\ \text{nm}}{100\ \text{kt}} \times 60 = 6\ \text{minutes}$$

Six minutes is enough for a pilot in the circuit to hear you, look for you and fit their circuit around your arrival. A faster aircraft covers the 10 nm sooner, which is why the note says earlier for fast aircraft.

Before the inbound call, listen to the CTAF for a while to learn which runway is in use and who is in the circuit. Then your call can state your joining intention in terms that fit what is already happening.

!!! example "Worked example: a training flight departing Northam"
    Situation: Cessna 172 Alpha Bravo Charlie at Northam, runway 23 in use, departing for the south-west training area. The calls, in order:

    1. Listen on the CTAF before starting to learn the runway in use and the traffic.
    2. Before taxiing: "Northam traffic, Cessna 172 Alpha Bravo Charlie, taxiing runway 23 for the south-west training area, Northam."
    3. At the holding point, after the run-up, look along the approach path and listen.
    4. Before entering the runway: "Northam traffic, Cessna Alpha Bravo Charlie, entering and rolling runway 23, departing to the south-west, Northam."

    Two calls, each bracketed by "Northam", give every pilot within range a complete picture of where you are and what you will do next.

Now try the return. The same aeroplane is 12 miles south of Northam at 2,500 ft, returning at about 100 kt, and expects to reach the circuit at 25 minutes past the hour. Write the inbound call, say when you will make it, and write the joining call for a crosswind join on runway 23.

??? solution "Show the solution"
    1. Listen to the CTAF first to confirm the runway in use and the traffic.
    2. The inbound call is due **by 10 nm**, so make it now or within the next mile: "Northam traffic, Cessna 172 Alpha Bravo Charlie, 10 miles south, 2,500, inbound, estimate circuit at 25, Northam."
    3. On joining: "Northam traffic, Cessna Alpha Bravo Charlie, joining crosswind runway 23, Northam."
    4. If other traffic is about, add "turning base runway 23" at the base turn.

    At 100 kt the 10 nm call gives about 6 minutes' warning before you reach the circuit.

![How do you join the circuit from overhead when you are unsure of the runway in use?](diagram:overhead-join "Cross overhead above circuit height, descend on the dead side, join crosswind at circuit height: the joining call then names the crosswind leg.")

??? check "Check yourself: a CTAF broadcast starts with the aerodrome name. Why say it again at the end?"
    Because one frequency is often shared by several aerodromes, and a listener who tunes in or starts paying attention partway through missed the first word. The name at the end tells them which aerodrome the call concerns.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    A straight-in approach is allowed only when no other traffic is disadvantaged. Calling "straight-in" does not give you priority over aircraft already in the circuit; if anyone would have to extend or go around for you, join the circuit instead.

## Calls at a towered aerodrome

At a towered aerodrome such as Jandakot, the tower controls the circuit and gives sequencing. Your calls change character: you are no longer telling everyone where you are so they can sort themselves out, you are telling the controller what you need so they can sort it out for you. Position reports are made when asked or at standard points.

The key one is **downwind**:

> "Alpha Bravo Charlie, downwind, touch and go"

State "full stop" or "touch and go". The downwind call is the controller's cue to fit you into the landing sequence, and your intention matters to that: a touch and go stays in the circuit, a full stop leaves the runway and frees a slot. The tower replies with a **sequence**:

> "Alpha Bravo Charlie, number two, follow the Warrior on base."

"Number two" means one aircraft lands ahead of you; "follow the Warrior on base" tells you which one to look for. Your job is to find it, follow it, and turn base behind it.

**Final** is reported if requested or where local procedures require: "Alpha Bravo Charlie, final."

Then comes the clearance. Readback the landing or take-off clearance in full:

> "Cleared touch and go runway 24 right, Alpha Bravo Charlie."

Reading it back in full lets the controller hear that you have the right runway and the right clearance. Jandakot has parallel runways, so "24 right" against "24 left" is exactly the kind of slip a readback catches.

!!! example "Worked example: a Jandakot circuit exchange"
    Situation: Alpha Bravo Charlie in the Jandakot circuit for runway 24 right, intending to land and stay in the circuit.

    1. On downwind: "Alpha Bravo Charlie, downwind, touch and go."
    2. Tower: "Alpha Bravo Charlie, number two, follow the Warrior on base."
    3. Find the Warrior, then follow it; turn base behind it.
    4. Tower clears you; read it back in full: "Cleared touch and go runway 24 right, Alpha Bravo Charlie."
    5. If no clearance has come by short final, you do not land: query the tower or go around.

    The downwind call carried your intention, the sequence told you whom to follow, and the full readback confirmed the runway.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    At a towered aerodrome you never land without hearing "cleared to land" (or the touch and go clearance you asked for). Being number one and seeing a clear runway is not a clearance.

??? check "Check yourself: why does the downwind call at a towered aerodrome include \"touch and go\" or \"full stop\"?"
    Because the controller is building the landing sequence, and the two intentions use the runway differently: a touch and go stays in the circuit, a full stop vacates. Stating it on downwind gives the controller time to plan.

## Local flights: out to the training area and back

A **local flight** leaves the circuit for a nearby training area and returns to the same aerodrome. Its calls bracket the flight: getting out, staying in touch while there, and getting back in.

### Departing a towered aerodrome

From Jandakot the sequence is: obtain the ATIS, then call ground with the departure request.

> "Jandakot Ground, Cessna Alpha Bravo Charlie, received Echo, request taxi for south-west training area, VFR, two POB."

Every item has a purpose. "Received Echo" tells Ground you have the current ATIS, so they need not read you the runway and weather. The request says where you are going, so the taxi route and departure can be planned. "VFR" and "two POB" (persons on board) complete the picture for search and rescue. On leaving the control zone by the published route, tower will say "frequency change approved" or hand you to Centre.

![Is the aerodrome controlled today?](diagram:class-d-tower-hours "During tower hours the zone is Class D and you need clearances; outside them it reverts to a CTAF aerodrome and the CTAF calls apply.")

### In the training area

Maintain a listening watch on the area frequency (and any training-area frequency listed in ERSA). Some areas have local broadcast procedures; ERSA shows them. The listening watch is what lets you hear another aircraft reporting its position in your area, and what lets ATS reach you.

### Returning

To a towered aerodrome: listen to the ATIS, then call tower at the inbound reporting point.

> "Jandakot Tower, Cessna Alpha Bravo Charlie, (reporting point), 1,500, received Golf, inbound."

The inbound reporting points and altitudes are on the Perth VTC and in ERSA. The call has the same logic as the CTAF inbound call, position, level and intention, plus the ATIS letter.

To a CTAF aerodrome: make the inbound call by 10 nm and the joining call, exactly as in the Northam example.

### SARTIME

If you have nominated a SARTIME for the local flight (with Centre or a responsible person), you must cancel it on landing. A **SARTIME** is the time by which, if you have not reported, search and rescue action starts. Nominating it is a safety net for a solo flight; forgetting to cancel it launches a search for an aeroplane that is already in the hangar.

![What happens if you do not cancel your SARTIME?](diagram:sartime-timeline "Nothing happens until the SARTIME passes; then the alerting phases start, so cancel on landing.")

??? check "Check yourself: in the Jandakot taxi request, what does \"received Echo\" tell Ground, and why does it save time?"
    That you have listened to ATIS information Echo, the current broadcast of runway, wind and conditions. Ground need not repeat that information, so the exchange is shorter and the frequency stays clear.

## Bringing it together

A Sunday local flight from Jandakot. You listen to the ATIS, information Echo, and request taxi from Ground for the south-west training area, VFR, two POB. Tower clears you for take-off; you read the clearance back in full, including "24 right". Leaving the zone by the published route, the tower approves the frequency change and you keep a listening watch on the area frequency in the training area. On the way home you divert to Serpentine for a few circuits: you listen on its CTAF to learn the runway in use, make the inbound call by 10 nm with position, level and intention, bracketed by "Serpentine", then a joining call naming the leg, and a "turning base" call because two others are in the pattern. Back towards Jandakot, you take the new ATIS, Golf, call tower at the inbound reporting point at the published altitude, and on downwind say "touch and go" or "full stop". The tower gives you a sequence, you find the aircraft to follow, and you land only after the clearance. On the ground, the last call is to cancel your SARTIME. Every call used the same leg names and the same order of items; only the listener changed.

## Key points

- **3.4.1** Legs are upwind, crosswind, downwind, base and final, plus joining crosswind, joining downwind, late downwind and straight-in; distances in miles. CTAF broadcasts go location traffic, type, call-sign, position, level, intentions, location, with the inbound call by 10 nm. Recommended calls: taxiing, entering the runway, inbound, joining, base or final when traffic needs it, clear of the runway. At a tower: downwind with "touch and go" or "full stop", follow the sequence, read clearances back in full, never land without a clearance. Local flights: ATIS, ground request, listening watch in the area, inbound at the reporting point with the ATIS letter, cancel the SARTIME.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    The inbound call at a non-controlled aerodrome is made by 10 nm, and every CTAF broadcast both starts and ends with the aerodrome name. At a towered aerodrome the downwind call states your intention (touch and go or full stop), and you never land without hearing "cleared to land". Expect a question offering four versions of a broadcast: pick the one in the order location traffic, type, call-sign, position, level, intentions, location.
