## What the exam expects (PAKC 2.1)

- **2.1.1** Explain what full throttle height means.
- **2.1.2** Describe how rising altitude and rising temperature change the power an engine can make.
- **2.1.3** Describe how engine performance depends on (a) the strength of the fuel/air mixture, and (b) density height and altitude, both for a normally aspirated engine and for a turbocharged or supercharged one.

## Why this matters

As a private pilot you will start flying further and higher: Jandakot to Kalgoorlie at 8,500 ft to stay above the afternoon bumps, or over the Darling Scarp to a station strip in the Wheatbelt on a 40 degree February day. Both flights ask the engine for power in air it was never rated in, and the throttle can no longer give you whatever you ask for. At some height it is fully open and the power you planned on is simply not available, and on a hot day that height arrives much lower than the book suggests. If you hire a turbocharged aeroplane the rules change again. You met the engine itself in [BAKC 3.1](/notes/BAKC/3.1) and the mixture control in [RBKA 2.1](/notes/RBKA/2.1); this lesson adds the altitude picture. The exam asks what full throttle height is and why it changes with the power setting, how density height eats engine power, where best power and best economy sit on the mixture scale, and how a turbocharged engine differs from a normally aspirated one.

## An engine is an air pump

Start with what you already know from [BAKC 3.1](/notes/BAKC/3.1): on every induction stroke the piston moves down and the cylinder fills with a fuel/air mixture, which is then compressed, burnt and exhausted. The fuel is never the limit. The carburettor or injector can supply as much fuel as you like; what limits each power stroke is how much **air**, measured by mass, gets into the cylinder, because the oxygen in that air is what burns the fuel. Twice the mass of air means you can burn twice the fuel and make roughly twice the power.

So the question "how much power can this engine make?" is really "how many kilograms of air can it pump through itself each minute?". At a given RPM the cylinders swallow a fixed **volume** on each stroke. The **mass** in that volume depends on the density of the air arriving at the intake. Everything in this lesson follows from that one idea: anything that lowers the density of the air reaching the cylinders lowers the power the engine can make.

Between the outside air and the cylinders sits the **throttle butterfly**, a disc in the intake that the throttle lever opens and closes. When it is partly closed it restricts the flow, and the pressure in the intake pipe downstream of it, the **manifold pressure**, is lower than the outside air pressure. Many PPL aeroplanes, especially those with a constant-speed propeller, have a **manifold pressure gauge** that reads this pressure in inches of mercury. It is the most direct measure you have of how much air is being offered to the cylinders. With the engine stopped there is no flow, nothing restricts it, and the gauge reads the outside air pressure: about 30 inches at a sea-level aerodrome on a standard day (29.92 inches is the same pressure as 1013.25 hPa).

??? check "Check yourself: with the engine stopped on the ground, what should the manifold pressure gauge read, and why?"
    About the outside air pressure, close to 30 inches at a sea-level aerodrome. With no air flowing there is no pressure drop across the throttle butterfly, so the intake pipe is at ambient pressure. A gauge reading well below that is faulty.

## What altitude and temperature do to power

Climb away from Jandakot and the air around you thins. Pressure falls by about 1 hPa for every 30 ft low down, which is roughly 1 inch of mercury every 1,000 ft, and with it the density. Each cylinder still swallows the same volume per stroke, but that volume now holds less air by mass, so it can burn less fuel and the power stroke is weaker. A **normally aspirated** engine, one that relies on atmospheric pressure alone to push air into the cylinders, loses power steadily from the moment it leaves the ground.

Temperature does the same thing by a different route. Warm air at the same pressure is less dense than cool air, because its molecules are moving faster and spread further apart. On a 38 degree afternoon the air at the aerodrome holds noticeably less oxygen per litre than on a 15 degree morning at the same pressure. The engine cannot tell the difference between thin air caused by height and thin air caused by heat; it responds only to density. Humid air costs a little more again, because water vapour displaces some of the oxygen-carrying air, but the effect is small next to height and temperature.

The single number that combines all of this is **density height**: the height in the International Standard Atmosphere at which the air would be as dense as the air the engine is breathing now. You learnt to calculate it in [BAKC 6.2](/notes/BAKC/6.2) from the pressure height and the temperature. An engine at a density height of 5,000 ft makes the same power whether it is at 5,000 ft on a standard day or at a Wheatbelt strip on a scorching afternoon.

![How much power is left at full throttle as you climb?](diagram:power-loss-with-altitude "Roughly 3 percent less per 1,000 ft of density height: by about 7,000 to 8,000 ft full throttle gives only about 75 percent.")

The rule of thumb for a normally aspirated engine is a loss of about **3 percent of sea-level power for every 1,000 ft of density height**. It is a rule of thumb, not a law, and your POH cruise tables give the real figures for your engine, but it is close enough to show how fast power disappears: roughly 75 percent is all that is left at about 8,000 ft, and about 55 percent at 15,000 ft.

![Work out the density height that the engine is really flying at](widget:density-height "Low QNH and heat both push density height up, and heat is the bigger of the two.")

!!! example "Worked example: power available on a hot afternoon"
    Given: a Wheatbelt strip, elevation 1,000 ft, QNH 1008 hPa, temperature 36 degrees. Rule of thumb: 3 percent power lost per 1,000 ft of density height.

    1. Pressure height: $1000 + (1013 - 1008) \times 30 = 1000 + 150 = \mathbf{1{,}150\ ft}$.
    2. ISA temperature at 1,150 ft: $15 - 2 \times 1.15 = \mathbf{12.7}$ degrees.
    3. Deviation from ISA: $36 - 12.7 = \mathbf{23.3}$ degrees above.
    4. Density height: $1150 + 120 \times 23.3 = 1150 + 2796 = \mathbf{3{,}950\ ft}$ (3,946).
    5. Power available at full throttle: $100 - 3 \times 3.95 = 100 - 11.9 = \mathbf{88\ percent}$.

    On the take-off roll, with the throttle wide open, the engine is making about seven-eighths of its rated power before you have left the ground. The take-off run is longer and the climb shallower, and both get worse as you climb.

Now try one before opening the solution: you are cruising at a pressure height of 9,500 ft and the outside air temperature is +10 degrees. Roughly what percentage of sea-level power can the engine make at full throttle?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    ISA at 9,500 ft is $15 - 2 \times 9.5 = -4$ degrees, so the air is $10 - (-4) = 14$ degrees warmer than standard. Density height $= 9500 + 120 \times 14 = 9500 + 1680 = 11{,}180$ ft. Power $\approx 100 - 3 \times 11.18 = 100 - 33.5$, so **about 66 percent**. If your flight plan assumed a 75 percent cruise, the engine cannot deliver it here.

Temperature has a second effect that the 3 percent rule does not show. An engine is cooled by air flowing over its cylinder fins and by the oil. On a hot day the cooling air is hotter, so for the same power the cylinders and oil run hotter, and the margin before the cylinder head temperature and oil temperature limits shrinks. In a long climb on a hot day the engine is making less power and is closer to its temperature limits at the same time. That is why the POH may call for a faster, shallower **cruise climb** on hot days: more airflow over the engine for a little less climb rate.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The exam may describe two aerodromes, a high one on a cool day and a low one on a hot day, and ask which gives better engine performance. Do not compare elevations. Work out, or estimate, the density height of each: the one with the lower density height wins, whatever its elevation.

??? check "Check yourself: why does a hot day cost engine power when the pressure is the same as on a cool day?"
    Warm air is less dense than cool air at the same pressure, so each cylinderful holds less air by mass, can burn less fuel and makes a weaker power stroke. The engine responds to density height, and heat raises density height.

## Full throttle height

Picture a climb out of Jandakot towards your cruising level, in an aeroplane with a constant-speed propeller and a manifold pressure gauge. The POH cruise table says that 65 percent power at your chosen RPM needs, say, 24 inches of manifold pressure at sea level. You set it. As you climb, the outside pressure falls about an inch per 1,000 ft, so the manifold pressure falls too, and the power with it. To hold 65 percent you open the throttle a little more every few hundred feet, letting more of the thinner air through the butterfly so that the cylinders keep getting the same mass of air.

That cannot go on for ever. At some height the throttle reaches its stop: the butterfly is fully open, there is no restriction left to remove, and the engine is only just making 65 percent. That height is the **full throttle height** for 65 percent power. Above it the manifold pressure, and the power, can only fall, at about an inch and 3 percent per 1,000 ft of density height, because there is no more throttle to give.

In an aeroplane with a fixed-pitch propeller, like most trainers, there is no manifold pressure gauge, and you see the same thing on the tachometer instead: above full throttle height the RPM sags in the cruise with the throttle wide open, and nothing you do with the throttle brings it back.

Three consequences follow, and the exam uses all of them.

**Full throttle height belongs to a power setting.** Each power setting has its own. A lower power needs less air, so the throttle can stay partly closed, with a reserve to open, up to a greater height. The full throttle height for 65 percent is higher than the full throttle height for 75 percent; the full throttle height for 100 percent is sea level (in density height terms), because at sea level full power already needs a wide-open throttle.

**Full throttle height is a density height.** Because the engine responds to density, the full throttle height for any power is reached at a lower pressure height on a hot day than on a standard day. On a hot day the power you planned may run out a couple of thousand feet below where the POH table, drawn for the standard atmosphere, says it should.

**Above full throttle height the cruise power is whatever full throttle gives.** Light aeroplanes are routinely cruised at full throttle at altitude. It does the engine no harm, because the thin air limits the power to well below its rating; you control the power by your choice of altitude and RPM and by leaning, and the POH cruise table tells you what you will get.

!!! example "Worked example: full throttle height for 65 percent on a hot day"
    Given: a normally aspirated engine, rule of thumb 3 percent power lost per 1,000 ft of density height. Today the air at cruising levels is 15 degrees warmer than ISA.

    1. Power that may be lost before 65 percent is all that is left: $100 - 65 = \mathbf{35\ percent}$.
    2. Density height at which that happens: $35 \div 3 = 11.7$, so about $\mathbf{11{,}700\ ft}$ density height.
    3. Height the hot air adds: $120 \times 15 = \mathbf{1{,}800\ ft}$.
    4. Pressure height at full throttle height: $11700 - 1800 = \mathbf{9{,}900\ ft}$.

    On a standard day you could hold 65 percent up to about 11,700 ft. Today the throttle hits the stop at about 9,900 ft on the altimeter set to 1013. Plan a lower cruise level, or accept less power and a slower cruise.

Now try the other way round: on a standard day, about what density height is the full throttle height for 75 percent power, and is it above or below the full throttle height for 65 percent?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    For 75 percent the engine may lose $100 - 75 = 25$ percent, and $25 \div 3 = 8.3$, so **about 8,300 ft** (a typical trainer's POH puts it at about 7,000 to 8,000 ft). That is **below** the 11,700 ft for 65 percent: the higher the power setting, the lower its full throttle height.

??? check "Check yourself: why is the full throttle height for 65 percent power higher than for 75 percent?"
    Sixty-five percent needs less air, so at any height the throttle needs to be open less far for it. You keep a reserve of throttle up to a greater height before the butterfly is fully open and the reserve is gone.

## Mixture strength and power

The mixture control has already been taught as a handling skill in [RBKA 2.1](/notes/RBKA/2.1): lean in the cruise by watching the RPM, go full rich for the descent and landing. The PPL question is a performance one: how does the strength of the mixture change the power and the economy you get?

### The chemically correct ratio, and why the engine is not run at it

Petrol and air burn completely, with no fuel and no oxygen left over, at a ratio of about **1 part fuel to 15 parts air by weight**. That is the **chemically correct** (or stoichiometric) mixture. It is natural to assume the engine makes its best power there. It does not, quite.

- **Best power** comes from a mixture slightly **richer** than chemically correct. With a little surplus fuel every molecule of oxygen in the cylinder finds fuel to burn quickly, the burn is fast and complete, and the extra fuel evaporating in the cylinder cools the charge so more of it can be packed in. You get the most power for a given throttle setting.
- **Best economy** comes from a mixture slightly **leaner** than chemically correct. With a surplus of air, every drop of fuel finds oxygen and burns completely; less power comes out of each stroke, but more of it per litre of fuel. You get the most kilometres per litre and the longest endurance, at a small cost in speed.

![What happens to power and temperature as you lean from full rich?](diagram:mixture-rich-to-lean "Power peaks a little rich of the correct mixture; leaner than that it falls away and the cylinders run hotter.")

Either side of that working band, performance falls away. **Too rich** wastes fuel that goes out of the exhaust unburnt, lowers the power, fouls the spark plugs with soot and shortens your endurance. **Too lean** slows the burn, lowers the power, raises the cylinder head and exhaust gas temperatures, and at high power invites **detonation**, where the last part of the charge explodes instead of burning smoothly and hammers the piston. Further lean still, the engine runs rough and stops.

![Why is a lean mixture at high power dangerous?](diagram:detonation-vs-normal-combustion "A lean, hot charge at high power can explode instead of burning smoothly: rough running, a sharp rise in CHT, and damage within minutes.")

### Why altitude makes the mixture rich

The carburettor meters fuel by the **volume** of air flowing through its venturi, because the pressure drop that draws fuel from the jet depends on the airflow speed, not on the air's density. As you climb, each litre of air through the venturi weighs less, but it draws roughly the same fuel. The mixture therefore gets **richer** with height. Left at full rich, the engine at 8,000 ft is well over-rich: down on power, burning fuel it does not need, and fouling its plugs. **Leaning restores the correct ratio for the thinner air**, which is why it gives back power as well as saving fuel.

![Why does the carburettor make the mixture richer as you climb?](diagram:carburettor-cross-section "The venturi draws fuel in proportion to the volume of air passing, so thinner air gets the same fuel and the mixture goes rich.")

That also explains the one exception to "full rich for take-off". At a hot, high strip the air is so thin that a full-rich mixture is over-rich even at take-off power, and the engine cannot make its best available power. Some POHs therefore call for **leaning for take-off at high density heights**, usually by leaning for maximum RPM at full throttle during the run-up. Where your POH says so, do it; where it does not, take off full rich, because the extra fuel is what keeps a hard-working engine cool.

??? check "Check yourself: which side of the chemically correct mixture gives best power, and which gives best economy?"
    Best power is slightly rich of chemically correct: the surplus fuel ensures all the oxygen is used quickly and cools the charge. Best economy is slightly lean of it: the surplus air ensures all the fuel is burnt, giving the most energy per litre at a small cost in power.

## Density height and the two kinds of engine

The final element asks you to compare how density height affects a **normally aspirated** engine with how it affects a **turbocharged or supercharged** engine. The mechanism of the turbocharger is the next lesson, [PAKC 2.2](/notes/PAKC/2.2); here you need only its effect: a compressor in the intake squeezes the air before it reaches the cylinders, so the engine can be given air that is denser than the air outside.

### The normally aspirated engine

The normally aspirated engine is at the mercy of density height from the ground up. Every 1,000 ft of density height takes about 3 percent of its power, so:

- **Take-off** at a high or hot aerodrome is made with less than full rated power. Combined with the thinner air the wing and propeller are working in, the run is longer.
- **Climb** performance falls steadily. The rate of climb depends on the spare power, the power available minus the power needed to fly level, and the power available is shrinking with every 1,000 ft. Eventually the rate of climb falls to 100 ft per minute, the **service ceiling**, and then to zero, the **absolute ceiling**, where the engine at full throttle can only just hold the aeroplane level.
- **Cruise** power is capped by full throttle height. Above it you cruise at full throttle and accept whatever the thin air gives.

### The turbocharged or supercharged engine

Add a compressor and the picture changes. On the ground and low down, the compressor could deliver far more air than the engine can safely use, so a control (the wastegate, taught in [PAKC 2.2](/notes/PAKC/2.2)) limits the boost to the engine's rated manifold pressure. As you climb, the control lets the compressor work harder and harder to keep that manifold pressure, and the engine goes on making its rated power, sea-level power or close to it, whatever the density height outside.

That continues up to the **critical altitude**, the highest altitude at which the compressor, working flat out with the throttle fully open, can still hold the rated manifold pressure. For a light aeroplane turbocharger it is typically 12,000 to 20,000 ft. Above the critical altitude the compressor has nothing left to give, and from there the power falls with density height just as a normally aspirated engine's does. The critical altitude is the turbocharged engine's equivalent of full throttle height: the point where the reserve runs out.

Three points stop you overrating the turbocharger.

**The aeroplane still feels density height.** The turbocharger feeds the engine, not the wing or the propeller blades. At a high density height the wing still needs a higher true airspeed to lift off, so the take-off run is still longer than at sea level, even though the engine is making full power.

**Compressed air is hot air.** Squeezing air heats it, and hot intake air is less dense and closer to detonation. That is one reason turbocharged engines have tighter temperature limits and why some have an **intercooler** to cool the charge. On a hot day the margins are smaller still.

**The mixture still needs managing.** The turbocharger keeps the air dense going into the engine, but the leaning procedure and the high-power full-rich rule still apply as the POH sets them.

| | Normally aspirated | Turbocharged or supercharged |
|---|---|---|
| Power as density height rises | Falls from the surface up, about 3 percent per 1,000 ft | Held at rated power up to the critical altitude, then falls |
| Where the reserve runs out | Full throttle height, for each power setting | Critical altitude, for rated manifold pressure |
| Take-off at a hot, high strip | Less power and a longer run | Full power, but still a longer run: the wing feels the density height |
| Pilot's main job | Open the throttle in the climb and lean | Watch manifold pressure and temperatures, never over-boost |

!!! warning "Common trap"
    A distractor says a turbocharged engine "is unaffected by altitude". It is unaffected only up to its critical altitude; above that it loses power like any other engine. And the aeroplane's take-off and landing distances still grow with density height, because the wing needs a higher true airspeed in thin air.

??? check "Check yourself: what is the critical altitude of a turbocharged engine, and how does it relate to full throttle height?"
    It is the highest altitude at which the turbocharger, at full throttle, can still maintain the rated manifold pressure. Like full throttle height, it marks the point where the reserve is used up; above it, power falls with density height as in a normally aspirated engine.

## Bringing it together

You are planning Jandakot to Kalgoorlie on a February afternoon, forecast 15 degrees above ISA at cruising levels, in a normally aspirated aeroplane whose POH cruise table assumes 75 percent power. The rule of thumb puts 75 percent's full throttle height at about 8,300 ft density height on a standard day; with 15 degrees of extra heat adding 1,800 ft, the throttle reaches its stop at about 6,500 ft pressure height. At 8,500 ft you will be making well under 75 percent, so your true airspeed and your fuel flow will both be lower than the 75 percent row, and your timings should come from the row for the power you will actually get. Leaned correctly in the cruise you will get best power or best economy as you choose; left at full rich, you will get neither. A friend's turbocharged aeroplane would hold its planned power all the way to 8,500 ft and beyond, but it would still need a longer take-off run out of Kalgoorlie on the way home, because its wing feels the heat just as yours does.

## Key points

- **2.1.1** Full throttle height is the height at which the throttle is fully open and the engine is only just making the selected power. Each power setting has its own; a lower power setting has a higher full throttle height, and on a hot day every full throttle height arrives lower.
- **2.1.2** Power depends on the mass of air the engine pumps. Height and heat both lower air density, so both lower power: about 3 percent of sea-level power per 1,000 ft of density height for a normally aspirated engine. Heat also reduces cooling margins.
- **2.1.3 (a)** Best power is slightly rich of the chemically correct ratio (about 1:15 by weight); best economy slightly lean of it. Too rich wastes fuel and fouls plugs; too lean at high power overheats and invites detonation. The carburettor makes the mixture richer with height, and leaning restores it.
- **2.1.3 (b)** A normally aspirated engine loses power from the surface up. A turbocharged or supercharged engine holds rated power up to its critical altitude (typically 12,000 to 20,000 ft), then loses it like a normally aspirated one. Either way the airframe still feels density height.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Full throttle height is specific to a power setting: the full throttle height for 65 percent power is higher than for 75 percent, because less power needs less air. Have the rule of thumb ready (about 3 percent of power per 1,000 ft of density height, so about 75 percent at 8,000 ft), know that best power is slightly rich and best economy slightly lean, and remember that a turbocharger holds sea-level power only up to its critical altitude.
