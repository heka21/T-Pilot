## What the exam expects (PAKC 3.1)

- **3.1.1** Use aeronautical radio correctly: (a) say the phonetic alphabet and transmit numbers the standard way; (b) use aircraft call-signs correctly; (c) know the standard radio procedures outside controlled airspace (OCTA); (d) say a time correctly; (e) listen effectively; (f) establish and keep up two-way communication; (g) explain what a clipped transmission is and the harm it does.

## Why this matters

On a Saturday morning the Jandakot training area, Murrayfield, Serpentine and Northam all have aircraft talking, and several CTAFs share a frequency. A letter misheard is a wrong runway, a dropped call-sign is an instruction followed by the wrong aeroplane, and a missed "hold short" is a runway incursion. Standard phraseology lets every listener pick out the meaning through engine noise, static and accents. The exam asks how numbers, call-signs and times are spoken, what the standard words mean, the readability scale, and why a clipped transmission is dangerous.

## Letters and numbers

### The phonetic alphabet

Over a noisy radio, letters that sound alike blur together: B, C, D, E, G, P, T and V all end in the same "ee" sound. The cure is to replace each letter with a word chosen so that no two can be confused, even by a listener whose first language is not English. This is the **phonetic alphabet**, used worldwide:

| | | | | | |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A Alfa | B Bravo | C Charlie | D Delta | E Echo | F Foxtrot |
| G Golf | H Hotel | I India | J Juliett | K Kilo | L Lima |
| M Mike | N November | O Oscar | P Papa | Q Quebec | R Romeo |
| S Sierra | T Tango | U Uniform | V Victor | W Whiskey | X X-ray |
| Y Yankee | Z Zulu | | | | |

You use it for registrations, taxiway names, ATIS codes ("information Golf") and anything you need to spell. The spellings Alfa and Juliett are deliberate, so that speakers of other languages pronounce them correctly.

### Numbers

Numbers have the same problem. "Five" and "nine" sound alike through static, and "three" is easily lost. So radiotelephony uses a set pronunciation for each digit, given in AIP GEN 3.4. The three that differ most from everyday speech are **TREE** (3), **FIFE** (5) and **NIN-er** (9); the rest are spoken clearly with the stress on the first syllable (WUN, TOO, FOW-er, SIX, SEV-en, AIT, ZE-RO), and the decimal point is **DAY-SEE-MAL**, usually written "decimal".

Most numbers are then sent **digit by digit**: headings, QNH, frequencies, transponder codes, wind direction and speed. Altitudes, heights and cloud bases that are **whole thousands and hundreds** are the exception: they use the words **TOU-SAND** and **HUN-dred**. The words themselves flag that this is a height.

| Item | Written | Spoken |
|---|---|---|
| Altitude | 2,500 ft | "two thousand fife hundred" |
| Flight level | FL125 | "flight level one two fife" |
| Heading | 090 | "heading zero niner zero" |
| QNH | 1013 | "QNH one zero one tree" |
| Frequency | 126.7 MHz | "one two six decimal seven" |
| Squawk | 3412 | "squawk tree four one two" |
| Wind | 270/15 | "wind two seven zero degrees one fife knots" |

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Altitudes in whole thousands and hundreds use "thousand" and "hundred"; headings, frequencies, QNH and codes are digit by digit. A distractor will offer "two five zero zero" for an altitude, or "thirty-five hundred", which is everyday speech, not phraseology.

??? check "Check yourself: how would you say a QNH of 1019 and a heading of 350?"
    "QNH one zero one niner" and "heading tree fife zero". Both are digit by digit, using the radiotelephony pronunciations for 3, 5 and 9.

## Call-signs

Your **call-sign** is how everyone on frequency knows which aeroplane is talking, and which one an instruction is for. An Australian private aeroplane uses its registration without the nationality prefix "VH": VH-ABC is **"Alpha Bravo Charlie"**.

On first contact with ATC, and in broadcasts at a non-controlled aerodrome, put the **aircraft type** in front: "Cessna Alpha Bravo Charlie". The type tells others what to look for and how fast it is coming.

Once you are in two-way contact, call-signs may be **abbreviated**, but never abbreviate your call-sign until the ground station has abbreviated it first (AIP GEN 3.4). The controller knows what else is on frequency; you do not. If another aircraft with a similar call-sign is on frequency, say Alpha Bravo Charlie and Alpha Bravo Delta, always use the full call-sign. Airline flights use their company designator and flight number ("Qantas 512") instead of a registration.

??? check "Check yourself: you are VH-PLK, and VH-PLM is in the same circuit. Why does it matter that both of you use full call-signs?"
    The two call-signs differ only in the last letter, so a shortened or partly heard call-sign could fit either aeroplane. Full call-signs make sure each instruction or broadcast is linked to the right aircraft, and that each pilot acts only on what was meant for them.

## Radio procedures outside controlled airspace

Outside controlled airspace, in **Class G**, nobody gives you a clearance or separates you from other traffic. You and the other pilots separate yourselves by looking out, and by telling each other where you are and what you intend. The radio procedures are built around that.

![What changes between controlled and non-controlled airspace?](diagram:controlled-vs-non-controlled-at-a-glance "In Class G no one separates you: lookout and radio broadcasts do the job that clearances do in controlled airspace.")

**Maintain a listening watch** on the right frequency: en route, the **area frequency** (Centre) shown on the VNC, ERC-L or in ERSA; near a non-controlled aerodrome, its **CTAF** (common traffic advisory frequency) from ERSA. With two COM radios you can monitor both as you depart.

**Make broadcasts** where they are needed to avoid a collision. A **broadcast** is a transmission to all aircraft, addressed to nobody in particular and needing no reply. The recommended points are before taxiing, before entering the runway, inbound by 10 nm, on joining the circuit, and others as needed; [PAKC 3.4](/notes/PAKC/3.4) teaches each call and why it sits where it does.

**Make reports to ATS** when you want a service: SARTIME, traffic information or a weather update from Centre. A **report** is addressed to a particular station and expects an answer.

Every CTAF broadcast follows one format: **location traffic, type, call-sign, position, level, intentions, location**. The aerodrome name is said at the start and the end because several CTAFs often share one frequency, and a listener who missed the start still learns which aerodrome the call was about.

> "Northam traffic, Cessna 172 Alpha Bravo Charlie, 10 miles south-west, 2,500, inbound, estimate overhead at 15, Northam."

!!! warning "Common trap"
    In Class G there is no clearance and nobody to "request" one from at a non-controlled aerodrome. You **broadcast** your position and intentions to other traffic; you **report** to ATS only when you want a service.

## Saying the time

Aviation runs on **Coordinated Universal Time (UTC)**, on the 24-hour clock, so that a flight plan, a NOTAM and a forecast mean the same instant to everyone, whatever their local time zone. Perth local time, Western Standard Time, is **UTC + 8**, with no daylight saving, so 0915 UTC is 1715 WST.

![How do UTC and Western Standard Time line up?](diagram:utc-and-wst-strip "WST is UTC plus 8 hours, so the WST date changes at 1600 UTC.")

On the radio a time is normally given as the **minutes past the hour only**: "estimate overhead at one fife". The hour is usually obvious from context. Where there is any chance of confusion, near the change of the hour or for a time well ahead, give the **hour as well**: "one two one fife". A **time check** is given to the nearest minute: "time one two four fife".

For example, you plan to be overhead Northam at 1048 WST. Subtract 8 hours: 0248 UTC. On the CTAF you would say "estimate overhead at four eight". If you were giving it as a full time, it would be "zero two four eight", never "ten forty-eight".

??? check "Check yourself: you estimate arriving at 0710 WST. What is that in UTC, and how would you normally say it?"
    0710 WST minus 8 hours is 2310 UTC on the previous day. Normally you would say only the minutes, "one zero"; if the hour is needed, "two tree one zero".

## Listening effectively

Half of good radio work is listening.

**Listen before you transmit.** Before you press the PTT, listen for a few seconds to make sure you will not cut across someone mid-exchange. A controller who has just asked a question is waiting for the answer, not for your call. After changing frequency, listen before you speak for the same reason.

**Set the radio up so you can hear.** Volume and squelch are set before departure ([PAKC 3.2](/notes/PAKC/3.2)). A squelch set too tight makes a busy frequency sound quiet.

**Build a picture of the traffic.** Place each aircraft you hear relative to you: "the Warrior inbound from the north will join crosswind about when I turn downwind".

**Process every call, not just your own.** Listen for your call-sign, but a conflicting aircraft will not use your name.

**Write things down and anticipate.** Write down clearances, the ATIS, frequencies and QNH. Knowing what ATC is likely to say next, a frequency change after departure, a sequence when inbound, makes it easier to hear correctly what they do say. But never hear what you expected instead of what was said.

## Establishing and keeping communication

### The first call

Making contact with a station follows a set pattern: **the station you are calling, your call-sign, then your position and message, or "request" if the message is long**. For example: "Melbourne Centre, Cessna 172 Alpha Bravo Charlie, request traffic". Then wait for the reply before giving the rest of the message. A short first call makes sure the controller is ready to listen before you give the details.

If there is no reply, wait at least about 10 seconds before calling again; the controller may be dealing with another aircraft or another frequency. Then check the basics: are you on the right frequency, is the volume up, is the MIC selector on the radio you think, and are you within range? Fault-finding is taught in [PAKC 3.3](/notes/PAKC/3.3).

### Readback

Once in contact, some messages must be **read back**: repeated by you so the controller can hear that you received them correctly. AIP GEN 3.4 requires readback of ATC route clearances; any clearance or instruction to enter, land on, take off from, backtrack on, cross or hold short of a runway; the runway in use; altimeter settings; SSR codes; level instructions; and heading and speed instructions. Check the AIP for the complete current list. End a readback with your call-sign, which tells the controller which aircraft has acknowledged.

A readback is a two-way check: it catches both your mishearing and the controller's slip of the tongue.

### The standard words

A small set of words carries a precise meaning, so that a short reply cannot be misunderstood:

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Roger** | I have received all of your last transmission |
| **Wilco** | I understand your message and will comply |
| **Affirm** | Yes |
| **Negative** | No, or permission not granted |
| **Say again** | Repeat all, or the following part, of your last transmission |
| **Standby** | Wait and I will call you |
| **Correction** | I made an error; the correct version is |

"Roger" is not "yes" and not "I will comply": it only acknowledges receipt. Do not say "Roger Wilco"; Wilco already includes Roger. Never answer a readback item with "Roger" alone.

### The readability scale

When you ask for a **radio check**, the reply grades how well you were heard on the **readability scale**:

| Scale | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 1 | Unreadable |
| 2 | Readable now and then |
| 3 | Readable but with difficulty |
| 4 | Readable |
| 5 | Perfectly readable |

??? check "Check yourself: the tower tells you to hold short of runway 24. Is \"Roger\" an acceptable reply?"
    No. A hold-short instruction must be read back in full, ending with your call-sign: "Hold short runway 24, Alpha Bravo Charlie". "Roger" only says you received something, not that you received it correctly.

## Clipped transmissions

A transmission is **clipped** when the first or last words are cut off. It happens when you start speaking before the press-to-talk (PTT) switch is fully pressed and the transmitter has come up to power, or when you release the PTT before you have finished the last word.

The words that get clipped are exactly the ones that matter most. The first words of a call are usually the **call-sign**; the last are often the **key word**: a runway number, "not", "hold short", your call-sign at the end of a readback.

Consequences:

- **The wrong aircraft acts on an instruction, or nobody does.** With the call-sign gone, two pilots may each assume it was theirs, or neither will.
- **The meaning reverses.** A lost "not" or "hold short" can turn a message into its opposite.
- **The frequency clogs** with every "say again".

Prevention is a habit: **press the PTT, pause about half a second, speak, finish, then release**. A **stuck microphone** is the opposite failure: the PTT stays keyed and blocks the frequency for everyone ([PAKC 3.3](/notes/PAKC/3.3)).

!!! warning "Common trap"
    If a clearance arrives with its call-sign clipped, do not assume it was yours, even if you were expecting it. Ask: "Station calling Alpha Bravo Charlie, say again". Acting on someone else's clearance is how two aeroplanes end up on the same runway.

## Bringing it together

Departing Northam, you listen on the CTAF before taxi, place a Cessna "joining crosswind runway 23", and broadcast your taxi call. Climbing out you call "Melbourne Centre, Cessna 172 Alpha Bravo Charlie, request SARTIME", wait for the answer, give your time as "zero tree tree zero", and read back the QNH: "QNH one zero one niner, Alpha Bravo Charlie". A moment later you hear "...harlie, traffic, Piper, 2 o'clock, 5 miles, same level". The call-sign is clipped, and a Kilo Lima Charlie is also on frequency, so you reply: "Station calling Alpha Bravo Charlie, say again".

## Key points

- **3.1.1 (a)** Use the phonetic alphabet for letters. Numbers are digit by digit with TREE, FIFE and NIN-er, except whole thousands and hundreds in altitudes, which use TOU-SAND and HUN-dred.
- **3.1.1 (b)** Registration without VH, type in front on first contact; abbreviate only after the ground station does.
- **3.1.1 (c)** Class G: listening watch on the area frequency or CTAF; broadcast to avoid collision; report to ATS for a service.
- **3.1.1 (d)** UTC on the 24-hour clock; normally the minutes only, the hour added if there is any doubt. WST is UTC + 8.
- **3.1.1 (e)** Listen before you transmit, build a traffic picture, process every call.
- **3.1.1 (f)** First call: station, call-sign, "request"; wait about 10 seconds before calling again. Read back the AIP GEN 3.4 items, ending with your call-sign. Roger is received; Wilco is will comply. Readability 1 to 5.
- **3.1.1 (g)** Clipping loses the call-sign or a key word. Press, pause, speak, release.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    A clipped call-sign is the classic trap: if a clearance arrives without a clear call-sign, do not assume it was yours. Ask "Station calling Alpha Bravo Charlie, say again". Have the readability scale by heart (3 is readable with difficulty, 5 perfectly readable), know that Wilco already includes Roger, and that altitudes in whole thousands and hundreds use "thousand" and "hundred" while everything else is digit by digit.
