## What the exam expects (PFRA 2.9)

- **2.9.1** Say what an aircraft means when it switches its navigation and landing lights on and off intermittently.
- **2.9.2** Tell an incident from an accident.
- **2.9.3** Work out what must be reported after an incident or accident, and how.
- **2.9.4** Explain what a SARTIME is and how you use one.
- **2.9.5** Name the document that holds the emergency procedures.

## Why this matters

These five elements are word for word the ones you met at RPL level, and the RPL lesson ([RFRC 2.7](/notes/RFRC/2.7)) remains the foundation. What changes with a PPL is how far from help you can be. An RPL holder flies near home; a PPL holder plans Jandakot to Kalbarri, Northam to Esperance, or a weekend out to Kalgoorlie and Laverton, over country where a forced landing could leave you a day's drive from anyone who knows you are there. In that country the SARTIME is not a box on a form: it is the thing that brings an aircraft overhead before your water runs out. This lesson revisits the definitions and reporting rules briefly, with new cases, and spends most of its time on what the PPL adds: choosing which flights need a SARTIME, lodging and changing it, how the search and rescue system responds when it expires, and what you carry so that a search finds you.

## Lights switched on and off: three meanings to tell apart

You are holding short at Geraldton when an aeroplane on a long final starts switching its **landing and navigation lights on and off**, again and again, without a word on the radio.

An aircraft that **uses its navigation and landing lights intermittently** is signalling that it is **in difficulty and compelled to land**, most often because its **radio has failed**, and it needs everyone else to **give way**. Your response is to stay clear of the runway and, if you are in the circuit, extend or leave the pattern to make room.

At PPL level you need to tell this signal from two others that also use lights, because the context decides the meaning:

- **Acknowledging a tower light signal at night:** a pilot flashes the landing lights in reply to a light signal from the tower (see the light signals in [RFRC 2.3](/notes/RFRC/2.3)). It is a brief reply to a signal, not a repeated pattern on approach.
- **Interception:** an intercepted aircraft switching all its lights on and off at **regular** intervals means it **cannot comply**; flashing them at **irregular** intervals means it is **in distress** (see [PFRA 2.8](/notes/PFRA/2.8)).

Read the situation: a single flash after a green from the tower is an acknowledgement; irregular, repeated switching on approach is a request for the runway; regular switching with a military aircraft alongside is a reply to an interceptor.

![What do lights switched on and off mean?](diagram:light-signals-compared "One flash after a tower signal acknowledges; repeated switching on approach asks for the runway; with an interceptor alongside, regular means cannot comply and irregular means distress.")

??? check "Check yourself: at night an aeroplane on final flashes its landing lights once, just after the tower has shown it a steady green. Is it in difficulty?"
    Probably not. A single flash in reply to a tower light signal is the night acknowledgement. The signal of an aircraft in difficulty and compelled to land is intermittent, repeated switching of the navigation and landing lights.

## Accident or incident

The first question after an occurrence is how serious it was, because that decides the reporting. The definitions, in the transport safety legislation as summarised in [RFRC 2.7](/notes/RFRC/2.7), turn on **outcomes**.

An **accident** involves **death or serious injury**, **serious structural damage** to the aircraft, or the aircraft being **missing**. It applies to an occurrence associated with the operation of the aircraft between the time someone boards with the intention of flight and the time everyone has disembarked. A useful way to test any occurrence is to ask three questions: what happened to the **people**, to the **airframe**, and to the aircraft's **whereabouts**? If none of the three answers is serious, it is not an accident.

Damage that is common, local and repairable does not by itself make an occurrence an accident: damage limited to the engine, propeller, wheels, tyres, brakes, fairings, or small dents and puncture holes in the skin.

An **incident** is **any other occurrence that affects or could affect safety**. The definition is wide on purpose, because incidents are where the lessons are cheap. A **serious incident** is a **near-accident**: one where an accident nearly occurred, such as a near collision, a runway incursion, or fuel exhaustion that ended without an accident.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    A dramatic event is not automatically an accident. An engine failure that ends in a successful landing in a paddock, with nobody hurt and no structural damage, is an incident. Test the outcome against the three accident questions (people, airframe, whereabouts), not against how frightening it was.

??? check "Check yourself: in severe turbulence a passenger who had loosened her belt hits the cabin roof and suffers a serious neck injury. The aeroplane is undamaged. Accident or incident?"
    An accident. A serious injury to a person on board, between boarding for flight and disembarking, meets the accident definition on its own; the aircraft does not need to be damaged.

## Reporting: from category to action

Once the category is settled, the reporting rule follows from it. Reports go to the **ATSB**, not CASA, because the ATSB investigates to prevent recurrence while CASA enforces the rules.

- **Accidents and serious incidents** (**immediately reportable matters**): report to the ATSB **as soon as practicable by phone**, and **in writing within 72 hours**. The ATSB's 24-hour line is **1800 011 034**.
- **Routine reportable matters**: **in writing within 72 hours**.
- **Defects** also go in the **maintenance release** and, where **airworthiness** is affected, to **CASA**.

![Accident or incident, and how is each one reported?](diagram:accident-incident-reporting "Accidents and serious incidents: phone the ATSB as soon as practicable, then write within 72 hours. Other safety occurrences: write within 72 hours.")

The full list of the pilot's duties, including who reports when the pilot cannot and preserving the site, is in [PFRA 2.7](/notes/PFRA/2.7).

!!! example "Worked example: determining what must be reported"
    Classify each occurrence, then apply the reporting rule.

    1. *Over the wheatbelt the engine stops from fuel exhaustion on final at Corrigin; you glide onto the runway. Nothing is damaged and nobody is hurt.* No accident outcome, but fuel exhaustion short of an accident: a **serious incident** and an **immediately reportable matter**. **Phone the ATSB as soon as practicable**, then the **written report within 72 hours**.
    2. *Taxiing at Northam, your wing tip touches a hangar door. The tip fairing is cracked; nothing else is damaged.* Damage limited to a fairing: **not an accident**. It affected safety, so it is an **incident**: a **written report within 72 hours**, the defect in the **maintenance release**.
    3. *Departing Kalgoorlie, a passenger door pops open on climb. You return and land normally.* No damage, no injury: an **incident**, **written report within 72 hours**.

    The method is always the same: outcome first, category second, reporting rule third.

??? check "Check yourself: why must an immediately reportable matter be phoned through as well as written up?"
    So the ATSB can start an investigation at once, while wreckage, weather records and witnesses' memories are fresh. The written report within 72 hours adds the detail; the phone call buys time.

## SARTIME at PPL level

### What it is, and what it buys you

A **SARTIME** (search and rescue time) is **a time, in UTC, that you nominate as the time by which you will report your arrival**. You lodge it with **Airservices**: through **NAIPS**, by **phone**, or by **radio**. From then on a system is waiting for you, and if the time passes without word from you, that system assumes you are in trouble.

The value of a SARTIME lies entirely in what it does when you cannot do anything. After a forced landing you may be injured, the radio may be damaged, and there may be no phone coverage for 100 km. None of that matters to the SARTIME: the clock keeps running, and when it runs out, the search starts.

![What happens if you do not cancel your SARTIME?](diagram:sartime-timeline "Nominate a time with a margin, extend it before it expires, cancel on landing; if it passes uncancelled, the alerting phases begin, ending in a physical search.")

### Which flights need one

The PPL question is which flights should carry a SARTIME: **anything over sparsely settled or inhospitable terrain**, **long over-water legs**, and **flights where nobody else would know you were missing**. In Western Australia that is most cross-country flying beyond the wheatbelt, the coast north of Geraldton, any crossing of the Great Southern towards Esperance, and the goldfields. The test is simple: if you went down halfway, who would notice, and when?

### Lodging and changing it

- **Before departure:** lodge it through **NAIPS** or by **phone to Airservices**.
- **In flight:** **amend it by radio to the FIS** (Flight Information Service).
- **If delayed:** **extend it before it expires**.
- **On arrival:** **cancel it**.

Choose the time with a **sensible margin after your planned arrival**: enough to absorb a stronger headwind or a short delay on the ground without setting off a search, but not so much that the search starts hours after you went down. Every minute of margin is a minute you would wait for help.

!!! example "Worked example: planning and managing a SARTIME for a remote leg"
    A private flight from Jandakot to Kalbarri, departing 0800 WST, with a planned flight time of 2 hours 50 minutes. Nobody will be waiting for you at Kalbarri.

    1. Planned arrival: $0800 + 2{:}50$ gives **1050 WST**.
    2. Add your margin, here 30 minutes: **1120 WST**.
    3. Convert to UTC (WST is UTC + 8; see [BAKC 2.3](/notes/BAKC/2.3)): $1120 - 8{:}00$ gives **0320 UTC**.
    4. Lodge a SARTIME of **0320 UTC** through **NAIPS** before departure.
    5. On landing at Kalbarri, **cancel it** straight away by phone.

    The 30 minutes is your choice as pilot in command, not a regulatory figure. The rules require that you extend before expiry and cancel on arrival.

Now try one. On the same flight, low cloud closes in north of Geraldton, so you divert and land at Geraldton at 1010 WST. What do you do about the SARTIME, and why does it matter even though you are safe?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    **Cancel it** (or amend it if you will continue later) as soon as you land at Geraldton, by phone or by radio before shutdown. If you do not, at 0320 UTC (1120 WST) the system starts the alerting phases for an aircraft it believes is somewhere between Geraldton and Kalbarri. Failing to cancel wastes search resources that may be needed by somebody who really is missing.

### The flight note alternative

For a **private VFR flight** the alternative is a **flight note** left with a **responsible person**, who contacts SAR if you do not arrive. The choice is yours as pilot in command. Weigh it honestly: a flight note works only if that person understands the plan, remembers the time, and acts promptly. A SARTIME is held by a system that does not forget and does not hesitate.

??? check "Check yourself: why is a SARTIME usually a better choice than a flight note for a flight over the goldfields?"
    Because a SARTIME is held by Airservices, which starts the alerting phases automatically when it expires. A flight note relies on one person remembering and acting. Over remote country, any delay in starting the search is time without water and shade.

## How the search and rescue system responds

When a SARTIME expires uncancelled, the response is staged, so that the effort grows only as the evidence that you are in trouble grows. The system moves through three **phases**: the **uncertainty phase**, the **alert phase** and the **distress phase**, with the **JRCC** (the Joint Rescue Coordination Centre of the Australian Maritime Safety Authority) coordinating.

As it moves through them, the actions escalate in the order you met at RPL level: first **attempts to contact you** (calls on frequency, a phone call to your destination), then a **communications search** that widens the net to aerodromes and contacts along your route, then a **physical search** by aircraft and ground parties. Each step is cheaper and quicker than the next, which is why the system tries them in that order, and why a pilot who simply forgot to cancel is usually found by the first one, at the cost of a lot of people's time.

![How does the SAR system respond to an uncancelled SARTIME?](diagram:sar-alerting-phases "Uncertainty, alert, distress: the effort grows step by step, from calls to you, to a communications search, to a physical search.")

The phases explain why the margin and the route matter so much. The search starts from what you told the system: your route and your time. A route that you flew but did not notify, or a SARTIME set hours too late, makes the search slower, longer, or in the wrong place.

## Being found: equipment and signals

A search is only as good as the chance of seeing you. What you carry turns a speck in the scrub into something a searcher can find. At PPL level the exam expects you to know the kit: an **ELT** (emergency locator transmitter), **water**, a **signalling mirror**, and the **ground-to-air visual signals in ERSA EMERG**. Flights over designated remote areas must carry the survival equipment prescribed in the Part 91 MOS (see [PFRA 2.4](/notes/PFRA/2.4)).

The standard advice after a forced landing is to **stay with the aircraft**: it is far easier to see from the air than a person on foot, it gives shade, and the search follows your notified route. Then work through the priorities in order: **protection** (first aid and shelter), **location** (help rescuers find you: activate the ELT or PLB, try 121.5 MHz, lay out signals), **water**, and only then **food**.

![After a forced landing, what comes first?](diagram:survival-priorities "Protection, location, water, food; stay with the aircraft and lay out the ground-to-air signals: V assistance, X medical assistance, N no, Y yes, an arrow for the direction you are going.")

The ground-to-air signals are large shapes laid out on the ground with whatever contrasts with it: a **V** means you **require assistance**, an **X** that you **require medical assistance**, **N** is **no**, **Y** is **yes**, and an **arrow** says you are **proceeding in this direction**. They work because a searcher overhead cannot hear you but can read a large, high-contrast shape on the ground.

## Where the emergency procedures are

In flight the emergency procedures are in the **ERSA EMERG section**, the pink pages, with the **full text in AIP GEN 3.6 (search and rescue) and ENR 1.12 (interception)**. ERSA is the one to answer with: it is the document you carry, laid out for use with one hand while you fly the aeroplane. It holds the distress and urgency calls, the transponder codes, the radio failure procedures, interception, light signals, ditching and survival, and the SAR material including the ground-to-air signals. How to use it in flight is the subject of [PFRA 2.7](/notes/PFRA/2.7).

![Which transponder code means what?](diagram:transponder-emergency-codes "7700 emergency, 7600 radio failure, 7500 unlawful interference: all in ERSA EMERG with the rest of the emergency procedures.")

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The AIP contains the full text, but the question "which document contains the emergency procedures?" wants **ERSA EMERG**, the in-flight reference. Do not pick the AIP GEN section, the VFRG or the VTC legend when ERSA is offered.

## Bringing it together

A May weekend: Northam to Leonora and back. Nobody lives at the strip and nobody is expecting you, so you lodge a SARTIME through NAIPS with a margin after your planned arrival, in UTC, and put the ERSA with its pink pages in the seat pocket along with water, the ELT and a signalling mirror. Near Menzies a stronger headwind than forecast eats into your margin, so you call the FIS and extend the SARTIME before it expires. Approaching Leonora, the radio fails. You squawk 7600 from ERSA EMERG and, seeing another aeroplane on the ground at the holding point, you switch your landing and navigation lights on and off repeatedly on final so that it holds clear. On rollout a tyre bursts. Nobody is hurt, and damage limited to a tyre is not an accident, so it is an incident: a written report to the ATSB within 72 hours and the defect in the maintenance release. Before any of that, with the radio dead, you borrow the other pilot's phone and cancel the SARTIME, because until you do, the system is counting down to the uncertainty phase for an aeroplane that is sitting safely on the apron.

??? check "Check yourself: in that flight, which two actions involving the SARTIME were essential, and why?"
    Extending it before it expired when the headwind delayed you, and cancelling it on landing. Either omission would have set off the alerting phases and a search for an aircraft that was not missing.

## Key points

- **2.9.1** Navigation and landing lights used intermittently: in difficulty and compelled to land, usually after a radio failure; give way. Distinguish a single flash acknowledging a tower signal, and the interception replies (regular: cannot comply; irregular: in distress).
- **2.9.2** Accident: death or serious injury, serious structural damage, or the aircraft missing, from boarding for flight to disembarking; damage limited to engine, propeller, wheels, tyres, brakes, fairings or small dents is not by itself an accident. Incident: anything else affecting safety; a serious incident is a near-accident.
- **2.9.3** To the ATSB: accidents and serious incidents by phone as soon as practicable (1800 011 034) and in writing within 72 hours; routine reportable matters in writing within 72 hours. Defects in the maintenance release, and to CASA where airworthiness is affected.
- **2.9.4** SARTIME: a UTC time by which you will report arrival, lodged with Airservices (NAIPS or phone before departure, radio to the FIS in flight); extend before expiry, cancel on arrival. Uncancelled: uncertainty, alert and distress phases, the JRCC coordinating the search. Use it over remote country, long over-water legs and when nobody else would miss you; the alternative is a flight note. Carry an ELT, water, a mirror; stay with the aircraft; know the ground-to-air signals.
- **2.9.5** Emergency procedures: ERSA EMERG, with the full text in AIP GEN 3.6 (search and rescue) and ENR 1.12 (interception).

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    The same five facts as the RPL exam: lights flashing means compelled to land; accident needs death, serious injury or serious damage; ATSB immediately and within 72 hours; SARTIME cancelled on arrival; procedures in ERSA EMERG. At PPL level add the SAR phases (uncertainty, alert, distress, with the JRCC coordinating), how to lodge and amend a SARTIME, and the regular and irregular light signals of an intercepted aircraft. Verify the reporting rules and the ATSB number against the current Transport Safety Investigation Regulations and ERSA.
