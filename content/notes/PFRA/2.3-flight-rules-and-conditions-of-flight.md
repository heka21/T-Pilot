## What the exam expects (PFRA 2.3)

- **2.3.1** Say which documents have to be on board for a flight in Australian airspace.
- **2.3.2** Apply the rules on (a) carrying and discharging firearms, (b) aerodromes where operations are not confined to the runways, and (c) flying in prohibited, restricted and danger (PRD) areas.
- **2.3.3** Give examples of situations that call for the "SECURITE" prefix before a radio call.

## Why this matters

Everything you learned in [RFRC 2.3](/notes/RFRC/2.3) still applies: the rules of the air, the VMC minima, altimetry, light signals, minimum heights, aerobatic limits and the daylight rule. The PPL exam assumes you know them and tests them in longer scenarios, so revise that lesson alongside this one. What the PPL adds comes from the flying the licence opens up. You will fly aeroplanes that are not your club's, so you need to know what paperwork the aeroplane and you must carry. A farmer near Merredin may ask you to fly him and his rifle to his outstation. An unfamiliar country strip may let you land anywhere on the field, or may not. Your track from Jandakot to Geraldton runs past the restricted areas around RAAF Pearce and the Lancelin defence training area. And on the CTAF you may need to warn everyone at once about kangaroos on the runway without declaring an emergency. The exam asks these as short "what must you do?" questions, with distractors that sound reasonable. This lesson gives you the reason behind each rule so you can pick out the right answer.

## The documents on board

Imagine landing at an unfamiliar aerodrome where a CASA inspector asks to see the aeroplane's papers and your licence. Or picture something more ordinary: halfway to Esperance the oil temperature creeps up and you want to check the limit, or the destination's runway has changed and you want the ERSA entry. Both cases come down to the same question. What has to be in the aeroplane so that you can show it is legal and fly it safely?

The rule follows that logic. Some documents tell you how to fly this aeroplane within its limits. Some show the aeroplane is fit to fly today. Some describe the airspace and aerodromes you are flying through. Some show who you are and that you are qualified. For a private VFR flight the pilot in command must make sure these are on board and **accessible**, meaning you can reach them when you need them in flight, not that they are in a bag in the baggage locker:

- **The aircraft flight manual** (the POH), or the approved equivalent, with its current amendments and the loading data. The limitations and procedures are then available in flight: the oil temperature limit, the emergency checklist, the weight and balance figures.
- **The maintenance release**, or the operator's equivalent document. It must be current and show that today's daily inspection is done. This is the aeroplane's evidence that it is serviceable for this flight.
- **Current aeronautical information** for the flight: charts, ERSA and the relevant AIP information, on paper or in an approved electronic form. "Current" matters. An ERSA superseded at the last AIRAC date may show a frequency or a runway length that has changed (see [PFRA 2.1](/notes/PFRA/2.1)).
- **Your licence, medical certificate and photo identification** (CASR 61.335), unless your licence is held electronically in a form CASA accepts. These show that the person flying is qualified to fly.
- **Any approval or permission the flight relies on**: a special flight permit, the clearance letter for a PRD area, or an approval for a particular operation. If the flight is only legal because of a piece of paper, that paper comes with you.

![Which documents show the aeroplane is serviceable today?](diagram:airworthiness-documents-flow "The maintenance release is the one you check before every flight: current in hours and date, daily inspection signed, no endorsed defect that grounds the aircraft.")

Two documents cause confusion: the **certificate of registration** and the **certificate of airworthiness**. They belong to the aeroplane rather than to any one flight. Whether they must be carried has changed with the flight operations regulations, so check CASR 91.105 (documents to be carried) for the current list before the exam. In practice a training or hire aeroplane carries a document folder with all of them, and the habit worth building is to open the folder and look rather than trust that it is complete.

!!! example "Worked example: is the paperwork ready for a hire aeroplane?"
    You are hiring a syndicate's aeroplane at Jandakot for a private flight to Kalbarri on Saturday. On Friday evening you go through what will be on board.

    1. The **POH** is in the seat pocket. You check the amendment record against the owner's list and find the **loading data** sheet on the last page. **Present.**
    2. The **maintenance release** is in the folder. The hours to the next service exceed the flight time and the date has not expired. You will sign the **daily inspection** on Saturday morning before flight. **Present; daily to be completed.**
    3. **Charts and ERSA**: your tablet app has updated to the current AIRAC cycle, and the app is an approved electronic form. **Present.**
    4. Your **licence and medical** are in your wallet with your **photo ID**. **Present.**
    5. **Approvals**: the flight is private, through Class G and the Perth control zone with a normal clearance. No special permit or PRD clearance letter is needed. **None required.**

    The folder also holds the certificates of registration and airworthiness, so whatever the current CASR 91.105 list says, they are covered. The flight's paperwork is complete once the daily inspection is signed.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    "On board" is not enough: the documents must be **accessible**. A POH zipped into a bag in the baggage locker is no help when you need the emergency checklist at 3,000 ft. And do not answer the certificates question from memory of older rules or older textbooks: the current CASR 91.105 list is the one the exam is set against.

??? check "Check yourself: why must the maintenance release be on board, rather than kept in the hangar office?"
    It is the evidence that the aeroplane is serviceable for this flight: maintenance current, daily inspection done and no grounding defects. You may need to read it or endorse it away from base, for example to record a defect found at a country aerodrome. A copy in the hangar is no use there.

## Firearms in the aeroplane

A firearm in a small cabin creates two risks. One is the obvious danger of a discharge in a confined space with fuel lines, control cables and people inches away. The other is security: a weapon that someone could reach in flight. The rules deal with both.

**Carriage.** A firearm must not be carried in an aircraft unless the regulations permit it. In broad terms that means:

- the **pilot in command knows about it and consents**;
- it is **unloaded**;
- it is **stowed so that it cannot be reached in flight**;
- the carriage **complies with the aviation security laws and the state firearms laws**.

Each condition closes off one risk. Your consent means nothing comes on board without your knowledge, and as pilot in command you are responsible for everything carried. Unloaded means a knock or a hard landing cannot fire it. Stowed out of reach means nobody can use it in flight. The security and firearms laws apply in the air as they do on the ground, so a licence to own the rifle does not lapse when you climb into the aeroplane.

**Discharge.** Firing a firearm from an aircraft is **prohibited**, except under a **CASA approval** for a specific purpose, such as feral animal control under an approved operation. The prohibition is absolute for a private flight. A pilot who sees a fox or a feral goat from 500 ft over a station has no authority to let a passenger shoot at it, however helpful the station owner would find it.

The provisions are in CASR Part 91 and the Aviation Transport Security Act, and the Part 91 plain English guide summarises them (verify current regulation numbers).

!!! example "Worked example: a rifle for the station"
    A grazier near Mullewa asks you to fly him to his outstation in your aeroplane. He wants to bring his licensed rifle to deal with wild dogs on the property, and he mentions that the dogs are sometimes visible from the air.

    1. **Consent**: he has told you, and you can decide whether to accept it. As pilot in command it is your decision. **Your call.**
    2. **Unloaded**: you ask him to show you the chamber and magazine are empty before it comes on board. **Required.**
    3. **Out of reach**: the rifle goes in its case, secured in the baggage area behind the rear seats where nobody can reach it in flight. **Required.**
    4. **Other laws**: carrying it must comply with the WA firearms laws and the aviation security laws. Being in an aeroplane suspends neither. **Both apply.**
    5. **Discharge**: if dogs are visible from the air, nobody fires from the aeroplane. That would need a CASA approval for a specific operation, and a private flight has none. **Prohibited.**

    You may carry the rifle under conditions 1 to 4, and you tell him before departure that it stays in its case until you have landed.

??? check "Check yourself: a passenger shows you a rifle in a soft case and says it is unloaded. What must you still satisfy yourself of before departure?"
    That it is actually unloaded, that it is stowed where nobody can reach it in flight, and that its carriage complies with the security and state firearms laws. Your consent as pilot in command is one condition of carriage, and each of the others must also be met.

## Aerodromes where operations are not restricted to runways

At most aerodromes you take off and land on a runway, and only on a runway. The reason is that the runway is the surface the aerodrome operator has prepared, inspected and declared: its length, strength, slope and obstacle clearances are known. The grass between two runways may look smooth from the circuit, but it may hide a drain, a soft patch, a sprinkler head or a gopher hole. Nobody has declared it fit for an aeroplane.

Some aerodromes, usually grass fields, are different. The operator has prepared and maintained the **whole landing area**, and the ERSA entry says so: operations are **not restricted to runways**. At such an aerodrome you may use any part of the landing area, choosing your take-off or landing path **into wind** within the marked area. The advantage is that you can always line up with the wind, which matters when a strong crosswind blows across the only marked strip.

The rule has two sides:

- **Where ERSA says operations are not restricted to runways**, the whole landing area may be used, within its marked boundaries.
- **Where ERSA does not say so**, operations are **confined to the runways**, and landing on the grass between them is an offence.

The default is the restrictive one. Silence in ERSA means runways only, and permission must be stated positively. That is how you protect yourself at an aerodrome you have never seen: you never assume a surface is usable because it looks usable.

How do you find the boundaries of the usable area? From the **aerodrome markings**. **Cone and gable markers** define the usable area: cones mark the edges of the landing area, gables the boundary of the strip around it. You will meet both in detail in [PFRA 2.5](/notes/PFRA/2.5). Read the ERSA entry before you go and confirm it from overhead before you land.

![Where are the edges of the usable area on an unsealed aerodrome?](diagram:runway-markings-and-markers "On the unsealed field, cones mark the edges of the landing area and gables the strip boundary; land inside the cones.")

!!! warning "Common trap"
    "Not restricted to runways" does not mean you can land anywhere on the aerodrome property. It means anywhere within the **marked landing area**. The fence line, the apron and the area beyond the gables are still not landing surfaces, and the ground signals of the day (such as a white dumbbell, which keeps you to the sealed surfaces) still apply.

??? check "Check yourself: an ERSA entry for a grass aerodrome lists two runways and says nothing about operations off them. With a strong wind blowing across both, may you land diagonally across the field into wind?"
    No. Without a positive statement in ERSA that operations are not restricted to runways, operations are confined to the runways. You either land on the runway with the better crosswind component, if it is within your limits, or go elsewhere.

## Flying in PRD areas

Some airspace is set aside because of what happens inside it: live firing, weapons testing, military jets at low level, parachuting, or a site that must not be overflown. These areas come in three kinds, and they are graded by **how much the activity inside conflicts with your flight**. You met them at RPL level in [RFRC 2.6](/notes/RFRC/2.6). Here you need to apply them when planning.

**Prohibited area.** Flight is **not permitted at any time**. The activity, or the thing being protected, cannot share the airspace with anyone. Australia has only a few.

**Restricted area.** When it is **active**, you must **not enter without a clearance** from the **controlling authority**. ATC may issue it, or the authority listed in ERSA. Once cleared, you must comply with its conditions. The **RA1, RA2 and RA3** status in the ERSA PRD section and AIP ENR 5.1 tells you how likely a clearance is, so you can judge before you plan a route through it. When the area is **inactive** it is treated as **Class G** (or the class of the surrounding airspace). The logic is that the restriction exists for the activity. With no activity there is nothing to protect, and the airspace reverts.

**Danger area.** **No clearance is required**, but a hazard exists inside. You should **avoid it or take care**, and you **must not interfere with the activity**. In Australia danger areas are **not prohibited to civil aircraft**, unlike in some countries. Responsibility for deciding whether to go in rests with you, so you find out what the activity is, when it runs and at what heights, and then decide.

![How do you find out whether an area is active?](diagram:airspace-active-check "Chart for the boundaries, ERSA PRD and ENR 5.1 for the hours and the controlling authority, NOTAMs for changes; if in doubt, ask.")

Going into an active restricted area without a clearance is a serious breach and a real safety risk: you could meet live firing, military traffic or parachutists. So plan with a **buffer**, not just a track that grazes the boundary, and **check the NOTAMs**, because a NOTAM can activate an area, change its hours or create a temporary one.

![Which document tells you what about a PRD area?](diagram:flight-documents-flow "The chart gives the identifier and vertical limits; ERSA PRD and ENR 5.1 give hours and controlling authority; NOTAMs change them.")

!!! example "Worked example: planning past a restricted area"
    You plan a private flight from Jandakot north to Geraldton, and the direct track crosses a restricted area along the coast.

    1. **Chart**: the VNC shows the area's identifier and its vertical limits, from the surface to well above your planned cruising level. **It affects your route.**
    2. **ERSA PRD and ENR 5.1**: the hours of activity are shown as "by NOTAM", and the entry names the controlling authority and the area's RA status. **Hours depend on NOTAMs.**
    3. **NOTAMs**: a current NOTAM activates the area for the whole of Saturday. **Active for your flight.**
    4. **Decision**: with an active restricted area you need a clearance to enter. You can ask for one, and the RA status tells you how likely it is, or you can plan around the boundary with a buffer. You choose a track that keeps several miles clear and add the extra distance to your fuel plan. **Route around, buffer included.**

    If no NOTAM had activated the area, it would have been treated as the surrounding airspace on Saturday, and the direct track would have been legal.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The three areas get mixed up because of their names. A "danger" area sounds the most forbidding but is the only one you may enter **without a clearance**. "Restricted" means **clearance when active**. "Prohibited" means **never**. Ask what each one requires of you and the order sorts itself out.

??? check "Check yourself: a restricted area on your track is shown in ERSA as active only by NOTAM, and no NOTAM covers your flight time. How is it treated?"
    As inactive, which means it is treated as Class G or as the surrounding airspace class, and you may fly through under that class's rules. The restriction exists for an activity, and without a NOTAM activating the area there is no activity to protect.

## The SECURITE prefix

On the radio you already know two prefixes that cut through routine traffic: **MAYDAY** for grave and imminent danger needing immediate help, and **PAN PAN** for an urgent condition that is not yet distress (see [PAKC 3.7](/notes/PAKC/3.7)). Both say "this is about me". Some messages are about a hazard to everyone else: an obstacle on the runway, a thunderstorm on the approach, a dust storm sweeping across the aerodrome. They need everyone listening to pay attention now, but nobody is in danger yet. That is what the third prefix is for.

A radio call prefixed with **"SECURITE, SECURITE, SECURITE (pronounced say-cure-ee-tay; AIP GEN 3.4, verify current text)"**, the ICAO **safety signal**, tells everyone on frequency that the message which follows concerns the **safety of navigation** or an **important meteorological warning**, without being a distress or urgency call (AIP GEN 3.4). It is used by **pilots** and by **ATS** whenever the content affects other aircraft.

![Which prefix fits which message?](diagram:security-prefix-ladder "MAYDAY and PAN PAN are about your own aircraft; SECURITE warns everyone else about a hazard that has not yet harmed anyone.")

The examples fall into three groups.

**A pilot reports a hazard to navigation.** An unlit crane or tower, a tethered balloon, a flock of birds on the runway, a vehicle or animals on the runway, an unserviceable runway light, a kite or a drone, a parachute drop in progress. In each case something physical is in the way, and other aircraft need to know where it is before they meet it.

**A pilot broadcasts a hazardous weather observation** that other aircraft need at once: a thunderstorm or microburst on the approach, severe turbulence or wind shear reported on final, a dust storm crossing the aerodrome, a sudden fog bank. Forecasts take time to update, and the pilot who has just flown through it is the best and fastest source.

**ATS broadcasts a safety message**, such as a **runway becoming unserviceable** or **an unusual activity** affecting traffic.

The structure is simple: **the prefix three times**, **the station called** (or "all stations"), **your callsign**, then **the message**.

!!! example "Worked example: making a SECURITE broadcast"
    You have just landed at a country aerodrome in the Wheatbelt on runway 27. As you vacate, a mob of kangaroos moves onto the far half of the runway. Another aeroplane called inbound on the CTAF a few minutes ago.

    1. **Is it distress or urgency?** You are safe on the taxiway, so it is **neither**. MAYDAY and PAN PAN are wrong.
    2. **Does it affect the safety of other aircraft?** Yes: animals on the runway are a **hazard to navigation** for the aircraft about to land. **SECURITE applies.**
    3. **Who needs it?** Everyone on the CTAF, so the station called is **all stations**.
    4. **Compose it**: "SECURITE, SECURITE, SECURITE, all stations [aerodrome name], [your callsign], kangaroos on runway 27, western half, [aerodrome name]."

    The inbound pilot now knows to look before committing to land, and to go around if the animals are still there.

Try one yourself before opening the solution. Departing a coastal aerodrome, you climb through 1,500 ft and meet severe turbulence and a sudden loss of 15 knots of airspeed. You recover and climb away safely. Is a SECURITE call right, and what goes in it?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    Yes. It is a hazardous weather observation, wind shear and severe turbulence on the departure path, that other aircraft need at once, and you are not in distress or urgency. "SECURITE, SECURITE, SECURITE, all stations [aerodrome name], [your callsign], severe turbulence and wind shear on climb-out runway [number], loss of 15 knots passing 1,500 ft, [aerodrome name]."

The SECURITE prefix is **not** for a routine traffic call. "Turning base, runway 27" is ordinary CTAF traffic. Nor should it be confused with MAYDAY or PAN PAN. If the hazard has already put you in difficulty, for example if you have hit a bird and the windscreen is cracked, your message is about your own aircraft and needs PAN PAN or MAYDAY. A SECURITE call warns others about a hazard that has not yet harmed anyone.

??? check "Check yourself: why is a report of an unlit crane near the approach a SECURITE call and not a PAN PAN?"
    The crane is a hazard to the navigation of other aircraft, but nobody's aircraft is in an urgent condition. PAN PAN signals urgency about a particular aircraft; SECURITE signals a safety-of-navigation or weather warning for everyone listening.

## Bringing it together

A Sunday trip from Jandakot to a grass farm strip east of Moora, with your friend and his shotgun for a weekend's pest control on the property. On Saturday night you check the folder: the POH with loading data, the maintenance release with hours and date current, today's charts and ERSA on the tablet, your licence, medical and photo ID. On the apron your friend shows you that the shotgun is unloaded, and it goes in its case in the baggage area, out of reach. You remind him nobody shoots from the aeroplane. The planned track passes a restricted area. ERSA says it is active by NOTAM, the NOTAMs show nothing for Sunday, and it is treated as the surrounding Class G, though you still keep a buffer from the boundary in case of a late change. On the way you stop for fuel at a registered aerodrome whose ERSA entry does not say operations are not restricted to runways, so the diagonal across the grass you might have used in the gusty north-easterly is off limits and you land on the runway with the better component. As you vacate, a flock of galahs settles on the threshold, and with a Cessna calling inbound you make a SECURITE broadcast to all stations. None of these needed a tower or an emergency, but each one needed you to know the rule.

??? check "Check yourself: which of today's decisions depended on reading ERSA, and what did ERSA tell you each time?"
    Two. For the restricted area, ERSA gave the hours (by NOTAM) and the controlling authority, so you checked the NOTAMs. For the fuel stop, ERSA's silence on non-runway operations meant runways only, so you could not land across the field into wind.

## Key points

- **2.3.1** On board and accessible: the flight manual with loading data, the current maintenance release, current charts, ERSA and AIP information, your licence, medical and photo ID (CASR 61.335), and any approval the flight relies on. Check CASR 91.105 for whether the certificates of registration and airworthiness must also be carried.
- **2.3.2** Firearms: carried only with the pilot in command's knowledge and consent, unloaded, stowed out of reach in flight, and within the security and firearms laws; never discharged without a CASA approval. Non-runway operations: only where ERSA positively says so, within the marked area, into wind; otherwise runways only. PRD: prohibited, never; restricted, clearance when active and Class G (or the surrounding class) when inactive; danger, no clearance but avoid or take care and do not interfere.
- **2.3.3** "SECURITE" three times, then station, callsign and message: hazards to navigation (obstacles, animals, birds, unserviceable lights, drones, parachuting) and urgent weather observations (thunderstorm, microburst, wind shear, dust storm, fog), from pilots or ATS. Not for routine calls, and not for your own emergency.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Danger areas may be entered; restricted areas need a clearance when active; prohibited areas never. The SECURITE prefix is for hazards to navigation and weather warnings, not for emergencies. For documents, think in four groups: how to fly the aeroplane (flight manual), whether it is fit to fly (maintenance release), where you are going (current charts and ERSA), and who you are (licence, medical and photo ID). For firearms and non-runway operations, the default is "no" and permission must be explicit. Verify the current Part 91 regulation numbers, especially CASR 91.105, before the exam.
