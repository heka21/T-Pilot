## What the exam expects (PFRA 2.6)

- **2.6.1** Tell the classes of airspace apart by what each gives a VFR flight and what each requires of it.
- **2.6.2** Explain FIR, FIA and OCTA; CTA and CTR; radio reports and broadcasts; VFR routes and lanes of entry; PRD areas; CTAF areas; and controlled aerodromes, and find their boundaries and vertical limits on the charts or in ERSA.
- **2.6.3** Use the VFR tracking tolerances to plan and fly clear of controlled airspace.
- **2.6.4** Know what is required, and what you do, in each class of airspace and when operating at a licensed aerodrome or a CTAF(R).
- **2.6.5** Altimetry: the datum the altimeter measures from with area QNH, local QNH, QFE or the standard setting; the transition altitude and layer procedures on climb and descent; deriving the transition layer from the area QNH; finding local QNH with the altimeter; the error from a wrong subscale setting; height, altitude and flight level; and the ISA sea-level temperature and pressure lapse rate.

## Why this matters

As an RPL holder you learned to stay out of controlled airspace (see [RFRC 2.6](/notes/RFRC/2.6)). As a PPL holder with the controlled airspace endorsement you will go into it: out of Jandakot's Class D, under or through Perth's Class C steps, and on long flights to Kalgoorlie or Geraldton where the area frequency is your only company. You need the controllers' vocabulary, the ability to read a boundary off a chart, a track that does not graze a step, and a correctly set altimeter, because every vertical boundary is only as accurate as your subscale. The exam tests extraction (read a label, find a limit), definitions (report or broadcast, CTR or CTA) and one calculation: the error from a wrong subscale setting. Every regulatory figure here is from the AIP, Part 91 MOS and VFRG as the source note recorded them; verify each against the current documents, because airspace rules and boundaries change.

## The classes: what each one gives you and asks of you

In **controlled airspace** a controller issues clearances and keeps some or all traffic apart; in **non-controlled airspace** you separate yourself by looking out and talking. At PPL level the exam compares the classes on four questions: what **service** you get, whether you need a **clearance**, who keeps you apart from whom (**separation**), and what you must **carry**.

| Class | Service for VFR | Clearance | Separation provided to VFR | Radio and transponder |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | Not available to VFR | | | |
| C | Air traffic control | Required | From IFR; traffic information about other VFR | VHF radio and transponder required |
| D | Air traffic control | Required | Traffic information (ATC separates IFR from IFR, and IFR from VFR on the runway) | VHF radio required, transponder where fitted |
| E | Flight information and traffic information where possible | Not required for VFR | None; IFR is separated from IFR only | VHF radio recommended; transponder required in Class E below the published level (check the current rule) |
| G | Flight information service and traffic information on request | Not required | None: see and avoid, broadcast and listen | Radio required above 5,000 ft and at certified or registered aerodromes and CTAF(R); transponder recommended |

*Source: AIP ENR 1.4 and ENR 1.1, as recorded in the source note; verify against the current AIP.*

Read the table as a sliding scale: the more ATC does for you, the more it needs from you.

**Class A** is high-level airspace for jets, all IFR: see and avoid at closing speeds of 900 knots does not work, so VFR is not available.

**Class C**, around Perth and in the steps above it, mixes you with fast IFR traffic, so ATC **separates you from IFR** and gives **traffic information about other VFR**. To do that it must see you on radar and talk to you: hence **VHF radio and transponder required**.

**Class D** surrounds towers such as Jandakot, where traffic is mostly slow VFR in the circuit. The tower gives **traffic information** and sequences you, but separates only IFR from IFR, plus everyone **on the runway**; spacing behind the traffic you were told about is your job. A **radio is required**; the transponder **where fitted**, because the tower works mainly by eye.

**Class E** is controlled for IFR only: **no clearance** and **no separation** for VFR. The radio is **recommended** so that you hear the IFR traffic, and the transponder makes you visible to the controller separating it.

**Class G** is the only non-controlled class: see and avoid, broadcast and listen, information **on request**. The radio is required **above 5,000 ft** and at **certified or registered aerodromes** (the former CTAF(R)).

Two limits apply on top of the table: a speed limit of **250 kt IAS below 10,000 ft** in all classes, **and 200 kt in Class D** unless ATC approves otherwise (AIP ENR 1.4; verify current text). And the VMC criteria differ between low-level Class G and everywhere else, as you met in [RFRC 2.3](/notes/RFRC/2.3).

![How do controlled and non-controlled airspace differ?](diagram:controlled-vs-non-controlled-at-a-glance "In controlled airspace ATC clears you and keeps some traffic apart; in Class G there is no clearance and separation is yours.")

![Which VMC minima apply in which class?](diagram:vmc-minima-by-airspace "Class C, D and E, and Class G up high: 1,500 m and 1,000 ft from cloud. Low in Class G: clear of cloud and in sight of the ground.")

!!! warning "Common trap"
    "Controlled" does not mean "ATC separates me from everyone". In Class D, ATC does not separate VFR from VFR in the air, and in Class E a VFR flight gets no separation at all, yet both are controlled airspace. Match the class to the separation column, not to the word "controlled".

??? check "Check yourself: you are VFR in Class E at 8,500 ft. What does ATC do for you, and why do you still listen on the frequency?"
    No clearance and no separation: Class E is controlled for IFR only, so at most you get flight and traffic information. You listen because the IFR traffic around you is on that frequency, and hearing it helps you see and avoid it.

## The language of the airspace system

Each term names a piece of a structure you can picture; start with the biggest and work down.

### FIR, FIA and OCTA

The whole of Australia's airspace, and a great deal of ocean around it, is split into two **flight information regions (FIRs)**: the **Melbourne FIR** and the **Brisbane FIR**. A flight information region is the volume within which a given air traffic services centre provides flight information and alerting. **Western Australia is in the Melbourne FIR.**

A region that size is too big for one frequency, so it is divided into **flight information areas (FIAs)**, each with its own **area VHF frequency**, drawn on the **ERC-L and the VNC** as frequency boundaries. Flying from Perth towards Kalgoorlie in Class G you change area frequency at each FIA boundary, to stay with the IFR traffic and Flight Information for your area.

**OCTA** means **outside controlled airspace**: Class G. "OCTA" in a clearance or a report tells the controller you are, or will be, in Class G.

### CTA and CTR

A **control area (CTA)** is controlled airspace **above a lower limit**: a "step", labelled on the chart with its class and its lower limit, such as **"C LL 4500"** or **"LL FL125"**. A **control zone (CTR)** is controlled airspace **from the surface** around a controlled aerodrome, so that aircraft taking off and landing there are inside it. Both are shown on the **VTC, VNC, ERC-L and TAC** with class and limits; the ERSA entry for the aerodrome gives the tower's hours.

![How do control zones and control area steps fit together?](diagram:airspace-cross-section "A CTR starts at the surface; each CTA step starts at a lower limit that rises with distance, with Class G beneath it.")

### Reports and broadcasts

A **report** is a transmission **to air traffic services**, such as a position report in controlled airspace or a request, and expects a response. A **broadcast** goes **to all stations** on the frequency with **no reply expected**: the CTAF calls, an "all stations" call in Class G. The logic follows the airspace: in Class G nobody controls you, so you **broadcast** for other pilots; in Class C and D a controller is responsible for you, so you **report** and are answered.

??? check "Check yourself: \"Traffic Northam, Cherokee ABC, five miles south-west, inbound, three thousand five hundred, joining crosswind runway 21, Traffic Northam.\" Report or broadcast, and why?"
    A broadcast: addressed to all traffic on the CTAF, with no reply expected and nobody controlling the Cherokee.

## Finding it on the chart: routes, lanes, areas and aerodromes

The remaining terms in 2.6.2 are features you find on a chart and look up in ERSA; none is visible out of the window.

**VFR routes and lanes of entry.** **VFR routes** are published tracks on the VTC that let VFR aircraft transit near or under controlled airspace without a clearance, with **altitude limits** and **reporting points**; around Perth there are coastal and inland routes and lanes into Jandakot. A **lane of entry** is a **Class G corridor through controlled airspace** with a strict altitude band: stay **inside the lane**, at the **published altitude**, and make the **published broadcasts**. Controlled airspace lies on both sides and often above, so wandering off the line is an infringement.

**PRD areas.** Prohibited, restricted and danger areas are identified on the charts and detailed in the **ERSA PRD section** and **AIP ENR 5.1** (see [PFRA 2.3](/notes/PFRA/2.3) and the activity check in [RFRC 2.6](/notes/RFRC/2.6)). Chart for identifier and limits, ERSA for hours and controlling authority, NOTAMs for changes.

![How do you find out whether a PRD area is active?](diagram:airspace-active-check "Chart for where, ERSA for when and who, NOTAM for today, and a call if still unsure.")

**CTAF areas.** Around a non-controlled aerodrome, everyone uses the **common traffic advisory frequency (CTAF)** published in ERSA, or **126.7 MHz** where none is specified. You use it **within 10 nm** of the aerodrome at a height where you could conflict with circuit traffic. **CTAF(R)** was the former designation for aerodromes where radio was mandatory; the current rule makes radio mandatory at all **certified and registered aerodromes**, so treat every CTAF with an ERSA entry as radio-required (Part 91 MOS; verify current text).

**Controlled aerodromes.** An aerodrome with a tower, inside a control zone: **taxi, take-off and landing need a clearance**, the **ATIS** gives runway and conditions, the **ERSA entry** gives tower hours and local procedures. Outside tower hours the zone reverts to Class G (or E above) and the aerodrome becomes a CTAF aerodrome.

![What happens to a controlled aerodrome when the tower closes?](diagram:class-d-tower-hours "Outside the ERSA tower hours the zone becomes Class G (or E above) and the aerodrome a CTAF aerodrome.")

### Reading vertical limits

On the VTC a step labelled **"C LL 2500"** means **Class C from 2,500 ft up**; a control zone from the surface is shown **"SFC"**, and ERSA gives the same in words. Two habits prevent most misreadings. A lower limit is the **bottom of the controlled airspace**: you are in Class G only while **below** it. And a limit written as a flight level (**"LL FL125"**) is a pressure level on 1013, not an altitude on QNH.

![Which chart shows which features?](diagram:chart-features-panel "The VTC and VNC show control zones, CTA steps, PRD areas and VFR routes; the WAC shows no airspace.")

!!! example "Worked example: extracting limits along a planned track"
    You plan a VFR flight that passes under two CTA steps and beside a restricted area. On the VTC, suppose you read:

    - Step 1: label **C LL 2500**.
    - Step 2, further out: label **C LL 3500**.
    - A restricted area beside your track, with vertical limits **SFC to 3000**.

    1. Step 1 begins at 2,500 ft, so below it you are in Class G up to but not including 2,500 ft: **your ceiling under step 1 is below 2,500 ft.**
    2. Step 2 begins at 3,500 ft: **your ceiling under step 2 is below 3,500 ft.** The ceiling rises as you get further from the airport, as the steps do.
    3. The restricted area runs from the surface to 3,000 ft. Look up its identifier in the ERSA PRD section for its hours, then the NOTAMs. If it is active and you have no clearance, **you must stay clear laterally**, because being "above" it is not possible under step 1 (3,000 ft would be inside Class C).
    4. Plan each altitude with a margin below the lower limit (next section), changing level only once clearly past each step boundary.

    The result is a profile, not a single altitude.

??? check "Check yourself: what is the practical difference between a CTA labelled \"LL 4500\" and one labelled \"LL FL125\"?"
    The first is an altitude, measured on QNH; the second is a flight level, a pressure level measured with 1013 set. On a low-QNH day FL125 is lower in true altitude than 12,500 ft, so you need the right altimetry to know where the boundary really is.

## Staying out by design: tracking tolerances

Infringements rarely happen because a pilot decided to enter controlled airspace. They happen because the pilot planned along the boundary and drifted, or cruised at the step's lower limit with the altimeter a little out.

AIP ENR 1.1 requires a VFR pilot to navigate so as to **remain clear of controlled airspace without a clearance**, allowing for navigation tolerances. Every means of navigation has an uncertainty, a visual fix, a navigation aid track or a GNSS position, and the tolerance is the band either side of your track in which you could actually be. If that band touches the boundary, so can you.

So near a CTA boundary you either stay **well inside the boundary**, with a buffer the source note gives as **at least a nautical mile or two laterally and a few hundred feet vertically below a step**, or **obtain a clearance before reaching it**. Vertically, an altimeter may legally be up to 100 ft out (see [RFRC 2.3](/notes/RFRC/2.3)) and a subscale a few hectopascals out adds tens of feet more, which is why pilots plan **500 ft below a step** rather than at it. The specific tolerance figures for each means of navigation are in **AIP ENR 1.1**; check the current text, because the source note does not record them.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    A step labelled "C LL 3500" does not mean you may cruise at 3,500 ft. At 3,500 ft you are at its lower limit, which is the bottom of the controlled airspace. Plan below it with a margin, typically 500 ft.

??? check "Check yourself: why does the rule talk about \"tolerances\" rather than just saying \"stay out\"?"
    Because no navigation is exact. Visual fixes, navigation aids and the altimeter all have errors, so a track or level planned on a boundary will sometimes cross it. The tolerance tells you how far back to plan.

## Operating in each class and at each kind of aerodrome

The procedures follow from the separation column of the class table: where ATC takes responsibility, you ask, read back and comply; where it does not, you look, listen and broadcast.

**Class C and D.** Before entering: get the **ATIS**, then **request a clearance** before you reach the CTA or CTR, giving **callsign, type, position, altitude and intentions**. When the clearance comes, **read back** the safety-critical parts: **altitude, heading, runway, frequency, transponder code and holding instructions**. Then **comply**, **report as directed**, and **request a clearance to leave** or **advise leaving**. A clearance is a specific contract about where you will be, and the read-back confirms it.

**Class E.** **No clearance.** Fly the **VFR levels**, keep **VMC**, **listen on the appropriate frequency** and **squawk the VFR code with altitude**, so the controller separating IFR can see you.

**Class G.** Fly the **VFR cruising levels above 5,000 ft** (and above 3,000 ft as hemispherical practice), keep a **listening watch on the area frequency**, **broadcast when needed for safety**, and use the **CTAF procedures** at aerodromes.

![Which cruising level goes with which track?](diagram:vfr-cruising-levels "Above 5,000 ft: tracks 000 to 179 degrees, odd thousands plus 500; 180 to 359 degrees, even thousands plus 500.")

**At a non-controlled aerodrome (CTAF).** Broadcast **taxiing**, **entering and backtracking the runway**, **inbound at about 10 nm** (position, altitude, intentions, ETA), **joining the circuit**, **turning base and final** where traffic requires, and **clear of the runway**. Join **overhead at 1,500 ft above aerodrome level** or on the circuit legs as the AIP allows; fly the **published circuit height and direction**; give way under the rules of the air. At a **certified or registered aerodrome** carry and use a **serviceable VHF radio**: the old CTAF(R) requirement. The calls do a controller's job: everyone in the circuit builds the same picture.

![How do you join from overhead at a CTAF aerodrome?](diagram:overhead-join "Cross overhead above circuit height, descend on the dead side, join crosswind at circuit height.")

**At a controlled aerodrome.** A **clearance for taxi, take-off and landing**, a **transponder**, the **ATIS code in your first call**, and a **full read-back of runway and holding instructions**: a misheard "hold short" is how runway incursions start.

??? check "Check yourself: you are approaching a certified non-controlled aerodrome with an ERSA entry. Its ERSA page shows no special radio requirement. Must you carry and use a radio?"
    Yes. The current rule makes radio carriage and use mandatory at all certified and registered aerodromes, replacing the old CTAF(R) designation.

## Altimetry: what the altimeter measures and from where

Every vertical limit, cruising level and minimum height depends on one instrument. The altimeter is a barometer: it converts the outside pressure to a height using the standard atmosphere, and shows the height difference between the **reference pressure** set in the **subscale** and the pressure level you are at.

### The standard atmosphere it is built on

The conversion uses the **ICAO standard atmosphere (ISA)**: a model with mean sea level **temperature 15 degrees Celsius**, pressure **1013.25 hPa**, a temperature lapse rate of about **2 degrees (1.98) per 1,000 ft**, and a **pressure lapse rate of about 1 hPa per 30 ft** in the lower atmosphere. That is a planning figure: the real rate is about **1 hPa per 27 ft at sea level** and **1 per 36 ft at 10,000 ft**, because thinner air spans more height per hectopascal. Thirty feet is the exam figure.

### Height, altitude and flight level

Three words, three datums:

- **Height** is vertical distance above a specified datum, usually the ground or an aerodrome (AGL).
- **Altitude** is vertical distance above **mean sea level**, which is what the altimeter shows with **QNH** set.
- **Flight level** is **pressure altitude** in **hundreds of feet**, with **1013.2 hPa** set: FL125 is 12,500 ft pressure altitude.

![What is the difference between height, altitude and elevation?](diagram:height-altitude-elevation "At the same altitude, your height above the ground changes with the terrain under you.")

### The four subscale settings

| Subscale set to | The altimeter shows | Term |
|---|---|---|
| Area QNH or local QNH | Height above mean sea level | **Altitude** |
| QFE (aerodrome pressure) | Height above the aerodrome reference point | **Height**; not used in Australian procedures but the term is examinable |
| 1013.2 hPa (standard pressure setting, QNE) | Pressure altitude, expressed in hundreds of feet | **Flight level** (FL125 = 12,500 ft pressure altitude) |

*Source: AIP ENR 1.7 and the Part 91 MOS altimetry provisions, as recorded in the source note; verify current text.*

**QNH** makes the altimeter read the aerodrome's elevation on the ground, so in flight it reads altitude. **Local QNH** is a particular aerodrome's value now, from the tower, ATIS or AWIS; **area QNH** is a forecast value for a wider area and period, from the area forecast. Local QNH is more accurate near its aerodrome. **QFE**, the pressure at the aerodrome itself, makes the altimeter read zero on the runway; Australia does not use it, but the exam asks what it shows.

The **standard setting, 1013.2**, ignores the day's pressure. Everyone above the transition altitude sets it, so every altimeter is wrong by the same amount and vertical separation still works: in the cruise, agreement between aircraft matters more than truth about the ground.

### Finding local QNH with the altimeter

If no QNH is available on the ground, **set the altimeter to read the aerodrome elevation**; the **subscale then shows local QNH** (see [BAKC 6.2](/notes/BAKC/6.2)), because that is how QNH is defined. With a known QNH the same check tests the instrument: more than 100 ft from elevation and it is unserviceable for VFR.

??? check "Check yourself: with 1013.2 set you read 7,000 ft. What is this number, and is it your height above the sea?"
    It is pressure altitude, flight level 70 in effect. It equals your altitude above mean sea level only if the QNH happens to be 1013.2; on any other day the two differ by about 30 ft per hectopascal.

## The transition altitude, layer and level

Low down you need the truth about the terrain (QNH); higher up, agreement between aircraft (1013). In Australia the **transition altitude is 10,000 ft** and the **transition level is FL110**, with the **transition layer** between them (AIP ENR 1.7; verify current text).

- **Climbing:** set **1013** when passing **10,000 ft**.
- **Descending:** set the **area or local QNH** when passing **FL110** (strictly, when cleared below the transition level or on leaving FL110).
- **Aircraft do not cruise in the transition layer.**

In the layer one aircraft could be on QNH and another on 1013, their altimeters disagreeing by the difference; keeping it empty means the two systems never meet.

### Deriving the transition layer from the area QNH

The lowest usable flight level depends on the day's pressure. With QNH 1013, FL110 is 11,000 ft altitude, 1,000 ft above the transition altitude. With a lower QNH a flight level sits **lower**, about 30 ft for every hectopascal below 1013. At QNH 1003, FL110 sits at about

$$11{,}000 - (1013 - 1003) \times 30 = 11{,}000 - 300 = 10{,}700 \text{ ft}$$

so the gap to an aircraft at 10,000 ft on QNH has shrunk. AIP ENR 1.7 gives a table: for **QNH 1013 or above** the lowest usable level is **FL110**; for **lower QNH** it is **FL115 or FL120**, so the flight level stays clear of 10,000 ft altitude. To derive it in the exam, find the area QNH in the table's bands and read off the level; the transition layer is then the airspace between 10,000 ft and that level.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The transition altitude (10,000 ft, on QNH) and the transition level (FL110, on 1013) are not the same thing and are not set at the same point. Climbing, the change to 1013 happens at 10,000 ft; descending, the change to QNH happens at FL110.

### The subscale error

If the subscale is not the real QNH, the altimeter measures from the wrong datum: each hectopascal is about **30 ft** of error.

Reason out the direction. A **higher** number tells the altimeter there is more air below you than there is: it **over-reads**, and **you are lower than indicated**. A **lower** number makes it **under-read**. Set 1003 when the QNH is 1013 and it reads 300 ft low; set 1023 and it reads 300 ft high. Flying from high pressure into low without resetting does the same: **"high to low, look out below."**

![What does a wrong subscale setting do to the reading?](widget:altimeter-subscale "Each hectopascal of difference moves the reading about 30 ft; too high a setting over-reads, so you are lower than it says.")

!!! example "Worked example: true altitude with the wrong subscale setting"
    Given:

    - indicated altitude **6,500 ft**
    - subscale set to **1020 hPa**
    - actual area QNH **1008 hPa**
    - planning rate **30 ft per hPa**

    1. Subscale error: $1020 - 1008 = $ **12 hPa** too high.
    2. Height error: $12 \times 30 = $ **360 ft**.
    3. Direction: the setting is too high, so the altimeter over-reads.
    4. True altitude: $6{,}500 - 360 \approx $ **6,140 ft**.

    Resetting to 1008 drops the reading to 6,140 ft, and you climb back to 6,500 ft. Until then you are 360 ft lower than you think, which over high ground works against you.

Now try one. You are cruising at an indicated 5,500 ft with 1025 set. The area QNH is 1012. How far are you from the altitude you think you are at, in which direction, and what do you do?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    1. Subscale error: $1025 - 1012 = $ **13 hPa** too high.
    2. Height error: $13 \times 30 = $ **390 ft**.
    3. Too high a setting over-reads: true altitude is $5{,}500 - 390 \approx $ **5,110 ft**.

    Reset to 1012. The reading drops to about 5,110 ft, and you climb back to 5,500 ft.

??? check "Check yourself: you fly from an area of QNH 1018 into one of QNH 1009 without resetting. Are you higher or lower than the altimeter says, and by about how much?"
    Lower, by about 270 ft. Your subscale still shows 1018, 9 hPa too high, so the altimeter over-reads by $9 \times 30 = 270$ ft. High to low, look out below.

## Bringing it together

Kalgoorlie back to Jandakot with the controlled airspace endorsement. The track is about 250 degrees magnetic, so above 5,000 ft you need an **even thousand plus 500**: 8,500 ft. You are in Class G in the Melbourne FIR, on each FIA's area frequency in turn, radio required above 5,000 ft. With no AWIS, you set Kalgoorlie's elevation and read local QNH from the subscale; en route you reset area QNH at each forecast area boundary, because the pressure falls towards the coast. Approaching Perth you read the "C LL" labels on your descent path and plan 500 ft below each step. Near Jandakot you take the ATIS, call the tower before the CTR with callsign, type, position, altitude and intentions, read back runway, altitude and code, and land on a clearance. Had the tower been closed, it would have been a CTAF aerodrome in Class G, and you would have broadcast instead of reported.

??? check "Check yourself: in that flight, which piece of altimetry most directly protects you from an infringement under the Perth steps?"
    Flying on the current QNH. The steps' lower limits are altitudes, so a stale setting puts you off by about 30 ft per hectopascal; with the 500 ft planning margin, the correct QNH keeps you out.

## Key points

- **2.6.1** A VFR flight in Class C gets separation from IFR, needs a clearance, a radio and a transponder; Class D a clearance, a radio and traffic information; Class E no clearance and no separation; Class G no clearance, no separation, radio above 5,000 ft and at certified or registered aerodromes. Class A is closed to VFR. 250 kt below 10,000 ft; 200 kt in Class D.
- **2.6.2** FIR (Melbourne and Brisbane; WA in Melbourne), FIA (area frequency), OCTA (Class G). CTA from a lower limit, CTR from the surface. A report goes to ATS and is answered; a broadcast goes to all stations. Lanes of entry: Class G corridors, stay in the lane at the published altitude. CTAF within 10 nm, 126.7 MHz if none published. "C LL 2500" means Class C from 2,500 ft up.
- **2.6.3** Navigate to remain clear of controlled airspace allowing for tolerances: stay well inside a boundary or get a clearance; plan 500 ft below a step, never on it. Figures in AIP ENR 1.1.
- **2.6.4** Class C and D: ATIS, request a clearance, read back, comply. Class E: VFR levels, listen, squawk with altitude. Class G: VFR levels, area frequency, broadcast for safety. CTAF: the standard calls, overhead join at 1,500 ft, radio at certified and registered aerodromes. Controlled aerodrome: clearances, transponder, ATIS code, full read-back.
- **2.6.5** QNH gives altitude, QFE height above the aerodrome, 1013.2 flight level. Transition altitude 10,000 ft, transition level FL110: set 1013 climbing through 10,000, QNH descending through FL110, never cruise in the layer; lower QNH raises the lowest usable level. Set elevation to find local QNH. 1 hPa is about 30 ft; too high a setting over-reads. ISA: 15 degrees Celsius and 1013.25 hPa at sea level.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Altitude is QNH, flight level is 1013, height is above a surface. Transition altitude 10,000 ft, transition level FL110, set 1013 climbing through 10,000 and QNH descending through FL110. One hectopascal is 30 ft, and a setting that is too high (or a low QNH you failed to set) means you are lower than the altimeter says. In airspace questions, separate the word "controlled" from the separation column: only Class C separates VFR from IFR. Expect to be given a chart label or an ERSA extract and asked for a limit; read the lower limit as the bottom of the controlled airspace. Verify the class requirements, speed limits and transition figures against the current AIP ENR 1.4, ENR 1.1 and ENR 1.7.
