## What the exam expects (PFRA 2.8)

- **2.8.1** Say what an ADIZ is, and find in the AIP (a) the general requirements for flying in one and (b) what the pilot of an intercepted aircraft must do.
- **2.8.2** State the powers the law gives a pilot in command.

## Why this matters

Most of your flying in Western Australia will never bring you near a military aircraft or a security incident, and that is exactly why this subtopic needs learning in advance: the first time you meet it, there will be no time to look it up. A PPL can take you into airspace where defence needs to know who you are, and an aeroplane that cannot be identified may find a fighter sitting off its left wing, with a pilot who expects you to know what its signals mean. Closer to home, every time you carry passengers you are the person the law makes responsible for their safety, and it gives you powers to match: to direct them, to refuse to carry them, and in the worst case to restrain them. The exam asks you to read the ADIZ and interception text from the AIP, to know the response to each interception signal, and to state what the pilot in command may do.

## What an ADIZ is

Start with a question a defence force has to answer: of all the aircraft approaching the country, which are friendly? Normal air traffic services do not need to answer it. A VFR aeroplane in Class G is not controlled by anyone and may not be talking to anyone, and that is perfectly lawful. For national security, though, some airspace needs every aircraft in it to be known.

An **Air Defence Identification Zone (ADIZ)** is that airspace: **airspace of defined dimensions within which aircraft must be identified, located and controlled for national security reasons, in addition to normal ATS requirements.** The words "in addition" matter. The ADIZ does not replace the airspace class underneath it. If the ADIZ overlies Class G, the Class G rules still apply, and the ADIZ rules are stacked on top.

How does defence identify you? By matching what it sees to what it was told. It uses **flight plan data and position reports** to identify every aircraft in the zone. A radar return that turns up where a flight plan said an aircraft would be, at the time it said, is identified. A return that matches nothing is an **unidentified aircraft**, and **an aircraft that cannot be identified may be intercepted**.

The ADIZ boundaries and procedures are published in **AIP ENR 2.2**. The exam expects you to read and apply that text, so find it in your current AIP and read it once before the exam.

??? check "Check yourself: an ADIZ lies over Class G airspace. Which rules apply to a VFR aeroplane flying through it?"
    Both. The Class G rules for a VFR flight still apply, and the ADIZ requirements are added on top. An ADIZ exists for national security, in addition to normal ATS requirements, not instead of them.

## Flying in an ADIZ: the general requirements

Every ADIZ requirement follows from one aim: defence must be able to match your aircraft to a plan, continuously, from the moment you enter. Read the requirements with that aim in mind and each one explains itself. Along these lines (the exact text and numbers are in AIP ENR 2.2):

- **Flight notification:** submit a **flight plan (flight notification) to ATS before entering the zone**, with the **time and position of entry**. This is the "what to expect" that defence will compare the radar picture against.
- **Two-way radio:** carry a **serviceable radio** and keep a **listening watch on the appropriate frequency**, so that if something does not match, someone can ask you about it before sending an aircraft to look.
- **Position reports:** report **at the entry point and as required**. The AIP sets **tolerances** on track and time between the planned and actual entry, and **outside them the aircraft may be treated as unidentified**. The tolerance is the size of the window inside which "the aircraft we expected" and "the aircraft we see" can be called the same thing.
- **Transponder:** operate it **with the assigned code and altitude reporting**, so the radar return carries your identity and level.
- **Deviations:** **advise ATS of any deviation from the flight plan as soon as possible**, because a deviation you have not reported looks exactly like an aircraft that is not the one in the plan.

Check the **current AIP ENR 2.2** for the exact numbers, the notification lead time and the track and time tolerances, rather than relying on memory. The numbers can change; the logic does not.

![What happens if you enter an ADIZ off your plan?](diagram:adiz-entry-tolerance "Inside the track and time tolerances you are matched to your plan; outside them, unreported, you may be treated as unidentified. Tell ATS before the boundary. The figures are in AIP ENR 2.2.")

!!! example "Worked example: planning a flight that crosses an ADIZ"
    You plan a private VFR flight whose route crosses the boundary of an ADIZ shown in AIP ENR 2.2. Work through the requirements in the order you meet them.

    1. **Before departure:** lodge a **flight notification with ATS**, giving the **position and time at which you will enter** the zone, and meet the notification lead time in ENR 2.2.
    2. **Equipment check:** the **radio is serviceable**, and the **transponder** works with **altitude reporting**.
    3. **Approaching the boundary:** set the **assigned code**, keep the **listening watch** on the frequency the AIP specifies.
    4. **At entry:** make the **position report**. You are within the track and time **tolerances** of your plan, so the radar return is matched to you and you are identified.
    5. **En route:** a cloud bank forces you 10 nm off your planned track. **Tell ATS as soon as possible**: an unreported deviation could make you look like an unidentified aircraft.

    Every step answers the same question for defence: is the aircraft we see the aircraft we were told about?

Now try one. On the same flight, a headwind means you will reach the entry point well after the time in your flight notification, outside the time tolerance in the AIP. What is the risk, and what should you do before you reach the boundary?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    Outside the tolerance, your aircraft may be treated as **unidentified**, and an unidentified aircraft may be intercepted. The requirement is to **advise ATS of any deviation from the flight plan as soon as possible**, so you call ATS on the radio before you reach the boundary with your revised entry time. Defence then expects you at the new time and the match is made.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    An ADIZ is not a prohibited or restricted area, and it is not closed to civil aircraft. You may fly in it. What it demands is **identification**: a flight notification with entry time and position, radio, position reports within tolerance, and the transponder. Distractors offer things like a minimum height or IFR only; neither is the point of an ADIZ.

## Interception: what the intercepted pilot does

### Why interception happens

An unidentified aircraft in an ADIZ, an aircraft straying towards a prohibited area, or an aircraft that has stopped answering ATC may be **intercepted**: another aircraft, usually military, flies up alongside to identify it, to guide it away, or to direct it to land. Interception of a civil aircraft is always a last resort, and the procedures are designed so that it can be done without a single radio word, because the intercepted pilot may not be on the right frequency.

The procedures are in **AIP ENR 1.12** and follow **ICAO Annex 2**, so they are the same anywhere in the world. The signals also appear in the interception part of ERSA EMERG (see [PFRA 2.7](/notes/PFRA/2.7)).

### The five actions

An intercepted aircraft should **immediately**:

1. **Follow the instructions** of the intercepting aircraft, interpreting and responding to its visual signals.
2. **Notify ATS** if possible.
3. **Attempt radio contact** with the interceptor on **121.5 MHz**, giving your identity and the nature of the flight.
4. **Squawk 7700** on the transponder, **unless instructed otherwise by ATS**.
5. If instructions from ATS **conflict** with the interceptor's, **follow the interceptor** and ask for clarification.

Each step has a reason. Following comes first because the interceptor is the one physically next to you and the one with authority over what happens next. Notifying ATS brings in the people who can explain who you are. **121.5 MHz** is used because it is the one frequency both aircraft are certain to have. **7700** makes your aircraft stand out on every radar screen as an aircraft in an abnormal situation, so the controllers watching can connect you with the interception. And when the instructions conflict you follow the interceptor because ATS cannot see you, but the interceptor can.

![What do 7700, 7600 and 7500 each tell ATC?](diagram:transponder-emergency-codes "An intercepted aircraft squawks 7700 unless ATS says otherwise; 7500 is reserved for unlawful interference.")

??? check "Check yourself: during an interception, ATS tells you to continue on your track, but the interceptor is signalling you to follow it. What do you do?"
    Follow the interceptor and ask ATS for clarification. The interceptor can see you and is acting on its instructions; when the two conflict, the intercepting aircraft's instructions win until the conflict is resolved.

### The visual signals

Without radio, the two aircraft talk with their wings, their lights and their landing gear. The signals from the interceptor are few, and each has a single matching response:

| Interceptor does | Meaning | Intercepted aircraft responds |
|---|---|---|
| Rocks wings from a position ahead and to the left, then a slow level turn onto the desired heading | You have been intercepted, follow me | Rock wings and follow |
| An abrupt breakaway: climbing turn of 90 degrees or more without crossing ahead | You may proceed | Rock wings |
| Lowers gear (if fitted), shows landing lights, overflies the runway | Land at this aerodrome | Lower gear, follow and land if safe |

![What does each interception signal mean, and how do you reply?](diagram:interception-signals "Rocking your wings is the universal 'understood'; the land signal is followed only if it is safe.")

The signals are built so they cannot be mistaken for normal flying. A gentle level turn after wing rocking draws you after it; an abrupt climbing breakaway that deliberately does not cross in front of you is a release; lowering the gear and flying low over a runway can only mean "here". Your **rocking of the wings** is the universal "understood". In the land signal, "if safe" matters: the interceptor's instruction does not oblige you to land on a runway too short for your aeroplane.

The intercepted aircraft has signals of its own, for the cases where it cannot simply obey:

- **Raising the gear and flashing the landing lights while passing over the runway**: the aerodrome you have been directed to is **inadequate**.
- **Switching all lights on and off at regular intervals**: you **cannot comply**.
- **Flashing lights at irregular intervals**: you are **in distress**.

The difference between "regular" and "irregular" carries the meaning, so read it carefully. The irregular flashing is the same idea as the general signal you learned at RPL level, where an aircraft switching its lights on and off intermittently is in difficulty (see [PFRA 2.9](/notes/PFRA/2.9)). **At night, flashing navigation lights replace wing rocking.** See AIP ENR 1.12 for the full table.

!!! example "Worked example: working through an interception"
    You are cruising VFR in a Cessna 172 when a military jet appears and takes up position ahead and to your left, rocks its wings and turns slowly onto a new heading.

    1. **Signal:** wing rocking from ahead-left, then a slow level turn: **"You have been intercepted, follow me."**
    2. **Respond:** **rock your wings and follow.**
    3. **The five actions:** notify ATS on your current frequency if you can; try **121.5 MHz** with your callsign and the nature of your flight; **squawk 7700** unless ATS tells you otherwise.
    4. **Next signal:** the jet lowers its gear, shows its landing lights and flies low along the runway of an aerodrome ahead: **"Land at this aerodrome."**
    5. **Respond:** **follow and land if safe**. Your gear is fixed, so the "lower gear" part of the response does not apply; you join and land.

    Had the jet instead pulled up in an abrupt climbing turn away from you without crossing your nose, the meaning would have been **"you may proceed"**, and your response would have been to rock your wings.

??? check "Check yourself: you are directed to land at an aerodrome whose runway is far too short for your aeroplane. How do you say so?"
    Raise the gear (if retractable) and flash the landing lights while passing over the runway: the signal for "the aerodrome you have designated is inadequate". Landing is only required "if safe".

## Powers of the pilot in command

### Where the authority comes from

Somebody has to be in charge of an aircraft. In a car, a dispute between driver and passenger can be settled by stopping at the kerb; at 5,000 ft there is no kerb. So the law puts the answer beyond argument. Under **CASR Part 91**, the **pilot in command** is **responsible for the safety of the aircraft and of everyone on board**, and has **final authority over the operation of the aircraft**.

Responsibility and authority come together. The law would be unfair if it made you answerable for everyone's safety without giving you the means to secure it, so it gives you powers to match.

### What the pilot in command may do

- **Give safety directions** to passengers and crew, who **must comply**. Part 91 makes failing to comply an offence (check the plain English guide for the regulation numbers). This is the legal backing behind your passenger briefing: "follow the pilot's instructions" is not a request.
- **Refuse carriage of, or disembark, a person whose behaviour endangers safety**, for example an intoxicated or disruptive passenger.
- **Take reasonable measures, including restraint,** against a person on board who is **committing or about to commit an offence that threatens safety**. The **Crimes (Aviation) Act 1991** and the **Tokyo Convention** give **legal protection for reasonable action taken in good faith**.
- **Refuse to carry cargo or baggage that is not safe**, including **dangerous goods** not permitted for carriage.
- **Depart from a rule or ATC clearance in an emergency** to the extent necessary for safety, and **report it afterwards**.

![What must every passenger briefing cover?](diagram:passenger-briefing "The last item, that passengers must follow the pilot's instructions, is the briefing's link to the pilot in command's legal authority.")

Notice the limits written into each power. Restraint must be **reasonable** and the protection covers action **in good faith**. Departures from the rules in an emergency go only **as far as necessary for safety**, and you **report them afterwards**. Final authority is not unlimited authority: it is authority used for safety.

![What may the pilot in command do, and where does each power stop?](diagram:pic-authority "Each power comes with a limit: reasonable, in good faith, only as far as necessary for safety, reported afterwards.")

!!! example "Worked example: which power applies?"
    You are flying three friends from Jandakot to Rottnest for lunch.

    1. *At the aeroplane, one of them is unsteady on his feet and smells strongly of beer.* A person whose condition endangers safety: you may **refuse to carry him**. He stays behind.
    2. *On approach to Rottnest, a passenger unfastens her harness to lean over and take photos.* You give a **safety direction**: fasten the harness now. She **must comply**, and failing to is an offence.
    3. *On the way home, smoke appears from the panel and the quickest way to a runway takes you into controlled airspace before you can get a clearance.* You may **depart from the clearance to the extent necessary for safety**, and you **report it afterwards**.

    In each case the power used is the smallest one that restores safety.

??? check "Check yourself: why does the law give the pilot in command such wide powers?"
    Because it makes the pilot in command responsible for the safety of the aircraft and everyone on board, and responsibility without the authority to act would be meaningless. In flight there is nobody else who can make the decision, so the law gives the pilot final authority.

## Everyday security for private pilots

Security is also the dull, daily habit of not making your aeroplane useful to someone else.

- **Secure the aircraft**: lock the doors, fit control locks and remove the keys; follow aerodrome security rules (security-controlled airports have **ASIC/VIC** requirements under the **Aviation Transport Security Act**).
- **Unlawful interference** (hijack, threat on board): squawk **7500**. ATC will confirm discreetly; **do not change it unless they ask**. The code exists because the person threatening you may be listening to every word you say on the radio.
- **Weapons and firearms**: carriage is tightly restricted under the Aviation Transport Security Act and its regulations; never carry them on board unless the law expressly permits it.
- **Dangerous goods**: carriage is controlled by **CASR Part 92**. Check before carrying anything doubtful: fuel cans, aerosols, lithium batteries, ammunition.
- **Report suspicious activity** around aircraft or aerodromes to the aerodrome operator or the national security hotline.

??? check "Check yourself: you have squawked 7500 and ATC asks you to confirm your code. Why might they ask, and what do you do?"
    ATC confirms discreetly to make sure the code was not set by mistake, without saying anything that would alert a hijacker listening on the frequency. You leave 7500 set; you change it only if ATC asks you to.

## Bringing it together

You are planning a PPL cross-country flight that, for one leg, crosses into an ADIZ. Before departure you read AIP ENR 2.2, lodge the flight notification with the entry point and time, and check the radio and transponder. One passenger shows up with a bag that includes a spare lithium battery pack and a can of insect spray, and as pilot in command you check the dangerous goods rules before agreeing to carry either. Approaching the zone you set the assigned code, keep the listening watch and report at the entry point. A thunderstorm makes you deviate, so you tell ATS at once. Even so, a military aircraft appears off your left wing, rocks its wings and turns away slowly. You rock your wings, follow, call on 121.5 MHz, and squawk 7700 unless ATS tells you otherwise. Within minutes the interceptor has identified you and breaks away in a climbing turn. You rock your wings and carry on. Every part of the lesson was there: the ADIZ requirements that made you identifiable, the interception responses when that was not enough, and the authority of the pilot in command over what came aboard.

## Key points

- **2.8.1** An ADIZ is airspace in which aircraft must be identified, located and controlled for national security, on top of normal ATS requirements (AIP ENR 2.2). Requirements: flight notification with entry time and position, two-way radio and listening watch, position reports within the track and time tolerances, transponder with assigned code and altitude, deviations advised at once. Intercepted (AIP ENR 1.12): follow the interceptor, notify ATS, call on 121.5 MHz, squawk 7700 unless ATS says otherwise, follow the interceptor if instructions conflict. Wing rocking then a slow turn: follow me; abrupt climbing breakaway: proceed; gear down over the runway: land here. Your replies: regular flashing, cannot comply; irregular flashing, in distress.
- **2.8.2** The pilot in command is responsible for the safety of the aircraft and everyone on board and has final authority: safety directions passengers must obey, refusing carriage or disembarking, reasonable restraint in good faith, refusing unsafe cargo and dangerous goods, departing from rules or clearances in an emergency as far as needed and reporting it. Hijack: 7500.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Intercepted: follow the interceptor, call on 121.5, squawk 7700 (unless ATS says otherwise). Hijack is 7500. The PIC has final authority for the safety of the aircraft and its occupants. Expect to match an interceptor signal to its meaning and your response, and to pick the ADIZ requirement (flight notification with entry details, radio, position reports, transponder) out of distractors. Check the current AIP ENR 2.2 and ENR 1.12 for the exact tolerances and the full signal table.
