## What the exam expects (PFRA 2.5)

- **2.5.1** Recognise and explain the markings at aerodromes, landing areas (LA) and helicopter landing sites (HLS): runway, threshold and runway end markings, cone and gable markers, taxiway markings, holding points and bays, the double white cross beside the primary wind indicator, the horizontal white dumbbell, movement areas and HLS markings.
- **2.5.2** Point out the downwind, base, crosswind and upwind legs of a circuit and its dead side.
- **2.5.3** Explain what a white cross on the movement area means.

## Why this matters

At RPL level you learned to read your own training aerodrome, where you know every marking before you see it (see [RFRC 2.5](/notes/RFRC/2.5)). The PPL takes you to aerodromes you have never seen: a sealed country runway at Katanning with a displaced threshold, a grass field near York after a week of winter rain, a station strip marked with old tyres, a hospital helipad beside the Kalgoorlie aerodrome. At each one the markings and ground signals are information you have to read from overhead, and they are sometimes the only information there is. Everything in the RPL lesson applies here: the parts of the movement area, the colours of runway and taxiway paint, cones and gables, the circuit legs and the dead side, the single and the double white cross. This lesson adds what is new at PPL level: the dumbbell, landing area and HLS markings, a closer look at thresholds, and the skill of putting the ground signals together with the ERSA entry and the NOTAMs. The exam does this with a diagram or a short scenario and asks what you may do.

## Reading the runway: designator, threshold and end

Picture yourself on a long final to a runway you have never landed on. Three questions matter: which runway is this, where may I touch down, and where does the usable surface end? The runway markings answer them in that order.

**Runway markings** are **white**. The **designator** is the runway's magnetic direction to the nearest 10 degrees with the last zero dropped, so 24 means 240 degrees magnetic. On parallel runways it carries **L, C or R**, as seen by the approaching pilot. A dashed **centreline** runs down the middle and **side stripes** mark the paved edges. On larger runways the **aiming point** bars sit **about 300 m from the threshold**, with **touchdown zone** bars on longer runways. The aiming point is where a normal approach path meets the runway. On a short country runway you usually aim nearer the threshold than that, but the bars tell you how the runway was designed to be used.

![What do the runway markings and markers show?](diagram:runway-markings-and-markers "White for runways, yellow for taxiways; on the unsealed strip, cones at the runway edge and gables at the strip boundary.")

**Threshold markings**, the white "piano keys", show where the **landing portion of the runway starts**. Usually that is the physical end of the pavement. Sometimes it is not.

### The displaced threshold

A threshold is **displaced** when the landing portion begins some distance along the runway rather than at its end. The common reasons are an obstacle under the approach (a line of trees, a road, power lines) or a weak or damaged section of pavement at the end. The displaced threshold is marked with **arrows along the centreline pointing to a transverse bar**, the line where landing may begin.

The rule follows from why the threshold moved. If an obstacle sits under the approach, the approach path to the new threshold clears it, but an approach aimed at the pavement's end would not. So you **land beyond the displaced threshold**. The pavement before it is still sound for an aeroplane already on the ground, so it **may be used for the take-off run**, and **for the roll-out after landing in the other direction**. Arrows mean "roll on me", and the bar means "touch down only past here".

The practical effect is on your performance planning: a displaced threshold **reduces the landing distance available** without reducing the take-off distance available from that end.

![Where may you touch down, and what may you use before the bar?](diagram:displaced-threshold "Touch down only past the bar; the arrowed pavement still counts for the take-off run and the roll-out, so only the landing distance shrinks: 1,250 m minus 220 m leaves 1,030 m.")

!!! example "Worked example: landing distance with a displaced threshold"
    A sealed country runway is 1,250 m long. NOTAM and ERSA show the threshold of the northern end displaced 220 m because of a new line of power poles under the approach. Your aeroplane needs 540 m to land over a 50 ft screen today (from the POH chart, with your safety margin applied) and 610 m to take off.

    1. **Landing towards the south**, over the displaced threshold: land beyond the bar.
    $$1{,}250\ \text{m} - 220\ \text{m} = \mathbf{1{,}030\ m}\ \text{available}$$
    Required 540 m: **adequate**.
    2. **Taking off towards the south**, starting from the northern end: the arrowed section may be used for the take-off run, so the **full 1,250 m** is available. Required 610 m: **adequate**.
    3. **Landing towards the north**: the approach is over the southern end, so you touch down at the normal threshold, and the roll-out may continue onto the arrowed section. **Full length available for the roll-out.**

    The displacement only costs you landing distance in the direction where the obstacle is under the approach.

Now try one where the unknown is different. Suppose instead the northern threshold is normal and the southern threshold is displaced 400 m for pavement works, and your aeroplane needs 880 m to land. Can you land towards the north?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    Landing towards the north, over the southern end, you must touch down beyond the displaced threshold.
    $$1{,}250\ \text{m} - 400\ \text{m} = \mathbf{850\ m}\ \text{available}$$
    850 m is less than the 880 m required, so **no**. Land towards the south if the wind allows, where the full length is available for the roll-out, or go elsewhere.

**Runway end markings** show where the usable surface stops. On a sealed runway that is the **side stripes and a transverse stripe**. On an unsealed strip it is the **markers at the ends**.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The arrowed section before a displaced threshold is not "closed". It is closed for **touchdown** only. Treating it as unusable throws away take-off distance; landing on it may put you into the obstacle the displacement was there to clear.

??? check "Check yourself: why may you take off from the arrowed section before a displaced threshold, but not land on it?"
    The displacement protects the approach path, not the pavement. An aeroplane landing short of the bar would have come down over the obstacle on too low a path. An aeroplane taking off starts on the ground, and climbs away from the obstacle at the other end.

## Unsealed runways, landing areas and cones and gables

Many WA aerodromes are gravel or grass, where paint will not last. There the job of the white paint is done by **markers**.

**Cone markers** are white cones along the **edges of the runway or landing area** on unsealed or unrated strips. The usable surface lies **between them**. **Gable markers** are white gables marking the **boundary of the runway strip**, the graded clear area around the runway that must be kept free so an aeroplane running off the edge has somewhere reasonable to stop. **A gable is not a runway edge.** From the centre outward: runway surface, cones, strip, gables.

A **landing area (LA)**, or **aeroplane landing area (ALA)**, is a place used for take-off and landing that is not a certified or registered aerodrome: a farm strip, a station strip, a mine's dirt runway. Nobody inspects it to an aerodrome standard, so its markings are whatever the operator has put out: **cones, drums, tyres or gables** for the boundaries and runway edges. Whether the LA is suitable for you, considering its **length, slope, surface and obstacles**, is decided by the **operator's markings and the guidance in AC 91-02** (places suitable for take-off and landing), and in the end by you as pilot in command.

That last point is the difference between an aerodrome and an LA. At a certified or registered aerodrome, someone has measured the runway and published it in ERSA. At an LA, a row of whitewashed tyres tells you where the owner thinks the strip is. You confirm the rest yourself: from the owner's information beforehand, and from a low inspection pass before landing.

??? check "Check yourself: a station strip is marked with white-painted tyres along both sides of a graded runway. What do the tyres tell you, and what do they not?"
    They mark the runway edges the operator has set out, so land between them. They do not tell you the length, slope, surface strength or obstacles; at an LA you judge suitability yourself, using the operator's information and AC 91-02.

## Taxiways, holding points and the movement area

The **movement area** is the **manoeuvring area** (runways and taxiways) plus the **aprons**. Aprons are marked with **parking positions and lead-in lines**, and apron edges with a **yellow line**. The yellow lead-in line takes you into a parking position clear of other aircraft and buildings, so you can follow it without guessing the clearances.

**Taxiway markings** are **yellow**: a centreline, edge lines, and the **runway holding position**, which is **two solid lines towards the taxiway and two dashed lines towards the runway**. **Hold on the solid side.** Approaching the runway, the solid lines face you and say stop. Vacating the runway, the dashed lines face you and let you cross. **Intermediate holding positions**, where you may be told to wait on a taxiway away from any runway, are a **single dashed line**.

The **holding point** is where you hold before entering or crossing a runway. It is set back so that an aircraft waiting there is clear of aircraft using the runway. A **holding bay** is a widened area where an aircraft can wait or complete its run-up checks **without blocking the taxiway**. At a country aerodrome with one stub taxiway and no holding bay, your run-up may block the only way onto the runway, so do it on the apron or look over your shoulder for anyone taxiing behind you.

## The signal area: what the ground is telling you

Beside the windsock at many aerodromes is a **signal area**: a square of ground where white symbols can be laid out. It is the aerodrome's message board, readable from overhead by an aircraft with no radio contact with anyone on the ground. Before every landing at an unfamiliar aerodrome you look at it.

The symbols you need follow one pattern: **each tells you what you may use or what activity to expect**.

- The **primary wind indicator** (the windsock) shows **wind direction and roughly its strength**. Where night operations are permitted it is **lit at night**.
- A **double white cross** beside the primary wind indicator means **gliding operations in progress**. Expect gliders and tugs in the circuit and winch cables across the field.
- A **horizontal white dumbbell** means **use only the sealed (hard) surfaces**: the runways and taxiways. Landing on the grass areas is prohibited while it is displayed, usually because the unsealed surfaces are **waterlogged or soft**. The shape is the clue: two discs joined by a bar, like two sealed areas joined by a sealed strip, with nothing outside them. A variant adds a **black bar across each disc**: it means take-off and landing must be on **runways only**, but taxiing and other ground movement **need not be confined to the hard surfaces** (ICAO Annex 2, Appendix 1; the older note had this the other way round; verify against the current AIP AD 1.1).
- A **white landing T** shows the landing direction where no windsock applies: **land parallel to the shaft, towards the crossbar**.
- A **right-hand circuit indicator** (a right-turning arrow) shows that right-hand circuits are in force.

![What do the ground signals and the windsock tell you?](diagram:ground-signals "A single cross closes an area, two crosses by the windsock mean gliding, a dumbbell keeps you to the sealed surfaces, and the landing T and turn arrow set the direction and the circuit.")

Why does the dumbbell matter more at PPL level? Because the PPL takes you to grass aerodromes where the whole field may normally be used (see [PFRA 2.3](/notes/PFRA/2.3) on aerodromes where operations are not restricted to runways). You may have planned a landing into wind across the grass, and the dumbbell takes that option away for the day. The ground signal overrides the plan.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    The double white cross and the single white cross mean different things, and the difference is **where** they are. Two crosses in the signal area beside the windsock report an **activity** (gliding). A single cross on a runway or taxiway reports a **closure**. Read the position before the shape.

??? check "Check yourself: at a grass aerodrome where ERSA says operations are not restricted to runways, you see a white dumbbell in the signal area. Where may you land?"
    Only on the sealed runway. The dumbbell restricts operations to the sealed surfaces, overriding the normal freedom to use the whole landing area, usually because the grass is soft or waterlogged.

## Helicopter landing sites

A **helicopter landing site (HLS)** is marked with a letter **H** inside a **circle, square or triangle** at the touchdown and lift-off area, often with a **lit or painted perimeter**. **Aeroplanes keep clear of it.** A helicopter arrives and departs steeply and may come from directions that have nothing to do with the runway in use, and its downwash can overturn a light aeroplane taxiing nearby. Helicopters may also operate from an HLS **located away from the runways**, such as a hospital pad, a pad at a mine site or a pad on a station. So an H anywhere in your field of view tells you to expect helicopter traffic that is not flying your circuit.

![What does a helicopter landing site look like?](diagram:hls-markings "An H in a circle, square or triangle means helicopters: keep clear of the downwash and expect steep arrivals from any direction.")

## The circuit at an aerodrome you do not know

The circuit legs and the dead side are as described in [RFRC 2.5](/notes/RFRC/2.5). **Upwind** after take-off. **Crosswind**, the first turn. **Downwind**, parallel to the landing direction and opposite to it. **Base**, the turn towards the runway. Then **final**. The **dead side** is **opposite the circuit side**, and it is where overflying aircraft descend to circuit height before joining crosswind.

![Which leg is which, and where is the dead side?](diagram:circuit-pattern "Upwind, crosswind, downwind, base, final; the dead side is the other side of the runway from the circuit.")

What is new at PPL level is that the **circuit direction is not always left**. ERSA may show **right-hand circuits** for a particular runway, usually to keep traffic away from a town, high ground or another aerodrome's circuit, and the right-hand circuit indicator in the signal area may show it. When the circuit is right-hand, every leg moves to the other side of the runway and **so does the dead side**. If you learn the dead side as "the right of the runway", you will descend through the circuit at the first aerodrome with right-hand circuits.

![Where is the dead side when circuits are right-hand?](diagram:right-hand-circuit-dead-side "With right-hand circuits for runway 31 every leg moves to the north-east side, so the dead side moves to the south-west: rebuild it from the landing direction every time.")

The safe method is to rebuild the circuit from the landing direction every time:

1. Find the **landing direction** (windsock, landing T or the runway in use).
2. Find the **circuit direction** for that runway (ERSA, the signal area, NOTAMs). Left-hand unless shown otherwise.
3. Put the **downwind leg** on the circuit side, parallel and opposite to landing.
4. The **dead side** is the other side.

![Fly the circuit and swap the runway in use](widget:circuit-3d "When the runway changes, the whole pattern moves, and the dead side moves with it.")

!!! example "Worked example: a right-hand circuit"
    You arrive overhead a country aerodrome. The windsock favours runway 31, and the ERSA entry says right-hand circuits for runway 31.

    1. **Landing direction**: 310 degrees, roughly north-west. **Final** points 310.
    2. **Circuit direction**: **right-hand** for 31, from ERSA.
    3. **Circuit side**: to the right of the runway looking along 310, which is the **north-east side**. **Upwind** continues on 310; **crosswind** turns right to about 040; **downwind** runs 130, parallel and opposite to landing; **base** turns right to about 220; **final** turns right again onto 310.
    4. **Dead side**: the other side of the runway, the **south-west**. That is to the **left** of the runway looking along the landing direction.

    For an overhead join you cross above circuit height, descend on the south-west side, cross the upwind end and join crosswind heading north-east.

![Where do you descend when joining from overhead?](diagram:overhead-join "On the dead side, away from the circuit, then across the upwind end to join crosswind at circuit height.")

??? check "Check yourself: why do right-hand circuits exist at all, when left-hand is the standard?"
    To keep circuit traffic away from something on the standard side: a town (noise and the populous-area rules), high ground, or another aerodrome's circuit. The direction is published in ERSA for that runway, which is why you must read it before arriving.

## The white cross

A **white cross** on any part of the movement area marks it as **unserviceable**: do not use it. **Crosses at the ends of a runway** close the runway, and a cross on a taxiway closes that taxiway. The cross is large and white so it can be read from the air in any direction against bitumen or grass, and its meaning has no shades: there is no "use with care".

At PPL level the cross is rarely a surprise, because a runway closure is normally also **published by NOTAM**. The cross is what you see; the NOTAM is what you read beforehand. If you read the NOTAMs, the cross confirms what you already know. If you skipped them, the cross is the last line of defence. And if a NOTAM says a runway is closed but no crosses are out, the NOTAM still applies.

??? check "Check yourself: a NOTAM closes one of two runways for resealing, and from overhead you see crosses at each end of it. The other runway has a strong crosswind. May you use the closed runway?"
    No. The crosses and the NOTAM both say the runway is unserviceable, whatever the wind. Use the open runway if the crosswind is within your limits, or divert.

## Bringing it together

A winter Sunday: you fly from Jandakot to a country aerodrome in the Avon Valley for lunch. On Saturday, ERSA shows two runways, 03/21 sealed and 12/30 gravel, right-hand circuits for runway 30, and operations not restricted to runways. A NOTAM says the northern threshold of 03/21 is displaced 150 m for works. On arrival you fly overhead above circuit height and read the ground. The windsock favours 30. In the signal area is a white dumbbell: the gravel is soft after the rain, so only sealed surfaces may be used, which rules out 30 and the grass. You check 21: landing towards the south over the displaced northern threshold, you subtract 150 m from the published length, and it still exceeds your landing distance with margin. 21 has no published right-hand circuit, so it is left-hand and the dead side is to the right of the runway looking along 210. You descend on the dead side, join crosswind and land beyond the bar. Taxiing in, you follow the yellow lead-in line to a parking position and keep clear of the H painted on the apron's edge for the rescue helicopter. Each marking, read together with ERSA and the NOTAM, changed a decision you had made the evening before.

??? check "Check yourself: in this flight, which two pieces of information overrode the plan from ERSA, and where did each come from?"
    The dumbbell in the signal area (seen on arrival) closed the gravel runway and the grass, overriding ERSA's "not restricted to runways". The displaced threshold (from the NOTAM, and marked with arrows) reduced the landing distance on 21.

## Key points

- **2.5.1** Runway paint is white: designator, centreline, side stripes, aiming point about 300 m in, touchdown zone bars. Piano keys start the landing portion; arrows to a bar mark a displaced threshold, before which you may take off and roll out but not land. Side stripes and a transverse stripe (or end markers on an unsealed strip) mark the end. Cones mark the runway edge, gables the strip boundary; at an LA, cones, drums, tyres or gables mark what the operator has set out, and AC 91-02 guides suitability. Taxiway paint is yellow; hold on the solid side; a single dashed line is an intermediate holding position; a holding bay lets you wait or run up clear of the taxiway. Movement area = runways, taxiways and aprons. Double white cross by the windsock: gliding. Horizontal white dumbbell: sealed surfaces only. H in a circle, square or triangle: an HLS, keep clear.
- **2.5.2** Upwind, crosswind, downwind (parallel and opposite to landing), base, final. Left-hand unless ERSA or the signal area shows right-hand; the dead side is always opposite the circuit side and moves with it.
- **2.5.3** A white cross on the movement area: unserviceable, do not use; crosses at both ends close the runway, usually with a NOTAM as well.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    New at PPL: the horizontal white dumbbell means sealed surfaces only. Together with the double white cross (gliding) and the single white cross (unserviceable), it makes up the three ground signals most often examined. Know the displaced threshold rule (arrows: take off and roll out, but no touchdown before the bar), cones against gables (edge against strip boundary), and HLS markings (H in a circle, square or triangle). For circuit questions, build the pattern from the landing direction and the published circuit direction, and never place the dead side from memory. Confirm marking conventions against the current AIP AD 1.1 and the workbook figures, which the exam's diagrams are drawn from.
