## What the exam expects (PFRA 2.2)

- **2.2.1** For the PPL with the aeroplane category rating, describe (a) what the licence lets you do and where it stops, (b) the recent experience you need to use it, and (c) how flights are classified into types of operation.
- **2.2.2** Apply the flight and duty time rules as they affect a PPL holder.

## Why this matters

The private pilot licence is the licence that lets you go places: Jandakot to Esperance with three friends, a weekend at Monkey Mia, a business trip to Kalgoorlie in your own aeroplane, through the Perth control zones without a second thought. The RPL's fences of weight, distance and airspace fall away. What remains are the fences that matter most, because they are the ones private pilots cross without meaning to: carrying passengers at night without recent night landings, flying a type of aeroplane with a feature they have never been trained on, or accepting money for a flight that turns it from private into commercial. The exam asks this subtopic as "may this pilot do this?" scenarios and as recall of a few figures. This lesson builds on [RFRC 2.2](/notes/RFRC/2.2) and teaches what changes at PPL level.

## How a licence is built

Before the privileges make sense you need the structure they sit on, because "may I fly this?" is answered by several layers at once.

The **licence** says what kind of pilot you are and in what kind of operation: recreational, private, commercial or airline transport. Each licence carries a **category rating**, the kind of aircraft: aeroplane, helicopter and so on. This lesson is about the **PPL(A)**, the private licence with the aeroplane category rating.

Under the category sit the **class ratings** and **type ratings**, which say which aeroplanes you may fly. Most light aeroplanes are flown on a class rating: the **single-engine aeroplane class rating** or the **multi-engine aeroplane class rating**. Larger or more complex aeroplanes need their own **type rating**; a useful rule of thumb is aircraft **over 5,700 kg or turbojets** (CASR Part 61; verify current text).

Then come the **design feature endorsements**, one for each feature that changes how an aeroplane is flown and that your basic training did not cover: **tailwheel**, **retractable undercarriage**, **manual propeller pitch control**, **pressurisation**, **floats** and **gas turbine**. If the aeroplane has the feature, you need the endorsement.

Then the **operational ratings**, which extend the conditions you may fly in: the **night VFR rating** and the **instrument rating** are the two a private pilot meets. Finally the **activity endorsements**: **aerobatics**, **formation**, **spinning** and others.

To fly a particular aeroplane in a particular way, every layer has to say yes. The licence permits the operation, the class or type rating covers the aeroplane, each design feature on it is endorsed, the conditions are within your ratings, and any special activity has its endorsement.

## What the PPL lets you do, and where it stops

Start from what you know. The RPL is built for flying close to home in light aeroplanes, and most of its limits are lifted by endorsements on the RPL itself. A few limits belong to the RPL and no endorsement removes them: single-engine, not more than 1,500 kg, private operations, day VFR.

![What does the RPL allow, and which limits does the PPL remove?](diagram:rpl-privileges-map "The left side is the RPL with its fences; the PPL removes the 1,500 kg, 25 nm and airspace limits, while private operations stay.")

The PPL removes the weight, distance and airspace fences. The holder of a PPL with an aeroplane category rating may fly **as pilot in command or co-pilot of an aeroplane** in a **private operation**, and as pilot in command in **flight training** of certain kinds, without the weight, distance or airspace limits of the RPL (CASR Part 61 Subpart 61.G; verify current text). Compared with the RPL, a PPL holder:

- may **carry passengers anywhere in Australia** (and overseas with the agreement of the country concerned), in any aeroplane within their class or type rating and design feature endorsements;
- may fly in **all classes of airspace**, at **controlled aerodromes**, and **above 10,000 ft**, subject to the oxygen rules; the controlled airspace procedures are part of PPL training rather than separate endorsements;
- may fly **at night only with the night VFR rating**, and **in IMC only with an instrument rating**;
- flies **under VFR by day by default**; **aerobatics, formation, spinning** and other activities need the relevant endorsement;
- flies single-engine aeroplanes on the **single-engine aeroplane class rating**; **multi-engine** aeroplanes need a class rating and endorsement; aircraft **over 5,700 kg or turbojets** need a **type rating**;
- must hold the **design feature endorsements** for tailwheel, retractable undercarriage, manual propeller pitch control, pressurisation, floats or gas turbine where the aeroplane has them;
- **must not fly for hire or reward**, or in any operation that requires a commercial licence, except for the cost sharing the regulations allow.

The limit that remains is the important one: the PPL is still a **private** licence. The licence's whole basis is that nobody is paying you to fly. Why should that matter to safety? A commercial pilot's passengers have bought a service and assume the pilot, the aeroplane and the operator meet a commercial standard; they cannot judge the risk for themselves. A private pilot's passengers are friends or family who know they are flying with a private pilot. The higher training, medical and maintenance standards of commercial flying exist to protect people who are paying and cannot see the risk.

### Cost sharing

The line between private and commercial runs through money, so the regulations draw it precisely. A PPL holder **may share the direct costs of a private flight equally with passengers** (fuel, landing fees, aircraft hire) **with no profit**, under the conditions in the Part 91 rules (verify the current cost-sharing conditions). Anything beyond that, such as a passenger paying more than an equal share, a payment for your time, or a profit, makes it a commercial operation, and a PPL is not enough.

The logic: an equal share means the pilot is also paying, so the pilot is flying for their own purposes and simply not paying for everyone else's seat. Once the pilot is paid, the flight exists for the passenger's purposes, and that is a service.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    "No profit" is not the whole test. The share must also be **equal**, and only **direct costs** can be shared. A pilot who flies free while the passengers split the whole bill is not paying an equal share, even though nobody made a profit.

### The medical behind the licence

A PPL is used with a **Class 2** medical certificate, or a **Basic Class 2** with its limits. A **RAMPC** supports only RPL-level privileges. In [RFRC 2.2](/notes/RFRC/2.2) you met the principle: the easier the medical is to get, the tighter its limits. The **Basic Class 2**, issued by a GP to the commercial driver standard, limits you to **private, day VFR, below 10,000 ft, piston aircraft up to 8,618 kg and no more than 5 passengers** (CASR Part 67 and CASA's Basic Class 2 page; verify current limits). The licence limit and the medical limit stack, so you are bound by whichever is tighter.

![Which medical supports which privileges?](diagram:rpl-medical-certificates "A Class 2 supports the full PPL; a Basic Class 2 keeps you day VFR, below 10,000 ft and to 5 passengers; a RAMPC is for RPL privileges only.")

That matters for a PPL holder in a way it did not for an RPL holder. A PPL holder with a night VFR rating on a Basic Class 2 cannot use the night rating, because the medical keeps them in day VFR. A PPL holder planning a flight at 11,500 ft over the Great Victoria Desert cannot do it on a Basic Class 2, which keeps them below 10,000 ft.

!!! example "Worked example: may this PPL holder fly this aeroplane?"
    Mei holds a PPL(A) with the single-engine aeroplane class rating, the retractable undercarriage and manual propeller pitch control endorsements, and a Class 2 medical. A syndicate member offers her a four-seat single-engine aeroplane with a retractable undercarriage, a constant-speed propeller and a tailwheel, for a day VFR private flight from Jandakot to Albany through the Perth control zone.

    1. Licence and operation: PPL, **private flight**. Allowed.
    2. Class rating: single-engine aeroplane. **Covered.**
    3. Design features: retractable undercarriage, **endorsed**; constant-speed propeller (manual propeller pitch control), **endorsed**; tailwheel, **not endorsed**.
    4. Airspace and distance: the PPL has no airspace or distance fences. **Allowed.**
    5. Conditions and medical: day VFR on a Class 2. **Allowed.**

    One layer says no: the tailwheel. Mei may not fly this aeroplane as pilot in command until she has the tailwheel endorsement, however experienced she is on the other two features.

??? check "Check yourself: a PPL holder with every design feature endorsement wants to fly a 6,500 kg turbojet. What else must they hold?"
    A type rating for that aircraft. Design feature endorsements cover features of aeroplanes flown on a class rating; aircraft over 5,700 kg or turbojets are flown on a type rating, which needs its own training and test.

## Recent experience: staying current

A licence does not expire, but skill does, and the recent experience rules match each kind of fade to a time window. You met the logic at RPL level: take-off and landing feel fades over weeks, general handling over years. The PPL keeps the same rules and adds one for the night.

- **Passengers by day:** at least **3 take-offs and 3 landings in the previous 90 days** in the same category of aircraft (CASR 61.395).
- **Passengers at night:** **3 take-offs and 3 landings at night in the previous 90 days**, in addition to the **night VFR rating**.
- **Flight review:** an aeroplane flight review within the previous **24 months** (CASR 61.400 and the class rating review rules); a **flight test for a licence or rating counts as a flight review** for that category.
- **Medical certificate:** Class 2, or Basic Class 2 with its limits.
- **General competency** (CASR 61.385): you must not fly an aircraft unless you are competent to do so, which covers currency on type and in the activity, whatever the formal rules say.

![How far back do the currency rules look?](diagram:rpl-recency-timeline "The same two windows apply to a PPL: 3 take-offs and 3 landings in 90 days for passengers, a flight review within 24 months to fly as pilot in command at all; the PPL adds a night version of the 90-day rule.")

Why a separate night rule? A night take-off is flown on instruments within seconds of lift-off, because the horizon disappears; a night landing is judged from runway lights and a sloping row of approach lights rather than a whole visible landscape. Those skills fade independently of your daylight flying. A pilot who flies every weekend by day but has not landed at night since autumn has fresh day skills and stale night ones, and the passenger in the back is relying on the night ones.

The **general competency rule** sits behind all of this, and the exam likes it because it overrides the arithmetic. Meeting 3 landings in 90 days in a Cessna 172 does not make you competent in a tailwheel aeroplane you last flew three years ago, even though you hold the endorsement. The formal rules set a minimum; competency is the real test.

!!! example "Worked example: is this pilot current to take passengers tonight?"
    Today is 3 October. Ravi holds a PPL with a night VFR rating and a Class 2 medical. His flight review was done in March last year. His logbook shows: 14 June, three night take-offs and landings at Jandakot; 30 August, two day take-offs and landings; 26 September, two day take-offs and landings. He wants to fly two friends on a night scenic flight this evening.

    1. Flight review: March last year is within 24 months. **Current.**
    2. The 90-day window before 3 October reaches back to early July, so the **14 June night landings do not count**.
    3. Day passengers: 30 August and 26 September give **4 take-offs and 4 landings**, so Ravi could take them by day.
    4. Night passengers: **no night take-offs or landings** in the window. **Not current.**

    Ravi may not carry passengers tonight. He can fly 3 night take-offs and landings solo first, then take his friends, or take them by day.

??? check "Check yourself: a PPL holder passed the night VFR rating flight test 20 months after their last flight review. When is their next flight review due?"
    The flight test counts as a flight review for that category, so the 24-month window restarts from the flight test, not from the earlier review.

## Classification of operations

Every flight falls into a class of operation, and the class decides which set of rules applies: who may be pilot in command, what licence and medical they need, how the aeroplane must be maintained, and whether an operator's certificate is involved. The classes are graded by how much the people on board rely on someone else for their safety.

| Classification | Examples | Who may be pilot in command |
|---|---|---|
| Private operations (CASR Part 91 only) | Flights for your own purposes, cost-shared flights within the rules, flying your own or a hired aeroplane for pleasure or business without hire or reward | PPL, with an RPL for the narrower RPL privileges |
| Flight training (Part 141 and 142) | Dual training, solo training flights, flight reviews | Instructors; students under supervision |
| Aerial work (Part 138) | Survey, photography, agricultural work, towing, dropping | CPL (or PPL only for specified non-commercial aerial work the rules allow) |
| Air transport (Parts 119, 121, 133, 135) | Passenger or cargo carriage for hire or reward, charter and airline | CPL or ATPL, under an air operator's certificate |

(CASR Part 91, Parts 119, 121, 133, 135, 138, 141 and 142, and CASA's plain English guide on classification of operations; verify current text.)

**Private operations** are flown under Part 91 alone. The pilot flies for their own purposes, alone or with people who know they are flying with a private pilot. "For business" is allowed: flying yourself to a meeting is a private flight, provided nobody pays you to fly.

**Flight training** is conducted under Parts 141 and 142, by an organisation that holds the approval. Instructors fly as pilot in command, and students fly under their supervision.

**Aerial work** is flying in which the aeroplane is the tool for a task: survey, photography, agriculture, towing, dropping. When it is done for reward it needs a commercial licence, and the operational rules in Part 138.

**Air transport** is carrying passengers or cargo for hire or reward: charter and airline flying, under an air operator's certificate.

The practical test is a single question: **is anyone paying for the flight beyond equal cost sharing?** If so, it is not a private operation, and a PPL is not enough.

![Which class of operation is this flight?](diagram:operations-classification "Private operations sit under Part 91 alone; the moment someone pays beyond an equal share of costs, the flight is no longer private and a PPL is not enough.")

!!! example "Worked example: classifying four flights"
    For each flight, ask who benefits and whether anyone is paying beyond an equal share of direct costs.

    1. *You fly your own aeroplane from Jandakot to a client meeting in Merredin, and your company reimburses nothing.* You fly for your own purposes and nobody pays you to fly: **private operation**. A PPL is enough.
    2. *A neighbouring farmer offers you $400 to photograph his paddocks from the air for an insurance claim.* The aeroplane is the tool for a task and you are paid: **aerial work** for reward. A PPL is not enough.
    3. *A mining company asks you to fly two of its geologists to Leonora, paying you a fee plus fuel.* Passengers carried for hire or reward: **air transport**. Needs a CPL and an air operator's certificate.
    4. *Two club members and you fly to Rottnest for lunch and split the hire, fuel and landing fees three ways.* Equal sharing of direct costs, no profit: **private operation** within the cost-sharing rules.

    Flights 2 and 3 fail on the same point: someone is paying the pilot to fly.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    "I'm not making a profit, I'm just covering my hours" is still hire or reward if the passenger pays more than an equal share or pays for the pilot's seat. The test is equal sharing of direct costs, not whether the pilot comes out ahead.

??? check "Check yourself: a friend offers to pay the whole cost of the aeroplane hire if you fly her to Busselton for a wedding. Private or not, and why?"
    Not a private operation within the cost-sharing rules: she would be paying more than an equal share, and you would be flying at no cost for her purposes. That is carriage for reward, which a PPL does not permit.

## Flight and duty time for a PPL holder

Fatigue kills pilots as surely as weather does, and commercial aviation manages it with prescribed limits on how long a pilot may fly and be on duty. Those limits are in **CAO 48.1** (fatigue management), and they apply to the crew of **commercial operations**.

For a **private operation** there are **no prescribed flight and duty time limits**. That does not mean you may fly as tired as you like. The PPL holder remains bound by the **general rules on fitness for flight**: you **must not fly while fatigued to the extent that it affects safety**. The human factors syllabus (PHFC 2.13) covers how to recognise and manage fatigue.

Why no prescribed limits for private flying? A commercial pilot's hours are set by a roster that someone else writes, under pressure to keep aircraft flying, so the law sets a ceiling. A private pilot chooses when to fly, and the law leaves the judgement with them, backed by the general obligation to be fit.

The picture changes when a PPL holder also flies in a commercial or training operation. For those flights they are **subject to that operator's fatigue rules**, and the **hours flown privately count towards the cumulative limits** in the operator's system (CAO 48.1; verify current text). A student working towards a CPL who flies privately on weekends and trains with a school during the week has to declare the private hours, because fatigue does not care which logbook column the hours went in.

So if the exam asks for the rules "pertaining to flight and duty time limitations for PPL licence holders", the answer is: **CAO 48.1 does not impose limits on private operations, but pilots must still be fit for flight and must comply with any operator or training organisation limits that apply to them.**

!!! example "Worked example: may she fly this afternoon?"
    Dana is a PPL holder and an emergency department nurse. She finished a 12-hour night shift at 0730 and has slept three hours. She plans to fly her own aeroplane from Murrayfield to Cervantes at 1400, a private flight.

    1. Prescribed flight and duty limits: none apply to a private operation, so **no rule on hours stops her**.
    2. Fitness for flight: after a night shift and three hours' sleep she is very likely fatigued to an extent that affects safety, so the **general fitness rule says do not fly**.
    3. If she were also a student in a CPL course, the school's fatigue rules would apply to her training flights, and today's private flight would count in that system.

    The absence of a prescribed limit is not a clearance. The decision rests with Dana, and the safe decision is to go tomorrow.

??? check "Check yourself: does CAO 48.1 limit how many hours a PPL holder may fly on a private weekend trip?"
    No. CAO 48.1 sets limits for commercial operations; private operations have no prescribed limits. The pilot must still not fly while fatigued to an extent that affects safety, and must count those hours if they also fly under an operator's fatigue system.

## Bringing it together

Tom holds a PPL(A) with the single-engine class rating, the night VFR rating and the retractable undercarriage endorsement, on a Basic Class 2 medical. His flight review was 18 months ago. He wants to fly three friends from Jandakot to Kalbarri on Friday afternoon, returning after dark, in a retractable single, with the friends paying for everything because "it's their trip".

Work through the layers. Licence and airspace: a PPL, so the Perth zone and the distance are no problem. Aeroplane: single-engine class rating and retractable undercarriage endorsement; if the aeroplane also has a constant-speed propeller he needs that endorsement too. Flight review: 18 months, current. Night: he holds the rating, but the **Basic Class 2 keeps him in day VFR**, so the return after dark is out whatever his night recency. Passengers: three is within the Basic Class 2 limit of 5. Money: friends paying everything is not equal cost sharing, so the flight as proposed is not a private operation. The fix is a four-way equal split of the direct costs and a return before last light. Every layer had to say yes, and two of them said no.

??? check "Check yourself: in the scenario, which single change would let Tom use his night rating, and why?"
    A Class 2 medical. The night rating and his recency would allow night flight, but the Basic Class 2 limits him to day VFR, and the tighter of the licence and medical limits applies.

## Key points

- **2.2.1(a)** The PPL(A) lets you fly as pilot in command or co-pilot in private operations, with passengers, anywhere in Australia, in all airspace, at controlled aerodromes and above 10,000 ft, in any aeroplane your class or type rating and design feature endorsements cover. Night needs the night VFR rating, IMC an instrument rating, aerobatics and formation their endorsements; over 5,700 kg or a turbojet needs a type rating. No hire or reward, only equal sharing of direct costs. Class 2 or Basic Class 2 (day VFR, below 10,000 ft, up to 5 passengers).
- **2.2.1(b)** 3 take-offs and 3 landings in 90 days for day passengers; 3 at night in 90 days for night passengers, with the rating; flight review within 24 months, and a licence or rating flight test counts as one; and always the general competency rule.
- **2.2.1(c)** Private (Part 91), flight training (Parts 141 and 142), aerial work (Part 138), air transport (Parts 119, 121, 133, 135). If anyone pays beyond an equal share of direct costs, it is not private and a PPL is not enough.
- **2.2.2** CAO 48.1 limits commercial crews, not private operations; a PPL holder must still not fly fatigued, and follows an operator's limits, counting private hours, when flying under one.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Know the recency triplet: 3 take-offs and landings in 90 days for day passengers, the same at night for night passengers, and a flight review every 24 months. And know the boundary of private flying: equal cost sharing is allowed, anything for hire or reward is not. In scenario questions, check each layer in turn (licence, rating, design features, conditions, medical, recency, money) rather than stopping at the first one that looks right. Confirm the current CASR Part 61 wording and the cost-sharing conditions before the exam.
