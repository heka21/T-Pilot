## What the exam expects (PFRA 2.1)

- **2.1.1** Describe how you obtain the aviation publications and why keeping them amended matters.
- **2.1.2** Given a question of operational significance, pick the right reference (CAR, CAO, AIP Book, CAAP or AC) and extract the current answer from it.
- **2.1.3** Decode what ERSA, a NOTAM and an AIP Supplement tell you.
- **2.1.4** Understand the terms and abbreviations in AIP GEN that matter for VFR flight.

## Why this matters

As an RPL holder you learned which document holds which kind of rule (see [RFRC 2.1](/notes/RFRC/2.1)). As a PPL holder you will plan a flight from Jandakot to Kalbarri, or across the Wheatbelt to Kalgoorlie, through restricted areas, past aerodromes you have never seen and into places where the only information is an ERSA entry and a set of NOTAMs written in abbreviations. Nobody is standing beside you to translate. The PPLA exam reflects that: it hands you extracts from the AIP, ERSA and NOTAM and expects you to find the answer in them quickly and correctly, with times in UTC and abbreviations undecoded. This lesson is about using the documents, not just naming them: getting them, keeping them current, finding your way around them, and reading what they say.

## Where the documents come from, and why they go out of date

Runways are resurfaced and shortened, frequencies change, a control zone boundary moves, a new restricted area appears around a mine site. Every change has to reach every pilot at the same moment, or two pilots will be flying to different versions of the same airspace. The publishing system exists to solve that problem.

There are two publishers, and the split follows the line between law and information you met at RPL level.

**The law** (the Civil Aviation Act, the Civil Aviation Regulations (CAR), the Civil Aviation Safety Regulations (CASR), the Manuals of Standards and the Civil Aviation Orders (CAO)) is published free on the **Federal Register of Legislation** and on **CASA's website**. CASA also publishes the material that explains the law: the plain English guides, the **Advisory Circulars (AC)** and the older **Civil Aviation Advisory Publications (CAAP)**, which are being replaced by ACs over time.

**The aeronautical information** (the **AIP Book**, **ERSA**, the **DAP**, the **charts**, the **AIP Supplements (SUP)** and the **Aeronautical Information Circulars (AIC)**) is published by **Airservices Australia**. You can buy it in print on subscription, with amendments delivered to you on the AIRAC cycle, or download it free from the **Airservices AIP website**.

Two more sources are for the day of the flight. **NOTAMs** and pre-flight briefings come from **NAIPS**, the National Aeronautical Information Processing System, used online or through the app, and from the Airservices flight briefing services. **Weather** comes from the Bureau of Meteorology aviation service and through NAIPS.

![Where does each kind of document sit, and who publishes it?](diagram:aviation-law-hierarchy "The Act, regulations, MOS and CAOs come from the Federal Register and CASA; the AIP family from Airservices; guidance such as ACs and the VFRG from CASA.")

### The AIRAC cycle

If every amendment took effect the day it was written, a pilot could never be sure their charts were current. So the AIP is amended on the international **AIRAC cycle** (aeronautical information regulation and control): a fixed **28-day** schedule of effective dates, with major amendments typically **twice a year** (AIP GEN 0; verify the current amendment pattern). Because the dates are agreed internationally and published in advance, charts and procedures change on known days worldwide, and every pilot and controller switches to the new information at the same moment.

### Why an out-of-date document is dangerous

An out-of-date document is not a slightly worse document; it is a wrong one that looks right. It can give you:

- the **wrong frequency**, so your calls go to nobody;
- the **wrong airspace boundary**, so you infringe a control zone while believing you are clear of it;
- a **runway that no longer exists** at the length you planned on, or one now closed;
- a **changed procedure** at an aerodrome you are joining;
- and, in the exam room, **lost marks**: the permitted materials must be **current for the date of the exam**.

Before each flight, then, check that the AIP and ERSA you are using are current, read the SUPs and AICs that apply to your flight, and get the NOTAMs. The NOTAMs are last because they are the newest layer: they carry what changed after the printed documents were issued.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    A current ERSA is not the end of the check. ERSA is only current to its effective date; anything that changed since then, or anything temporary, is in a NOTAM or a SUP. An option that says "ERSA is current, so no further check is needed" is wrong.

??? check "Check yourself: why are AIP amendments made on fixed AIRAC dates rather than as soon as each change is ready?"
    So that every pilot and controller switches to the new information at the same, known moment. Changes on random dates would leave people working from different versions of the same airspace or procedure.

## Choosing the right reference

At RPL level the question was "is this law, information or guidance?". At PPL level the exam goes one step further and names the specific document families: the **CAR**, the **CAO**, the **AIP Book** and the **CAAP** (or its replacement, the AC), alongside the CASR Parts you already know. The skill is the same: work out what *kind* of fact you need, and the document follows.

![Which document answers which question?](diagram:where-to-find-it "Rules about you are in Part 61 and 67, rules about how you fly in Part 91 and its MOS, facts about places in ERSA, today's changes in NOTAM, the aircraft's state in the maintenance release.")

### What each family holds

The **CASR** holds most of the modern rules. **Part 61 and the Part 61 MOS** answer questions about your licence: privileges, recency, flight reviews. **Part 67** answers questions about your medical. **Part 91 and the Part 91 MOS** answer questions about how you fly: rules of the air, VMC, fuel, oxygen, minimum heights and the passenger rules. CASA's plain English guide to Part 91 makes it readable.

The **CAR 1988** is the older set of regulations still in force where the CASR has not replaced it. For a private pilot its important corner is **Part 4A**, the maintenance release and daily inspections, and **Schedule 8**, the list of maintenance a pilot may carry out on their own aircraft (CAR 1988 Part 4A and Schedule 8; verify current text, because the maintenance rules are part of the CAR-to-CASR transition).

The **Civil Aviation Orders** are what remains of the orders made under the CAR. There are now few that touch private flying, so a CAO is rarely the answer to a private operations question, but it remains on the exam's list of documents.

The **AIP Book** is the operational handbook, in three parts, and knowing which part to open saves minutes in the exam:

- **GEN** (general): units, abbreviations, services, the way the AIP itself is organised and amended.
- **ENR** (en route): the rules as they apply in flight, airspace, altimetry, cruising levels, radio procedures, flight planning, navigation, charts and search and rescue.
- **AD** (aerodromes): aerodrome information and standards.

**ERSA** is the book you carry: its sections are **GEN** (general), **FAC** (facilities, the aerodrome entries), **PRD** (prohibited, restricted and danger areas), **EMERG** (emergency procedures) and **NAV/COMM** (navigation aids and communications).

**AIP Supplements and NOTAMs** carry temporary changes to all of the above.

**Advisory Circulars** (the AC 91 series for general operations), the remaining **CAAPs** and the **VFRG** are guidance: they explain how to comply, but they are not the rule itself.

| Question | Reference |
|---|---|
| Licence privileges, recency, flight reviews | CASR Part 61 and the Part 61 MOS |
| Medical requirements | CASR Part 67 |
| Rules of the air, VMC, fuel, oxygen, minimum heights, passenger rules | CASR Part 91 and the Part 91 MOS (plain English guide for readability) |
| Maintenance release, daily inspections, pilot maintenance | CAR 1988 Part 4A and Schedule 8 (pilot maintenance) |
| Remaining orders | Civil Aviation Orders, now few for private flying |
| Airspace, altimetry, cruising levels, radio procedures, flight planning, SAR | AIP Book: GEN, ENR and AD |
| Aerodrome data, PRD areas, emergency procedures | ERSA |
| Temporary changes to AIP information | AIP Supplements and NOTAMs |
| Guidance and interpretation | Advisory Circulars (AC 91-xx series), CAAPs, the VFRG |

!!! example "Worked example: which document answers this?"
    You are planning a private flight from Murrayfield to Geraldton in your syndicate's aeroplane. Four questions come up. For each, ask what kind of fact it is.

    1. *What radio calls do I make when I enter and leave the Geraldton CTAF, and how is a flight plan or SARTIME lodged?* Radio procedures and flight planning are en route rules: **AIP Book, ENR**.
    2. *What is the runway length and surface at Geraldton, and is there fuel?* A fact about a place: **ERSA FAC**.
    3. *The aeroplane's oil filler cap is cracked. May I, as the owner-pilot, replace it myself?* Maintenance a pilot may do: **CAR 1988 Schedule 8**, read with Part 4A for how the work is recorded on the maintenance release.
    4. *Is there anything happening at Geraldton this week, such as works or a closed taxiway?* A temporary change: **NOTAM** (or a SUP if it is a long, planned change).

    None of the four answers is "the VFRG": it explains several of them, but none is set out there.

### Extracting the answer

The second half of element 2.1.2 is extracting the *current* information, and the method is the same every time:

1. **Identify the kind of fact**, and so the document and the part of it (AIP ENR, ERSA FAC, ERSA PRD).
2. **Find the entry**, using the contents and the index. In ERSA the aerodromes are alphabetical; in the AIP each section is numbered (ENR 1.1, ENR 5.1).
3. **Read all of it**, including the notes and remarks, because conditions and exceptions live there.
4. **Check for later changes**: a SUP or NOTAM that alters what you have just read.

![For a restricted area, which document gives which part of the answer?](diagram:flight-documents-flow "The chart gives the boundaries and limits, ERSA PRD and AIP ENR 5.1 the hours and the controlling authority, NOTAM any activation or change.")

??? check "Check yourself: where would you look for the rule that sets the fuel you must carry, and where for the fuel available at the aerodrome you are flying to?"
    The rule is in CASR Part 91 and the Part 91 MOS, because it is a rule about how you fly. The fuel available is a fact about a place, so it is in the aerodrome's ERSA FAC entry, subject to any NOTAM.

## Reading an ERSA entry

Everything you need before landing at an aerodrome you have never seen (runway length and surface, frequency, fuel, whether you need permission) is in its ERSA **FAC** entry. The entry always runs in the same order, so once you know the order you can find any item in seconds.

![What is in each block of an ERSA aerodrome entry?](diagram:ersa-entry-anatomy "The header gives elevation, position, variation, time zone and operator; the runway table gives designator, dimensions in metres, surface, slope, strength and lighting; then comms, nav aids and remarks.")

The order is:

1. **Header**: name, identifier, position, elevation, magnetic variation, time zone and operator.
2. **Operational information**, including **PPR** (prior permission required: you must contact the operator before you arrive).
3. **The runway table**: designator, length and width in **metres**, surface, slope, lighting and pavement strength.
4. **Aerodrome diagram.**
5. **Obstacles.**
6. **Communications and navigation aids**, including the **CTAF** (common traffic advisory frequency).
7. **Weather services**: **AWIS** (automatic weather information service) or **ATIS** (automatic terminal information service).
8. **Local traffic regulations and noise abatement.**
9. **Remarks.**

The abbreviations you will meet in nearly every entry: **CTAF**; **PAL**, pilot activated lighting, which you switch on by keying your microphone on a published frequency; **RWS**, the runway strip, the cleared area around the runway itself; **PCN**, the pavement classification number, the runway's published strength; **AVGAS** and **AVTUR** availability (AVGAS for piston engines, AVTUR for turbines); **HJ**, sunrise to sunset; and **H24**, continuous.

!!! example "Worked example: extracting from an ERSA entry"
    An invented entry for a Wheatbelt aerodrome (not a real one) reads, in part: elevation 1,050 ft; runway 05/23, 1,250 m long, 18 m wide, sealed, PAL; runway 15/33, 820 m, natural surface; CTAF; AVGAS available; remarks: "PPR for ACFT above 5,700 kg. Kangaroos on and in vicinity of RWY HN."

    1. *Can you land there without phoning ahead in a light aeroplane?* PPR applies only above 5,700 kg in this entry, so **no call is required** for you, though a courtesy call is always good practice.
    2. *What circuit height do you fly?* Standard 1,000 ft above aerodrome level: $1050 + 1000 = $ **2,050 ft** on the QNH.
    3. *Can you use the runway at night?* Runway 05/23 has **PAL**, so its lights can be activated; 15/33 has none listed.
    4. *When is the kangaroo hazard worst?* **HN**, the hours of night, sunset to sunrise.

    The answers came from reading every line, including the remarks, which is where the PPR condition and the wildlife warning were.

??? check "Check yourself: an ERSA entry gives a runway as 1,100 m long. Is that in feet or metres, and where else in the entry is height given in feet?"
    Runway length and width in ERSA are in metres. Elevation in the header, and obstacle heights, are in feet, which is why a quick read can mix them up.

## Decoding a NOTAM

A **NOTAM** (notice to airmen) is how a change reaches you between the printed issues: a runway closed for works this week, a navigation aid unserviceable, a restricted area activated for a military exercise, a crane near the approach. Because there may be dozens on your route, they are written in a fixed, compressed format, and you have to unpack it.

Each NOTAM carries, in order:

- the **identifier and series**, so it can be referred to and later cancelled;
- the **location**, as an ICAO four-letter code, such as **YPJT** for Jandakot;
- the **validity period**, in **UTC**: **B** is the time it takes effect, **C** the time it ends, each written as a ten-figure date-time group (year, month, day, hour, minute). C may be **PERM** (permanent, until the documents are amended) or carry **EST** (estimated: the end time may change, and a further NOTAM will confirm it);
- the **subject and text**, in abbreviations from AIP GEN 2.2.

Common content is runway or taxiway closures, unserviceable lighting or navigation aids, airspace activations, temporary restricted areas, cranes and obstacles, aerodrome works and changed frequencies. **Head office NOTAMs** cover a whole flight information region rather than one aerodrome. In NAIPS, a location or route briefing lists the NOTAMs relevant to your flight: read them all, and convert every validity time.

![How do you read a NOTAM?](diagram:notam-anatomy "B and C are ten-figure UTC date-time groups: add 8 hours for WST, and check whether your arrival falls inside the period.")

![How does a UTC time line up with Western Standard Time?](diagram:utc-and-wst-strip "WST is UTC plus 8 hours, so the WST date changes at 1600 UTC; an early-morning WST time is the previous day in UTC.")

The conversion is the part that catches people. Every aviation time is UTC unless stated otherwise. Western Australia is **UTC plus 8 hours** and does not use daylight saving, so you add 8 hours to a UTC time to get WST, and when the sum passes 2400 the WST date moves to the next day.

!!! example "Worked example: when does this NOTAM apply in local time?"
    An invented NOTAM for Geraldton (YGEL) reads: RWY 03/21 CLSD DUE WIP, B 2611140100, C 2611150930.

    1. Decode the text: runway 03/21 is **closed** due to **work in progress**.
    2. B: 26 11 14 0100 means year 2026, 14 November, **0100 UTC**.
    3. Convert: $0100 + 8\ \text{h} = \mathbf{0900\ WST}$ on 14 November.
    4. C: 26 11 15 0930 means 15 November, **0930 UTC**.
    5. Convert: $0930 + 8\ \text{h} = \mathbf{1730\ WST}$ on 15 November.

    The runway is closed from 0900 WST on the 14th to 1730 WST on the 15th. A planned arrival at 1600 WST on the 15th meets a closed runway.

Now try one that crosses midnight. A NOTAM for a danger area reads B 2612022130, C 2612030500. When is it active in WST?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    B is 2 December, 2130 UTC. Adding 8 hours gives 2930, which is 0530 on the next day: **0530 WST on 3 December**. C is 3 December, 0500 UTC, plus 8 hours: **1300 WST on 3 December**. So the area is active from 0530 to 1300 WST on 3 December, a morning activity, even though the B time is printed with the 2nd.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    Reading the NOTAM date as the local date. An early-morning WST start appears in UTC as the evening of the previous day. Convert the whole date-time group, not just the hours.

## AIP Supplements and Circulars

Some changes are too big for a NOTAM: an air show that needs a temporary airspace arrangement and a page of procedures, a runway works program that runs for months with staged closures, a trial of a new procedure. These go in an **AIP Supplement (SUP)**, which carries a **temporary or advance change to the AIP that is too long for a NOTAM**. SUPs are numbered by year (for example H23/26), and the current ones are listed in a checklist so you can see which are in force. A SUP is **part of the AIP**, and it is **permitted in the exam** with it.

An **Aeronautical Information Circular (AIC)** is different in kind: it carries explanatory and advance information that does not change the AIP itself, such as background to a coming change or a safety message.

A simple way to place the three: the AIP and ERSA are the base layer; a SUP is a long temporary or advance overlay; a NOTAM is a short or urgent overlay, and the newest one you read.

??? check "Check yourself: a two-week military exercise needs temporary restricted areas and several pages of procedures. Would you expect it in a NOTAM or a SUP?"
    A SUP, because the change is too long and detailed for a NOTAM. A NOTAM may still be issued to activate the areas or to draw attention to the SUP.

## AIP GEN terms and abbreviations

**AIP GEN 2.2** is the abbreviation list and **GEN 2.1** the units and conventions. You do not need all of them; you need the ones a VFR pilot meets in ERSA, NOTAMs and forecasts. They fall into groups, and learning them by group is easier than learning a list.

**Heights and altimetry.** **AGL** and **AMSL** say what a height is measured from: above ground level or above mean sea level. **HGT** is height (above a stated datum, often the ground) and **ALT** altitude (above mean sea level). **QNH** is the altimeter setting that makes the altimeter read altitude; **QFE** is the setting that makes it read height above the aerodrome; **QNE** refers to the standard setting, 1013. **FL**, a flight level, is pressure altitude in hundreds of feet with 1013 set. The altimetry itself is in [RFRC 2.3](/notes/RFRC/2.3).

**Airspace.** **CTA** is a control area, **CTR** a control zone (controlled airspace from the surface around an aerodrome), **CTAF** the common traffic advisory frequency and **OCTA** outside controlled airspace. **FIR**, **FIA** and **FIS** are the flight information region, area and service. **PRD** is prohibited, restricted and danger, and **RA1, RA2, RA3** are the conditional status levels of a restricted area. **LL** and **UL** are the lower and upper limits of a piece of airspace. **MSA** is minimum sector altitude and **LSALT** lowest safe altitude.

**Flight rules and conditions.** **VMC** and **IMC** are visual and instrument meteorological conditions; **VFR** and **IFR** visual and instrument flight rules.

**Times.** **UTC** is Coordinated Universal Time, and all aviation times are UTC unless stated. **ETA**, **ETD**, **EET** and **ATD** are estimated time of arrival and departure, estimated elapsed time, and actual time of departure. **HJ** is sunrise to sunset, **HN** night, **H24** continuous and **HO** hours of operation.

**Search and rescue.** **SARTIME** is the search and rescue time you nominate, **SAR** search and rescue, and **JRCC** the Joint Rescue Coordination Centre.

**Information products and services.** NOTAM, SUP, AIC, PPR, PAL, AWIS and ATIS you have met above; the forecast and report products (TAF, METAR, GAF and the rest) are taught in the meteorology lessons.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| AGL, AMSL, HGT, ALT | Above ground level, above mean sea level, height, altitude |
| QNH, QFE, QNE | Altimeter setting for altitude; for height above the aerodrome; the standard setting 1013 |
| FL | Flight level, pressure altitude in hundreds of feet with 1013 set |
| CTA, CTR, CTAF, OCTA | Control area, control zone, common traffic advisory frequency, outside controlled airspace |
| FIR, FIA, FIS | Flight information region, area, service |
| PRD, RA1/2/3 | Prohibited, restricted, danger; restricted area conditional status |
| VMC, IMC, VFR, IFR | Visual meteorological conditions, instrument meteorological conditions, visual and instrument flight rules |
| SARTIME, SAR, JRCC | Search and rescue time, search and rescue, Joint Rescue Coordination Centre |
| ETA, ETD, EET, ATD | Estimated time of arrival and departure, estimated elapsed time, actual time of departure |
| TAF, METAR, SPECI, GAF, GPWT, SIGMET, AIRMET | The forecast and report products (see the meteorology notes) |
| HJ, HN, H24, HO | Sunrise to sunset, night, continuous, hours of operation |
| LL, UL | Lower limit, upper limit (of airspace) |
| MSA, LSALT | Minimum sector altitude, lowest safe altitude |
| NOTAM, SUP, AIC | Notice to airmen, AIP Supplement, Aeronautical Information Circular |
| PPR, PAL, AWIS, ATIS | Prior permission required, pilot-activated lighting, automatic weather information service, automatic terminal information service |
| UTC | Coordinated Universal Time; all aviation times are UTC unless stated |

!!! warning "Common trap"
    HJ and HN are easy to swap. J is for "jour", day: HJ is sunrise to sunset. HN is the night hours. And an altitude (ALT, on QNH) is not a height (HGT, AGL): a 1,500 ft obstacle near an aerodrome at 1,050 ft elevation sticks up only 450 ft.

??? check "Check yourself: an ERSA remark says a restricted area is RA2 and active H24. What does each part tell you?"
    RA2 is the restricted area's conditional status level, which tells you how likely a clearance through it is. H24 means it is active continuously, day and night, unless a NOTAM changes that.

## Bringing it together

A Friday evening at home, planning Saturday's flight from Jandakot to Kalbarri. First the currency check: your ERSA's effective date is the latest one on the Airservices site, so it is current. In the AIP ENR you confirm how to lodge your SARTIME. The ERSA FAC entry for Kalbarri gives the runway in metres, the surface, the CTAF and whether AVGAS is available; you read the remarks for PPR and wildlife. Your track passes near a restricted area: the VNC shows its limits, the ERSA PRD section its hours and controlling authority. Then NAIPS: a NOTAM activates that restricted area from B 2611212200 to C 2611220400. Adding 8 hours puts that at 0600 to 1200 WST on Saturday the 22nd, right across your planned 0830 departure, so you route around it. A SUP lists an air show at Geraldton next month; not your problem this weekend. Five documents, one flight, each one answering a question the others could not.

??? check "Check yourself: in the scenario, why was the NOTAM the last thing checked rather than the first?"
    Because it is the newest layer and can change anything in the printed documents. Reading the base documents first tells you what normally applies; the NOTAMs then tell you what is different today.

## Key points

- **2.1.1** Law (Act, CAR, CASR, MOS, CAO) is published free on the Federal Register of Legislation and CASA's site, with ACs and CAAPs; the AIP Book, ERSA, DAP, charts, SUPs and AICs come from Airservices, in print by subscription or free online; NOTAMs and briefings from NAIPS; weather from the Bureau. The AIP is amended on the 28-day AIRAC cycle so changes take effect on known dates worldwide. Out-of-date documents give wrong frequencies, boundaries and runways, and the permitted exam materials must be current.
- **2.1.2** Licence: Part 61. Medical: Part 67. How you fly: Part 91 and its MOS. Maintenance release and pilot maintenance: CAR Part 4A and Schedule 8. Airspace, altimetry, radio, flight planning, SAR: AIP Book (GEN, ENR, AD). Places, PRD and emergencies: ERSA. Temporary changes: SUP and NOTAM. Guidance: AC, CAAP, VFRG. Read the whole entry, then check for later changes.
- **2.1.3** ERSA FAC: header, PPR, runway table in metres, diagram, obstacles, comms and nav aids, weather, local rules, remarks. NOTAM: identifier, ICAO location, B and C times in UTC (PERM, EST), text in abbreviations; WST is UTC plus 8. SUP: a long temporary or advance change, numbered by year, part of the AIP and permitted in the exam.
- **2.1.4** AIP GEN 2.2 is the abbreviation list. Know the height, airspace, time, SAR and service abbreviations; HJ is day, HN night, H24 continuous; all times are UTC unless stated.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    The PPLA exam gives you AIP, ERSA and NOTAM extracts and expects you to navigate them quickly. Know where each kind of information lives (AIP GEN, ENR, AD; ERSA GEN, FAC, PRD, EMERG, NAV/COMM), read NOTAM validity times as UTC and add 8 hours for WST, watching for the date change, and remember that an AIP Supplement is part of the AIP and permitted in the exam. When a question asks where a rule is set out, the answer is the regulation or MOS, not the guidance that explains it.
