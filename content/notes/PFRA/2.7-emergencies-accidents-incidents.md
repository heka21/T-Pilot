## What the exam expects (PFRA 2.7)

- **2.7.1** Find an emergency procedure in the ERSA and apply what it says.
- **2.7.2** State when a pilot may declare a mercy flight, and recognise the occasions when one must not be flown.
- **2.7.3** Find in the AIP what a pilot must do to notify an accident or incident.
- **2.7.5** Give examples of hazards to navigation that a pilot must report.

(Element 2.7.4 is reserved in the MOS.)

## Why this matters

As an RPL holder you learned the shape of this subject: what an accident is, that the ATSB wants to hear about it, and that the emergency procedures live in the pink pages of ERSA (see [RFRC 2.7](/notes/RFRC/2.7)). The PPL takes you further from home, and the questions become harder ones. Halfway to Kalbarri the radio dies and you need the procedure, not the name of the book it is in. A station owner near Wiluna asks you to fly an injured stockman to Meekatharra at dusk, and you have to know whether the law allows it and whether you should. A flock of corellas settles on the runway at Northam, and you are the only person who has seen it. This lesson teaches the four things the PPL exam adds: reading ERSA EMERG for the actual procedure, the mercy flight, the pilot's full set of reporting duties, and the hazards you are expected to pass on.

## Using ERSA EMERG in flight

The **EMERG section** of ERSA (the **En Route Supplement Australia**) is printed on pink paper and placed where you can find it by feel. Its organisation follows the order in which a pilot in trouble needs things: first how to tell someone, then what to do about the specific problem, then what happens afterwards if you end up on the ground somewhere you did not plan to be. The RPL exam asked you to name the document. The PPL exam hands you an extract from it and asks you to use it, so you need to know what each part holds and what it tells you to do.

![Which transponder code tells ATC what?](diagram:transponder-emergency-codes "7700 emergency, 7600 radio failure, 7500 unlawful interference: the three codes sit in ERSA EMERG beside the calls and procedures they go with.")

### Telling someone: distress and urgency

The first part covers the two levels of emergency call. **MAYDAY** is the **distress** call: grave and imminent danger, and you need immediate help. **PAN PAN** is the **urgency** call: a problem that needs attention, without immediate danger. The difference matters because a MAYDAY clears the frequency and commits help at once, while a PAN PAN warns everyone and lets the system prepare.

ERSA gives the format of the call, and the order is built around what a rescuer needs first: your **callsign**, the **nature of the emergency**, your **intentions**, your **position**, **altitude** and **heading**, and the **persons on board**. Think of it as answering, in order, who you are, what is wrong, what you are going to do, where to find you and how many people to look for. If the message is cut off after the first few items, the most useful ones have already gone out.

It also tells you where to make the call: on the **frequency you are already using**, because someone there is already listening to you, and then on **121.5 MHz**, the international emergency frequency, if that gets no answer.

Alongside the calls are the **transponder codes**: **7700 emergency**, **7600 radio failure**, **7500 unlawful interference**. Each one tells a radar controller something without a word. Setting 7700 makes your return stand out on every screen that can see you; 7600 says that you cannot hear them but are following the radio failure procedure; 7500 says what you may not be able to say aloud.

### Radio failure

The radio failure procedure is the one you are most likely to use, and ERSA splits it by where you are, because what other people expect of you depends on the airspace.

In **Class G as a VFR flight**, nobody is controlling you, so the procedure is about staying safe and staying predictable: **continue, remain in VMC, land at the nearest suitable aerodrome, broadcast blind** (transmit your calls as normal in case your transmitter works even though your receiver does not), and **squawk 7600**.

In **controlled airspace**, ATC has a plan for you and has cleared other traffic around it. The procedure is to **follow the last clearance** you received, fly the **expected procedures**, and watch for **light signals** from the tower. The logic is that the controller can only keep others clear of you if you do what they were expecting.

### Interception, ditching, SAR and signals

The remaining parts are the ones you hope never to open: the **interception** signals and the responses required (follow, rock your wings, land), which the security lesson covers in detail ([PFRA 2.8](/notes/PFRA/2.8)); **ditching and forced landing** preparation, the sequence and survival afterwards; the **search and rescue** material, covering the SAR phases, how to operate the **ELT** (emergency locator transmitter) and the **ground-to-air visual signals**; the **light and visual signals** from a control tower; and the **emergency contact numbers** for the ATSB accident line, Airservices and the JRCC (Joint Rescue Coordination Centre).

![What is in ERSA EMERG?](diagram:ersa-emerg-contents "Tell someone, deal with the problem, then survive and be found: grouped by purpose, so check the page order in your current ERSA.")

!!! example "Worked example: extracting a procedure from ERSA EMERG"
    You are VFR at 4,500 ft in Class G between Jandakot and Bunbury when the radio goes silent. The intercom still works; no transmissions are heard on any frequency. You open ERSA EMERG.

    1. Find the right part: the problem is the radio, so **radio failure**, and you are **VFR in Class G**.
    2. Read the actions: **continue, remain in VMC, land at the nearest suitable aerodrome, broadcast blind**.
    3. Read the transponder entry: **squawk 7600**, so any radar that sees you knows the problem without a call.
    4. Apply it: you stay visual, choose the nearest suitable aerodrome on your route, and make every normal call "blind", because your transmitter may still be working.

    The skill the exam tests is matching the situation (which problem, which airspace, VFR or IFR) to the right paragraph before reading the actions.

Now try one. Twenty minutes later, a smell of burning comes from behind the panel and a little smoke appears. You decide to land at the next aerodrome but there is no immediate danger to the aeroplane yet. Which call does ERSA give you, and what goes in it?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    A problem needing attention without immediate danger is an urgency situation: **PAN PAN**. The content follows the ERSA format: callsign, nature of the emergency (smoke from behind the panel), intentions (landing at the next aerodrome), position, altitude, heading and persons on board. Make it on the current frequency, then 121.5 MHz if that gets no answer. If the smoke becomes a fire, upgrade to **MAYDAY** and squawk **7700**.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    In controlled airspace, radio failure does not mean "leave and land at the nearest aerodrome". The procedure there is to follow the last clearance and the expected procedures, because ATC has separated other traffic from the path it expects you to fly. The "nearest suitable aerodrome, remain VMC" procedure is the Class G one.

??? check "Check yourself: why does the MAYDAY format put callsign, nature and intentions before position?"
    So that the most useful information is sent first. A rescuer needs to know who you are and what kind of help you need, and the position, altitude, heading and persons on board follow. If the call is cut short, the essentials have already gone out.

## Mercy flights

### What a mercy flight is for

Picture a cattle station east of Meekatharra. A child has been badly hurt, the road is cut by floodwater, the Flying Doctor is committed elsewhere, and the station has an aeroplane and a private pilot. The flight that would save the child cannot be made inside the normal rules: it has to leave as the sun goes down, and the pilot has no night rating.

That situation is what a **mercy flight** is for. It is a flight **made to relieve a person in grave danger or urgent need**, such as carrying a critically ill person out of a remote area, **where the flight cannot comply fully with the normal rules**. The departure from the rules might be flight **at night by a VFR-only pilot**, flight in **weather below VFR minima**, or flight beyond the **normal fuel or aircraft limits**.

The idea behind it is a weighing of risks. The rules exist because flying outside them raises the chance of an accident. When a life is at stake on the ground, the law accepts that a pilot may sometimes reasonably choose the higher risk in the air, and the mercy flight gives that choice a legal footing.

### When you may declare one

The **pilot in command, or the operator**, may declare a mercy flight when **all** of these are true:

- the flight is **necessary to relieve a person's suffering or to save life**;
- **no other means of transport is available in time**;
- the pilot in command has **considered the risks** and decided the flight **can be made with an acceptable level of safety**;
- **CASA or ATS is notified** of the declaration and of the departures from the rules it involves, **before the flight where possible** (ATS passes the declaration to CASA).

Read the list as a set of tests, each closing off a way the provision could be abused. The first excludes convenience. The second excludes cases where an ambulance, a road trip or the Flying Doctor would do. The third makes it a judgement, not a reflex. The fourth means the system knows a non-standard flight is under way and can give it priority and watch for it.

### When you must not

A mercy flight **must not** be undertaken:

- when the **risk to the aircraft and its occupants outweighs the benefit**;
- when the **medical need could be met by other transport or by waiting**;
- when the **pilot is not competent for the conditions**: an instrument-rated pilot and aircraft should be used for IMC;
- simply to **carry a sick person who is not in grave danger**.

One sentence sums it up: **declaring a mercy flight does not change the physics or the weather; it changes the legal position only.** A VFR pilot who flies into cloud on a mercy flight loses control in the same time as one who flies into cloud on a sightseeing trip. If the honest risk assessment says the likely outcome is a second accident, there are now two casualties instead of one.

![May this be a mercy flight?](diagram:mercy-flight-decision "Every condition must hold, and any one 'no' means it must not be flown; if no rule needs breaking, there is nothing to declare.")

!!! example "Worked example: may this be a mercy flight?"
    Test each case against the four conditions, then against the "must not" list.

    1. *A farmer near Southern Cross has a suspected heart attack. The ambulance is 90 minutes away; you could fly him to Merredin in 30 minutes, in daylight and good weather, within all the normal rules.* No rule needs to be broken, so there is **nothing to declare**: it is an ordinary private flight carrying a passenger, though you may use the urgency procedures if needed.
    2. *A station hand near Wiluna has a severe crush injury. The RFDS cannot reach the station before tomorrow, the road is cut, and the flight to Meekatharra would end 30 minutes after last light. You have no night rating, the sky is clear, there is a moon, and the destination has lighting.* Grave need, no other transport in time, a departure from the rules (night flight by a VFR-only pilot) that you judge acceptable after weighing the risks. This **can be a mercy flight**, declared to ATS before departure.
    3. *Same patient, but a band of low cloud and rain lies across the route and you hold no instrument rating.* The risk now outweighs the benefit and you are **not competent for the conditions**. It **must not** be flown; the need is for an instrument-rated crew or for waiting.

??? check "Check yourself: a friend's relative is unwell and wants to get to Perth tonight to see a specialist tomorrow. Your flight would end after last light and you have no night rating. Mercy flight?"
    No. The person is not in grave danger or urgent need, and the need can be met by other transport or by waiting until tomorrow. A mercy flight is for relieving suffering or saving life when nothing else will do in time.

## Notifying accidents and incidents: the pilot's duties

The RPL lesson taught the two categories and the 72 hours. At PPL level the exam asks you to extract the pilot's responsibilities as the AIP (GEN 3.6) and the ATSB rules set them out, which means knowing who must report, by what means, and what else must happen before and after.

![Accident or incident, and how is it reported?](diagram:accident-incident-reporting "Accidents and serious incidents: phone the ATSB as soon as practicable, then write within 72 hours. Routine reportable matters: write within 72 hours. Reports go to the ATSB, not CASA.")

### Who reports, and how fast

The duties fall on the **responsible persons**: the **pilot in command**, the **operator**, and others listed in the regulations. Naming several people is deliberate. The pilot is the obvious reporter, but after a serious accident the pilot may be injured or dead, and the report must still reach the ATSB. So **if the pilot is unable, the operator must report**.

There are two speeds, set by how serious the occurrence is:

- **Immediately reportable matters** (accidents and serious incidents): report to the **ATSB as soon as practicable**, **by phone on 1800 011 034, 24 hours**, then a **written report within 72 hours**.
- **Routine reportable matters** (other safety occurrences): a **written report within 72 hours** to the ATSB.

The phone call exists because evidence decays. Wreckage is moved, weather passes, and witnesses forget, so an investigation that might be needed must be able to start the same day.

### What else you must do

- **Preserve the aircraft and the site** until the ATSB releases it, **except to rescue people or protect property**. A fuel selector position or a bent control rod is evidence; moving them destroys it.
- **Report to the operator** and **record the defect in the maintenance release**, so that nobody flies the aeroplane before it has been dealt with (see [PFRA 2.1](/notes/PFRA/2.1) for the maintenance release).
- **Report airworthiness defects to CASA** through the **defect reporting system**. The ATSB investigates occurrences; CASA tracks defects across the fleet so that a fault in one aeroplane can be found in the others.
- **Airspace infringements, near misses and ATC-related occurrences are also reported by ATS**, but **the pilot's own report is still required**. The controller's report gives ATC's view; yours gives what happened in the cockpit, and the investigator needs both.

!!! example "Worked example: what are the pilot's duties after this occurrence?"
    You land a hired aeroplane at a farm strip near Narrogin. On rollout the left main gear leg collapses into a hidden hole; the wing tip strikes the ground and the wing spar is buckled. You and your passenger climb out unhurt.

    1. **Category.** Structural damage to the wing affecting its strength, between boarding and disembarking: an **accident**, so an **immediately reportable matter**.
    2. **Who.** You are the pilot in command and uninjured, so **you** report. (Had you been unable, the **operator**, the flying school that hired you the aeroplane, would have to.)
    3. **How and when.** **Phone the ATSB on 1800 011 034 as soon as practicable**, then the **written report within 72 hours**.
    4. **The site.** **Leave the aeroplane where it is** unless the ATSB releases it; the only exceptions are to rescue people or protect property.
    5. **The paperwork.** Tell the **operator**; the defect goes in the **maintenance release**, and the operator reports the airworthiness defect to **CASA**.

??? check "Check yourself: after an airspace infringement, the controller tells you they will file a report. Do you still need to report?"
    Yes. ATS reports airspace infringements, near misses and ATC-related occurrences, but the pilot's own report is still required. The two reports describe the same event from different seats, and the investigator needs both.

## Hazards to navigation

You are often the first, and sometimes the only, person to see a hazard. A controller cannot see a new mast from the tower; a NOTAM office cannot know the windsock blew away this morning. The system relies on pilots passing on what they see, and the rule is that a pilot who observes a **hazard to navigation** reports it **to ATS as soon as possible**, **by radio**, using the **SECURITE** prefix where the message is a safety broadcast, and **afterwards in writing if required**.

The examples fall into groups, and the groups are easier to remember than the list:

- **Things you could hit that the chart does not show:** an **unlit or unmarked obstacle** such as a crane, mast or wind turbine not on the chart, or an **obstacle light that has failed**.
- **Things on or near the runway:** **birds** concentrated on or near an aerodrome, and **bird strikes** (reported to the ATSB as well); **animals, vehicles or people** on a runway or landing area.
- **Aids that are not working:** **unserviceable navigation aids, runway lighting or a wind indicator**.
- **Weather nobody forecast:** **thunderstorms, severe turbulence, wind shear, dust storms, volcanic ash, a fog bank forming**.
- **Other things in the air:** **unmanned aircraft, model aircraft, kites or balloons** near an aerodrome or at altitude, and **laser illumination** of the cockpit.
- **Activity nobody told you about:** **smoke, bushfire activity** or a fire-fighting operation affecting the airspace, and **military activity, parachuting or gliding** not promulgated.

The common thread is that each one could catch out the next pilot, and each is something that pilot would not expect from the chart, the forecast or the NOTAMs. The point of the report is that **ATS can warn other aircraft and issue a NOTAM**, while **your SECURITE broadcast warns aircraft on the frequency at once**.

![Which hazards to navigation must you report?](diagram:hazard-to-navigation-groups "Six groups are easier to remember than the list: things to hit, the runway, failed aids, unforecast weather, things in the air, unannounced activity.")

!!! example "Worked example: is it a hazard, and what do you do?"
    You are joining the circuit at Wongan Hills on a summer afternoon.

    1. *A large flock of galahs is feeding on the threshold of the runway in use.* Birds concentrated on an aerodrome: a **hazard to navigation**. Broadcast on the CTAF straight away so others on the frequency know, and report to ATS. If you strike one, the **bird strike also goes to the ATSB**.
    2. *Ten miles south you saw a thick column of smoke from a new bushfire, with a water bomber working it.* **Bushfire activity and a fire-fighting operation affecting the airspace**: report to ATS so that it can warn other traffic and, if needed, issue a NOTAM.
    3. *The wind is gustier than the TAF suggested but within your limits.* A difference from the forecast that is not hazardous (no severe turbulence, no wind shear) is **not** in the hazard list; you may pass it on as useful information, but it is not a hazard report.

??? check "Check yourself: why report a failed obstacle light to ATS rather than just avoiding the mast yourself?"
    Because the next pilot, perhaps at night, does not know it has failed and expects the light to show them the mast. ATS can warn other aircraft and issue a NOTAM; your own avoidance protects only you.

## Bringing it together

A Sunday in autumn: you have flown a friend from Jandakot to a farm strip near Kulin. On the ground a neighbour arrives with news: a farm worker has collapsed with severe chest pain, the ambulance is two hours away by road, the nearest hospital with surgery is in Perth, and it is three hours to last light. You weigh it. The flight back to Jandakot is within the normal rules, in daylight, in good weather, so no mercy flight is needed: you simply carry the patient, and you consider a PAN PAN to get priority into Jandakot. Climbing out, you see a new wind turbine on a ridge west of the strip that is not on your chart, so you tell ATS on the area frequency. Half an hour later the radio fails. ERSA EMERG, radio failure: you are VFR, still in Class G, so you continue in VMC, squawk 7600, broadcast blind and plan the nearest suitable aerodrome rather than entering Jandakot's zone without a clearance. You land at Beverley, where the patient is collected by an ambulance that drove out to meet you. Taxiing in, a wheel drops into a soft patch and the propeller tips strike the ground. Nobody hurt, damage limited to the propeller: not an accident, but an incident that affected safety, so a written report to the ATSB within 72 hours, the defect in the maintenance release, and a call to the operator. Every element appeared, and each decision came from a reason you could explain.

??? check "Check yourself: in that scenario, why was the flight with the injured worker not a mercy flight?"
    Because it could be flown entirely within the normal rules: daylight, good weather, a qualified pilot. A mercy flight is a declaration that the flight departs from the rules for a person in grave danger; when no departure is needed there is nothing to declare.

## Key points

- **2.7.1** ERSA EMERG (pink pages): MAYDAY for distress, PAN PAN for urgency, in the order callsign, nature, intentions, position, altitude, heading, persons on board, on the current frequency then 121.5 MHz; 7700, 7600, 7500. Radio failure VFR in Class G: continue, remain VMC, land at the nearest suitable aerodrome, broadcast blind, squawk 7600; in controlled airspace follow the last clearance. Also interception, ditching, SAR, light signals and emergency contacts.
- **2.7.2** A mercy flight relieves grave danger or urgent need when the rules cannot be fully met: necessary to save life or relieve suffering, no other transport in time, risks judged acceptable, CASA or ATS notified. Never when the risk outweighs the benefit, other transport or waiting would do, the pilot is not competent for the conditions, or the person is not in grave danger. It changes the law, not the weather.
- **2.7.3** Responsible persons: the pilot in command, the operator if the pilot cannot. Immediately reportable: ATSB by phone 1800 011 034 as soon as practicable, written within 72 hours; routine: written within 72 hours. Preserve the site, tell the operator, defect in the maintenance release and to CASA; your report is required even if ATS also reports.
- **2.7.5** Report hazards to ATS at once by radio (SECURITE broadcast) and in writing if required: uncharted or unlit obstacles, birds and animals on runways, unserviceable aids and lighting, unforecast hazardous weather, drones, kites, balloons and lasers, bushfire smoke and unpromulgated activity.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    Expect an ERSA EMERG extract with a scenario: identify the problem and the airspace first, then read the action. Have the three codes by heart (7700, 7600, 7500), the ATSB reporting rule (phone as soon as practicable, then written within 72 hours; routine matters written within 72 hours), and the mercy flight conditions: grave danger, no other transport in time, acceptable risk, notified to ATS or CASA. Mercy flight distractors offer convenience or a non-urgent patient. Verify the mercy flight provisions and the ATSB number against the current Part 91, AIP and Transport Safety Investigation Regulations.
