## What the exam expects (PFRA 2.4)

- **2.4.1** Apply the rules on (a) the pilot's responsibilities before flight, (b) the meteorological minima at aerodromes, and (c) the carriage of cargo, sick and handicapped persons, parachutists, flotation and survival equipment, animals and dangerous goods.
- **2.4.2** State the requirements to test the radio before taxiing and to keep a listening watch.

## Why this matters

As an RPL holder you flew from an aerodrome you knew, in weather you could see, usually back to where you started. In [RFRC 2.4](/notes/RFRC/2.4) you learned the pilot in command's duties for that kind of flight, and every one of them still applies. The PPL changes the kind of flight. Jandakot to Esperance is more than three hours, and you do not know at departure what the weather will be at the destination on arrival. Kalgoorlie to Laverton crosses country where a forced landing means a long wait for rescue. The flight to Rottnest crosses open water. Your passengers may bring a dog, a bag of farm chemicals or a parachute. So the PPL subtopic adds the planning and loading decisions a pilot makes before going somewhere new. The exam asks them as "is an alternate required?", "how much holding fuel?" and "may this be carried?", and it uses the exact figures, so the figures are here with the reasoning that makes them easier to remember.

## Before flight: from checking the aeroplane to planning the flight

The RPL list in [RFRC 2.4](/notes/RFRC/2.4) applies in full: fuel and oil quantity physically checked and reserves carried, refuelling precautions, a safe engine start, the passenger briefing, control locks removed, doors and caps secure, controls checked, the aircraft clean of frost and ice, instruments checked, harnesses secured, fuel drains checked and children restrained. Those duties are about the **aeroplane in front of you**.

At PPL level you add duties about the **flight ahead of you**. On a local flight the plan is in your head. On a long cross-country the plan carries the safety: the weather, the airspace, the fuel and the aerodromes are all things you can no longer see from the run-up bay. The pilot in command must:

- **obtain a weather and operational briefing**: the forecasts for the route and the aerodromes, and the NOTAMs. The weather decides whether the flight can be flown VFR at all, and the NOTAMs tell you what has changed since the charts were printed;
- **plan the route and cruising levels** so the flight complies with the VFR and the airspace, including the VMC minima, the hemispherical cruising levels and any clearance needed (see [RFRC 2.3](/notes/RFRC/2.3));
- **calculate the fuel**, including any **holding and alternate requirements** set out in the next section, on top of the fixed reserve;
- **check the weight and balance** for this load, because the PPL lets you fill every seat and the baggage area;
- **submit flight notification or a SARTIME** where required, so someone knows to look for you if you do not arrive (see [RFRC 2.7](/notes/RFRC/2.7));
- **confirm the aerodromes are suitable**: runway length against the performance charts (see [BAKC 6.2](/notes/BAKC/6.2)), whether **PPR** (prior permission required) applies, and **lighting** if you could arrive near last light.

![Who finds you if you do not arrive?](diagram:sartime-timeline "Nominate a SARTIME before flight, extend it before it expires, cancel it on landing; if it lapses, the search begins.")

The list answers one question: **will this flight still be safe at the far end?**

??? check "Check yourself: why does the PPL list add 'confirm the aerodromes are suitable' when the RPL list did not stress it?"
    At RPL level you mostly operate from aerodromes you know. As a PPL you plan into unfamiliar ones whose runway length, PPR requirements and lighting you can only learn from ERSA and NOTAMs. If you find a short runway or a PPR you lack only on arrival, the plan has already failed.

## Aerodrome meteorological minima: is an alternate needed?

### The problem the minima solve

You depart Jandakot for Albany in fine weather. Albany is two and a half hours away, and by the time you get there a band of low cloud may have come in off the Southern Ocean. You cannot see Albany from Jandakot, so you rely on the forecast. If the forecast is wrong, or if it says conditions might deteriorate, you need a way out: somewhere else to land, or enough fuel to wait until the weather clears. The **alternate minima** set the point at which the forecast is bad enough that you must plan that way out before you leave.

A VFR flight may depart only if the forecast for the **destination** allows a visual arrival. The forecast for the **departure aerodrome** matters as well if you may need to return. The Part 91 MOS and AIP ENR 1.1 set the **alternate minima for VFR flights** (Part 91 MOS Chapter 7; verify current text).

### The trigger figures

An **alternate aerodrome** (or holding fuel, where that option applies) is required when, for the period from **30 minutes before to 60 minutes after the ETA**, the forecast at the destination has:

- **more than scattered cloud** (more than 4 oktas) **below 1,500 ft** above the aerodrome;
- **visibility less than 8 km**;
- a **probability of fog, mist, dust, thunderstorms or other phenomena** that would reduce conditions below those figures (the PROB and TEMPO/INTER groups); or
- a **crosswind or tailwind component** beyond the aircraft's limits.

An alternate is also required when **no forecast is available**. In that case a flight may still depart if it can return and land within **1 hour**, and it must obtain a forecast within **30 minutes** of departure or return (check the current MOS wording).

Each figure has a reason. **More than scattered** means broken or overcast: a layer that forms a **ceiling** you cannot see through to the runway. **Below 1,500 ft** leaves too little room under it for a circuit at 1,000 ft above the aerodrome with margin. **Visibility below 8 km** is getting close to the 5,000 m VMC minimum, with little margin if the forecast is optimistic. And the **time window** exists because your ETA is an estimate. A headwind stronger than forecast can make you 30 minutes late, and the weather does not wait for you. The window is longer after ETA than before because delays are far more common than early arrivals.

![Which forecast change groups touch my arrival window?](diagram:taf-arrival-window "The window runs from 30 minutes before to 60 minutes after ETA; any change group that overlaps it and goes below the trigger figures counts.")

![What does 'more than scattered' look like?](diagram:cloud-amounts-in-oktas "SCT is 3 to 4 oktas; BKN and OVC, 5 oktas or more, form a ceiling. The alternate minima trigger on more than SCT below 1,500 ft.")

!!! warning "Common trap"
    "Scattered at 1,200 ft" does **not** trigger the cloud criterion: scattered is 3 to 4 oktas, and the trigger is **more than** 4 oktas. "Broken at 1,400 ft" does. Read the cloud amount before the cloud height.

### Holding fuel instead of an alternate

Sometimes the forecast says the destination will be fine except for a **temporary** deterioration: a shower passing through, or fog lifting slowly. Diverting a hundred miles to an alternate for a shower that will be gone in half an hour makes little sense. You can wait for it to pass instead, provided you carry the fuel to wait. That is the **holding fuel** option.

The two change groups describe how long a deterioration lasts (see [RMTC 2.2](/notes/RMTC/2.2) for reading them in a TAF): **TEMPO** for temporary changes, each lasting up to an hour; **INTER** for intermittent changes, each lasting less than 30 minutes. The holding fuel follows the same logic: **60 minutes** for a TEMPO and **30 minutes** for an INTER deterioration below the minima (Part 91 MOS; verify current figures). **PROB** conditions are treated in the same way as the group they qualify. You carry enough to outlast the worst spell the forecast describes.

![How long does each change group last?](diagram:taf-change-groups "TEMPO spells last up to an hour, INTER fluctuations under 30 minutes: the holding fuel matches each.")

If instead you plan an **alternate**, it must itself **meet the alternate minima** for your arrival period there. An alternate under the same band of cloud is no alternate. You must also carry **fuel to fly to it plus the fixed reserve**. Holding or alternate fuel is always in addition to the fixed reserve, never instead of it, because the fixed reserve is the fuel you plan never to use.

!!! example "Worked example: holding fuel for an INTER group"
    You plan a flight from Jandakot to Geraldton with an ETA of 0400 UTC. The Geraldton TAF has main conditions of wind 200 at 15 knots, CAVOK, with **INTER 0300/0700 3,000 m SHRA BKN012**. Your aeroplane burns 32 L/h.

    1. **Window**: 30 minutes before to 60 minutes after ETA is **0330 to 0500 UTC**.
    2. **Main conditions**: CAVOK, above the minima. **No trigger.**
    3. **INTER group**: from 0300 to 0700, overlapping the whole window. It forecasts 3,000 m visibility (less than 8 km) and broken cloud at 1,200 ft (more than scattered below 1,500 ft). **Below the minima: alternate or holding fuel required.**
    4. **Holding option**: INTER means **30 minutes** of holding fuel.
    $$32\ \text{L/h} \times 0.5\ \text{h} = \mathbf{16\ L}$$
    5. **Total**: trip fuel + any variable reserve + **16 L holding** + the fixed reserve.

    If the showers arrive as you do, you hold clear of them for up to 30 minutes and still land with your fixed reserve intact.

Now try the one that varies what the exam varies: the change group and the trigger.

The Esperance TAF for your ETA of 0630 UTC has main conditions of 9999 and SCT025, with **TEMPO 0700/1000 4000 DZ BKN010**. Your aeroplane burns 36 L/h. Is holding fuel needed, and if so how much?

??? solution "Show the solution"
    1. **Window**: 0600 to 0730 UTC.
    2. **Main conditions**: 9999 and scattered at 2,500 ft. Visibility 10 km or more and cloud not more than scattered, so **no trigger**.
    3. **TEMPO group**: starts at 0700, inside the window. It forecasts 4,000 m in drizzle and broken cloud at 1,000 ft, both below the minima. **Alternate or holding fuel required.**
    4. **Holding option**: TEMPO means **60 minutes**.
    $$36\ \text{L/h} \times 1\ \text{h} = \mathbf{36\ L}$$
    The window catches a TEMPO that begins half an hour after your ETA. That is the reason the window runs to 60 minutes after ETA.

??? check "Check yourself: why is the holding fuel for a TEMPO twice that for an INTER?"
    Because the groups describe deteriorations of different length. An INTER spell lasts less than 30 minutes and a TEMPO spell up to an hour, so the holding fuel is sized to outlast the longest spell each one forecasts: 30 and 60 minutes.

## Carrying people, cargo and animals

Everything you load changes the flight. It adds weight, it moves the centre of gravity, it may be able to move, block an exit, interfere with the controls or harm someone. Each of the carriage rules below applies that idea to one kind of load. As pilot in command you decide what comes on board, and you are responsible for it once it does.

### Cargo

Picture a hard landing, or turbulence over the Darling Scarp, with a 20 kg box of engine parts loose on the back seat. The cargo rule stops that box from becoming a projectile. Cargo must be:

- **secured** so it cannot shift or injure anyone;
- placed so it does **not block the exits** or the **pilot's access to the controls**;
- within the **floor loading limits** and the aeroplane's **loading system**;
- **included in the weight and balance**.

Cargo carried in a passenger seat area must be **restrained to the seat's limits**. A seat belt is designed for a person's weight and shape, and a heavy box strapped into a seat may exceed what that seat and its belt are certified to hold.

### Sick and handicapped persons

A sick or handicapped person **may be carried** if the pilot in command is satisfied they can be **carried safely and will not endanger the flight**. They must **not occupy a seat where they could obstruct an exit or the controls**, and they **should be briefed and assisted**. The logic is the exit: in an emergency every occupant must be able to get out, and someone who cannot move quickly must not be the one between the others and the door.

A person who is **medically unfit to fly**, for example after surgery, with a contagious disease, or needing oxygen, needs **medical advice and the equipment to cope**. Altitude lowers the oxygen available (see [PHFC 2.12](/notes/PHFC/2.12)), cabin pressure changes affect recent surgical wounds, and a light aeroplane carries no medical help. A private pilot should think hard before carrying such a passenger.

### Parachutists

Parachute descents are regulated by **CASR Part 105**. The pilot must hold the **required experience and approvals**. The operation must be conducted by an **approved parachute organisation** at an **approved drop zone**. Parachutists are carried under the **restraint and briefing rules in Part 105**, and the drop is **coordinated with ATS**. A private pilot cannot casually drop a parachutist. The reasons are an open door in flight, people moving about the cabin near the controls, and bodies falling through airspace other aircraft are using. Each needs its own procedures and training.

### Flotation equipment

The flight to Rottnest is short, but for part of it you cannot glide to land. If the engine stops there, you ditch. The flotation rule follows the glide:

- **Life jackets** must be carried, and worn or readily accessible, for each person when a **single-engine aeroplane flies over water beyond gliding distance of land** (and for larger aircraft on longer over-water sectors). They must be **within reach of the seated occupant**, because after a ditching there is no time to climb into the back for a locker.
- **Life rafts and signalling equipment** are required for **extended over-water flights** beyond the distances in the Part 91 MOS (Chapter 26; verify current distances).

The trigger is **gliding distance of land**, not a fixed distance from the coast, so it depends on your height. A climb to a higher level makes your glide longer, and a stretch of water you could not cross within gliding distance at 1,500 ft may be within gliding distance at 6,500 ft (see [RBKA 3.4](/notes/RBKA/3.4) on the glide).

![When do life jackets become mandatory?](diagram:overwater-gliding-distance "The same crossing can be beyond gliding distance of land at 1,500 ft and within it at 6,500 ft: the rule follows the glide, not the coastline.")

### Survival equipment

Western Australia's interior is a place where a survivable forced landing can turn fatal for lack of water. So flights over **designated remote areas**, shown on the remote area map in the AIP and ERSA, must carry the survival equipment prescribed in the Part 91 MOS: **water**, an **ELT**, **signalling equipment** and a **first aid kit**. In Western Australia most of the interior is designated remote. The list matches the survival priorities (see [PHFC 2.14](/notes/PHFC/2.14)): treat injuries, help rescuers find you, and stay alive with water until they do.

![What do you need after a forced landing in remote country?](diagram:survival-priorities "Protection, location, water, food: the remote-area equipment covers the first three, and staying with the aircraft makes location easier.")

### Animals

Animals may be carried **only with the pilot in command's consent**, **restrained in a container or harness** so they cannot interfere with the controls or the occupants, and with their **weight included in the load**. A frightened kelpie in the footwell is one of the more dangerous things you can put in a cockpit. **Assistance animals** have specific rights under the transport rules.

### Dangerous goods

**CASR Part 92** prohibits the carriage of **dangerous goods** except as permitted. These are things that can burn, explode, corrode or poison in an aircraft: **flammable liquids and gases, explosives, corrosives, oxidisers, and lithium batteries in bulk**. Passengers may carry **limited quantities of specified items**: toiletries, medicines, and small lithium batteries in devices. The pilot in command must **know what is on board** and **refuse anything not permitted**. Ask before loading: passengers rarely think of camping gas or pool chlorine as dangerous.

!!! example "Worked example: loading for a station trip"
    You are flying a family of three from Jandakot to a station strip north-east of Meekatharra, over a designated remote area, with a 15 kg box of groceries and their working dog. On the apron they produce the following. Work through each item.

    1. **The dog**: with your **consent**, in a **crate secured** in the baggage area, its **weight in the load sheet**. **Allowed.**
    2. **The groceries**: **secured** in the baggage area by the cargo net, clear of the exits, inside the baggage area's **floor limit**, and in the **weight and balance**. **Allowed.**
    3. **A 4 kg pressurised camping gas canister**: a **flammable gas** and a **dangerous good** under Part 92, and not one of the limited items passengers may carry. **Refuse**: it goes by road.
    4. **Phones, a laptop and the grandmother's medicines**: small lithium batteries in devices and medicines are within the **limited quantities** passengers may carry. **Allowed.**
    5. **Survival equipment**: the route is over a remote area, so you need **water, an ELT, signalling equipment and a first aid kit** on board. You confirm all four are loaded. **Required and carried.**

    The grandmother uses a walking frame. You seat her where she does not block the door and brief the family on helping her out in an emergency.

!!! warning "Common trap"
    "The passenger packed it, so it is their responsibility" is wrong. The pilot in command must know what is on board and refuse what is not permitted. Ask about the bags before you load them, not after a smell of fuel fills the cabin.

??? check "Check yourself: why do life jackets have to be within reach of the seated occupant, rather than stowed together in the baggage area?"
    After a ditching the cabin fills quickly and the aeroplane may sink within minutes. Nobody will have time to climb into the back for a locker, so each person must be able to reach a life jacket from their seat.

## The radio: test before taxi, listen in flight

### Testing the radio before you move

The radio is your link to the other aircraft and to ATS. A fault you find in the run-up bay costs you a few minutes, while the same fault found on a busy CTAF downwind costs a great deal more. So **before taxiing, test the radio**:

- select the **correct frequency**;
- check the **squelch and volume**;
- make a **radio check call** where appropriate.

How you confirm the radio works depends on the aerodrome. At a **controlled aerodrome**, the **taxi call** confirms two-way communication: if the controller answers and you hear them, both directions work. At a **non-controlled aerodrome**, a **radio check on the CTAF**, or a check against the **AWIS or ATIS**, confirms the receiver. Signal quality is reported on the **readability scale**, from **1 (unreadable)** to **5 (perfectly readable)**.

### The listening watch

A radio is no use if you are not listening. In flight, an aircraft with a VHF radio must **maintain a continuous listening watch** on the appropriate frequency (AIP GEN 3.4):

- the **CTAF** when **in the vicinity of a non-controlled aerodrome**, which is within **10 nm** and at the aerodrome;
- the **ATC frequency** when **in or near controlled airspace**;
- otherwise the **area frequency** for the flight information service.

Where you are able, **monitor 121.5 MHz** as well. That is the emergency frequency, and an aircraft in distress, or an ELT, may be heard on it.

The logic follows who needs to reach you. Near a non-controlled aerodrome the other circuit traffic needs to reach you, and they are on the CTAF. In or near controlled airspace ATC may need to. In the cruise away from both, the flight information service may need to pass you traffic or weather, or relay a SARTIME message.

**Carriage of a VHF radio** is **mandatory** in **controlled airspace**, at **certified and military aerodromes**, in **CTAF(R) areas** and for flight **above 5,000 ft**. It is **required for all flights at registered aerodromes** and **strongly recommended everywhere else** (verify the current requirements in the Part 91 MOS and the VFRG).

!!! example "Worked example: which frequency, when?"
    A flight from Jandakot to Northam, then on to Kellerberrin.

    1. **Taxiing at Jandakot** (controlled): the ground frequency. Your taxi call is answered, so **two-way communication is confirmed**.
    2. **Departing** through the control zone: the **tower frequency**, then the ATC frequency while near controlled airspace.
    3. **Cruising over the Wheatbelt** in Class G, away from aerodromes: the **area frequency**, with 121.5 MHz on the second radio if you have one.
    4. **Within 10 nm of Northam**: the **Northam CTAF**, where the other circuit traffic is listening.
    5. **Departing Northam**: CTAF until clear of the vicinity, then the area frequency again until within 10 nm of Kellerberrin, then that CTAF.

    At each stage you listen where whoever may need to reach you is transmitting.

??? check "Check yourself: at a non-controlled aerodrome with nobody else on the CTAF, how can you confirm your radio receives before taxiing?"
    Listen to the AWIS (or ATIS) broadcast on its frequency: if you hear it clearly, the receiver works. A radio check call on the CTAF confirms transmission only if someone answers.

## Bringing it together

Easter Saturday: a PPL flight from Jandakot to Esperance with two friends and one of their dogs. On Friday you get the area forecast, the TAFs and the NOTAMs. The Esperance TAF has a TEMPO for showers and broken cloud at 1,200 ft starting 30 minutes after your ETA, which is inside the 30-before-to-60-after window. You either plan an alternate that itself meets the minima or carry 60 minutes of holding fuel, and with Esperance's forecast you choose holding. Your fuel plan becomes trip fuel plus 60 minutes holding plus the fixed reserve. You run the weight and balance and trim the bags to fit. Part of the route crosses a remote area, so water, the ELT, signalling equipment and the first aid kit go in. On Saturday morning you refuse a gas canister from the camping gear, secure the bags and the crate, and lodge a SARTIME. In the run-up bay the tower answers your taxi call, so the radio works. Across the Wheatbelt you listen on the area frequency with 121.5 MHz on the second radio, and within 10 nm of Esperance you change to its CTAF. Every decision on that list was made before you could see Esperance.

??? check "Check yourself: in this flight, which two decisions depended on the timing of the TEMPO group, and why?"
    Whether an alternate or holding fuel was needed at all (the TEMPO fell inside the window from 30 minutes before to 60 minutes after ETA) and how much holding fuel to carry (60 minutes, because each TEMPO spell may last up to an hour).

## Key points

- **2.4.1 (a)** On top of the RPL duties, plan the flight: weather and NOTAM briefing, a route and levels that comply with VFR and the airspace, fuel including holding and alternate requirements, weight and balance, flight notification or a SARTIME, and aerodromes confirmed suitable (runway length, PPR, lighting).
- **2.4.1 (b)** Alternate (or holding fuel) required if, from 30 minutes before to 60 minutes after ETA, the destination forecast has more than scattered cloud below 1,500 ft, visibility under 8 km, a probability of fog, mist, dust or thunderstorms below those figures, or an excessive crosswind or tailwind; or if there is no forecast. Holding: 60 minutes for TEMPO, 30 for INTER. An alternate must itself meet the minima, and all of it is on top of the fixed reserve.
- **2.4.1 (c)** Cargo secured, clear of exits and controls, within floor limits and in the weight and balance. Sick and handicapped passengers carried only if safe, not by an exit or the controls, and briefed. Parachuting only under Part 105. Life jackets within reach beyond gliding distance of land in a single-engine aeroplane. Water, ELT, signalling equipment and first aid kit over remote areas. Animals with consent, restrained and weighed. Dangerous goods refused except the limited passenger items Part 92 permits.
- **2.4.2** Test the radio before taxi (frequency, squelch and volume, check call or AWIS/ATIS); keep a listening watch on the CTAF within 10 nm of a non-controlled aerodrome, ATC in or near controlled airspace, the area frequency otherwise, and 121.5 MHz where able.

!!! tip "Exam tip"
    The alternate minima are the examinable numbers here: more than scattered cloud below 1,500 ft, visibility under 8 km, or a probability of fog, dust or thunderstorms, for the period 30 minutes before to 60 minutes after ETA. Holding fuel: 60 minutes for TEMPO, 30 minutes for INTER. Read the cloud amount before its height ("scattered" never triggers), and check whether a change group overlaps the window, not just the ETA. For carriage questions, ask what the item could do in the cabin: shift, block an exit, distract the pilot, burn or leak. Confirm the figures in the current Part 91 MOS before the exam.
